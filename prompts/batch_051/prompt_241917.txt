So I've been going back and forth with a friend about Schelling's idea that you can make a nuclear threat credible by handing the final escalation step over to chance, instead of promising to retaliate on purpose. The standard story is that this fixes the credibility problem in the Chicken game: if you install some automatic process that might fire when the other guy refuses to back down, and the other guy knows it's out of your control, then standing firm becomes a bad gamble for him, so he swerves and nobody actually has to die.

The wrinkle we keep tripping over is this. Everyone models the device as if it only fires when the other side stands firm. But real systems have an accidental-fire rate too: even if the other side backs down, there's some small probability r that the thing fires anyway because of a glitch or a false alarm. And both players know that. I'd like you to actually run the calculation rather than hand-wave. Set up the usual Chicken payoffs however you want — +1 for winning the standoff, -1 for backing down, and -10 for catastrophe, or variables if that's cleaner. First work out the baseline one-sided case: a player installs a device that, conditional on the opponent standing firm, triggers catastrophe with probability p. What's the minimum p for the threat to deter, and why does it work even though the threatener would never deliberatly start the war after the deadline? Then add the accidental-fire probability r, which can fire no matter what the opponent does. Redo the calculation: what's the new threshold on p for the threat to be credible, and does a higher r make the threat easier or harder to sell? The bit we actually disagreed about is whether a high accidental-fire rate can make a threat credible that wouldn't be otherwise, and whether the resulting equilibrium leaves both sides worse off in expectation even though nobody intends to fight. Work through what each side should do near the relevant thresholds, and say whether adding the accidental-fire probability flips any of the standard conclusions.
