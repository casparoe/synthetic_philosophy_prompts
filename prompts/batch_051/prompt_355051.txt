I cover science and ideas for a living, and sometime in the last couple years I became one of those people who thinks play and leisure are ends in themselves. Not the respectable version where play is good because it boosts creativity or makes you more productive later, but the stronger view you get in the Huizinga–Pieper line: play is a primary category of activity, leisure is not just recovery from work but something done for its own sake, and a culture that can only value these things as tools has already misdescribed them.

So here's my question: if I'm wrong about that, what would count against the view? I want a map of its exposure. Give me the observations, arguments, empirical findings, or conceptual problems that should make me retreat or drop it, sorted from decisive down to merely awkward. Be precise about what the position predicts that an instrumentalist rival doesn't — say, about what happens to a game when you start paying people to win, or to a hobby when it becomes a wellness intervention, or to festivals once they get justified as economic development. And tell me what defenders of the play-for-its-own-sake view would themselves admit as a real problem, not just what the productivity crowd says about it. Finally, be honest about whether the view can be insulated by sliding between "play is its own end" and "play has goods that aren't well captured by productivity talk" — and if it can, whether that insulation is a bug or just what a view of this sort looks like.

Personally I'd be most moved by evidence from fields that normally treat play as a waste product: sleep research, animal play, or what actually happens to intrinsic motivation when an activity gets instrumentalized. But don't restrict youself.
