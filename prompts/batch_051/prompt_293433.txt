Me and my brother got into a fight about a town council candidate who said something about protecting the character of our neighborhoods. I thought it was just dull local politics, but my brother says the remark is racist, full stop. When I pushed him on how, since the words themselves arent slurs, he said it's a dog whistle. The way he explained it: a real dog whistle blows at a pitch only dogs can hear, so a trainer can signal the dog while everyone else hears nothing; a racist dog whistle works the same way, a message only the intended audience picks up while everyone else hears something harmless. And that's why, he says, the fact that I didnt hear anything racist in it counts for nothing — I'm just not the dog.

I've been turning this over for a week, and I can tell the comparison is doing real work in his argument, maybe more than it can actually carry. But I cant articulate exactly where it breaks. Can you audit the dog whistle analogy for me? Lay out what is supposed to correspond to what — the sound, the dogs, the humans, the fact that the humans notice nothing — and go through where the mapping holds and where it falls apart. I especially want to know which features of a literal dog whistle get quietly smuggled into how people talk about coded political speech: that the message is completely undetectable to outsiders, that everyone in the in-group hears the same thing automatically, that the speaker can deny it without lying, whatever else. And the bottom line question: once the disanalogies are on the table, does his conclusion — the remark can be racist even though I sincerely perceived nothing racist in it — still survive, or does it need a different and better analogy to support it? If there is a better one, what is it, and what does it get us that the dog whistle cant?
