Construct a realistic one-paragraph thought experiment that isolates the gains-from-trade mechanism in moral compromise. The case should have two parties with distinct value systems, each of which contains strong duties against two contested practices, X and Y. The two systems must weight the duties oppositely: one regards violating the duty against X as graver than violating the duty against Y, the other the reverse. In the compromise, each party violates the duty it ranks as lesser in exchange for the other's ceasing the practice it ranks as graver. Each party must judge the resulting arrangement an improvement over the status quo by its own standards, while treating its own concession as a genuine wrong rather than a mere preference. Set it in an ordinary workplace, hospital, or municipal context; the parties must be reasonable, well-informed, and free of coercion. The point is to test whether deontological side constraints survive a case in which trading a duty leaves each side better off by its own lights, so the case must generate the verdicts rather than stipulate them.

Then take definite verdicts from two standpoints. First, a side-constraint deontologist, who holds that an agent may not violate a duty even when doing so secures a countervailing moral gain by that agent's own standards. Second, a compromise-friendly moral bargainer, who holds that concessions between value systems are justified when the exchange is a Pareto improvement by each system's own lights. Walk through what each says about the deal and why. Then identify the single feature of the case that drives the two verdicts apart, explain what changes when that feature is removed, and finish with the strongest objection the side-constraint deontologist could press against the bargainer's reading of the case.
