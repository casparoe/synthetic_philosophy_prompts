I'm a novelist and I've caught myself following a rule while I revise: if a belief about the book is doing emotional work for me — like 'the slow middle is just patient pacing' or 'that rejection doesn't count because the agent was overworked' — I mark it as not-evidence and refuse to let it guide edits. I thought this was Pascal's-wager hygiene: if the main reason to hold a belief is that it's useful to hold, that's the wrong kind of reason, and I don't want to be the writer who believes the novel is good because she cant afford not to. But I think I've overcorrected. Sometimes 'the middle is patient' is both comforting and true, and my rule still bars it, which is just throwing information away. Is this policy defensible, or am I misapplying the whole wrong-kind-of-reasons idea? What would a better version of the rule look like — one that lets me rely on useful beliefs without losing track of whether they're actually true? Also, please answer in American English.
