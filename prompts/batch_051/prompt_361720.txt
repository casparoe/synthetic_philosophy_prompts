Me and my freind went back and forth for like two hours last night about whether a human langauge could genuinely lack pronouns and indexicals — not just avoid them stylistically, but have no words for "I," "you," "here," "now," "this," "that." He kept saying it's impossible in practice because you couldn't coordinate anything or indicate what you're talking about, and I kept saying "impossible in practice" isn't the interesting question: the interesting question is what thought and communication would be like for people whose language forced them to route around deixis entirely, using names, kin terms, roles, place-descriptions, distances, clock-times, whatever they had to invent instead. He thinks the speakers would just mentally possess indexicals anyway and the language would be a weird surface veneer; I'm not so sure. Maybe the language would reshape what it's like to be a person in that world in ways that are hard to state from the outside.

So I want to think through it as fiction instead of more argument. Could you write a short story set in a community whose language truly has no pronouns or indexicals, told from the point of view of an outsider linguist or trader who's been there long enough to half-understand how they talk? Give it one concrete scene where the absence of "I" and "now" and "here" actually matters — a rescue, a quarrel, a marriage proposal, a funny misunderstanding, anything like that. Don't make it a lecture where characters explain the philosophy to one another; i want the language itself to do the work in the story.

And since a story can't be exactly right about the philosophy, add a short afterword: where does the story take liberties with the actual linguistic and philosophical issues, where does it lean on a contested reading of what an indexical is, and how confident are you in the moves the story makes — what's the single point in the afterword you're least sure of?
