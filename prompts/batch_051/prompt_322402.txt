So I've been rereading Turing's 1950 *Mind* paper with a reading group, and we ended up arguing about whether passing the imitation game is *sufficient* for genuine mindedness, as opposed to just being a good conversational simulation. I started compiling the standard objections, and I keep finding myself stuck at the same set. Here's what I have so far:

- Searle's Chinese Room: the system can produce the right input/output mappings without anyone or anything in it understanding what's going on.
- Block's Blockhead: a giant lookup table could in principle pass any finite behavioral test, so behavioral success doesn't require anything like general intelligence, just lots of stored input–output pairs.
- The ELIZA effect: humans over-ascribe mind to shallow linguistic patterns, so a test that relies on ordinary judges may be measuring our projection more than the machine's competence.
- The chauvinism problem in reverse: a language-heavy behavioral test would exclude locked-in humans, prelinguistic children, and maybe some nonhuman animals, so it clearly isn't *necessary* for mindedness even if it turns out to be sufficient.
- The "unnaturalness" worry: passing the test can reward a system that fakes the surface human limitations—typos, evasions, playing along with the convention—rather than exercising whatever capacities we actually care about.

I want one more objection that isn't just a restatement or special case of any of these. In particular, I don't want another version of "external behavior underdetermines internal states," because the Chinese Room and Blockhead already cover that from different angles. Is there something genuinely distinct I'm missing? And if there is, tell me honestly whether it's a real objection to behavioral criteria for mind or just a clever thought experiment that complicates the picture without changing it.
