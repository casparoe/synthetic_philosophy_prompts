I've been in a reading group on global labor justice for a few weeks now, and the thing I keep getting tangled up on is the debate about sweatshop wages. The pro-sweatshop side (if I can call it that) says a lot of workers are better off in these jobs than they would be in their next best alternative—say, scavenging or subsistence farming—so buying from sweatshops, or just not banning them, is actually good for the workers on balance. The other side still wants to say no, these arrangements are exploitative: the company profits massively, the worker is desperate, and the choice to take the job doesn't feel like a real choice. I've seen the phrase mutually beneficial exploitation thrown around, and honestly it sounds like a flat contradiction to me. If the worker is better off by their own lights and they consented to the arrangement, what exactly is left for the moral complaint to be about?

I'd love an explanation that gets under the surface of that specific puzzle. In particular: how can one and the same voluntary exchange be both beneficial to the worker and wrong? Is the wrongness located in the terms of the deal themselves, in the background conditions of poverty that make the worker's consent not fully free, or in the employer's attitude in taking advantage of that desperation? And there's a move I've heard made—roughly, that if an exchange makes someone better off and doesn't worsen their situation relative to the status quo, then it can't be morally objectionable. Could you unpack that claim too and say what the strongest response to it is? I'm less interested in a bibliography than in just getting the concepts straight, maybe with a concrete example to make it intuitive.
