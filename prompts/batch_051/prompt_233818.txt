As part of a faculty reading group on hedonism, two colleagues have been tangled up over a specific passage in Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics, Book II, Chapter III. In that passage Sidgwick argues, to paraphrase, that the hedonistic impulse, when it becomes dominant, tends to defeat its own end because pleasure is best attained not by making it the explicit object of pursuit but by directing attention toward other activities. One colleague reads this as a purely instrumental or psychological claim: pleasure is still the only rational ultimate end, and Sidgwick is merely warning that the direct pursuit of it is causally inefficient. The other reads it as a stronger, constitutive claim about the structure of pleasure: pleasure cannot serve as the intentional object of a successful ultimate aim in the way egoistic hedonism requires, because the experience of pleasure is dependent on the pursuit of other ends. The first replied that the stronger reading makes nonsense of Sidgwick's later willingness to treat egoistic hedonism as one of the rational methods, alongside intuitionism, even if it issues in the dualism of practical reason. The second replied that the later argument requires only that egoistic hedonism be formally coherent as an abstract maxim, not that it be a state of mind one can stably occupy; the passage, she said, explains why common sense recoils from the pure egoist. Please adjudicate their disagreement point by point, say which colleague is right about which claims, and state which reading of the passage is more defensible overall. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then reason without preamble or restating the question.
