In a discussion group I belong to, one participant has been defending a rigid view about the word "civility." He insists that it can properly mean only what the Latin civilitas meant — something like conduct befitting a citizen — and that the later senses (politeness, decorum, even the tolerant tone of public discourse) are corruptions of the word's real meaning. He keeps summing this up with a one-liner: "The true meaning of a word is the meaning it had in the beginning." When I answered that "awful" once meant roughly "worthy of awe" and "nice" once meant "foolish," and asked whether he wanted his principle to apply there too, he said yes: we have simply become sloppy, and the original senses remain the words' real meanings underneath all the later drift.

My professor calls this kind of move the etymological fallacy — roughly, the idea that a word's earlier meaning settles what it now means, or should mean. But I suspect the slogan collapses several different commitments, and that they should not be accepted or rejected as a block. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims this sentence could be making — about where meaning resides, whether semantic change can be a mistake, what an etymology actually establishes, and what authority the history of a word gives us over present usage — and say for each whether it is trivial, plausible, or false, and which of them would actually support the conclusion that "civility" is being used incorrectly today?
