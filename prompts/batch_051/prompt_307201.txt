My housemate keeps insisting that liberal societies are weirdly complacent about open conflict—protests, partisan media, school-board fights—and that a "harmony first" political culture would be better than liberal pluralism's habit of just letting groups clash in public. He proposed a concrete version of this: a city would create a Civic Harmony Board with real power. Any group organizing a public rally above a certain size, any school adopting a new textbook or curriculum, and any local ordinance touching on religion, family, or ethnic communities would have to go through the Board first. The Board wouldn't need to find a rights violation or a clear harm; its test would be whether the thing would, in its judgment, "sharply undermine trust and mutual accommodation between recognized communities." If so, it could impose a cooling-off period, demand changes, or block the proposal outright. The Board would be appointed, not elected, with seats reserved for leaders of recognized cultural and religious associations, and its decisions could be overturned only by a two-thirds vote of the city council.

I don't want to argue about whether harmony is a nice ideal. I want to know how this thing would actually behave if it existed. Who would capture it, and what would organizers, school boards, and local politicians do once they learned to predict its judgments? Would conflicts go underground, get relabelled as technical adjustments, or migrate to other cities and online spaces? And does the mechanism actually deliver anything like the Confucian or conservative image of social order and harmony, or does it just add one more arena for exactly the conflict it's supposed to suppress? If some of the failure modes are fixable—for example with sunset clauses, random selection of members, the right of a community to opt out, or a rule that the Board can only delay rather than block decisions—which fixes would help without gutting the whole scheme?
