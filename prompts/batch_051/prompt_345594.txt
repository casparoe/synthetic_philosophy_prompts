I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a course on judgment and decision-making, and we have just finished the unit on the sunk cost fallacy. The conclusion we were given is clean: only forward-looking costs and benefits matter, and time or money already spent should be ignored except insofar as it bears on future outcomes. In the abstract I accept this, but I have noticed that I follow a personal policy that seems to violate it, and I want to know whether the policy can be defended or is only a sophisticated rationalization.

The policy covers long projects into which I have sunk substantial time: a research placement, a language I have been studying for two years, a difficult course sequence, a half-finished novel. I will not abandon such a project unless I can identify something that has concretely changed since I began—new information about feasibility, a hard external constraint, a change in what I actually need the project for. What I refuse to count as a change is my own having re-run the expected-value calculation and found it negative. In effect, I treat the sunk cost as a standing epistemic filter: when my present self concludes that a project is a mistake but cannot name anything that has changed since I started, I discount the present estimate as mood-driven noise and continue. My justification is that my evaluations fluctuate badly; on a low week nearly every long project looks like an error, and on a high week nearly every one looks wise. The accumulated investment is a stable, externalized record of the judgment that emerged across many moods over time, so it anchors me against flitting between projects in response to transient states.

My question is whether this rule is rationally defensible, or whether I have simply found a way to launder the sunk cost fallacy through a story about noise. If some amended version is defensible, what would the amendments be—should the anchor's force decay over time, should there be fixed checkpoints at which re-evaluation is allowed regardless of mood, should I distinguish the value of finishing from the cost already borne, or would that distinction dissolve the rule? And how could I tell from the inside whether the policy is protecting me from noisy self-evaluation or entrenching me in a genuine mistake?
