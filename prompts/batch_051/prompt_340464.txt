I teach moral philosophy and serve as the non-clinical member of a fertility clinic's policy review group. The group meets monthly and reviews about eight requests for assisted reproduction, including cases involving known genetic conditions and requests for embryo selection. I need a decision procedure that the clinic's physicians and administrators — none of whom have philosophical training — can actually run in the twenty minutes we have per case. The clinic director must sign off on every case in which the group declines treatment, so the procedure has to make its value judgments explicit enough to defend in front of a nonspecialist.

I want the procedure to encode the procreation asymmetry. The view is this: the absence of pain is good even when no one exists to enjoy that absence, whereas the absence of pleasure is not bad unless someone exists and is deprived of it. So we have a reason not to bring into existence a person who will suffer, but no corresponding reason to bring into existence a person merely because that person would be happy. Please do not quietly weaken the asymmetry into the commonplace that we should not create lives of net suffering; the asymmetry's entire bite is that a life's expected happiness does not offset the reason given by its expected pain.

But there is a complication the procedure must handle rather than suppress. The clinic is often not choosing between creating a child and creating no child at all. Many patients have already decided to have a family, and we are choosing which of several possible children to help bring about. It is one thing to ask whether a life is worth starting; it is another to ask which possible child should exist once it has been settled that some child will. The procedure should therefore contain a step that distinguishes bare creation from selection among possible children, and it should say how the answer changes the analysis.

Please write a checklist or ordered sequence of questions that a physician could administer, with clear criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a refusal, where it continues with approval or counseling, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts.

Then apply it to three cases:

(a) A couple, both carriers of a severe inherited condition, request IVF without genetic testing or selection. In affected children, the condition causes severe chronic pain from early childhood and death by late adolescence. The couple says they will accept and love whatever child results.

(b) A Deaf couple with a family history of hereditary deafness request preimplantation genetic diagnosis so they can select an embryo that shares their deafness. They regard deafness as a cultural identity rather than a harm.

(c) A couple request preimplantation genetic diagnosis to select against an inherited condition that is generally manageable with treatment but that imposes real, ongoing limitations. The treating physician believes the condition is not serious enough to justify selection; the couple disagrees.

Finally, say at least one place where your procedure returns a recommendation the clinic will be uncomfortable with, and say clearly what the procedure cannot decide.
