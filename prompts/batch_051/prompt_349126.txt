Okay, here's what I'm after: a sorting quiz, like one of those 'which character are you' magazine quizzes except it's for the debate over whether practical stakes make a difference to knowledge. I want it to sort someone into one of the main positions around pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism, but without the quiz ever mentioning the labels to the respondent. Assume the respondent is a smart undergrad who has read a little epistemology but doesn't know the technical vocabulary.

The positions to sort into are roughly: (a) purist invariantism — stakes dont change whether someone knows, they only change what it's sensible to do or say on the basis of the belief; (b) subject-sensitive / interest-relative invariantism — what changes with stakes is the subject's knowledge itself, even though 'knows' keeps one meaning; (c) contextualism — what changes is the standard of 'knows' in the mouth of the ascriber, so 'S knows p' can shift truth-value by context; and (d) an assessment-sensitive/relativist option where a low-stakes and a high-stakes speaker genuinely disagree but can each be right from their own standpoint. If you can fit a split between (a) and a nearby view where high stakes make 'S knows' unassertable but not literally false, that would be great too.

The angle I want: what exactly goes wrong in the high-stakes case — the truth of the sentence, the subject's title to the knowledge, or just the propriety of saying/acting on it. Build the quiz around that, not around which philosopher the respondent likes.

Give me 8 to 10 concrete forced-choice items. Use cases like the bank on Saturday, a changed airport gate, a peanut allergy and a food label, a bet for a dollar versus your house, two strangers overhearing each other at a bar. Each item should give a tight scenario and a forced choice — 'what should she do,' 'which claim is true,' 'are these two people really disagreeing' — phrased so someone can answer from the gut without knowing what position their answer feeds. No escape hatches where the respondent gets to say what their theory is.

Then give me a scoring key. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: show which questions do the separating, and for each answer-combination nobody seems to hold, say wether the cell is empty because it's incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because nobody's gotten around to defending it. If two positions collapse into the same answer pattern under your questions, flag that explicitly and say what extra question would split them if you had one more slot.

And at the very end, write a short author's note (this is the self-critical part) saying where the scoring key leans on a contested reading of one of these views — for instance whether you're reading position (b) as requiring that the subject's belief actually be destroyed in the high-stakes case, or just that the belief stops being eligible to ground action. Then tell me which single question's answer, if the respondent went the other way, would most rearrange the whole layout. Casual tone's fine; a typo or two is fine.
