The principle I started from is this: when choosing among acts, an act's rational choice-worthiness is its expected value, understood as the sum, over all possible outcomes, of the probability of each outcome multiplied by how good that outcome would be.

The argument:

(1) Take any act A that is certain to produce some finite amount of good, G.

(2) Choose any probability p greater than zero, no matter how small.

(3) Consider an alternative act B that produces nothing unless some low-probability event occurs, in which case it produces a very large finite amount of good, H.

(4) Suppose H is larger than G divided by p. Then B's expected value, namely p times H, is larger than A's expected value, which is G. This is just arithmetic: a sufficiently enormous payoff at a fixed positive probability outweighs a smaller certain payoff.

(5) Therefore, if we should always maximize expected value, then for every act with a certain finite payoff there is another act, with a minuscule probability of an astronomically large payoff, that we should prefer to it.

(6) So the expected-value principle commits us to judgments like this: it is rational to prefer a one-in-a-billion chance of an outcome more than a billion times better to a guaranteed finite good; and no guaranteed finite good can ever be our top choice, because we can make the probability smaller and the payoff correspondingly larger without end.

(7) That conclusion seems absurd on its face.
