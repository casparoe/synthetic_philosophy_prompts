For several years I have opened my graduate seminar on free will with the stock example of the digger wasp. The wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of its burrow, leaves it at the threshold, enters alone to inspect the nest, returns, and only then pulls the cricket inside. If an experimenter moves the cricket a few inches while the wasp is inside inspecting, the wasp does not simply drag it over the threshold upon returning; it drags the cricket back to the threshold and runs the entire inspection routine again — and this can be repeated indefinitely, with the wasp never doing the one thing that would actually solve its problem. The behavior looks purposive, even cautious, but the perturbation reveals a fixed loop. Sphexishness names that combination: apparent intelligence borne by mechanical rigidity.

A colleague of mine now defends the following claim: what separates an agent of the sort relevant to free will from the wasp is not that the agent lacks triggering conditions, but that the agent's behavior is revisable in a way the wasp's is not — upon perturbation, the agent can notice the intervention, re-represent the problem, and alter its means rather than re-enter the interrupted routine. She says genuine agency just is the opposite of sphexishness, and she insists the difference is at least partly empirical: under the right perturbations, the two kinds of system should come apart. I keep pressing back that her thesis is either a definition dressed up as a discovery or an untestable one, since any finite demonstration of flexibility can be redescribed as a larger library of blind routines.

I would like you to show that the debate can be made empirical without pretending it can be made fully empirical. Please do the following.

(1) Design the strongest concrete intervention, or short battery of interventions, that would separate merely high-dimensional sphexishness — a large set of routines, each one as blind as the wasp's — from genuinely revisable control. You may choose the population best suited to the test, whether human subjects, nonhuman animals, or a well-specified artificial system, but the design must be concrete enough that the result could in principle be observed.

(2) State precisely which result would support each reading, and say what a positive result — for instance, subjects reliably bypassing the interrupted routine or improvising a new means — would and would not show about the colleague's claim.

(3) Address the hardest skeptical reply: that any finite battery of tests can be absorbed into a more complicated set of routines, so that what we call flexibility is always relative to our level of description. Does your test do better than this, or does it merely push the threat back one step?

(4) Conclude by identifying what no such test could settle in principle — for example, whether the capacity successfully demonstrated deserves the name free will rather than merely non-sphexish control, and whether the revisability at issue is itself only higher-order rigidity.
