I spent my working life in isotope geochemistry, so I am used to the quiet embarrassment that "before present" means before 1950, and that every date I ever produced rested on a convention somebody else chose. Since retiring I've been reading philosophy, and I keep coming back to a bigger, civilian version of the same problem: our year numbering starts from a parochial event, and most of human history ends up in negative numbers, like an accounting error. I've been mulling over alot what a calendar is actually for.

Here is the exercise I'd like you to walk me through. Suppose a new international commision is designing a global civil calendar from scratch and must pick a year zero. The candidates on the table are: (a) keep the traditional epoch, i.e. the presumed birth of Christ, give or take a few years; (b) the date of the earliest surviving written documents, roughly 3000 BC; (c) add ten thousand years to the current CE count so that all of recorded history comes out positive—I've seen this called the Holocene or Human Era; (d) the Unix epoch, 1 January 1970, chosen purely for computational convenience; (e) the first controlled nuclear chain reaction, on the grounds that that is when our power to alter the planet changed; (f) the first artificial satellite, on the grounds that we became a space-going species. Score each candidate from 0 to 10 as a choice of year zero, with a sentence of justification, and then tell me which single criterion—neutrality, continuity, computational ease, historical fit, future-proofing, or something else—is doing most of the work in your scoring. I am less interested in which candidate wins than in what the pattern of scores shows about what a calendar is for.
