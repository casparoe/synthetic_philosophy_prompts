heres a case I keep going back and forth on. I've been reading about consequentialist cluelessness — the idea that for a lot of genuinely important decisions, the long-run consequences branch out so far and so unpredictably that a consequentialist can't actually compute what they should do, and is better off admitting they're flying blind. I want to pressure-test it against a real episode: the decision to drop the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1945.

I'm not asking you to reargue whether the bombings were right. I'm asking what the cluelessness lens does to the case, in both directions. So first, be precise about the decision situation as the actors actually faced it: what did Truman and his advisors know at the time (they framed the choice mostly in expected-consequence terms — end the war fast, avoid a horrific invasion of the home islands, maybe signal to Stalin), what did they not know, and what did they think they knew but were actually just guessing about? The casualty estimates for an invasion were all over the place, and I know some scientists and advisors warned an arms race was coming; treat those as things that were 'in the room' rather than things only visible in retrospect.

Then run cluelessness over it. Which later consequences — the US–Soviet arms race, the Cold War's nuclear standoffs, the nuclear taboo, the non-proliferation regime, the fact that today most states with the capacity chose not to build bombs — were genuinely unforeeseeable in detail, and which were foreseeable enough that 'cluelessness' starts to look like a convenient alibi? Does the case confirm the cluelessness worry (namely, that the official expected-value story was made-up numbers and false precision), or does it instead show cluelessness is overstated because the broad direction of some big effects was forecastable? And then the payoff: what does this episode suggest about how a consequentialist should actually cope with cluelessness if they want to keep being a consequentialist — precaution, procedural constraints, treating deontic side-constraints as safe defaults, or just radical honesty about uncertainty? I want a verdict, not a 'we need more research' shrug.
