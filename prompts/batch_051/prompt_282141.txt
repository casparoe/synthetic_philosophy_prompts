The classic puzzle, of course, is: can God make a stone too heavy for him to lift? If yes, then there's something he can't lift; if no, then there's something he can't make. Either way, omnipotence looks internally inconsistent. Anselm's answer, as I understand it, is that the right response isn't to say God can't do these things but to say that the things in question aren't really abilities or powers at all. Being able to sin, lie, or be corrupted isn't a power but a defect, so lacking that ability is a perfection, not a limitation. I get the general shape of that move, but I'm suspicious that it's just redefining omnipotence to dodge the problem. It seems like there's a difference between saying an ability is morally bad and saying it isn't an ability at all. A surgeon might have the ability to botch an operation even if she never does, and we might think she's actually more excellent because she could but doesn't. So why couldn't God have those capacities and simply never exercise them? Or does that already assume God has unrealized potential, which maybe conflicts with something like divine simplicity?
