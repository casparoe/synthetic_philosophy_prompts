Ok here's an argument I half-believe and half-distrust—tell me whether it actually goes through. The conclusion is that no belief is really justified unless it traces back to a foundational reason; infinite chains of reasons are always vicious. Here are the steps:

(1) A belief P is justified only if you can give some reason R1 for it.
(2) But R1 only does any justifying work if R1 is itself justified.
(3) So P is justified only if R1 is justified, and by the same reasoning R1 needs a further justified reason R2, and so on forever—unless the chain bottoms out at some reason that doesn't need support.
(4) If the chain doesn't bottom out, then to complete the justification of P you'd have to run through an infinite chain.
(5) An infinite chain can't be run through to completion.
(6) So without a bottoming-out reason, P is never justified.
(7) Therefore every justified belief rests on a foundational reason.

I keep thinking the fishy move is either (1), because it makes justification turn on what you can give—something you do—rather than on what the chain has—something that's true of it—or (4) and (5) together, because maybe justification consists in the chain being there rather than in anyone finishing it. But both replies feel a little desperate. Which numbered step actually fails, if any? If its valid, why do infinitists think infinite chains can be fine? And what's the most defensible rewrite of the premise you'd reject? Also please answer in American English.
