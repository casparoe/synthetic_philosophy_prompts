I'm a student in a seminar on consequentialism and its critics, and we've just been through the cluelessness objection: the idea that, because the consequences of any ordinary act fan out through indefinitely long causal chains, we are in no position to trace them well enough to compare the act with its alternatives, so the consequentialist seems to be recommending decisions on the basis of comparisons nobody can actually complete. I keep noticing that this is not just a seminar-room problem. I work part-time as an intake assistant at a small municipal public-health foundation, and the program officers there make exactly this kind of decision every week. Their grants are small (rarely above $50,000), they review one or two full proposals a week, they have perhaps fifteen minutes of thinking time per proposal before a committee of non-philosophers signs off, and none of them has any background in ethics. When I asked my supervisor how she actually chooses, she said she mostly goes by whichever proposal makes the strongest immediate case on the page, and admitted that the downstream effects of the programs are anyone's guess. I want to give her something better.

Please write a reusable decision procedure for those program officers, grounded in what the cluelessness discussion actually supports rather than in a vague "try to think long-term" instruction. It should be a checklist or flowchart in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. In translating the theoretical material into a fifteen-minute procedure, say explicitly which distinctions from the cluelessness literature must survive in the simplified tool (for example, the difference between short-run effects we can estimate and long-run effects we cannot, or between symmetrical and one-sided uncertainty) and which can be dropped, and mark every step that is a judgment call dressed up as a procedural one. Then run the procedure on three actual-style proposals: (a) a school-based deworming program whose short-run effects on attendance and health are fairly well evidenced but whose longer-run effects on education, earnings, and future health are genuinely uncertain in both direction and size; (b) a community media campaign intended to reduce sugary-drink consumption among teenagers, with a plausible but unproven direct effect, a known risk of backlash and stigma, and cultural ripple effects nobody can trace; and (c) a proposal to build an internal monitoring and follow-up system so the foundation can actually track outcomes from its past and future grants, which delivers no direct health benefit now but would reduce how clueless the foundation is going forward. Include at least one verdict that a program officer will find unwelcome, and finish by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do. Please end the response with a one-sentence summary of the decision rule on its own line, formatted as \answer{...}.
