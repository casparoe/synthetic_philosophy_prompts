I've been trying to put Rousseau's case against the theater — specifically his claim that tragedy does not improve us by exciting pity but corrupts the very pity it seems to cultivate — into a chain of reasoning I can test. Here is my reconstruction. Tell me whether the argument goes through, and if not, exactly which step fails; if it does go through, say which premise is doing the real work and what the strongest objection to it is.

(1) The moral effect of an institution on those who take part in it is fixed not by the ideas it represents but by the habits of feeling and response it exercises in them.

(2) What tragedy exercises in a spectator is the first part of compassion — being moved by another's suffering — while systematically preventing the second part, acting to relieve it, because there is no real sufferer whose condition the spectator's response can alter.

(3) If a feeling is repeatedly aroused while its characteristic act is unavailable, that feeling is gradually trained to complete itself in the experience of being moved rather than passing over into the act.

(4) A compassion that completes itself in the experience of being moved is not the virtue of compassion but a surrogate for it: a susceptibility that can be enjoyed without any settled disposition to help a real person.

(5) Therefore tragedy tends to produce, not a greater stock of active compassion, but a cultivated sensitivity to represented suffering, which can develop alongside the active virtue or even at its expense.

(6) So a city that takes itself to be forming civic virtue in its citizens has reason to deny the theater a central place in public life.

I am fairly sure that (2) is correct and that (4) is just moral grammar. I half-suspect that the word "exercises" is doing double duty between (1) and (2), but I cannot make the suspicion precise, since (3) looks to me like nothing more than what it is to train a habit. Am I right? Is the argument valid, and if not, where precisely does it come apart? And is the claim in (3) an empirical generalization or in some way a conceptual truth?
