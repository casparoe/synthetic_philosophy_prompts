I keep going back and forth with my cofounder about a fix for our link-sharing site, Ranklet. Right now every post shows its total upvote count and the feed is sorted by that count. We're fairly convinced the counts create information cascades: a post that grabs a few early upvotes looks hot and keeps pulling in more, while a just-as-good post sitting at +2 gets skipped, so early luck controls what takes off. The proposal on the table is: keep sorting the feed by the true score, but the score a logged-in user actually sees on a post is the true score multiplied by a random factor drawn fresh each time the page loads, uniform between 0.5 and 2, rounded to a whole number. So a post whose real score is 40 shows as anything from 20 to 80, and refreshing the page re-rolls the number. We keep the factor and its range secret; users just see a number that jumps around, which we hope they'll chalk up to staleness or a glitch. Its the classic "make the herd signal too noisy to follow" play: the pitch is that people will stop counting votes and start reading the posts instead. I don't trust the mechanism, but I don't want a yes/no verdict. I want a concrete account of how it behaves over the first six months: what regular users, obsessive users, and authors each learn to do; where it gets gamed first; what happens to the market for bought upvotes when a buyer can't see a stable count; whether this actually kills herding or just slows it and pushes it onto the feed order (which still reflects the true scores); and what it does to trust in the numbers once people figure out the noise. If the failure modes are predictable, is there a version of this idea—different noise distribution, showing the uncertainty rather than hiding it, changing what is displayed or how the feed is sorted—that keeps whatever was good about it?
