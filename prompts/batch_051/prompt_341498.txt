People keep summarizing Kant's Perpetual Peace to me as: republics don't fight each other because the people who declare war are the people who have to fight and pay for it. I get that this is the ancestor of the whole democratic-peace empirical literature, and I know he also has articles about federating republics and about trade making war against a country's own interests, but I'm interested in the cost-bearing mechanism specifically. In the essay, the argument is that citizens who have to consent to a war will be reluctant because they personally eat the costs — doing the fighting, paying the taxes, rebuilding the ruins — while a monarch is just spending someone else's lives and treasure. Now update that assumption: modern wars are fought by all-volunteer professional militaries and contractors, financed by borrowing, with a lot of the violence done by drones, so the median citizen deciding in a democracy doesn't expect to directly bear any of those costs. Does the argument survive this, or does it silently convert into something else — audience costs, norms, the trade mechanism — that Kant didn't actually argue for here? I want to know which exact premise is under preasure, whether there's a repaired version that still does the original work, or whether we're left with the empirical regularity and a dead argument.
