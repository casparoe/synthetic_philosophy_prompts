I have been reading about embedded agency and got into an argument with a friend about Newcomb's problem. The standard setup assumes a predictor whose prediction is made before your choice, and the usual disagreement is about whether your choice is independent of the prediction or is merely evidence about it. We wanted to vary one assumption. Suppose the predictor is not an outside oracle but an actual finite physical machine in the room with you. It has to run an approximate simulation of your decision procedure in order to fill the boxes, and it has limited time and energy. You can see whether the machine finishes its computation quickly or struggles for a long while before filling the boxes, and you know the machine's code and hardware. After observing that, you choose one box or two. The payoffs are the classic ones: the first box always contains a thousand dollars; the second box contains a million dollars only if the machine's simulation output was one-boxing.

I am trying to figure out whether this version still has the structure that makes the original paradox interesting. In particular, does causal decision theory's claim that your act does not causally influence the already-made prediction collapse, because the prediction is now an observable physical process that your own decision procedure may figure in? Or does evidential decision theory become more plausible, because watching the predictor struggle gives you evidence about what it computed? I would like an analysis of how the standard arguments change under this variation, and whether one of the original positions is better supported or both miss something.
