Consider the following exam question and student answer from an upper-level philosophy of mind course.

Question: Millikan and Neander both appeal to biological functions to explain mental content. Explain how a teleosemantic theory assigns content to a simple perceptual representation, using the frog’s fly-detecting visual mechanism as an example, and show how the account can distinguish correct representation from misrepresentation.

Student answer: Teleosemantics identifies the content of a representation with what the mechanism has the biological function to indicate. In the frog, the visual detection unit was selected because firing in response to small dark moving objects helped the frog’s ancestors catch flies. The representation therefore means small dark moving object. Misrepresentation becomes possible because the state can occur even when its historically selected trigger is absent: if the unit fires in response to a shadow or a pellet, it is misrepresenting, since those are not small dark moving objects. Correct representation is then straightforwardly defined as occurrence under normal conditions, where normal conditions are the conditions that obtained most often during the history of selection.

As the grader, assess the student’s answer. Assign a numerical grade out of 10 and provide point-by-point comments that explain what the answer gets right, where it goes wrong, and why the errors matter for the teleosemantic account. In particular, evaluate whether the answer’s treatment of normal conditions is adequate, and whether assigning the content small dark moving object is defensible within a Millikan-style biosemantic framework. Do not offer a general impression; make your comments itemized and tied to specific claims in the answer.
