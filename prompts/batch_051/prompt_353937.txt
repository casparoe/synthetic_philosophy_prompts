Consider a jurisdiction that is contemplating a constitutional amendment to dismantle the doctrine of sovereign immunity. The proposal provides that any person may sue the state in the ordinary courts for injuries caused by the unlawful acts or omissions of public officials, and the state may not plead sovereign immunity, non-justiciability, or similar bars. Judgments and settlements are to be paid exclusively from the budget of the agency whose official caused the harm, and the government may not finance such payments with new taxes or additional borrowing. If an agency's budget cannot cover a judgment in the year it becomes final, the unpaid portion is to be recovered over subsequent years through an automatic salary surcharge imposed on the agency's three most senior tiers of management and a freeze on that agency's non-essential expenditures.

Treat this as institutional design to be analyzed rather than as a position to defend or attack. What does the amendment incentivize among frontline officials, agency heads, budget offices, litigants and their lawyers, and the courts that hear these cases? Where would the scheme be gamed first, what second-order effects would likely emerge, and could the most serious failure modes be corrected without abandoning the amendment's underlying aim?
