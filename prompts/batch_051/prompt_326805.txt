I've been working through Heath's translation of Euclid's *Elements* and I can't stop brooding over the two definitions that open Book VII. He writes: "A unit is that by virtue of which each of the things that exist is called one. A number is a multitude composed of units." So far so clear, until I watched my kid's math app tell her that 1 is a number, 0 is a number, 1/2 is a number, and −3 is a number. On Euclid's wording those all look like failures of the rule: 1 is a unit rather than a multitude, 0 is no multitude of anything, 1/2 is less than a unit, and −3 would be a negative multitude, which isn't a multitude at all. I dont want a history lesson. I want to know: if I take those two definitions as the rule and apply them to those four cases, which of them are numbers and which aren't? Where the plain words wont decide, does the purpose of the definitions decide, and what would Euclid have said if you handed him a zero or a negative number? And the thing that actually bothers me: is the app just using the word 'number' for a different concept Euclid was never trying to define—and if so, what happened to Euclid's concept of a unit? Is there anything in modern math that still does the job he wanted 'unit' to do, or did the job itself quietly change?
