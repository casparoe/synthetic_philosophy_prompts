Here's the thing I want you to construct. In the background is Friedman's F-twist — roughly, the view that it doesn't matter whether a model's assumptions are false, only whether the model predicts well. I don't want the usual armchair discussion of it; I want a built-to-order case, since I'm using it in a seminar to force students to actually take a side.

Design a small, concrete prediction problem — commute times, crop yields, secondhand furniture prices, beach café attendance, anything ordinary a bright undergrad could grasp without special background — and give me two models of that same phenomenon. Model A contains an assumption that is straightforwardly false, and false in a way we know on independent evidence, not just a harmless idealization in the frictionless-plane sense. Model B corrects that assumption to the measured true value. Keep the two models otherwise as close as possible: same functional form, same number of free parameters, fit on the exact same data. And yet Model A has to predict out-of-sample noticeably and robustly better than Model B.

Then do the philosophy. Does the case show Friedman was basically right — predictive success is what matters, and the truth of assumptions is a side issue? Or does it show something narrower, like a false assumption acting as a stand-in for another misspecification neither model corrects? Tell me exactly wich structural feature of the case does the work, and what happens when you remove it: does Model A's advantage disappear, or does the setup collapse into a boring case where the false model is just a decent approximation? Also say how a realist about assumptions — someone who thinks false assumptions are a vice even in predictively successful models — would attack your case, and whether the attack succeeds.

Constraints: no exotic physics, no perfect predictors, no demons, no appeals to radical uncertainty, and don't make the false assumption secretly true in disguise or the case is doing no work. The predictive gap should be big enough to matter but not so big it looks rigged. If you're cheating by making Model B's correction introduce some independent new error, I'll count the exercise a failure — the false assumption itself has to be doing the work.
