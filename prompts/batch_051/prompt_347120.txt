After a working life in experimental physics — mostly spectroscopy and the measurement of fast processes — I became accustomed to treating questions about when something happens as having straightforward empirical answers: improve the temporal resolution of the detector and read the clock. In retirement I have been reading philosophy of mind, and I have encountered a case where that assumption may fail.

The case is the color-phi phenomenon. A brief red spot is flashed at one location, and shortly afterward a brief green spot is flashed at another location. With the right timing, observers do not report two stationary flashes; they report a single spot that moves from the first location to the second and changes color midway along its apparent path. That content seems to require information about the second spot's color before the second spot itself could have been seen. Daniel Dennett has framed the explanatory choice as follows. One account is Orwellian: the subject first consciously sees the red spot at the first location, and then, once the green spot has been processed, the earlier content is revised in memory, so the subject comes to remember a moving spot that changed color. The other account is Stalinesque: consciousness receives nothing until both stimuli have been processed, at which point the whole moving, color-changing episode is produced already complete; the subject never consciously saw either spot alone. The two accounts agree on everything the subject reports, but they disagree about the actual character and timing of conscious experience.

I want your numerically expressed credence — a probability between 0 and 1, not a statement that the answer depends on one's theory of consciousness — in the following proposition:

Proposition: For a subject in the standard color-phi paradigm, there is a determinate fact of the matter, fixed by the actual temporal character of the subject's conscious experience and not merely by a choice of descriptive framework, as to whether the experience is Orwellian (an earlier experience subsequently revised) or Stalinesque (a delayed construction with no prior experience of the first spot alone).

Please decompose your credence. At minimum, separate and assign a rough probability to each of the following, with a number and one or two lines of justification for each:

(1) Conscious experience has a well-defined temporal microstructure at the relevant scale — determinate onset times, durations, and an objective order of events within experience — rather than being silent or indeterminate below about one or two hundred milliseconds.
(2) Conditional on such microstructure existing, first-person judgment or sufficiently refined psychophysics could in principle reveal which account is right, so that the two hypotheses are not empirically equivalent under every possible test.
(3) A determinate answer does not itself presuppose a Cartesian theater — a single finishing line at which contents become conscious and at which the revision or delay could be said to occur — and is therefore a coherent claim in the first place.
(4) Any further consideration you judge material.

Then show how these components combine into your overall credence in the Proposition. Finally, identify the component to which your overall number is most sensitive, and say what specific evidence — psychophysical, neurophysiological, or conceptual — would move that component the most.
