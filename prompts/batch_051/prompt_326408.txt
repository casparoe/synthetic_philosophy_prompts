I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about the weird grammar of safety talk — the way a chemical can be 'toxic' on a safety data sheet even when the actual exposure can't hurt anyone, or a phone can be 'fragile' even if it's never once been dropped. I've started asking myself what we're actually saying about a thing when we call it fragile, poisonous, dangerous, that sort of thing. My gut verdicts on some cases are below. I keep being told these are what philosophers call dispositions, but the labels aren't what I'm after.

- An ordinary wine glass. If you dropped it on concrete, it would shatter. Obviously fragile.
- The same wine glass, packed inside a foam crate that would fully cushion the fall. If it were dropped, it wouldn't break. I still say the glass is fragile; the foam is not the glass.
- A live electrical wire hooked to a safety relay: if a person touched it, the relay would cut the current before any shock. I'd still call the wire live, and dangerous, even though the bad outcome couldn't happen.
- A wire that's disconnected from any power source, sitting on a bench. Not dangerous. Touching it also wouldn't shock you, but for an entirely different reason.
- A poison mixed with an antidote in the same syringe, so injecting the contents wouldn't harm the person. I'd still call the poison poisonous.
- A glass-like material that's been chemically treated so it flexes rather than shatters on impact. Not fragile — this isn't the same stuff plus a safety net, it's different stuff.

The obvious simple rule — fragile means it would break if dropped, poisonous means it would sicken if ingested, and so on — gets the foam-crate case wrong, and probably the relay-wire case too. But the fix that says it would manifest unless something interferes starts to feel like it makes everything fragile, or nothing. So: is there one principled account of what it is to have one of these properties that yields all six verdicts, or close to it? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn? And what does your account say about a case I genuinely can't settle: a powder that is inert in the bag, but as soon as it meets ordinary moisture — like the lining of a person's lungs — it turns into a poison, so breathing it in would kill. Do I say the powder is poisonous?

Last thing, for my reporting: could you seperate what's actually settled among philosophers who work on this from what's still live dispute, and flag when you're giving me a contested view rather than the consensus?
