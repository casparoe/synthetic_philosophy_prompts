I am a self-taught programmer. I have never formally studied biology or philosophy, but I have thought a great deal on my own about what it is for something to be alive, and every proposed definition I come across seems to fix on a different feature. One proposal treats life as self-maintenance against decay: a living thing takes in energy and actively works to preserve its own organized state. Another treats life as reproduction with variation: what matters is copying with errors and selection, so that a lineage can evolve. A third treats life as information: a living thing stores a description of itself and reads that description out to direct its own construction and operation. A fourth treats life as a kind of recursive self-production: a network of processes whose activity continually rebuilds the very components that carry that activity out.

I would like you to port each of these four proposals, as faithfully as possible, into the vocabulary in which I actually think: code that is executing versus code that is merely stored, processes and persistence, memory, copying, garbage collection, exception handling, and the hardware-software relation. For each proposal, tell me which parts compile cleanly, which parts compile only with a shim — a thin bridge forced between two mismatched interfaces — and what that bridge distorts, and which parts cannot be expressed in this vocabulary at all. Then give me the residue report. Where the port fails, is that a defect of the software vocabulary, or was the original proposal quietly relying on a word such as 'self', 'information', or 'maintain' in a way that did more work than it admitted? Finally, I would like to know whether any of the four proposals survives the port substantially intact, and whether the usual borderline cases — a virus, a fire, a dormant seed, a program executing in memory — are exactly the places where the translation breaks, or the places it illuminates.
