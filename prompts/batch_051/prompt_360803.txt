Here's something I've been going back and forth about since last semester's applied ethics class. Take the Smith and Jones setup everyone reaches for in the euthanasia literature: Smith will inherit a fortune if his young cousin dies, so he drowns the kid in the bath. Jones is in the same position and sneaks in to do the same, but the kid slips, hits his head, and starts drowning on his own; Jones just watches and doesn't life a finger, even though he could easily pull him out. The standard move is that Smith and Jones are morally on a par, so the bare difference between doing and allowing can't carry the whole weight of the usual ban on active euthanasia while passive euthanasia is permitted.

Now change one parameter. Keep Smith exactly the same. But in Jones's case, suppose that if Jones does nothing the child doesn't drown with certainty: the kid has a thirty percent chance of recovering, grabbing the side of the bath, and not dying, and Jones knows this. Make the actual outcome the same as before—the child drowns anyway—so that after the fact the two cases look identical, but at the moment of decision Jones is knowingly running only a seventy percent chance of the child's death while Smith is imposing a certain one.

Run the original analysis on the new Jones. Does he come out less bad than Smith, equally bad, or something more complicated? Which principle is doing the real work now—intention, expected harm, actual outcome, or the doing/allowing line? And what does that mean for the policy argument? Real passive euthanasia cases usually involve substantial uncertainty about whether the patient would die anyway, while active euthanasia is pretty certain. Does the Smith/Jones argument silently assume equal certainty, and does the symmetry fall apart exactly where end-of-life decisions get messy? Or can the equivalence survive the uncertainty?
