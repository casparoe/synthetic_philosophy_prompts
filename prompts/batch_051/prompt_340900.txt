I am a novelist working on a book set in a near-future cosmology institute. The central character must choose between two ways of reasoning about his own existence: the self-sampling assumption, which says to reason as if one were randomly selected from all observers in one's reference class, and the self-indication assumption, which says that one's own existence is evidence favoring hypotheses with more observers in one's reference class. A physicist friend showed me that the second assumption seems to lead somewhere absurd. We numbered the argument as follows.

(1) The self-indication assumption says that the observation that I exist favors hypotheses containing more observers in my reference class over hypotheses containing fewer.
(2) The strength of this favor scales with the number of observers the competing hypotheses predict, without any principled upper bound.
(3) For any cosmological hypothesis H that fits all my current evidence, I can form H+ by adding to H an immense population of near-duplicates of me in a region forever outside my light cone. H+ fits my current evidence just as well as H, since the added observers are observationally inaccessible.
(4) Because I can make that added population as large as I like, I can make the anthropic favor from (2) as large as I like; I can therefore ensure that H+ is favored over H, whatever prior penalty H+ incurs for its extra content.
(5) So the principle lets me overturn any well-confirmed cosmological theory by postulating enough hidden near-duplicates.
(6) A principle that allows anyone to overturn any theory by postulating invisible doppelgängers is absurd, so something in (1) through (5) must have gone wrong.

I would most like to keep (1), because my character's arc depends on it, and I find (6) impossible to deny. Which step do you reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
