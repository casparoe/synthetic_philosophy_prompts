I'm a science journalist who covers physics and its conceptual aftershocks, and I've been asked to write the final essay prompt for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of spacetime. I'd like you to produce one exam question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme. The question should be answerable in 90 minutes.

The question must turn on the following passage from Einstein's *Relativity: The Special and General Theory*, section 1:

> Geometry which has been supplemented in this way is then to be treated as a branch of physics. We can now legitimately ask as to the “truth” of geometrical propositions interpreted in this way, since we are justified in asking whether these propositions are satisfied for those real things we have associated with the geometrical ideas. In less exact terms we can express this by saying that by the “truth” of a geometrical proposition in this sense we understand its validity for a construction with rule and compasses.

The issue I want tested is whether students can use Einstein's account of how pure geometry becomes physics to evaluate the Rietdijk–Putnam argument for the block universe. Specifically: in the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, the relativity of simultaneity is taken to show that events in different observers' pasts and futures are all equally real. Does Einstein's passage give us a way to diagnose what is sound and what is overreaching in that argument? The exam question should force students to explain the passage, state the Rietdijk–Putnam argument carefully, and then use Einstein's distinction between formal systems and supplemented physical systems to evaluate its central move.

The wording should be formal enough for a written exam, and the question should not be answerable by merely reciting what special relativity says about simultaneity or what Rietdijk and Putnam concluded. If you think this is a bad framing—for example, because Einstein's passage is too early in his exposition, or because the Rietdijk–Putnam argument doesn't actually rest on the move I'm suggesting—please say so plainly instead of producing a forced question, and tell me what a better question would be.

For the marking scheme, use five must-hit points worth 4 marks each, total 20, and after each point add a one-line note about how students typically miss it. Write in American English.
