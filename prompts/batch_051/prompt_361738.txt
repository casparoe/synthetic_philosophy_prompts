I am a political philosopher presenting a paper at a law school faculty workshop next month, and I want a Q&A prep sheet keyed to the room I will actually face. The paper defends an asymmetrical version of public reason. The standard view holds that, when advocating coercive laws, citizens and legislators should offer reasons that could be accepted by people holding any reasonable comprehensive doctrine. My thesis is narrower: the requirement applies with full force to laws whose immediate effect is to impose or maintain a legal burden on others, but weakens substantially, perhaps to nothing, for laws whose immediate effect is to remove an existing legal burden or prohibition. The argument is that the underlying wrong public reason is meant to avoid is being coerced on terms one cannot reasonably accept; a law that shrinks the state's coercive footprint does not coerce in that sense, so the demand for shareable reasons does not get the same grip. I use examples such as a legislator appealing to the idea that human beings are made in the image of God to argue for abolishing debt bondage, or to a divine command of hospitality to argue for narrowing immigration detention powers.

The audience is a law school workshop: mostly constitutional law scholars and scholars of law and religion, plus two or three political philosophers sympathetic to Rawls but not specialists in public reason. I know the objections I would get from a philosophy-department ethics crowd; I need the objections this room will actually raise. Draft the prep sheet as follows. Give me eight to ten objections, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way a member of this audience would put it, for example, the constitutional lawyers will reach for Establishment Clause and free exercise concerns, the law-and-religion scholars for the equal status of religious voices, and the Rawlsians for the instability of the burden/removal distinction. For each objection, give a reply I could deliver in roughly two minutes, and a note on where that reply is weakest. Concede what should be conceded up front, hold what can be held, and include the specific objection I should be hoping nobody asks. Write the prep sheet in American English.
