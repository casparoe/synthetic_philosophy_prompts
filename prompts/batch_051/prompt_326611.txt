I’m preparing a talk for an interdisciplinary seminar on how scientific categories get made, and I keep coming back to one puzzle. In the nineteenth century, George Perkins Marsh argued in Man and Nature that human activity had become a geological force in its own right, something on the scale of rivers, ice sheets, and species migrations rather than a mere disturbance of a stable natural order. Today, researchers routinely speak of the Anthropocene as a proposed geological epoch; it appears in journals, policy documents, museum exhibits, and popular environmental debate. Yet the body that formally defines the geological time scale has so far declined to ratify the Anthropocene as a chronostratigraphic unit, while continuing to treat older units such as the Holocene or the Pleistocene as formal and settled.

The asymmetry I want you to examine is this. For past boundaries, we expect a globally identifiable physical signal in rock or ice before we call something an epoch, and once a boundary is ratified, disagreement about it is treated as a technical matter internal to stratigraphy. For the Anthropocene, many of the same people who use the word seem content with a diffuse set of possible markers—plutonium fallout, changes in sediment chemistry, industrial plastics, land-use shifts—without waiting for a single ratified type section. They defend the term as indispensable even though no formal unit exists. So the same scientific culture seems to apply stricter standards to units set in the past than to the unit it most wants to use for the present.

Act as a professional philosopher of science and a historian of geology, and think carefully before answering. Brainstorm possible justifications for this asymmetry: perhaps the Anthropocene is fundamentally a political or ethical category rather than a geological one; perhaps formal stratigraphy is too slow for urgent public discourse; perhaps an ongoing event cannot meet the same evidentiary standard as a completed boundary. Then assess whether any of these justifications survives a nearby case, such as a proposed Industrial Revolution epoch or another politically charged term with weak physical markers. State clearly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up, and defend your verdict against the strongest objection you can imagine.
