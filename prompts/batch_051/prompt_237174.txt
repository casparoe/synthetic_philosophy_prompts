I've been rereading Thomas Nagel's "Moral Luck" and I can't stop thinking about the opening truck-driver example — you know, the one where a driver runs over a child, and Nagel says our judgment of the driver depends on whether he was at fault, but the difference between killing and not killing is luck. My friend Marisol and I have been arguing about this, so I started making up pairs of cases to see how she responds. Her verdicts are weirdly consistent, but I can't tell what rule she's running.

Here are the earlier cases:

- Two people are both texting while driving. One hits and kills a pedestrian; the other looks up just in time and no one is there. Marisol says the first driver is far more blameworthy and should go to prison; the second should lose their license for a while but is nowhere near as bad.
- Two doctors both ignore a lab result flagged as abnormal. One patient dies from the missed diagnosis; the other patient turns out fine because the lab result was a false alarm. Marisol says the two doctors are equally blameworthy — the mistake was complete when they ignored the result.
- Two parents both leave a toddler alone in the bath to answer the door. One child drowns; in the other case the parent gets back and the child is fine. Marisol says the first parent is far more blameworthy, and the second is just careless.
- Two airline pilots both skip the same checklist item. One plane crashes and kills people; the other flight lands fine because the skipped item didn't matter that day. Marisol says they're equally blameworthy.

Now the new case I want to predict: two schoolteachers on a field trip both get absorbed in their phones while supervising six-year-olds near a lake. In one case a child wanders into the water and drowns; in the other case the child wanders in but a bystander pulls them out. Would Marisol say the first teacher is far more blameworthy, like the driver and the parent, or equally blameworthy, like the doctor and the pilot?

I'm less interested in the bare answer than in the rule she seems to be using. The rules I can see that fit her earlier verdicts are a "professionals are judged on conduct, not outcome" rule and a "personal care vs formal procedure" rule, but they split on the teacher case. Are there other rules that fit too, and which further case would best tell them apart? Also, does Nagel's own argument commit him to one side of the teacher case, or is he only describing an asymmetry in our reactions?
