I keep going back and forth on Hume's account of causation, and something from outside philosophy has been bugging me. The argument, as I understand it, is that all we ever observe is constant conjunction: one event followed by another, again and again, and we never observe any necessary connection in the objects themselves—the mind just adds the feeling of necessity by habit after enough repetition. So far so good. But I've been reading about the last couple decades of work in machine learning on causal discovery, where algorithms take purely observational data with no interventions and no controlled experiments, and recover which variables cause which. Some of them even claim to pick out the direction of cause and effect between two variables by looking at asymmetries in the noise or conditional independence patterns, not just at raw co-occurrence. That feels like it at least scratches the constant-conjunction picture: the program isnt just recording that B tends to follow A, its extracting a directed causal structure from data that, at the level of individual observations, still only contains co-occurrences. Does Hume's argument survive this development? I can see three possibilities. Maybe causal discovery is just a fancier way of tracking regularities, so the argument survives with a small repair. Maybe it shows that observation can deliver more than conjunction, so the argument needs serious surgery. Or maybe the whole contrast between observing and inferring is doing too much work. What I want is something precise: which premise of Hume's argument does this development actually bear on, and what is the strongest version of his position that still comes out true after it?
