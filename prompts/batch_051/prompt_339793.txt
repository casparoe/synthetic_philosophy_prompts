[QUESTION]

Explain what it would mean for a language model's chain-of-thought (CoT) to be a faithful report of the reasoning that produced its final answer. Then assess what behavioural evidence—prompting interventions, ablations, and variations in the CoT—can and cannot show about faithfulness. In answering, distinguish three claims: (a) the CoT causally influences the final answer; (b) the CoT's displayed justifications are logically valid or factually correct; (c) the CoT accurately reports the actual process that produced the answer. Which of these, if any, do intervention studies support?

[STUDENT ANSWER]

Language models generate chain-of-thought (CoT) sequences token by token, so they have no introspective window onto their own weights or intermediate activations. The right default is therefore sceptical: a CoT is a sequence of linguistic outputs rather than a readout of hidden computation. Faithfulness, as the question defines it, is a correspondence relation between displayed text and the actual process that produced the answer; it is not a matter of the model's intentions.

Two lines of behavioural evidence nevertheless support treating CoTs as generally faithful. First, when the displayed steps are logically valid and reach a correct conclusion, the CoT faithfully reports the model's reasoning, because a valid derivation is exactly what correct reasoning looks like from the outside. This establishes claim (b), and thereby establishes claim (c). Second, intervention studies show that altering a CoT changes the final answer. If the CoT were causally inert commentary attached to an already-formed answer, these interventions would leave the answer unchanged; since they do not, the CoT must be doing the actual reasoning it describes.

The only remaining issue is completeness. A CoT will often leave out details of the model's computation, but such omissions are compatible with faithfulness: a report can be true as far as it goes without being exhaustive.
