I've been going around in circles about promising and I think I finally have a view worth getting slapped down. I call it the two-ledger account. The claim is that a valid promise writes into two books at once, and neither entry reduces to the other. First ledger, second-personal: by accepting a promise the promisee acquires a special standing to hold me to it. That's why only she can release me, and why compensating someone else doesn't settle the debt even if she ends up equally well off. Second ledger, diachronic: the promise also works like a contract I make with my own future self, so breaking it isn't just failing her, it's repudiating a self I deliberately built. I like that this explains the residual pull of a promise after the promisee has released me or forgotten about it — ledger two's entry is still there. The motivation is that the usual stories only capture one ledger at a time: assurance-style views get the promisee's role right but make secret or forgotten promises weird, and self-constitution-style views get the internal pull but make the promisee a bystander to her own wrong. I think both are necessary and neither is sufficient.

Tell me honestly if this is a real view or just both intuitions stapled together and relabled. I assume something close is already in the literature under some name I don't know. Give me the three strongest objections, and in particular say whether the release problem is fatal: when the promisee releases me, does what's in ledger two stay or go? If it stays, I seem to owe myself something no one else can waive, which is spooky; if it goes, the second ledger wasn't actually doing independent work.
