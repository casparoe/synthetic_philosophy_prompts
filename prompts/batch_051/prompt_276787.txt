At a dinner party last month, three of us got drawn into an argument that none of us managed to settle, and I have been turning it over since. The background is simple: a mutual friend, Daniel, is fifty-eight, smoked for roughly twenty years but quit five years ago, runs regularly, and has mildly elevated blood pressure. Mara, who works as an actuary, said the probability that Daniel reaches eighty is just what the life table gives for a man of his age—about seven in ten. Our other friend Liang, an economist, pushed back: that table lumps Daniel together with men of fifty-eight who never smoked and with men who have very different health histories. Since Daniel's smoking history and exercise habit are among the things we know about him, ignoring them isn't "neutral"; it is knowingly using a poorer class. The right number, Liang said, comes from the narrowest class that still contains enough data to estimate reliably, and it should always be reported alongside the class that produced it. Mara replied that "narrowest class with enough data" is itself a judgment call, and a malleable one: refine further and further—by blood pressure, family history, diet, genomics—and eventually the class contains exactly one person, Daniel, at which point there is no frequency left to compute. She added that because the number can be moved around simply by re-describing Daniel, the phrase "the probability that Daniel reaches eighty" has no unique content; each number is only an artifact of a chosen reference class.

Liang answered that this shows only that every probability is conditional—including Mara's own life-table number, which was also conditional on a class she had simply left unstated. A conditional probability is not false merely because the condition had to be selected, any more than a temperature reading is false because it was taken in the sun rather than the shade. Mara replied that sun and shade pick out a physical difference, whereas Daniel's class membership is fixed but the relevance of any one class is not; the choice of class is a judgment call dressed up as data, and the insurance industry's ability to issue different quotes for the same man on the basis of the same underlying facts is, she thinks, the proof. Liang conceded that no class is forced on us by the facts, but argued that this is unproblematic because competing reference classes can be compared by how well they predict; we should simply adopt the class that yields the better-calibrated survival rates. Mara said that calibration is itself computed over a class, so the dispute has only moved up a level. We went in circles for another hour.

I would like a careful, point-by-point adjudication rather than a scoreboard. Where is each speaker right, where does each overstate or conflate, and what would a defensible overall position on "the probability that Daniel reaches eighty" look like? If the truth splits between them, say exactly how.
