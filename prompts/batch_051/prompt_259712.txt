Next week I present to a mixed workshop on dynamic choice a paper defending this thesis: the money-pump argument, properly reconstructed, does not primarily impugn intransitive preferences at a time; its real target is the reduction of rational choice over time to a sequence of independent, time-slice decisions. The audience falls into three groups: decision theorists who think of money pumps as a heuristic for adding transitivity and dynamic consistency to the axioms of expected utility; behavioral economists who regard intransitive or dynamically inconsistent choice as an empirical regularity rather than a normative failing; and formal epistemologists who hold that a sophisticated agent who anticipates her own future choices can refuse the first step of any standard pump, so no normative conclusion follows. The paper's compressed argument is this: the victim is exploited only because she re-deliberates at each stage; a sophisticated agent can foresee the cycle and refuse the first trade, while a resolute agent can stick to a plan and refuse the later trades; so the argument either presupposes or licenses a plan-sensitive, diachronic notion of rationality and cannot on its own deliver a static transitivity requirement.
