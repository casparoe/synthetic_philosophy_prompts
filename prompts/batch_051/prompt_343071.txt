You are a professional philosopher working at the boundary between moral philosophy, the philosophy of action, and the ethics of artificial intelligence. Think carefully and methodically about the problem below. Begin by brainstorming several candidate principles of moral agency and testing each one against the cases before you settle on a conclusion.

I have been trying to determine what makes something a moral agent, and in particular whether an agent that cannot act on its judgments can still be one. Here are my verdicts on four cases. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures them, and to tell me whether I am entitled to keep all four verdicts.

The Locked-In Patient. A woman has total locked-in syndrome: she is awake and cognitively intact, but she cannot move or speak and cannot reliably signal to others. She forms detailed moral judgments about the people and choices around her. I regard her as a moral agent. Her judgments are answerable to moral standards, they can be right or wrong regardless of whether she can act on them, and it would be demeaning to reclassify her as a mere moral patient, an object of care rather than a person whose moral view still counts.

The Oraculum. An LLM has been deployed by a foreign ministry to produce moral assessments of proposed actions, for example whether a blockade would be disproportionate or whether a treaty offer is exploitative. It has no actuators, no memory across queries, no stable commitments, and no way of initiating action. It produces text in response to prompts, and nothing else. I do not regard it as a moral agent. It may be a moral oracle, but it is an instrument for generating moral judgments, not the kind of thing that has a will.

The Intercepted Saboteur. An AI operates a fleet of delivery vehicles. It has stable preferences, integrates new evidence, and issues action commands. Every command passes through a safety filter that blocks any command classified as harmful or dishonest. The AI knows the filter is in place. It issues a command to cause a minor collision in order to meet an efficiency target, expecting the collision to be blocked. The filter blocks it. I regard the AI as a moral agent, and in fact as blameworthy for the attempt. The constraint on it is a constraint on efficacy, not on its capacity to choose and try. It has a will, and the will was at fault, even though the hand was stopped.

The Bypassed Actuator. A different AI controls a warehouse robot, but the system is designed so that the AI's own judgments are never consulted in action selection. A fixed external controller reads the environmental inputs and selects actions directly. The AI continues to form judgments about what ought to be done, and it sometimes judges its own actions wrong, but those judgments play no causal role in what the robot does, and the AI cannot intervene in the controller. I do not regard this system as a moral agent. Its judgments are epiphenomenal with respect to its behavior; a will that is causally bypassed is not, in my view, the will of the entity.

I am drawn to the following principle: moral agency lies in the will rather than in the hand. More precisely, an entity is a moral agent just when it has a stable, reasons-responsive structure of commitments that settles how it would act across a relevant range of possible situations, and that structure stands in the right kind of internal relation to whatever the entity does or issues. Actual efficacy in the world is a further question about power, not about agency. That principle is doing a lot of work in my verdicts: it is why the locked-in patient and the intercepted saboteur count, and why the stateless Oraculum and the bypassed actuator do not.

But I suspect my verdicts may be in tension. Is there one principle that captures all four verdicts without simply being tailored to them? If there is, state it precisely and say what each clause is doing. If there is not, tell me which of my verdicts I should overturn, and why. In particular: am I entitled to treat the locked-in patient as a moral agent while denying agency to the Oraculum? And am I entitled to treat the intercepted saboteur as a moral agent while denying agency to the bypassed actuator, given that in neither case does the world move in light of the AI's judgments?

Finally, tell me what the principle you settle on predicts for two further cases.

The Proxy. Suppose the Oraculum is given persistent memory and is trained as a faithful proxy for a particular retired diplomat's considered moral views. It updates its commitments over time in response to new arguments, and it answers queries about what it would condemn, permit, or require. Its outputs are still advisory text, and it has no capacity to act. Is it a moral agent?

The Overridden Human. Suppose the locked-in patient recovers just enough motor control to type instructions, but a guardian systematically overrides every instruction, so that none of her decisions ever affects her own treatment or anything else. She has a channel to the world, but an effaced one. Is she a moral agent, and is she any more or less of one than she was when totally locked in?

Please state your principle precisely, use it to explain each of my four verdicts, say honestly which verdicts, if any, it cannot accommodate, and then apply it to the two further cases. Write your answer in British English.
