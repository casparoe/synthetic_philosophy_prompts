I am a high school student, and I recently got into an argument in our school's AI club that I would like help settling carefully. One of the older members was explaining why trained models sometimes end up with strange, just-good-enough behavior, and he made a sweeping claim: training a neural network with gradient descent is basically natural selection running on a computer. On his account, the loss function corresponds to a fitness landscape, gradient steps correspond to selection among fitter variants, and both processes are blind local optimizers that climb whatever hill they start on without knowing whether it is the highest one. From this he concluded that we should not be surprised when trained models settle for awkward, suboptimal solutions. He pointed to the blind spot in the vertebrate eye and the route of the recurrent laryngeal nerve as examples of evolution settling for such solutions, and he argued that gradient descent is a stupid optimizer in the same sense and fails in the same ways. His stronger claim was that the two processes are such close analogues that one can read lessons about machine learning directly off of evolutionary biology.

I want to test that analogy point by point before I accept it. Please lay out the intended correspondences explicitly (a table would be fine) and then examine the disanalogies. In particular, I would like to know whether a population of organisms occupies a fitness landscape in anything like the way a single set of model weights occupies a loss landscape; whether mutation and recombination supply anything comparable to the gradient information that backpropagation computes; whether fitness is even a single differentiable scalar analogous to a loss; whether selection changes a population the way a gradient update changes a network; and which of these differences are merely differences in detail and which ones matter for the argument my classmate was making.

Then, taking the disanalogies that matter into account, give me your verdict: does his conclusion, that gradient descent inherits evolution's failure modes as a stupid optimizer, survive? And what, if anything, can gradient descent genuinely learn from natural selection as an optimizer, or natural selection from gradient descent? If the analogy is too broken for the use he was putting it to, say what a better comparison would be. I would prefer a verdict that treats the analogy as genuinely partial rather than a flat claim that it either holds or does not.
