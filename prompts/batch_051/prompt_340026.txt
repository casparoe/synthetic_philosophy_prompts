I am a novelist, and I have been reading James Clerk Maxwell’s *Theory of Heat* while researching a novel about a Victorian actuary who becomes convinced that sorting is a kind of power. I want to make sure I understand one famous passage before I hand it to him. It reads:

"... if we conceive of a being whose faculties are so sharpened that he can follow every molecule in its course, such a being, whose attributes are as essentially finite as our own, would be able to do what is impossible to us. For we have seen that molecules in a vessel full of air at uniform temperature are moving with velocities by no means uniform, though the mean velocity of any great number of them, arbitrarily selected, is almost exactly uniform. Now let us suppose that such a vessel is divided into two portions, A and B, by a division in which there is a small hole, and that a being, who can see the individual molecules, opens and closes this hole, so as to allow only the swifter molecules to pass from A to B, and only the slower molecules to pass from B to A. He will thus, without expenditure of work, raise the temperature of B and lower that of A, in contradiction to the second law of thermodynamics."

Mechanically, I think I understand what is being described. But I keep tripping over two phrases. Why does Maxwell insist that the being’s attributes are "as essentially finite as our own"? And can the being really be said to act "without expenditure of work" when it is still observing, judging, and working some kind of trapdoor? I am less interested in the standard modern resolution than in what the sentence itself commits Maxwell to: whether the contradiction is meant to be located in the gas alone, or in the larger circuit of perception, decision, and action. My character is going to misuse the idea in a sermon about free will and the fantasy of perfect sorting, and I need to know where the genuine ambiguity lies.
