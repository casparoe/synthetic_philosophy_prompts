I am writing a novel in which a naturalist must decide how much the various species he meets count for, and the question running beneath it is whether brain size matters morally. I have settled on three judgements about these invented cases, and I want to know whether any single principle can capture them.

First, the brine-singers have brains forty times the mass of a dog's, yet their pleasures and pains are, as I have written them, no richer than a dog's. I judge that a brine-singer's pain does not count for more than a dog's merely because the brain producing it is larger.

Second, the clerk-apes are strong reasoners with excellent memory and foresight, but nothing feels like anything for them. I judge that they have no welfare to be harmed, despite their impressive brains.

Third, between two of the wall-dwellers, one whole and one whose memory was cut away by illness, I judge that the same burn harms the whole one more. It does not hurt more at the moment; rather, he can dread it, remember it, and place it in the story of his life, while the other merely suffers it and lets it go.

I can feel the tension between the first and third judgements. If brain size alone adds nothing, and reasoning without felt experience adds nothing, why should the further cognitive feature of reflecting on one's own suffering add anything? And if it does add something, is that not brain size mattering by proxy?

What principle, or small set of principles, stated precisely, could deliver all three judgements? What does it predict about a fourth case I have not settled: a creature whose brain is surgically enlarged so that it is cleverer but whose emotional life is unchanged? Which of my three judgements, if any, would have to be surrendered for the principle to remain simple? Please answer in British English.
