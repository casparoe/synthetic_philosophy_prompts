Over the past term I have been entangled in a running dispute in a reading group over Moore's proof of an external world, and I have begun drafting a short essay on it. I would be glad of comments on the following outline before I take it further.

The essay's central claim is that the standard charge of begging the question rests on a conflation of two things a proof may be expected to do. A proof may transmit justification: it takes premises the audience already accepts and carries their warrant to a conclusion the audience has not yet accepted. Alternatively, a proof may display justification: it shows that the arguer already stands in a knowledge-grade relation to the conclusion, whether or not the audience antecedently accepts the premises. I argue that Moore's gesture of holding up a hand and affirming that there is a hand before him needs only the second reading. The sceptic demands a proof of the first sort and therefore calls Moore circular; but this demand misdescribes the role the proof is playing. I then propose that the real remaining difficulty is not circularity but what I call demonstrative fragility: the proof works only for a subject whose perceptual capacities are engaged in the unreflective manner of ordinary action, and the sceptical challenge itself tends to disturb exactly that manner of engagement. I close by asking whether this means Moore offers an answer to scepticism at all, or only a graceful refusal to play its game.

I would especially welcome comments on whether the transmission/display distinction can be made sharp, whether demonstrative fragility is more than a rebranding of the circularity objection, and whether the final concession gives away too much.
