For several years I have held a version of what gets called expected value fanaticism: that in decisions with well-defined alternatives, the correct choice is the one with greatest expected utility, that utility need not be bounded, and that consequently a sufficiently tiny probability of a sufficiently enormous payoff should dominate a near-certain modest payoff whenever the expectation says it does. I am not defending the view that one should accept any old hypothetical with a huge number attached; the view is conditional on the credences and utilities genuinely being what the calculation claims. But within those constraints, I treat the dominance of expected value as decisive even when the expectation is almost entirely driven by the remote branch.

I would like a map of what would count against this position, not a fresh restatement of why its verdicts feel absurd. Underneath it sit several load-bearing commitments: that utility is real-valued and can be unbounded; that arbitrarily small probabilities are meaningful, comparable, and usable by a bounded agent; that expected utility rather than stochastic dominance or another criterion is the norm; and that uncertainty about value itself can be absorbed into the expected-utility framework. For each commitment, tell me what the world or the formal results would have to look like for it to fail, and whether fanaticism dies, gets restricted, or shrugs it off.

I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want them distinguished by which part of the view they hit. Specifics I care about: money-pump and infinite-expected-value pathologies from unbounded utility; the complaint that a finite agent cannot stabilize or distinguish probabilities like 10^-30 and 10^-60 and so the position reduces to arbitrariness; the idea that moving to uncertainty over which utility function is right deflates the fanatical branch; and the claim that bounded agents must apply a discount or leverage penalty to remote, hard-to-verify payoffs in a way that licenses a non-fanatical principle. For each, I want to know whether it exposes a real flaw in the view or just a real constraint on its application.

Finally, ask the question from the inside. Which of these would a committed fanatic concede as counting against the position rather than as a cost to be priced in? Does the view make any predictions or impose any constraints by which it could actually fail—for instance about the behavior of agents who follow it—or has it been refined into unfalsifiability through qualifications about well-behaved priors, computable approximations, and the difference between real and fabricated possibilities? If that is what has happened, say whether it is a defect in a normative decision theory or simply the shape such a theory must have, and give an honest bottom line about how exposed the view actually is.
