Biologists, physicians, and engineers routinely speak in language that looks teleological. A cardiologist will say that the function of the heart is to pump blood, and that a heart that cannot maintain sufficient output is malfunctioning. An evolutionary biologist will say that the peacock's tail is for attracting mates, and that a trait that no longer confers a fitness advantage is maladaptive. A physician will classify a condition as a disease partly on the grounds that some organ or process is failing to do its job. An engineer will say that a relief valve is supposed to open once pressure exceeds a threshold, and that one that does not is defective. Yet many of the same people would also deny that nature has purposes; modern science, at least officially, explains events by antecedent causes rather than by ends toward which things strive. Reconstruct the implicit theory of function, purpose, and normativity that this practice presupposes. Does the practice commit its users to anything like Aristotle's final cause, or can their language be fully translated into talk of selection, causal role, or the intentions of designers? Pay attention to the points where the practice is not uniform: an evolutionary function, a medical dysfunction, and an engineering purpose are arguably three different kinds of 'for,' and they may not presuppose the same thing. Is the practice coherent, and what would have to change in biological, medical, and engineering discourse if one of the rival accounts of function were adopted consistently? Finally, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is genuinely philosophically underdetermined.
