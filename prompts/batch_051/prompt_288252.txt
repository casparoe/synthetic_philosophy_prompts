I'm putting together a talk on pain asymbolia, and I've realised I don't actually know what the cases show. The standard line is that these patients feel the pain but the unpleasantness is gone, so the badness of pain isn't part of the pain itself—it's a seperate evaluative reaction. I don't think the usual anecdotes settle that.

So: how would you test the claim that pain asymbolia shows pain can occur without being intrinsically bad, supposing you had access to one such patient and some matched controls? I want the concrete measures, the result that would favour the claim, the result that would count against it, and a careful statement of which rival readings the test can and cannot tell apart. The rivals I worry about are: the pain is actually less intense than they report; they use the word 'unpleasant' oddly; the suffering is still there but they can't access or report it; and the badness is there but they can't act on it. And then say what no test could settle—whether the leftover question is empirical, or conceptual and not the sort of thing any experiment could decide.
