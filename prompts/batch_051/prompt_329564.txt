Next week I am presenting to my department's fortnightly ethics workshop, and I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis itself — I can write that myself. The thesis is this: the dualism of practical reason with which Sidgwick concluded The Methods of Ethics is not a flaw that better theory could have repaired. Both principles — rational prudence, understood as directing each person to their own good on the whole, and rational benevolence, understood as directing each person to the good of all impartially considered — survive Sidgwick's own tests for an axiomatic first principle, and every attempt to unify them under a single master principle covertly assumes one of the two contested principles as its ground. My positive view is that practical reason is irreducibly two-sourced, and that in a genuine conflict between what prudence requires and what impartial benevolence requires there is no uniquely rational verdict: reason leaves the matter unresolved, and a choice made in that space is not the dictate of practical reason speaking with one voice.

My argument, in outline, is this. I run prudence and impartial benevolence through Sidgwick's tests for an axiom — clarity of content, reflective acceptance, and the practical impossibility of coherently denying them from within a life — and argue that they pass equally well. I then argue that the familiar ways of collapsing the pair all fail. Reduce prudence to a rule of thumb for maximizing the impartial good, and you deny the standing conviction that I have reason to save my own life even when doing so does not maximize the good, and perhaps even when it frustrates it. Reduce benevolence to an instrument of self-interest or reciprocity, and you deny the standing conviction that another's pain is reason-giving for me even when it no longer touches my own good. Ground both in some third thing — autonomy, humanity, universalizability — and the derivation has to run through one of the contested standpoints, so the unification begs the question.

The audience is what makes me ask for a prep sheet rather than a defense, because this room will not object in Sidgwick's own idiom. It includes: one direct act-utilitarian who will say the dualism arises only because prudence was admitted as an axiom when it is at most a psychological tendency; one sophisticated consequentialist who is more sympathetic to Sidgwick than the direct utilitarian and will push less on subordinating prudence than on whether a two-sourced view can tell an agent how to reason through a decision; one Kantian constructivist who will insist that a divided practical reason is not practical reason at all, since the point of the faculty is to settle what is to be done, not to return a hung jury; one constitutivist who will hear "self-evident axioms" as dogmatism and ask what makes a practical principle normative if not the constitutive standards of agency itself; and one moral epistemologist who is broadly intuitionist but treats apparent self-evidence as highly defeasible and will press on whether my confidence in the two axioms survives disagreement and debunking. I should also note that nobody in the room is a card-carrying ethical egoist, but one member defends the view that any acceptable moral theory must preserve each person's rational permission to give their own life and projects special weight — and she will welcome my refusal to dissolve prudence, then attack from a different direction than the utilitarians.

Draft the prep sheet: the objections I will actually get, in the form and vocabulary each speaker would actually use, ordered from the certain-to-arrive to the one I am hoping nobody asks, each with what I should concede, what I should hold, and a two-minute reply. Mark, for each reply, the point where it is soft and could be turned against me. And include that last question — the one I should be praying nobody raises — stated in the room's own words, with an honest account of why it could end the talk.
