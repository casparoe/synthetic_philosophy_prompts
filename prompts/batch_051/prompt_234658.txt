I'm a reporter working on a long feature about cities that try to tackle housing affordability and low-wage work at the same time, and I keep hitting the same dead end: rent control protects sitting tenants but scares off construction, while minimum wage hikes help workers but get partly clawed back by higher rents. An economist I talked to floated a mechanism I want to take apart before I put it in the piece. The proposal: a city sets the maximum annual percentage rent increase for an occupied apartment equal to the percentage increase in the city's own minimum wage two years earlier — with a floor of zero and a ceiling of eight percent. Vacant units are unregulated, and the index only moves when the city itself raises its minimum wage (not the state's, not inflation). So if the city raises its minimum wage 10 percent in 2026, then in 2028 landlords can push sitting tenants' rent up by eight percent, and if the city never raises it, rents stay frozen. The stated logic is that tenants' ability to pay goes up before landlords get to capture it, and the two-year lag means the paychecks actually arrive first.

What I want is a walkthrough of how this machine would actually run, not whether its morally the right policy. What does it incentivize for landlords, sitting tenants, would-be tenants, employers, and the city council itself? How would it get gamed — around the vacancy exemption, the lag, the eight percent ceiling, or the definition of "the city's own minimum wage"? Where does it break first, and can any of the failure modes be fixed without just collapsing it back into ordinary rent control or an ordinary minimum wage? I'm especially curious about who ends up capturing the gains and who eats the costs, including people who don't show up in the usual rent-control debate.
