Before I read any more arguments about whether forgiveness can be obligatory or unconditional, I want the specification of what a theory of forgiveness actually has to deliver. Write the desiderata, each with the case or consideration that forces it. An adequate theory must fix the boundaries between forgiveness and the neighboring things it is often collapsed into — excusing, justifying, condoning, forgetting, pardoning, and reconciling — and say which of those distinctions are load-bearing and which are optional. It must give an account of standing: why it is normally only the wronged person who can forgive, whether third parties or representatives can ever do it, and what exactly goes wrong when someone without standing tries. It must say what role the wrongdoer's repentance, apology, or amendment plays, and whether treating those as preconditions simply rules out unconditional forgiveness rather than illuminating it. It must specify the object of forgiveness: only unexcused moral wrongs, or also justified harms, accidents, institutional wrongs, and self-inflicted wrongs; and it must say whether self-forgiveness is the same kind of thing or only a metaphor built on the interpersonal case. It must take a position on what kind of thing forgiveness is — an act, a process, a disposition, the forswearing of resentment, or a change in how the wrong figures in the victim's practical life — and on whether one can forgive insincerely, too early, too cheaply, or without the wrongdoer knowing. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp. Then identify the subsets of these desiderata, if any, that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most to eliminate the familiar accounts: forgiveness as wiping the slate, as forswearing resentment, as a performative speech act, and so on. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; a brief scorecard at the end is sufficient.
