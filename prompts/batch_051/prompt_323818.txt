I am a retired physicist who has recently been reading decision theory, and I have been working through a variation of the Death in Damascus puzzle that seems to me to change the standard analysis in an instructive way. I would like you to work through it with me and say exactly where the usual decision-theoretic moral does and does not survive the modification.

The canonical case, as I understand it, runs as follows. A traveller in Aleppo must decide whether to stay in Aleppo or go to Damascus at noon. Death is a highly reliable predictor of the traveller’s choice and will be waiting in whichever city Death predicts the traveller will choose. If traveller and Death end up in the same city, the traveller dies; if they end up in different cities, the traveller survives. The traveller knows all this. The standard difficulty is that the evidentialist seems to find either choice self-undermining, while the causalist insists that the choice cannot affect Death’s already fixed location but also seems to leave the traveller without usable information about that location.

Now here is the variation. Suppose the traveller can make an earlier, observable, but reversible move. At 9 a.m. he may buy a train ticket to Damascus, which costs him 1 unit of utility. At 9.15 Death observes whether the ticket has been bought. Death must then fix a city to wait in, and cannot move afterwards. Death is not a perfect predictor of final behaviour; he is a good statistical forecaster who must rely only on the ticket-purchase signal. If he sees a ticket, Death assigns probability 0.9 to the traveller’s final destination being Damascus and probability 0.1 to its being Aleppo. If he sees no ticket, the probabilities are reversed: 0.9 for Aleppo and 0.1 for Damascus. We may assume Death then randomises his waiting place according to these probabilities. At noon, the traveller makes his final choice. If he bought the ticket, he may either board the train for Damascus or stay in Aleppo. If he did not buy it, he cannot board and must stay in Aleppo. The traveller knows all of this before he makes any choice. Treat survival as worth 100 and death as worth 0, with the cost of the ticket being incurred at 9 a.m. and therefore sunk by noon. The traveller is sophisticated in the sense that he knows he will choose rationally at the later stage.

Please answer in British English, without external references or a bibliography, and structure your response under these six headings, in this order:

**First, the canonical puzzle restated.** Briefly restate the original problem and the standard divergence between evidential and causal decision theory, so the variation can be compared with it.

**Second, the variation as a decision tree.** Lay out the possible early actions, the possible later actions, the probabilities of Death’s location conditional on the early action, and the terminal utilities.

**Third, the final choice after buying a ticket.** Analyse the noon decision for a traveller who has bought a ticket: should he board for Damascus or stay in Aleppo? Show the expected-utility calculation.

**Fourth, the earlier choice of whether to buy the ticket.** Given the answer to the previous section, analyse the 9 a.m. decision, taking account of the sunk ticket cost and the traveller’s knowledge of his own future rationality. Show the expected utilities for buying and not buying.

**Fifth, where evidential and causal decision theory part ways.** Compare what each theory says at each of the two choice points. State clearly whether they still disagree anywhere in the variation, and if so, at which node the disagreement arises and what drives it.

**Sixth, what the variation shows.** Assess whether the modified case dissolves the original Death in Damascus difficulty, merely relocates it, or turns it into a different kind of problem altogether. Be precise about which feature of the original case is lost and which remains.

I would like the answer to be about 1,400 to 1,800 words in total.
