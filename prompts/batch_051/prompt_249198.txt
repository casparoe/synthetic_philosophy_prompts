I keep coming back to the old Harnad paper that kicked off the symbol grounding problem. The basic worry, as I understand it, is that a system could shuffle symbols according to rules forever without any of those symbols actually being connected to the world except through other symbols—so the whole thing might be syntax without semantics. I came up through the rationalist/alignment cluster, and I keep seeing people invoke this idea as a catch-all criticism of LLMs, but the actual arguments feel underdeveloped. A friend who works in deep learning says the old framing is basically obsolete: if you train a model on multimodal data, let it act in an environment, and fine-tune it with human feedback, then the symbols are grounded enough for any useful sense. Another friend who is more philosophically inclined says no—whatever LLMs have is just parasitic on human-generated text and image captions, so the grounding is entirely borrowed, and the original problem hasn't been solved so much as dodged.

I can think of a few distinct subquestions here: whether grounding is a property of a single system or can be distributed across a human-machine loop, whether the original problem was really about meaning or about behavioral capacities, whether interventional causal structure matters rather than statistical co-occurrence, whether grounding should be understood representationally or can be operationalized as downstream task performance. But I'm probably missing things, and I don't have a good sense of what would actually change the overall argument. If I had a year of full-time conceptual research to spend on whether current AI systems are symbol-grounded in any nontrivial sense, which open question or line of work should I prioritize? I want a dependency-aware answer: which subquestions are upstream of which, which could actually be settled with current tools—philosophical or empirical—and which are overated or would not change much downstream even if settled.
