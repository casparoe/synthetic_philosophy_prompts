I have been reading about lexical threshold negative utilitarianism—the view that the moral priority of suffering is tiered, so that a threshold separates intolerable, lexically prior suffering from lesser suffering, and no amount of lesser suffering can outweigh a single instance of suffering above the threshold—and I keep noticing that its formal structure looks just like the lexicographic preferences familiar from decision theory and social choice theory. In the latter, an agent's ranking over bundles can be dictionary-ordered: bundle A is preferred to bundle B if it has more of the first good, regardless of how much more of the second good B has, and the second good is consulted only to break ties in the first. Replace the first good with above-threshold suffering averted and the second good with below-threshold suffering averted, and the two orderings seem to be the same kind of object: a strict priority relation across tiers, with ordinary, possibly continuous trade-offs permitted within each tier.

I am trying to determine whether this is a real identity of structure or merely a verbal parallel. If it is real, do the standard results about lexicographic preferences carry over to the ethical view—for instance, that lexicographic orderings cannot be represented by a real-valued utility function and violate the usual continuity or Archimedean conditions? Would a defender of lexical threshold negative utilitarianism then be committed to saying that moral betterness over outcomes is not representable by a single real-valued badness number, and is that a serious cost or just an accurate reflection of the view's non-Archimedean commitments? And where does the analogy break? I am less interested in a verdict than in a precise mapping: which properties of the two orderings genuinely correspond, what would have to be true for results to transfer, and whether the analogous conclusions would be forced for reasons that do not actually apply on the moral side.
