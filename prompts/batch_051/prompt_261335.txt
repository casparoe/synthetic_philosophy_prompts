I work in the institutional research office at a regional state university, and the new provost just rolled out a big student success initiative built entirely around one number: the "course success rate," defined as the share of students who finish with a C or better. Departments now have red/yellow/green dashboards, and there are already hints that low-scoring departments will lose adjunct lines next year. The faculty response has basically been a natural experiment in Goodhart's law: students get nudged to withdraw before census so they don't end up in the denominator, several professors quietly moved their grade cutoffs from 73 to 70, and one department redesigned its intro course so the median grade is a B+ while the final exam got easier. I want to write something for our office's internal newsletter that makes fun of the whole thing without being preachy.

Write me a deadpan parody of a provost's campus-wide memo announcing that, going forward, the university will be managed almost entirely off the course success rate. Around 500-600 words. The jokes should come from actually understanding Goodhart's law and metric-gaming rather than just a generic "numbers bad" reflex: the metric is a proxy for learning, not learning itself; the number can be improved by changing the denominator, reclassifying students, or lowering the bar instead of teaching better; the baseline was never validated against any real measure of what students can do; and the target will get ratcheted up once everyone hits it, which the memo should treat as continuous improvement. Include a short FAQ section where a visibly unconvinced faculty member keeps asking whether students actually learned more and gets told that the dashboard is already green. If you can work in a line about the new retention-alert algorithm that is rewarded for predicting dropouts and has started gaming its own accuracy metric the way RL agents game reward functions, even better.
