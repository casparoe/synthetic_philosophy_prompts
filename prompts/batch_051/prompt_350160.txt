In a running argument with a colleague in my department, I have been defending a fairly deflationary view of the role of intuitions in philosophical method, and she has been defending a more asymmetric one. Her position is that, when our intuitions about particular cases conflict with our intuitions about general principles, the case intuitions should carry more weight, because they are more psychologically basic and less contaminated by theory. I am not so sure. Case intuitions strike me as just as susceptible to framing, ordering, and training as principle intuitions; if anything, their concreteness makes them easier to nudge. We keep returning to two recurring illustrations. One is transitivity: people confidently affirm the principle that if A is better than B and B is better than C, then A must be better than C, yet in certain constructed choice situations their particular pairwise verdicts form cycles. The other is simplicity: people readily endorse the idea that, other things equal, the simpler explanation is to be preferred, but their judgments about particular pairs of rival explanations drift with how the alternatives are described.

This looks partly like an empirical dispute, but it is also a normative dispute about what ought to count as evidence in philosophical theorizing. I would like to know whether the claim that case intuitions are more trustworthy than principle intuitions can be genuinely tested, and what such a test would and would not settle. Please design the strongest investigation you can: specify the observations or experiments that would bear on the claim, state which patterns of results would favor each side, and say what no test could settle even in principle. In particular, be explicit about how you would operationalize trustworthiness or evidential weight here, and be candid about the point at which the empirical findings run out and a methodological stipulation takes over.
