The one question I want is an essay/problem question (worth 20 marks, students get 90 minutes) that can't be answered by just reciting the standard definitions people memorize: demographic parity, equalized odds, calibration, the whole fairness-criteria laundry list. The crux should be the impossibility-style result — roughly, that a risk score can't simultaneously be calibrated and have equal false-positive and false-negative rates across two groups when the two groups have different base rates of the outcome, unless the score is perfect or the criteria trivialize. I don't want the question to name the theorem or ask for citations or literature; I want students to hit the tension from a concrete hypothetical, so a model without web access (or a student in a closed-book room) could do it from the prompt alone.

So please write:
(1) the exam question itself, self-contained, with a small hypothetical (e.g., two groups, unequal base rates, a risk score with a threshold) and definitions of calibration and error-rate balance stated plainly inside the scenario;
(2) a model answer of about 600 words that actually derives the conflict — ideally by assuming calibration and then showing the false positive / false negative rates are forced apart, or the reverse — and then says a sentence or two about what a sensible design response would be (e.g., pick a different target, or be explicit about which error you're willing to tolerate for which group and why);
(3) a marking scheme out of 20 that seperates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain — e.g., stating the two criteria precisely enough to use them, converting base rates into the required high-risk proportions, showing the tpr/fpr divergence, and addressing the "so what" design point — plus the usual mistake students make at each step (like confusing calibration with demographic parity, or forgetting that equal error rates at a threshold is not the same as equal scores).

Make it hard enough that a student can't just answer from memory, but fair enough that a strong student who's actually understood the material can do it cleanly in the time.
