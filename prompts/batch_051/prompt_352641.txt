I am trying to discover what I actually believe about self-examination by recording my verdicts on concrete cases, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures the pattern behind them—or which of my verdicts the most defensible principle forces me to revise.

Case 1. After a dinner party I replay the evening and notice that I interrupted a friend twice and talked over a quieter guest; by the end of the evening I have formed a specific intention about how to behave next time. I count this self-examination as beneficial.

Case 2. For about a year I kept an evening journal about a long conflict with my brother, rereading entries once a month; I eventually recognized a recurring pattern in my own conduct and changed how I handled the relationship in a way that improved it. I count this as beneficial.

Case 3. After a romantic rejection I spent months reconstructing the conversations, asking what each thing I said revealed about me and whether I repeat the same faults everywhere; I ended more anxious, more avoidant, and no better at understanding what went wrong. I count this as harmful.

Case 4. A friend who was laid off kept going over one or two sentences from an old email to his manager, trying to determine whether they had cost him his job; months later he has an elaborate theory but no work and more shame than before. I count this as harmful, yet he says exactly what I said in Case 2: he just wants to understand what happened.

Case 5. After each lost game I review my chess moves, including the painful mistakes, and my play reliably improves; I count this as beneficial.

Case 6. After a minor health symptom, I started checking my pulse and rereading my body each evening, tracking fluctuations and looking for explanations; the symptom did not change but the anxiety worsened, and I stopped. I count this as harmful.

Case 7. A therapist has two patients do the same exercise—recording automatic negative thoughts and testing them. One patient improves; the other simply produces more and more negative thoughts to test and deteriorates. I count the exercise beneficial in the first case and harmful in the second, though the outward behavior looks nearly identical.

My working rule is that self-examination helps when it ends in an intention, a change in behavior, or an answer to a question with a genuine stopping point, and harms when it loops without an exit or terminates only in self-condemnation. But this feels like a redescription of my verdicts rather than an explanation, and it makes the value of self-examination depend on outcomes I cannot know while doing it.

Is there a defensible principle that gets all or most of these verdicts and is not merely restating them? What does the best version of that principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on: the parent of a chronically ill child who spends an hour each evening reading about the illness, writing down symptoms and questions for the doctor, and says the routine keeps her anxiety manageable but has stopped sleeping well? And which of my verdicts, if any, does the best principle require me to give up?

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. The five items should be: the principle or candidate principles stated precisely; how the main clauses are motivated by the cases above; what the best principle says about the parent case; which of my stated verdicts it cannot accommodate; and an honest statement of the largest unresolved gap in the principle.
