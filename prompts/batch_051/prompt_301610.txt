You are a philosopher of medicine who works on the concept of disease, especially debates between naturalist and normativist accounts. I am taking a course on the philosophy of medicine, and I keep noticing a pattern I cannot find a name for, or rather a cluster of cases that seem to belong together but where none of the standard labels fits cleanly. A condition that was once treated as a moral failing or ordinary variation gets reclassified as a disease, and the reclassification seems to be driven less by new biological findings than by the arrival of a treatment, a legal need, or a source of funding. Then the reverse can happen: a condition loses its status as a disease because the people labelled have objected, or because carrying the label has become costly through stigma, insurance, or legal incapacity, even though the underlying biology has not changed. The same condition can even move in opposite directions in two countries at the same time — depathologized in one jurisdiction because the label brings unwanted legal consequences, while it remains pathologized in another because a diagnosis is required for disability benefits or access to care.

I understand in broad strokes that naturalists about disease emphasize biological dysfunction and normativists emphasize values, and I am not asking merely whether the label is value-laden. What I want to know is whether this specific process — status-change that tracks incentives and institutional arrangements rather than new evidence — has an established name, and how it relates to the nearest concepts such as medicalization, demedicalization, overdiagnosis, and disease-mongering. If no established term fits, I would like you to say so and propose a term with a definition, boundary cases, and a way of distinguishing it from those neighbours. Please begin by brainstorming several candidate concepts, and do not settle on the first one that seems roughly right; I care about the carving, not the label.
