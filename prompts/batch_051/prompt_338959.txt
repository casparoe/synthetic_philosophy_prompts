We seem to reach for our warmest moral vocabulary when describing people who cannot be moved by certain incentives, and for our harshest when describing people who cannot be moved by certain reasons. A judge who declines a bribe without hesitation, and whose ruling would have been identical had the money never been offered, is called incorruptible, and this is meant as high praise. A legislator who does not revise his position, or even lower his confidence in it, after confronting careful studies showing that his signature policy fails at its own stated goal is not called incorruptible; he is called dogmatic, obstinate, or unreasonable. Yet the two figures share a striking formal feature: each displays a stable insensitivity to an input that would have moved many others — the judge to money, the legislator to evidence.

What, if anything, justifies treating these two insensitivities so differently? One might appeal to the content of the influence, arguing that money is corrupting in a way evidence is not; or to the direction of influence, arguing that the judge resists a pull away from a correct decision while the legislator resists a pull toward one; or to something about the agent's wider dispositions rather than the single act. But each explanation runs into difficulties once we press on it, since money is not always an improper consideration and evidence is not always a reliable guide. I would like the most plausible principle that could account for our pattern of judgment — one that explains why incorruptibility is a virtue without collapsing it into the very rigidity we criticize as dogmatism — and an honest assessment of whether that principle survives hard cases. Is incorruptibility, properly understood, always a good thing, or are there forms of it we should regard as flaws rather than strengths?
