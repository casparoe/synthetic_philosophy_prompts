Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about whether you can call something a cause just from observational data — it started with education and income — and eventually he brought in Woodward's interventionism as if it settled the whole thing. His one-paragraph version went like this. Woodward's key move is to say causal claims are claims about manipulation. X causes Y just in case, if you were to wiggle X while isolating it from whatever else influences it, Y would reliably change. The isolating is what an intervention does, and interventions get defined in purely statistical terms: you set X from outside the system, the way randomization works in an experiment, so no confounder can get a grip on X. Since the whole definition just appeals to what variables would do under interventions like that, the circularity worry dissolves — you never have to say what a cause is before you say what a intervention is, and it doesn't matter whether any actual human could perform the intervention. And the difference between real causal relations and mere correlations, he said, is invariance: a causal relation is just a correlation that stays put across a wide range of different populations and contexts, while an accidental correlation will eventually fall apart somewhere. So the account is suppose to deliver a reductive, non-circular definition of causation in terms of experimental manipulation.

I left the argument thinking it was probably right in broad strokes, but I don't see how the circularity worry can just disappear, and I also don't buy that invariance is just "correlation that holds across contexts." Still, he's read more of this stuff than I have. Can you tell me how fair his summary actually is? Go through it sentence by sentence: which parts a careful Woodwardian would accept, which are harmless simplifications, and which are just wrong or backwards. Then give me a committed overall verdict — is this a fair gloss of the view, or does it leave someone with a real misunderstanding?
