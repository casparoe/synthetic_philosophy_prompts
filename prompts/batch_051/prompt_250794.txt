George Berkeley argued that money is not itself wealth. On his account, money is best understood as a ticket or counter: it entitles its holder to a portion of the real wealth of a community, where real wealth consists in useful goods and productive labour, and the tokens have value only through custom and consent. A nation, he held, does not enrich itself by multiplying the tokens; at the same time, he believed that a national bank could issue new paper tickets and thereby set idle hands to work, so his account already allowed new money to call forth production rather than merely record it.

Now consider a modern bank-credit ledger economy. Almost the whole money supply consists of deposit entries created when banks lend: a loan creates a new deposit, the borrower spends it on inputs, and the corresponding goods and services must be produced later out of the activity the loan finances. The money is thus created by private banks against borrowers' promises of future payment, extinguished as debts are repaid, and held as a private liability of the issuing bank rather than as a ticket issued by public authority.

Does Berkeley's argument survive this development? Identify the specific premise under pressure — for example, the claim that money is only a ticket on real wealth, or the sharpness of the distinction between tickets and wealth — and say whether the ticket-and-counter account can be repaired to cover privately created credit money. If it can, give the repaired version; if it cannot, state what account of money should replace it and why.
