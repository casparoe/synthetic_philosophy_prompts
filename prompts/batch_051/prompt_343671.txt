I am a novelist, and I have been revising a passage in which a character breaks a private resolution, and my writing group's reaction has unsettled me. The man has resolved to finish a novel by autumn. At a dinner he drinks too much, loses a night and a morning, and the work stalls. My readers found him weak-willed and faintly contemptible. But I had also drafted a companion passage about a second man at the same dinner: no resolution, no manuscript, the same drinking, the same lost morning. The readers found him merely sociable and a touch self-indulgent. Act for act, the passages are identical; the only thing I changed is that the first man had first formed an intention whose terms he then failed to keep.

Because the difference seemed to matter, I pushed it. I gave the second man the same long-term ambitions, the same values when the future is distant, and the same steep decline in the value of the present as temptation approaches—the pattern usually called hyperbolic discounting. He simply never formulated the ambition as a resolution, so no promise of his was broken; and he is judged far more lightly. Is there anything in the agent or in the structure of his preferences that justifies this asymmetry, or is it a narrative and moral artifact, a superstition that there is a special blame reserved for the man who, as we say, knew better? If you can defend it, defend it against the pair I have described, in which preference, temptation, and action are identical and the resolution is the only difference. If you cannot, tell me what that implies for how I should write the first man, and for how we should judge him outside the novel.
