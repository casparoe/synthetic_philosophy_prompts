I am a novelist, and I am writing a book whose central character is a museum restorer who discovers that a recently rediscovered painting by a long-dead master is a modern forgery. The dramatic fact I want to get right is that the canvas is treated as a different and lesser object the moment its history is known, even though every perceptible feature remains exactly as it was. I would like to build that moment on more than a vague appeal to the mystique of provenance.

Act as a professional philosopher of art. Rather than presenting me with a single theory, compile the list of requirements that a theory of the value difference between originals and forgeries — or, more broadly, between perceptibly similar objects with different histories of production — must satisfy in order to be adequate. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it. Among the cases I expect you to weigh are: a painting that loses value when revealed as a forgery despite being visually unchanged; an artist's own later autograph replica of one of his earlier works, which typically does not command the status of the first version; an unauthorised cast taken from the artist's own mould or plate; a sculpture executed by fabricators from the artist's instructions, with the artist never touching the final object; a literary work or a piece of music, where the notion of a forgery in the painter's sense seems not to arise in the same way; and a work so extensively restored that most of its original material has been replaced. Mark clearly which requirements are non-negotiable constraints on any acceptable theory and which are merely commitments of particular camps — the pure perceptualist, the institutionalist, the historicist about artistic value, or the art-market economist. I would especially like to know whether some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, so that any theory must quietly abandon or reinterpret one of them, and which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate views.

Please think this through carefully before writing the final answer: begin by brainstorming candidate requirements, testing them against the cases above and against a few further edge cases of your own choosing, and then present only the refined desiderata. I am less interested in a detailed scorecard of existing theories than in the criteria themselves and their motivation, though you may mention a theory briefly where it illustrates a requirement.
