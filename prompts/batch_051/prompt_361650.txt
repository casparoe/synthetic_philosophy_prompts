I'm a philosophy PhD student working in formal epistemology, and I keep getting stuck on the reference class problem. I'm not after a survey; I want someone to force my scattered verdicts into a definite principle, or tell me I can't have one.

The same event belongs to a million reference classes, and my probability depends on which class I consult. Here are my gut verdicts on cases:

1. A machine makes coins that are exactly half double-headed and half double-tailed, and a shuffled batch lands on my desk. I draw one coin and say the next toss has a 50% chance of heads. The right class is the batch, not the individual coin, even though the coin is already one or the other.

2. The probability that my 58-year-old aunt, a nonsmoker with well-controlled high blood pressure, dies in the next ten years: I use the actuarial class of similar women. I would reject the class 'women whose phone number ends in 7' even if someone handed me data for it.

3. For a single atom of an isotope with a known half-life, I'm comfortable taking its chance of decaying in the next hour from the physics: the class is all atoms of that isotope.

4. A friend's startup: overall survival rate 10%, SaaS 15%, teams with a previous successful exit 25%. I go with the 25%, even though 'previous exit' is a coarse and vaguely defined class, and I refuse to let the n=1 class of 'this exact team' dominate.

5. If I knew the full microphysical initial state before a coin toss, I'd still say the chance of heads is 1/2, not 0 or 1. The maximally specific class of 'worlds with these exact initial conditions' feels like the wrong kind of reference class. I realize I'm not sure how to say why without appealing to what I need the probability for.

The case I keep flip-flopping on: an oncologist has a broad trial showing 30% response for my tumor type, and a small model trained on 12 patients with my exact mutation and sex that gives 55%. I genuinely dont know whether that narrow class is like the phone-number class or like the factory class.

My own best stab is some kind of causal relevance requirement — use the narrowest class whose defining features are causally relevant to the outcome, and stop refinning before you hit noise or gerrymandered groups. But that sounds circular, because I probably need probabilities to say what 'causally relevant' amounts to.

So: give me a committed answer. State the principle that best captures all five verdicts, precisely enough that it makes a definite call on the oncologist case, tell me which of my five verdicts (if any) it forces me to drop or soften, and say what the principle predicts for one more case of your own construction that wasn't on my list. Dont just lay out candidate principles and leave me to choose.
