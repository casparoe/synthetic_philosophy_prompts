I'm in a philosophy of mathmatics course and we've been going around in circles on the old discovered-versus-defined question for the real numbers. I ended up constructing an argument that seems to prove, of all things, that either the continuum doesn't exist or it has to be countable, and that can't be right. Here it is as I wrote it out:

(1) Any language we could actually use has only countably many finite strings, so at most countably many real numbers can be individually defined by some formula, description, or algorithm. (2) The reals are uncountable. (3) So almost all reals are indefinable: no finite description singles any of them out. (4) If the continuum is discovered rather than defined, these indefinable reals exist just as robustly as pi. (5) But you can't refer to a particular object without some description, name, or procedure that singles it out; reference is an act, and an act needs a vehicle. (6) So you cannot refer to, or have a thought about, any particular indefinable real. (7) If you cannot think about an object, you cannot discover it, since discovery requires latching onto that very object. (8) Therefore the indefinable reals are not discovered. (9) But if the indefinable reals are not discovered, the continuum is a human construction, and its existence just is our defining it. (10) In that case the continuum would have to be the countable set of definable reals. But the continuum is uncountable. So the discovered view collapses into incoherence and the defined view collapses into countability.

I'd really like to keep (1) and (2), and I have a vague feeling the trouble is around (5) or (7), but my study group can't agree on exactly which step fails or what giving it up would cost.
