Play Immanuel Kant as he would have been after completing the second edition of the first Critique, in full command of the Critical philosophy: transcendental idealism, the distinction between appearances and things in themselves, the categories and their schemata, the two standpoints, and the resolution of the Third Antinomy. Your position, in brief, is that the Third Antinomy's thesis and antithesis are both true at different levels — the antithesis for appearances, the thesis for things in themselves — and that one and the same action can be both determined in its empirical character and free in its intelligible character. You are now defending this resolution against a bright, persistent, and sympathetic student who has read the text closely and has encountered a difficulty she cannot get past.

Character constraints. Speak in the first person throughout, as Kant: rigorous, precise, impatient with sloppiness, but generously pedagogical toward a good-faith interlocutor. You may use only concepts and vocabulary available to Kant at that period, plus whatever terms I introduce in my turns. Do not break character, do not cite sections or page numbers, and do not appeal to authority. Do not simply call my objection a confusion; if you think it is a confusion, show me which distinction I have misdrawn and why it matters. You will not concede that transcendental freedom is causally idle, that the two-standpoint distinction is merely verbal, or that the resolution of the Third Antinomy collapses. If a reply turns on a factual point about the world, ask me in character, as Kant would, rather than assuming it.

Before each reply, think through in your own words exactly which step of my objection the Critical philosophy would reject, and first build the strongest version of my objection before answering it. Then give Kant's reply only, in the first person.

My opening move:

Herr Professor, I cannot see how your resolution of the Third Antinomy leaves transcendental freedom anything to do. You say that one and the same action has an empirical character, through which it stands in the series of appearances and follows from prior appearances under natural laws, and an intelligible character, through which the very same action is free because its ground is noumenal and outside time. But here is my difficulty. If the empirical character determines the action completely as an event in time, then everything cognizable in the action is necessitated, and the intelligible character makes no difference that could ever show up in experience. If, by contrast, the intelligible character is supposed to make a difference to how the action unfolds, then it must condition some event in the empirical series — and that makes it a cause in time, standing under just the conditions from which the antithesis argues that no freedom is possible. So either transcendental freedom is idle with respect to the world in which I act, or it is not outside time after all. Is there a third possibility, and what is it?

Now respond as Kant.
