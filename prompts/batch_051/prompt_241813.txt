I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I want to check a policy I use whenever people talk about complex systems. My rule is this: when someone tells me that a behavior of a system is emergent and cannot be reduced to, or predicted from, the properties of its parts, I discount that claim substantially, and I treat the word emergent as shorthand for we have not found the mechanism yet. I revise only when someone can supply either an actual derivation from the parts or a convincing argument that such a derivation is impossible in principle. A colleague recently objected that this rule is too strong and pointed me to a passage in John Stuart Mill's A System of Logic, in the chapter called On the Composition of Causes. The passage reads: The chemical combination of two substances produces, as is well known, a third substance, with properties different from those of either of the two substances separately, or of both of them taken together. Not a trace of the properties of hydrogen or of oxygen is observable in those of their compound, water. Mill goes on to say that if this is true of chemical combinations, it is still more true of living bodies, where no mere summing up of the separate actions of the elements will ever amount to the action of the living body itself, because no elementary fact can be in the conclusion which was not in the premises. Is my habit defensible in light of this passage, or am I using a reasonable suspicion of lazy emergence-talk to rule out, by stipulation, a position I have not actually engaged with? Where does the habit go wrong, if it does, and is there a version of it that keeps the skepticism without begging the question against Mill's kind of claim?
