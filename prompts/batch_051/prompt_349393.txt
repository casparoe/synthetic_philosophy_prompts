I teach an interdisciplinary undergraduate seminar on how historians and scientists divide time into periods; the group is 18 first- and second-year students, and I have one 90-minute session for this. We have just finished a unit on the formal machinery of geological time, and I want to turn to the Anthropocene as a live problem in periodization. I would like you to design a structured role-play in which the class acts as a boundary commission charged with recommending when the Anthropocene begins and with giving reasons for that choice. The point I want them to feel, rather than receive as a lecture, is that this is only partly an empirical question: the physical evidence underdetermines the choice, and the decision is entangled with judgments about which human transformations matter most, who has standing to make the call, and what the boundary is supposed to be useful for. Please design the exercise so this problem emerges from the mechanics of the deliberation itself, for example through role cards that give participants different evaluation criteria, a shared evidence packet, a fixed time horizon, and a voting procedure that cannot by itself dissolve a persistent disagreement. Include at least five roles, such as a stratigrapher, a climate systems scientist, a world historian, an archaeologist, and a representative of a community with a direct stake in climate impacts; a slate of candidate start dates, such as the spread of agriculture, the early modern exchange of biota between hemispheres, the Industrial Revolution, and the mid-twentieth-century Great Acceleration; and an explicit timeline for the 90 minutes.

Then provide the debrief. In it, map specific moments from the exercise to the distinction between discovering a boundary and deciding on one; tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, for instance students assuming that a better-resolved stratigraphic record would settle the question; and be honest about which feature of the real Anthropocene debate this classroom simulation cannot reproduce. The students should leave seeing that the question when the Anthropocene begins is not something more data alone could settle, and that calling a stretch of time an epoch is itself an intervention with political and historical stakes. Please write in American English.
