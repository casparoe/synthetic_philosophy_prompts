A friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about a thought experiment and came away unable to locate our disagreement. Imagine a forgery that is visually indistinguishable from an original painting — not just to a casual glance, but to any expert, under any viewing conditions, with any instruments one likes — and suppose viewers can examine both canvases and are told which is the original, even though, by stipulation, they can detect no visible difference. My friend thinks this shows that the premium placed on originals is a kind of fetish. I kept repeating that history and provenance matter, but he pressed me to explain how a fact about an object's past can enter into what it is like to stand in front of it, and I could not say anything that satisfied either of us.

By the end of the night he had written his argument out like this:

(1) By hypothesis, the original and the perfect forgery are visually indistinguishable to any observer under any viewing conditions.
(2) If two objects are visually indistinguishable, then looking at one and looking at the other produce the same visual experience.
(3) What makes one painting better than another, considered as a painting, is exhausted by the visual experience it produces.
(4) Therefore, the original and the perfect forgery are equally good, considered as paintings.
(5) If two works are equally good, considered as paintings, then a preference for one over the other cannot be grounded in their quality as paintings.
(6) When pressed, most buyers and curators say they pay more for the original because it is the better painting.
(7) Therefore, the premium paid for an original cannot be justified by the quality of the two works as paintings; if it is justified at all, it must be justified by history, authenticity, or some other fact that does not show up in the visual experience.
(8) But it is not reasonable to pay a premium for a fact that makes no difference to how good the works are as paintings.
(9) So, unless one of the steps above fails, the premium on authenticity is unreasonable.

I want to know whether this argument is valid and, if not, exactly which step breaks. I have a vague sense that step (3) is doing too much, and that "the same visual experience" may be playing two roles in steps (2) and (4), but I could not make the equivocation precise. If the argument as written fails for a narrow reason, give me the strongest repaired version and say whether the conclusion survives. If it is valid, tell me which premise one would have to reject and how costly that rejection is. Please also separate what actually follows from these premises from what is genuinely contested among people who know this topic, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
