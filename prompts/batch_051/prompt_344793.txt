A friend of mine who does trade law gave me this argument and now I can't find the flaw. (1) A state is sovereign over some matter only if it has the last word on that matter — if another body can set the rule and the state cannot refuse it, then its not really in charge of it. (2) In a customs union, the external tariff is decided collectively and applies to every member: no member can veto the common tariff, impose its own temporary tariff, or refuse to collect it. (3) So a member state has no last word over its external trade policy. (4) But control over tariffs and borders is close to the core of what it is to be a state; a body that cant say no to a trading regime it didn't get to set has given up part of its sovereignty. (5) So upon joining, a state is no longer fully sovereign. (6) Yet only a sovereign can bind itself by treaty: joining is an exercise of the very sovereignty that joining destroys. (7) So a customs union membership is self-undermining — the act that creates it destroys the capacity the act presupposes, and the accession was never valid. (8) But states plainly do join customs unions without turning into provinces. So one of these steps has to be false, and each of them feels like a definition. Which one do you give up, and what does giving it up do to the concept of sovereignty? I'd like to keep (6), because self-binding seems like the whole point of treaties, but I suspect that's the step that has to go. And before you say the right to withdraw saves it: an exit option isn't a say, and while a state is in the union, the union's tariff is not its decision.
