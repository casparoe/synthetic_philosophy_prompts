Analyze the Fritz Fischer controversy over the origins of the First World War as a case study in what it means to hold a nation responsible for a historical wrong. In the early 1960s the Hamburg historian Fritz Fischer argued in Griff nach der Weltmacht (published in English as Germany's Aims in the First World War) that imperial Germany's leadership had deliberately risked a general European war in July 1914 in pursuit of expansionist aims, and that Germany therefore bore the principal responsibility for the outbreak. The book set off a ferocious public dispute among West German historians and in the broader public; influential critics such as Gerhard Ritter defended the older view that Germany had acted defensively or that the great powers shared responsibility, and the argument became entangled with the question of what the Federal Republic, then less than two decades old, should acknowledge about the German past. It was never purely academic: the Versailles reparations settlement had assigned responsibility for the war and its losses to Germany and its allies, and Fischer's critics worried that accepting his thesis would grant that assignment retrospective moral standing.

Treat the controversy as a case for philosophical theories of collective and national responsibility. I am not asking you to settle the historiographical dispute or retell the war's origins; take the framing above as given and do the philosophical work. Bring in the standard distinctions — collective agency, moral blame versus legal liability, backward-looking guilt versus forward-looking accountability, and the problem of holding present generations responsible for the acts of their predecessors — and be disciplined about the mapping. Separate causal responsibility for the war from legal responsibility under the Versailles settlement and from moral responsibility in the philosophers' sense; say who the relevant agent is supposed to be (the state, the nation, the leadership, the military, the electorate, or some combination of these); and address whether Germany in 1914 and Germany in 1961 are the same bearer of responsibility in any defensible sense. Then run the analysis in the other direction: what does the Fischer controversy reveal about the strengths and blind spots of the theories you invoked? Where do the theories clarify what the historians were actually fighting over, where do they misdescribe a historiographical dispute as if it were a moral one, and where are they simply silent?
