I have come to accept that the distinction between epistemic bubbles and echo chambers is a difference in kind, not a difference of degree. On the view I hold, an epistemic bubble is a structural gap in exposure: relevant sources or voices never reach the person inside it. An echo chamber, by contrast, is a structure of engineered trust, in which contrary voices are not merely missing but are actively discredited in advance, so that the chamber survives not despite exposure to outside evidence but through an acquired distrust of it. I want to know what would count against this way of drawing the distinction. Which actual or possible cases, mechanisms, or empirical findings would show that the contrast is overstated, that one phenomenon is a special case of the other, or that the distinction locates the wrong feature as the epistemically damaging one? Please rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, say which of them a committed defender of the distinction should concede as counting against it, and assess whether the view has been qualified in ways that insulate it from refutation—and, if so, whether that is a problem for a distinction of this kind. Throughout, separate what is settled among those who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
