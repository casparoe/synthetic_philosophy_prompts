A small group of people who have left various religious traditions proposes a residential community called the Unsponsored Order. Its stated purpose is to settle by internal experiment whether anything worth calling spirituality remains once every trace of religious doctrine and supernatural commitment has been removed. The community has about sixty adult members, and its founding charter contains the following rules.

1. Every member must complete a daily round of practices: a period of silent sitting, a period of chanting in semantically empty syllables rather than words, a period of ritualized manual work, and, on specified days, a fast and an all-night vigil. Members may perform each practice with whatever private attitude they choose; the rules govern conduct only.

2. No member may, on the premises or in any communication that represents the Order, assert, avow, or openly presuppose any proposition about non-natural beings, forces, or orders — gods, souls, afterlives, karmic law, cosmic purpose, or the sacred as a real feature of the world — except in closed sessions explicitly framed as commentary on existing texts. The ban is on expression, not on thought: there is no belief test, and private belief is officially unmonitored.

3. Once a year, the members vote by a three-fifths majority on which practices to keep, retire, or modify. The vote must be based on only two sources of evidence: anonymized self-reports of awe, calm, purpose, and social trust, and each member's public statement of how the year's practice has changed them. Any member may propose a new practice; proposals are piloted with volunteers for one season before the vote.

4. A practice borrowed from any existing religious tradition may remain in the rotation for at most five consecutive years, after which it must be retired or replaced. New practices may be invented by members.

5. The Order issues no official account of what the practices mean or why they work. Individual members may offer naturalistic descriptions of what a practice does, but the Order endorses none.

The founders argue that the Order is itself a clean experiment: if anything necessary to spirituality is lost when doctrine is removed, the internal evidence will show it, and if nothing is lost, the Order will have shown that spirituality is only a bundle of techniques.

Treat the proposal as a mechanism, not as a claim about the truth or value of any religion. Walk through how the Order would actually behave over a ten-year horizon: what kinds of members it selects for, how the rules would be gamed — the speech ban, the self-reports, the five-year borrowing limit, the voting rule — what unofficial norms and de facto quasi-doctrines would form, where the first serious stresses would appear, and whether anything the founders would recognize as spirituality, rather than mood regulation or group ritual alone, is likely to remain. Say which failure modes could be fixed without reintroducing religion, and which could not.
