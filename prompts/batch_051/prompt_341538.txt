I am the head of pricing at a regional homeowners insurer. Every quarter my team must set an individual renewal price for each policyholder, and the heart of that decision is a single number: the expected annual loss cost for that specific house. But we never observe a frequency for the individual house. We observe historical frequencies for groups the house belongs to, and any given house belongs to many groups at once — the county it sits in, the wildfire-risk band, the construction type, the claims-history cohort, the credit tier, the distance-to-protection class, the age of the roof — and the historical loss rates of these groups can point in sharply different directions. Underwriters currently handle this by picking the group that feels right and then writing a justification around it, which produces inconsistent prices and has twice drawn regulator criticism. I want a procedure, not a lecture: something a working underwriter can run start to finish in about 45 minutes per renewal, producing no more than one page of documented reasoning that a pricing committee can audit.

Please write that procedure for me, grounded in what the philosophy of probability and formal epistemology actually say about the reference class problem. Do not give me citations or a reading list; give me the usable residue of the theory. The procedure should specify: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; when the procedure stops with a "no" or with "use no class at all"; and where it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining choice is one of values or interests rather than facts. Be explicit about which steps look procedural but are actually judgment calls in disguise, and say which distinctions from the underlying theory must survive the simplification and which can be dropped for a working underwriter. It should not pretend to solve the reference class problem; it should be a disciplined way to live with it under a deadline.

Then run the procedure on three concrete renewals, showing the reasoning at each step.

1. A moderate-value, wood-frame house in a county with a stable 3.1 claims per 1,000 policy-years across many years. The same house also falls into a "wood-frame in wildland-urban interface" class with 11.4 claims per 1,000 policy-years, but that number comes from only 60 policy-years in a fast-changing fire environment. Show how the procedure trades off the size and stability of a class against its similarity to the case.

2. A house held in a family trust. The trust-owned-property class in our book has noticeably worse loss experience than the general book of comparable homes, for reasons that look accidental and partly driven by a handful of severe losses. The underwriter strongly believes the trust status is irrelevant to this particular house's risk. Show what the procedure does when a statistically visible class and an underwriter's judgment of similarity conflict.

3. A long-tenured customer with no claims for eighteen years, whose house sits squarely in a wildfire-exposure class that has deteriorated in the last three seasons. I expect the procedure to give my team an answer we will not like — roughly, that the clean-history class absorb the deterioration class — or else to force us to say openly that we are choosing a class for commercial reasons. Show which of the two happens, and why.

Close by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do: what it cannot settle about fairness, about how much historical data is enough, and about which similarities between houses are supposed to matter.
