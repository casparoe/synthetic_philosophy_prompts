I serve on a research institute's internal review group that is drafting guidance on when an AI system should be called honest or deceptive, as opposed to merely accurate, inaccurate, or evasive. One member proposes a purely functional criterion. On her view, a system is deceptive just in case its misleading outputs are best explained by an internal model of how its audience will update on them: the behavior should change when the system has reason to believe the audience will not be taken in. Likewise, a system is honest only if its outputs are controlled by a process that does not let the audience's expected reaction override what the system's own available information would otherwise support. Another member objects that this criterion has dropped the thing that matters, because honesty and deception ordinarily require an intention to produce or withhold a belief, and a model has no such intention.

I have been asked to draft a short technical note for the group. The note should design the strongest empirical test of the functionalist's criterion that you can construct: specify the kind of system, the probe conditions or training-history comparisons involved, and say exactly what pattern of results would count in favor of describing the system as deceptive and what pattern would count against it. Finally, the note should say what no test of this kind could settle — in particular, whether the system is genuinely deceptive in the ordinary intentional sense or merely indistinguishable in its behavior from one that is. Be explicit about the difference between evidence that a system tracks a listener's credulity and evidence that it is trying to mislead them.
