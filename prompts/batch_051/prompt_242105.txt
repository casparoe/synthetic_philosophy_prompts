Good's theorem in Bayesian decision theory says that an expected-utility maximizer cannot be made worse off in expectation by costless information: for any free signal about the state of the world, the expected value of information is weakly nonnegative. The standard proof assumes that the information changes beliefs and not preferences, that the agent updates by conditionalization, and that a single utility function is used both before and after the information arrives. Now consider a development the theorem was not written to handle. A growing share of the costless decision-relevant information people actually use is delivered in environments deliberately built to reshape their preferences rather than merely inform their beliefs. Suppose someone is deciding how to spend the next year on one of two projects. A free service will report, for each project, the likely income, recognition, and time spent on drudgery; the reports are accurate, but the person knows from past experience that reading them makes the reported dimensions loom larger in her choices, while unreported dimensions such as daily autonomy and craft become less influential. Does Good's theorem still license the conclusion that reading the free report cannot make her worse off in expectation? Say exactly which premise of the argument the development bears on. Then consider whether the argument can be repaired, for instance by treating the preference shift itself as an outcome under higher-order preferences, or whether it simply becomes inapplicable once preferences are not fixed. Conclude by saying whether the informal principle that free information can never hurt should be retained, revised, or abandoned, and under what condition.
