I'm a software engineer, so I keep trying to solve conceptual problems with infrastructure. Lately I've been thinking about credit for scientific ideas, mostly because I've been sketching out a little system where researchers post their early notes to a public timestamped log, and the rule is that the first verifiable timestamp gets priority for the idea—so if two people independently hit on the same thing, there's no ambiguity about who was first. I went down a rabbit hole on Darwin and Wallace to see how the classic case of simultaneous discovery would have looked under a system like that, and I found the actual letter Darwin sent to Lyell on 18 June 1858 after Wallace's manuscript arrived. The bit that keeps nagging me is this:

"Please return me the M.S. which he does not say he wishes me to publish; but I shall of course at once write & offer to send to any Journal. So all my originality, whatever it may amount to, will be smashed. Though my Book, if it will ever have any value, will not be deteriorated; as all the labour consists in the application of the theory.
I hope you will approve of Wallace's sketch, that I may tell him what you say."

So here's my question. Suppose Darwin had my timestamping system: he could have posted his 1842 and 1844 sketches years earlier and locked in priority. Do you think he would have used it? And more importantly, would he have thought 'first public timestamp wins' was a good rule for settling these cases? I can't tell wether that last bit—that his book "will not be deteriorated; as all the labour consists in the application of the theory"—means he would have seen the timestamp as a nice bureaucratic fix for a real problem, or as sort of missing the point about what actually earns scientific credit. I want your best guess at Darwin's actual view, grounded in what he wrote here, not a generic pro/con of preprints.
