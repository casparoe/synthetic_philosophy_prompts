I've got an epistemic policy I only recently noticed myself following, and I can't tell if it's a feature of my Platonism or a bug. I'm a moral realist in a Plato-flavored way: I think things like justice, fidelity, and courage are forms, real independently of what anyone believes about them. My policy is this: when someone disagrees with me about a moral question, I almost never treat the disagreement as evidence that there's no fact of the matter; instead I file their view as one more prisoner in the cave reporting on the same form from a different angle and worse lighting. Concrete case: a friend and I argued about whether a promise to someone who has since become deliberately cruel still binds. I said fidelity doesn't dissolve just because the person it binds you to got worse; she said the promise's normative force depends on the relationship it creates. Neither of us moved. My policy told me: add her position to my set of partial perspectives on fidelity, don't lower confidence in the form. The odd part is I don't do this for factual disagreements — if someone said London was landlocked I wouldn't treat that as another shadow of some London-form, I'd just look it up. So the policy is moral-specific.

Part of me thinks this is just what the cave story predicts: if we're all stuck watching shadows, disagreement is exactly what you'd expect, and convergence would be the surprising thing. But that cuts both ways — it makes the cave story compatible with every possible pattern of agreement and disagreement, which is exactly my worry. Is there a version of this policy that stays responsive to evidence, or should I ditch it as Plato-shaped motivated reasoning? What would a disagreement have to look like before I was rationally required to update toward "there's no form here, just conflicting attitudes" rather than "three more people, three more angles"? And is the moral/factual asymmetry doing real work, or is that where the whole thing goes wrong?
