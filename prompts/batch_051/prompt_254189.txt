Two members of my rationalist reading group recently spent an evening arguing about a piece of hinge epistemology they had both read, and I still cannot tell which of them had the better argument. The dispute was over whether the commitments called hinges—roughly, the things that must stay fixed if ordinary inquiry is to get a grip, such as that there is an external world, that memory is broadly reliable, and that the Earth has existed for more than five minutes—are simply empirical beliefs held with very high confidence, or whether they stand outside the space of evidence and probability altogether. This is roughly how the exchange went.

(1) The first insisted that a hinge is not a proposition held with very high probability. A hinge, he said, is part of the frame within which probabilities get assigned: it is not a hypothesis ranked at 0.999 alongside the others, because any piece of evidence we could cite is already interpreted inside a framework that presupposes the hinge. You cannot mount a non-question-begging likelihood argument for the existence of the external world, since any experience you cite is equally compatible with the hinge and with its denial, because the denial can always be elaborated to reinterpret the experience.

(2) The other replied that this is psychology rather than epistemology. If a claim is insulated from every possible observation, that is a cost, not a virtue: he has made it unfalsifiable and then helped himself to it. Moreover, the first does not really treat his hinges as beyond evidence. If a vastly more advanced being credibly announced that it had created the universe five minutes ago, complete with apparent memories and geological records, he would at least consider the announcement. So what he calls a hinge is just an empirical claim he regards as overwhelmingly supported.

(3) The first answered that the five-minutes-ago scenario is not evidence against the hinge from within the present framework; it is a proposal to change frameworks. To evaluate the announcement, he would have to rely on memory and the senses—the very hinges in question—so there is no neutral standpoint from which to compare the two frameworks. The claim that the hinge is overwhelmingly supported therefore presupposes the hinge and cannot support it.

(4) The other answered that this move proves too much: it can protect any claim whatever, since a defender of any position can dismiss a challenge as just another framework. When we say the Earth is old, we mean ordinary evidence—geology, carbon dating, historical records, the mutual consistency of millions of independent traces—and if one can reply that all of that is framework-dependent, then the word evidence has stopped doing any work. The Bayesian view, unlike the hinge view, at least says which frameworks are better supported by the traces we actually have.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the four moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say which specific points each side is right about, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. I want the verdict on the arguments as actually made, not a general essay on whether hinge epistemology or Bayesianism is the better overall position.
