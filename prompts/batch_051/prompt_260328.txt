Take the He Jiankui affair of 2018, in which a researcher announced the birth of twin girls whose genomes he had edited with CRISPR, as a real-world episode to analyse through the lens of the unilateralist's curse. I use that term in the following sense: when a community faces a collectively consequential action that any single member can effectively take on the community's behalf, the decision may be made by whichever member has the most optimistic assessment of risks and benefits, the strongest private incentive, or the weakest internal restraint, even if a majority would have refused. Treat the international biomedical research community, and where relevant the Chinese research institutions He belonged to, as the community, and treat He as the unilateral actor. Begin by making the mapping explicit: who are the parties whose views should have counted, what was the action under collective consideration, what evidence or threshold should have governed it, and what would a properly collective decision procedure have required? Then assess the fit. Which features of the episode does the curse explain well — for example, the gap between the widespread disapproval of human germline editing and the fact that it happened anyway, the difficulty of enforcing soft norms, the way individual initiative can overtake slower deliberative bodies — and which features does it explain badly or not at all? In particular, consider whether the episode is better understood as a divergence of risk estimates, or as a story about regulatory arbitrage, institutional prestige, national scientific ambition, or a researcher seeking media prominence. Finally, reverse the direction of analysis: what does the He Jiankui case show about the unilateralist's curse? Does it confirm the mechanism, reveal that the mechanism only operates under specific institutional preconditions, or suggest that the theory needs to be extended to handle actors whose motivations are not primarily epistemic?
