In the dilemma often attributed to Carl Hempel, an attempt to define the term “physical” is said to face a choice between two unpalatable options. I want a precise explanation of what the dilemma is, what it does and does not show, and where its real force lies. Please lay out the two horns as carefully as possible: one on which physicalism is defined by reference to the ontology of present-day physics, thereby making the thesis hostage to future scientific revision; and another on which physicalism appeals to a future, completed physics that no one actually possesses, thereby threatening to make the thesis indeterminate or vacuous. Then analyze whether the dilemma is best understood as an epistemic problem, a semantic problem, or a metaphysical problem. In particular, does it rest on a hidden assumption that there is a privileged, uniquely correct physical description of the world? Could a physicalist escape by defining “physical” negatively—for example, as whatever is not fundamentally mental, or as whatever is in principle accessible to the methods of the physical sciences rather than by any specific inventory of entities—and if so, what exactly would such an escape concede? Does it leave physicalism with enough content to be a substantive metaphysical claim?
