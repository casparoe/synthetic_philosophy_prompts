Act as a professional philosopher specialising in value theory and the nature of achievement. Think carefully before you answer, and do some silent brainstorming of candidate principles first.

I've been collecting my own verdicts on private achievements — cases where someone becomes genuinely excellent at something but no one else ever benefits — and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them.

Here are my gut reactions. A mathematician spends a decade proving a hard theorem and then burns the notebooks: I think that was good, really good, something valuable happened even though no one ever knew. A person trains alone and eventually deadlifts 300kg in a home gym, never filming it or telling anyone: also good, though maybe a notch below the maths. A person becomes a flawless forger of old master etchings and then destroys every plate and print: skilful, sure, but I dont find myself calling it a valuable achievement. A person drills alone until they are the best in the world at memorising shuffled decks of cards, then stops and never uses it: I sense this isn't good, but I can't rule out that this is just snobbery about what counts as worth doing. A person practises on a lifelike dummy until they can cause maximum pain with minimal tissue damage, never harming anyone: not good; the skill itself seems rotten. A person privately works out a cure for a rare disease and then the records burn: good, and also tragic.

My best candidate principle so far is: an achievement is good in itself just when it actualises a distinctively human capacity to a high standard of excellence, regardless of whether anyone benefits. That gets the mathematician and the lifter, but it wobbles badly on the forger and the card memoriser, and I'm not sure what it says about the torturer.

What principle, if any, captures the pattern? State it (or a short list of candidates) precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop or can't accommodate, and what it says about this further case: someone becomes a brilliant chess player purely by playing themselves, never a real opponent or a recorded game, and then stops.

Please answer in British English. Format: a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
