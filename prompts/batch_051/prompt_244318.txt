My friend and I spent a long evening arguing about what should count as rational betting once you take seriously the fact that wealth compounds multiplicatively across repeated bets — the family of ideas usually called Kelly betting or geometric rationality. We produced a four-part test, and then we got stuck on a case one of us insisted shows that our own test is ill-formed. I want your verdict, not a survey of the literature.

Our four criteria for calling a betting policy geometrically rational:

1. Log-growth optimality. Holding the available alternatives fixed, the policy maximizes the expected logarithm of wealth per bet, or equivalently maximizes the long-run compound growth rate of the bankroll.

2. Bankroll preservation. The policy keeps the gambler in the game: it assigns probability zero to the bankroll ever reaching zero, and it never stakes the entire bankroll or takes on leverage.

3. Time consistency. After any realized history of wins and losses, an agent with unchanged preferences and unchanged information who re-solved the problem from the new bankroll would adopt the same policy; the policy is not something one can only endorse before the losses arrive.

4. Repetition robustness. The policy's advantage or disadvantage relative to alternatives is not a small-sample fluke: if the same opportunity were offered indefinitely many times, the policy's long-run ranking against the alternatives would be preserved rather than reversed.

The borderline case. A gambler faces a long sequence of independent, identically distributed binary bets. On each trial she may stake any fraction f of her current bankroll. If the bet wins, she receives a net profit of b times the stake; if it loses, she loses the stake. The win probability is p, with p and b positive and such that the full Kelly fraction f* = p − (1 − p)/b lies strictly between 0 and 1, so the bet is genuinely favorable but not a sure thing. Now suppose the gambler adopts a fixed policy of staking exactly half of the full Kelly fraction on every trial: f = f*/2. This half-Kelly policy is standard advice among practitioners who fear parameter error and deep drawdowns, but by construction it fails criterion 1: its expected log growth is strictly lower than full Kelly's, though still positive.

Does half-Kelly count as geometrically rational by our four criteria? Work through the criteria one by one, flag any criterion whose wording underdetermines the verdict or smuggles in an assumption, and then give the bottom line. In particular, tell me whether the case shows that one of our criteria needs revising — for example, whether criterion 1 is the real test and the others are doing little work, or whether our test conflates two different projects that happen to share a name: what an idealized agent with a known model should do, versus what a bounded or ambiguity-averse agent should do.

Also, tell me how confident you are in the overall verdict and single out the point you are least sure of.

End with a machine-readable line, picking exactly one of these three labels: \answer{qualifies}, \answer{does not qualify}, or \answer{underdetermined by the criteria}.
