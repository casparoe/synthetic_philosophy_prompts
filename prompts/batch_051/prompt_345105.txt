I've been asked to lead a seminar discussion on Sophocles' Oedipus Rex, and I keep tripping over the play's moral logic. Thebes is ravaged by a plague because the killer of Laius has never been brought to justice, and Oedipus, as king, pronounces a solemn curse on the unknown man. As the investigation closes in, it becomes clear that Oedipus himself is Laius's killer, and further that he has married Laius's widow, his own mother. Yet when he committed these acts he did not know the identity of the man he killed or the woman he married; the parricide and the incest were not intended. The play nonetheless treats them as a pollution that must be uncovered and expiated. Oedipus does not let his ignorance cancel the guilt; he blinds himself and begs to be cast out, and the city's deliverance seems to require discovery and punishment. The moral universe of the play therefore looks like one in which a person can be defiled and punished for acts whose wrongness depends entirely on facts outside his awareness, without any guilty intent.

I want to use the play to think about strict liability and the requirement of a guilty mind (mens rea) in the philosophy of criminal responsibility, but I do not want to force the play into a modern legal category. Could you give me a reading that takes both the play and the philosophical problem seriously? In particular, reconstruct what theory of responsibility the play's cosmos seems to presuppose, say whether the play itself complicates or resists that theory (through Oedipus's self-mutilation, through the chorus's mixture of pity and horror, or through the distance it opens between ritual pollution and moral blame), and then commit to a view about what the tragedy ultimately shows regarding whether ignorance excuses. I would like a definite, committed interpretation, not a menu of options.
