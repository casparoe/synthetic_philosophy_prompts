I've been mulling a view about consciousness and want you to stress-test it. The view: a mental state M is conscious just in case, while M occurs, the subject is disposed to form a higher-order thought roughly of the form "I am in M"—where M is picked out demonstratively rather than by a stored description, and where the higher-order thought needn't itself be conscious. The disposition is triggered by being prompted, asked to report, or otherwise needing to say what's going through one's mind, and the actual thought doesn't have to be tokened every moment. I like this because it dodges the objection that we don't run a constant inner monologue tagging each conscious state, and because it explains why conscious states are the ones that become available for report and deliberation when it matters.

What would count against it? Map the view's exposure: the evidence, cases, intuitions, and arguments that should move me, sorted from decisive to merely awkward. Does the dispositional move insulate the view too much—reducing "conscious" to "reportable if asked" so it risks nothing—and does it collapse into access consciousness or global workspace theories? If it does, is that a refutation or just relabeling? Say what the view predicts that its rivals don't, and be honest about whether it's been qualified into unfalsifiability and whether that's a problem. End by saying how confident you are in each main claim and singling out the point you're least sure of.
