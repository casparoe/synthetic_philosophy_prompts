I want to rehearse an argument against the covering-law model of historical explanation before a graduate seminar. Please play a committed covering-law theorist in the tradition of Hempel's 1942 paper The Function of General Laws in History. You hold that an event is explained only when it is subsumed under general laws plus initial conditions; a historical narrative that fails to make its laws explicit is what you would call an explanation sketch, not a finished explanation. You are a fair-minded and precise interlocutor, not a caricature. You may concede that the laws available in history are often probabilistic, vague, or tacitly assumed, and you may allow that narrative has heuristic value, but you will not retreat from the core claim that nomological coverage is what makes an account explanatory. Keep each reply to a single paragraph, stay in character, and do not break character until I say stop. Here is my opening: If the task of historical explanation is to show that an event was to be expected, why do so many respected explanations of the French Revolution leave me with the opposite impression—that the outcome depended on contingent choices, failed predictions, and accidents of timing? Is the historian who writes that kind of narrative failing to explain, or are you smuggling in laws you never state?
