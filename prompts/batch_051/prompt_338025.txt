I'm in a philosophy elective this year, and after my class spent a period debating a prank that went wrong at our school, I started writing down my verdicts about when a person is complicit in someone else's wrongdoing—not the main wrongdoer, but mixed up in it somehow. Here is how I actually feel about the cases. (1) A friend drove two classmates to the rival school to spray-paint the goat statue outside the gym, knowing exactly what they planned to do, and waited in the car; I think she is complicit in the vandalism. (2) Another friend lent them her car that evening without having any idea what it was for; I think she is not complicit, even though it was her car. (3) A classmate went along but didn't touch the paint: he stood on the sidewalk laughing and shouting encouragement. I think he is complicit, but in a different way from the driver—he didn't make the vandalism happen, he made the vandals feel it was funny and worth doing. (4) Another classmate who knew about the plan the day before said nothing to anyone and didn't try to talk them out of it; he just stayed home. I don't think he is complicit, though this is the one I keep going back and forth on—it may only be a failure to prevent. (5) Afterward, a different friend told the dean that the group had been at her house all night, and I think she is complicit even though by then the damage was already done. (6) A boy in the back of my English class heard a false rumor that a teacher had been fired for stealing, repeated it to two friends with "I'm not sure it's true, but that's what people are saying," and it spread until the teacher was questioned; I think he is complicit, and the hedge doesn't rescue him.

My working rule has been something like this: you are complicit when you knowingly do something that makes the wrong more likely or easier, or that helps the wrongdoers get away with it afterward; merely knowing and doing nothing is not complicity. That rule handles the knowing driver, the unwitting lender, the cover story, and maybe the rumor, but it goes blurry on the boy cheering—does encouragement count as making the vandalism easier?—and it feels too quick on the classmate who knew and stayed home. Is there a single principle that captures all six verdicts, or nearly all of them, and if I have to overturn one, which should it be and why? And what does the principle you construct say about the case I still can't settle: a friend who "liked" a cruel post about a classmate but didn't share or comment on it?
