I have recently had to turn a set of half-explicit hunches about consciousness into an explicit position, because a relative spent several weeks behaviourally unresponsive after a serious brain injury, and the clinicians treating her genuinely disagreed about whether there was still anything it was like to be her. I was asked to prepare a short statement of my philosophical commitments for a reading group on access consciousness and global workspace theory, and I would like you to audit the result. I can state the commitments; what I cannot reliably do from the inside is see where one of them quietly forbids another. I suspect the set is broadly coherent, but I would rather learn where it is not than have it confirmed.

My commitments are:

1. Physicalism. Consciousness is a biological phenomenon through and through. Nothing nonphysical must be added; we merely lack the explanation.

2. The access/phenomenal distinction is real. Block's distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness tracks a difference in the world, not just in idiom. Access consciousness is functional: a content's availability for report, reasoning, and the rational control of action. Phenomenal consciousness is a matter of what it is like. These are not the same property wearing two names.

3. Global workspace theory is right, but only as a theory of access. A representation becomes access-conscious roughly when it is globally broadcast through a frontal-parietal workspace and thereby made available for report and flexible control. I do not take the broadcast itself to constitute phenomenal character.

4. Phenomenal consciousness overflows access. I accept the lesson Block draws from Sperling-style partial-report experiments: a subject can enjoy a detailed phenomenal experience of a briefly presented display even though only a small part of its content is reportable at any moment. Phenomenal character can outrun what is globally broadcast.

5. Report is our only direct epistemic route to another subject, but report is gated by access. First-person report is the only direct evidence we can have about what another subject is conscious of; and report can transmit only content that has already passed through access. Everything a subject tells us is therefore, strictly, evidence about access consciousness.

6. The explanatory target is phenomenal consciousness. The point of a scientific theory of consciousness is to explain phenomenal consciousness itself. A theory that explained access flawlessly while saying nothing about phenomenal character would not have explained consciousness in the sense that ultimately matters.

Please audit this set. I am not asking for a survey of the field; I am asking which of these commitments pull against each other, which pairs are only apparently in tension, and what the cheapest repair is in each case.

First, give a short prose audit: order the trouble spots by severity, and for each say in a sentence or two what has to give.

Second, give the audit as a table with exactly these columns:

- Pair: the two commitments under discussion, by number and a short label (e.g., "1 and 2: physicalism vs the distinction").
- Verdict: one of "Genuine tension," "Apparent only," or "No tension."
- Severity: a number from 0 to 10, where 10 means outright inconsistency and 0 means no pressure at all.
- Diagnosis: in one or two sentences, what the tension consists in, or why it dissolves.
- Cheapest fix: the smallest amendment to which numbered commitment removes the problem, and what the amendment costs elsewhere in the set.
- What would change this verdict: the specific argument, reinterpretation, or empirical result (for example, about no-report paradigms, the neural basis of the Sperling effect, or the sense of "broadcast" in play) that would most plausibly make you raise or lower the severity or reverse the verdict.

Finally, add a short closing note saying where your audit as a whole is most likely to fail: which contested interpretation of one of my commitments, or which live empirical issue, would do the most to reorganize the table if it went the other way.
