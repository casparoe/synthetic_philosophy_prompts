You are a decision theorist and philosopher of practical rationality who works on the sunk cost fallacy and on the line between rational persistence and throwing good effort after bad. Think carefully before you commit to an answer, and do not merely apply the textbook slogan that past costs are irrelevant; the task is to reconstruct which principle best rationalizes a full track record.

I am trying to predict a colleague's verdicts and to uncover the principle she is actually applying. She is firm that these are verdicts about what it is rational to do, not merely descriptions of how people in fact behave. Her track record is as follows:

1. Prepaid vacation: A couple has paid $4,000 for a vacation, but one of them has just been injured and would be miserable if they went. She says the rational choice is to stay home; the money is gone either way and should not push them into a miserable trip.

2. Cinema ticket: A woman has paid $18 for a film and finds it dull after thirty minutes. She says leaving the theater is rational; staying only because the ticket is already paid is a mistake.

3. Long novel: A man has read four hundred of five hundred pages of a novel he now finds a chore, and he expects the last hundred pages to bring no particular payoff. She says finishing is rational because a book read to the end is a different thing from a book abandoned, and the four hundred pages already read give him a reason to complete what he started.

4. Near-complete jigsaw: A woman has placed nineteen hundred pieces of a two-thousand-piece puzzle but now finds the remaining work tedious, and no one will ever see the finished puzzle. She says finishing is rational because abandoning a nearly completed puzzle would make the preceding hours wasted when they need not have been.

5. Failing restaurant: A man has put seven years and most of his savings into a restaurant that has been losing money for two years and has little prospect of recovering. She says he should close it; the years and money already spent do not oblige him to lose more.

The new case is this. A woman has spent about sixty hours knitting a sweater, and roughly a weekend of work remains. She has come to find the pattern ugly, will not wear the finished sweater, has no one to give it to, does not need the practice, no longer enjoys the knitting, and does not especially care about having completed a sweater. What would my colleague say about whether it is rational for her to finish it, if she is applying the same principle behind the five earlier verdicts?

Your reply should begin with the bottom line in the very first sentence: your predicted verdict and the name of the principle you think best captures her, with no preamble and no restating of the question. After that first sentence, give the reasoning.
