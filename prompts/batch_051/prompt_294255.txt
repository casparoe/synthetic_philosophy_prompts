I teach an upper-division PPE seminar on social choice, 18 students, 80 minutes. Next session is on Sen's liberal paradox and I want to run the class as a structured game before I give any lecture, because the usual board-and-brackets presentation makes it look like a math curio instead of a problem about rights and collective choice. Design an activity where students register individual rankings over three or four alternatives — maybe weekly seminar topic, whether the class fund goes to pizza or a book prize, and one 'protected' issue per student where we honor their ranking no questions asked. Pairwise votes determine the social ranking, and we also apply a unanimity rule: if literally everyone prefers X to Y, the group must rank X over Y. The trick I want them to discover is that the rules they all thought were fair produce a cycle — A beats B, B beats C, C beats A — with each pairwise result coming from a rule they consensually accepted. Paper ballots or colored cards are fine, no real money, but give them a point scheme (e.g., 2/1/0 based on whether the eventual winner is their top/middle/bottom) so preferences actually bite. Then debrief: retrace which rule produced which pairwise outcome, name unrestricted domain (they can have any preference ranking, we don't censor it), Pareto, and minimal liberalism, and ask whether they'd rather give up protecting an individual's private issue or give up unanimity, and what each loss costs. Tell me what predictably goes wrong the first run — e.g., a group by accident gets a Condorcet winner and misses the paradox, or students start strategically misreporting to break the cycle — and how I should handle those. Be honest about one thing the game can't capture: it can't get them to feel why a person gets a protected sphere in the first place, so I'll have to supply the moral argument for rights afterward. Timeline in minutes, materials, and the exact debrief questions, please.
