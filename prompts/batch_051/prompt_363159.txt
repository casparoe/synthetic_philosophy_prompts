Give me a working definition — or two, if one word is doing two jobs — of the phenomenon we call pity, which sometimes shades into compassion and sometimes into something more like contempt. Test it against these cases: (1) the tightness in the chest on passing a photograph of a dead child, after which one scrolls on; (2) a surgeon whose hands stay steady while operating on a stranger, but who feels almost nothing while doing it; (3) sending money to an aid organization for people one will never meet; (4) giving a panhandler a coin mainly to make the interaction stop; (5) a family that keeps paying a sibling's gambling debts long after the help starts to look like the problem; (6) a colleague who performs grief over a coworker's misfortune but is privately relieved. Some of these are meant to be clear, some borderline. I want you to run the definition against each case, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising result. I suspect the word does two jobs: one names an unselfish, direct responsiveness to another's suffering — the thing Schopenhauer took to be the foundation of morality — and the other names a self-regarding, condescending, or morbid reaction to suffering, the sort of thing Nietzsche treated as pity's pathology. If you agree, give two definitions and say which cases move between them. If you disagree, defend a single definition and show how it handles all six cases.
