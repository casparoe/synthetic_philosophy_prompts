I am a novelist drafting a book about work itself: two interleaved lives, a Tokyo project manager who is destroyed by the expectations of his firm and a Parisian civil servant organizing against the erosion of the 35-hour week. In my research I keep meeting the same slogan, printed on productivity posters and quoted by managers as though it settled something: time is money. I know the line is usually traced to Benjamin Franklin's Advice to a Young Tradesman. What I need from you is not a history of the phrase but a working disambiguation for my two characters. In the essay Franklin urges a young tradesman to remember that time is money, and he illustrates the maxim by inviting the reader to imagine a man who can earn a certain amount by his labor in a day but instead spends half the day idle or in diversion. Franklin argues that the man should not count only the small sum he spent on his amusement; he has also lost, or thrown away, the wages he might have earned in that time. Franklin then extends the same logic to credit and to a reputation for punctuality, suggesting that wasted time is wasted capital and that a reputation for keeping exact hours is itself an asset. Lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making, from the relatively anodyne to the very strong, for example that time has an opportunity cost or that all waking hours are properly measured by the wage rate. For each precise claim, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and whether Franklin's illustration actually supports it. Then tell me which of these readings are in play when a manager uses the saying to justify expecting employees to answer email after hours; when a labor economist uses it to argue that a 35-hour week is an arbitrary constraint on voluntary exchange; and when an advocate for a legal right to disconnect uses it to diagnose what is wrong with karoshi. I want to know which reading my manager character is really invoking, and which reading my Parisian character should attack. At the very end, give me a one-sentence summary of your overall verdict, prefaced by the word Summary:.
