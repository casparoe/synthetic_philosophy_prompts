I've been down an inverse reinforcement learning rabbit hole, and I keep thinking there's a real structural parallel hiding in the non-identifiability problem that nobody around me find as exciting as I do, so I want to run it by someone not already tired of hearing about reward shaping. The setup is this: you watch a person acting in a known MDP, assume their behavior is optimal for some reward function over states, and try to recover that reward. The standard result is that behavior pins the reward down only up to a big equivalence class — scaling and additive constants are the boring part, but potential-based reward shaping gives you whole families of distinct-looking reward functions (reward plus γ times the potential of the next state minus the potential of the current state, for arbitrary state-potential) that make exactly the same policy optimal. So the observed behavior underdetermines the reward.

This feels structurally identical to Quine's indeterminacy of translation. There, the totality of verbal behavior — including dispositions to assent or dissent to sentences like gavagai — is supposedly compatible with multiple translation manuals that assign non-equivalent meanings, and Quine says there's no fact of the matter which manual is correct. In both cases you have observable behavior, an assumption that the behavior is generated by some unobservable item (a reward function / a translation manual or meaning), and an argument that the observable data leave a whole family of candidates. So is this parallel deep or superficial? Specifically, is reward identification in IRL literally a special case of the same underdetermination Quine is pointing at, so that a Quinean should say the 'true reward function' has no fact of the matter beyond behavioral equivalence? Or do the disanalogies dominate — in IRL the state and action spaces and transition function are fixed and known, the optimality assumption is a modeling choice we could drop, people often add a regularizer to select a canonical reward, and the shaping equivalence itself breaks if the transition function changes; none of which has an obvious analogue in radical translation.
