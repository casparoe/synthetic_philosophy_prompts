I am trying to settle what I actually think Kant's universalizability test is testing, case by case. Here are my present verdicts. A maxim like 'when I need cash, I will make a false promise to repay' plainly fails the test: I cannot even conceive of that maxim as a universal law, because the practice of promising would collapse. A maxim like 'when a job application asks whether I was dismissed, I will lie' fails too, and seems to fail the same way. A maxim like 'I will give no help to anyone in need' also fails, but the failure feels different: a world of universal non-help is thinkable, yet I cannot rationally will it, since I would be willing away help I might need. A maxim of letting my talents go to waste in favor of idle enjoyment fails in that same second way. So far I am comfortable. But then I consider a maxim like 'I will deceive the murderer at my door about where my friend is hiding,' and I do not think it fails — or if it does, it fails for a merely technical reason I do not trust. Likewise, 'I will break my promise to return a borrowed weapon when the owner has gone mad and says he will kill someone with it' seems plainly all right to me. I suspect the familiar distinction between a contradiction in conception and a contradiction in will cuts across these cases, but I do not think that distinction alone resolves the two hard ones. Is there one principle behind the test that delivers all these verdicts, or nearly all of them? If not, which of my verdicts should I revise, and what does the resulting principle say about a maxim of telling a small untruth to protect someone's privacy?
