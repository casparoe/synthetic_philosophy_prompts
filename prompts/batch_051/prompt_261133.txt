I'm in a third-year political philosophy module and we've just done two seminars on Rawls's treatment of envy and resentment, and I came out more confused then I'd like to admit. My tutor kept insisting that for Rawls resentment is a moral feeling — it responds to an injustice — whereas envy isn't really a moral feeling at all, because someone else simply having more than you isn't a wrong done to you. But then Rawls also says envy can be excusable in the right conditions, and the seminar split in half: some people said excusable envy is just resentment that hasn't found its proper object yet, others said it's still envy and Rawls is trying to have it both ways. I'm trying to write a short piece on this and keep going in circles, so I want a working definition of envy that separates it from resentment (or two definitions, if you think they do different jobs), plus a criterion for when envy is excusable rather than vicious.

Here are the cases I want the definition to get right. (a) A student gets a high mark after their parent, who is a professor, quietly pressures the examiner; this student's coursemate, who worked harder and got less, feels pure fury at them — I'm confident this is resentment. (b) The same coursemate notices the person next to them scored five marks more with no hint of unfairness and feels that hot twist whenever that person is praised — I'm confident this is envy. (c) A junior doctor is bitter about consultants who earn several times her salary for what she regards as less demanding work, in a pay structure nobody individually controls — I can't tell whether that's envy of the better-off or resentment about an unjust structure. (d) Someone living in poverty watches diners through the window of a Michelin-starred restaurant and feels genuine hostility towards them while believing the diners have done nothing wrong individually — I want to call that excusable envy, but then I have to say what makes it different from (b). (e) A tenant is angry at their landlord's large profit even though the landlord has stuck to the terms of the contract and the law, and the anger attaches to the whole landlord and tenant relation rather than to any breach — I wanted to call this resentment, but I'm not sure Rawls would let me. (f) A person finds themselves hoping their successful friend's new business fails, purely because the success sharpens their sense of their own failure; they're ashamed of the thought and never act on it. Run each case through your definition, tell me where it gives no verdict or the wrong one and whether you'd revise it, and say which verdict is least secure — and if any case shows envy working as a signal that a distribution is unjust rather than just a personal vice, I'd want to know why.
