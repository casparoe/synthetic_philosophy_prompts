In my world religions class we learned about the historical-critical method, and I have been stuck on a puzzle ever since. The method reads the Bible the way a historian would read any ancient text: it refuses to appeal to miracles as explanations, and it assumes that the best account of a passage is built from ordinary human causes — authors, editors, communities, literary conventions, institutional interests. Our teacher called this methodological naturalism and stressed that it brackets divine action as a rule of procedure, not as a claim that God did not act. Yet the more I think about it, the less I can see a stable middle position for a believer. Suppose a historian concludes that a passage does not record events in the way it describes, because it was shaped by a community decades later to address its own situation. If the believer says, "That is fine, but it does not rule out God working through that community," then the historical finding makes no contact with their faith. But if the believer lets the finding constrain their reading — for example, by deciding the passage cannot be straightforward history and must be interpreted differently — then a method that was supposed to be neutral about God has gained authority over what revelation can mean. It looks as though believers are left with two options, both bad: either secular historical results get to set limits on revelation, or faith becomes something history can never meaningfully engage. Is that dilemma real? If not, what is the third possibility, and which assumption of mine is doing the work?
