I am a novelist writing a political novel in which a serving minister repeatedly faces situations in which the only available means of preventing a serious harm appears to be an act that is itself wrong. I want to depict this condition with some precision, not as a vague mood of moral compromise, and I need a stable working definition to keep the depiction consistent from scene to scene. The term I want defined is *dirty hands*, understood narrowly: not corruption, not hypocrisy, not the ordinary trade-offs of office, but the predicament of a political agent who takes a genuine wrong upon themselves in the service of something they take to be required, and who is not simply exonerated by the necessity. My ordinary grasp of the term is confident; the definition is what I lack. I am not asking for a bibliography or an account of who has said what about this; I need a definition I can actually operate with.

Here are the cases the definition must handle, with my provisional sense of each.

1. A president authorizes an operation to sabotage an enemy's supply line, knowing with near certainty that it will kill a civilian worker at the site, because the alternative is the fall of a defended city and the loss of far more lives. The operation is carried out and the city is saved. I take this to be as central a case of dirty hands as there is; whatever the final moral verdict on the act, the concept names it.

2. A city councillor approves a developer's permit in exchange for a concealed campaign contribution. The approval harms no one directly, but it violates a public trust. I take this not to be dirty hands at all. It is ordinary wrongdoing: the councillor is not caught between a good and a necessary wrong; he is simply selling something that was not his to sell.

3. A health minister imposes a stay-at-home order during an outbreak, foreseeing that it will cost some vulnerable people their livelihoods and, indirectly, some of them their lives, while preventing many more deaths. The order is lawful and, on its face, an ordinary regulatory act; the deaths are foreseen but not intended. My instinct is that this is not dirty hands in the technical sense, but I cannot yet say which feature excludes it, and I suspect my instinct rests on a distinction the definition needs to earn.

4. A finance minister tells her cabinet and the press a deliberate lie — that no informal understanding has been reached with creditors — in order to prevent a run on the currency and a sovereign default that would immiserate millions. The lie succeeds. My instinct is that this should count, if wrongful means rather than wrongful ends are what dirty hands is about; but I also suspect that not every justified deception can carry the weight the concept carries, and I want the definition to tell the difference.

5. A minister approves the same kind of operation as in the first case, and it succeeds, but she is afterward genuinely untroubled: she speaks of the civilian death as a regrettable line item and sleeps soundly. I do not know whether this is still dirty hands, or whether it is dirty hands plus a further moral defect, or whether the absence of the stain means the concept no longer applies. The definition should force an answer.

6. A prime minister refuses to order an unlawful mass expulsion of a restive population, an expulsion that would in fact have defused an uprising at far lower loss of life than the uprising ultimately takes. I think this is not dirty hands, but I want the definition to explain why the concept seems adjacent here: what the prime minister failed to do is different in kind from what the presidents and ministers above did, and it matters to me whether the definition can say why without collapsing the two.

7. A legislator votes for an omnibus budget that includes a provision she regards as a serious injustice, because rejecting the bill would cause a government shutdown and a worse crisis. She does not author the provision and has no means to amend it, but she votes to enact it. I am genuinely uncertain whether this is dirty hands or merely ordinary political cost, and I want a definition that draws the boundary rather than leaving it to intuition.

Please do the following: propose a single working definition of dirty hands; run it through all seven cases without skipping any; say plainly where the definition gives a verdict that differs from my provisional one or gives no clear verdict; and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. I am asking for a committed answer: make a definite choice about the definition and about each case, give your reasons, and do not conclude with a menu of options.
