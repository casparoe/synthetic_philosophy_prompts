I have been trying to get clear about what I actually think about intention by working through a series of cases, and I would like you to help me extract the principle behind my reactions — or tell me which of my reactions I must give up. The baseline case is the familiar toxin puzzle. A reliable benefactor offers me a large sum to form, before midnight tonight, the genuine intention to drink a mildly unpleasant but harmless toxin tomorrow at noon; the money is paid as soon as the intention is verified, whether or not I ever drink. I find that I cannot form the intention simply by deciding to. I can tell myself that I will drink, but I know I will not, and the intention never actually takes hold. By contrast, if a friend promises to give a large donation to a cause I detest in the event that I fail to drink, the intention forms without difficulty, because tomorrow the act will still have a reason speaking for it. Likewise, if a second, equal payment is offered but only on condition that I actually drink, the intention forms easily. I am also entirely confident that I can intend to undergo painful surgery tomorrow when it is the only way to treat an illness: the pain is a means to something I want, so the reason carries through to the act itself. So far I think my verdicts hang together. But I am torn about the following variant. I try to form the intention by resolving to drink in order to be the sort of person who does not make hollow self-directed intentions. If I really value being that sort of person, then it seems I now have a reason to drink tomorrow — my integrity — and the intention should form; yet this also feels like bootstrapping, as though I have manufactured a reason to drink out of a reward that was only for forming the intention in the first place. I would not want to claim that this makes the original case trivially easy. One further datum: I can form the conditional state expressed by "I will drink tomorrow if I feel like it at noon," but I would not call that an intention of the sort the reward is for, and I would not claim the money on its basis.

My provisional hypothesis is that one can intend to φ only if one expects to have, at the time of acting, a reason to φ that will survive until the moment of action. That handles the surgery, the penalty, and the additional-payment cases, but it appears to fail on the integrity variant unless I treat the desire to preserve my own integrity as such a reason — which would make the original puzzle too easy, against my first datum; and the hypothesis says nothing about a neutral variant I keep going back and forth on, in which someone offers the same reward for intending to count silently to one hundred tomorrow at noon, an act I have no reason to perform and no reason to avoid. Is there a single account of intention that captures all of these verdicts, and if not, which verdict should I overturn? Please state the account precisely, apply it to each case, and tell me what it predicts about the counting variant and about one further case of your own devising.
