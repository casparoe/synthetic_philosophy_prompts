I've spent the last few years running a graduate seminar on rational belief, and everytime we hit the unit on imprecise probabilities the same two camps line up and talk past each other until I referee them into exhaustion. I'd rather make them write something down. One camp, call them the sharp Bayesians, holds that any rational agent's uncertainty can be represented by a single probability function; if two reasonable people assign different probabilities to the same proposition, at least one of them is missing evidence, processing it badly, or being sloppy about what the evidence supports. The other camp, the imprecise probabilists, holds that rational agents are sometimes stuck with irreducibly imprecise beliefs, and the right representation is a set of probability functions, an interval, or some other partial ordering rather than one number; they point to cases like Ellsberg's urn and to dilation, where the sharp answer feels forced and, they think, leads you into dutch books or incoherent valuations if you insist on one.

Write the joint statement the two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, the thing a referee would staple to the syllabus next year. I want a run of claims they can literally both sign, including what a representation of uncertainty has to do in the first place for them to even argue about it, and the points about Ellsberg and dilation that neither side has to distort. Then the disagreement, precise enough that each side would accept the wording of its own position and not feel caricatured by the other. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side, each tied to a concrete case or argument both camps would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case recorded in advance. I suspect the cruxes will include whether dilation is a bug or a feature and what happens in sequential decisions where a choice now forecloses information later, but don't let my guesses limit you. And if some chunk of the fight is verbal, say which chunk and why.

Please write it all as continous prose, like a memo or a letter, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. If you must number the agreed claims or the cruxes, do it inside the running text, first second and so on, not as an actual list. I don't want references or a bibliography either; the whole thing should rest on the arguments and the cases, not on who said what.
