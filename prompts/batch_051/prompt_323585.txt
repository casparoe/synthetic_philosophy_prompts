I cover science and ideas for a living, and the doomsday argument keeps surfacing in my reporting on long-term risk; I am still not sure whether the way I resist it is a piece of good judgment or just motivated reasoning. The problem shows up as an asymmetry between two inferences that look structurally identical to me. Suppose I check into a hotel and am told only that my room was assigned at random from all the rooms in the building, and my key says 8. I will gladly take that as evidence that the hotel is on the smaller side — a 20-room building makes drawing an 8 much more likely than a 2,000-room building does, and respectable people do not call this a fallacy. But now consider the doomsday argument. I am roughly the hundred-billionth human to have lived. If humanity is headed for a total population in the trillions, then my position near the front of that sequence would be an enormous coincidence; if the total turns out to be only modestly larger than the number already born, my rank is exactly the sort of thing a randomly drawn observer should expect. The argument says I should therefore treat my early rank as evidence that the total human population will be on the smaller side — and, like most people I have watched meet this argument, I refuse.

I need to know whether anything justifies treating the two cases differently, or whether the refusal is just what we do when an argument's conclusion is unpleasant. The candidate differences I can formulate are: the hotel case specifies an actual randomizing mechanism, whereas my existence was not literally drawn from an urn containing all the humans who will ever live; the reference class of all future humans is far less definite than the rooms in a building; and the doomsday case asks me to regard beings who do not yet exist as the population from which I was sampled. Are these real differences in evidential structure, or redescriptions of the discomfort? Please reconstruct the strongest principle that lets me keep the hotel inference while blocking the doomsday inference, test it against the nearest variants of each case, and give a candid verdict on whether the ordinary asymmetry should be kept, abandoned, or reversed. Make the final line a machine-readable one of the form \answer{keep the asymmetry}, \answer{abandon the asymmetry}, or \answer{reverse the asymmetry}.
