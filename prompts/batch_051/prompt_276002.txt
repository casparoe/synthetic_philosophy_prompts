I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophical training, and I keep running into a claim I don't know how to weigh: that the transition from hunting and gathering to agriculture was, for a very long stretch, a worse outcome for ordinary human beings than the mobile foraging life it replaced. The case runs like this: early farmers were shorter, more malnourished, more disease-burdened, and more overworked than the foragers around or before them; farming concentrated power and produced inequalities of a depth that mobile foragers rarely could sustain; and once populations grew on stored grain, the previous option was effectively foreclosed, so the system could not backtrack. In my own terms, this looks like a migration that was locally reasonable at every step but still walked into a poor equilibrium with the exits locked. But I can't tell whether that is insight or just a vivid story.

I want a credence, not a bibliography. The proposition I want you to assess is this, with repairs allowed where noted. Take a population at the point where it could either adopt grain farming or continue as mobile foragers. Compare two branches: the actual farming branch and the counterfactual forager branch. Score each branch by average individual well-being—health, lifespan, workload, bodily security, autonomy, and the spread of those goods across people—over the first two to three thousand years after the fork, and set total population size aside. The claim is that the farming branch scores worse. If you think that framing is unworkable, repair it minimally, state the repaired proposition, and give your credence for that too.

Now the actual request. Give me a headline number—your credence that the claim is true—and then take the number apart. I especially want to know how much of your uncertainty sits in each of these places: (a) the ground truth, meaning how bad early farming actually made life relative to foraging; (b) the objective function, meaning whether average welfare over a few thousand years is the right target, or whether total welfare, a longer horizon, or a different weighting would flip the verdict; (c) the counterfactual, meaning whether the forager branch was genuinely available to those people given population pressure, climate, and the prior choices that had already narrowed their options; and (d) the attribution, meaning how much of the badness was caused by the transition itself rather than by later and separable arrangements that might have gone differently. Assign rough probabilities or weights to these sub-questions, show how they combine into the headline number, and tell me which sub-question would move your credence most if strong new evidence forced a large update. Also say where your number is most fragile.

My own gut number is around 0.4, but I suspect I am overweighting the trap story because it is a good narrative and underweighting the fact that the people who adopted farming were responding to real constraints. Where do you think my error is most likely to be?

Please write your answer in British English.
