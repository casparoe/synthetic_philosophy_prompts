I have been reading my way through the revisionist argument that collapse is a category mistake when applied to complex societies, and I find much of it almost impossible to resist. Taken seriously, the position has the virtues a good theory should have: it refuses to treat past people as failed versions of us; it argues that a familiar list of supposed collapses—the Maya lowlands, the post-Roman Mediterranean, Easter Island, the end of the Bronze Age palace economies—look very different once you stop assuming that a society is a single organism that either endures or fails; and it diagnoses the appetite for collapse stories as a modern projection, a way of flattering ourselves with apocalypse. And yet I have an equally firm intuition that this account, pressed all the way down, becomes a kind of euphemism. When a region loses most of its population within a few generations, when writing and record-keeping fall silent for centuries, when a great center is emptied and never reoccupied, something has been lost that the language of transformation and resilience does not capture. Most of the people who made up that world are dead; the survivors did not choose their new circumstances, they managed them. Calling that mere change seems to borrow the legitimacy of ordinary cultural drift for what is, in human terms, catastrophe.

The theory and the intuition collide, and I do not know which should yield. I am not asking for a side; I am asking for a negotiated verdict. Should I treat my insistence that something was lost as the very moralizing romanticism the revisionists warn about, and abandon it? Should the resilience framework be amended so that it can say that some transformations are forced, lethal, and genuinely losses while still denying that societies fail as organisms? Or is there a reading on which the two claims are compatible, and what would that reading commit me to? Please lay out what each move costs and what would justify it.
