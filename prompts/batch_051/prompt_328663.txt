Act as an expert on secession and constitutional negotiation, someone who knows the Scottish independence debate of 2012–14 at a granular level. Think carefully and don't rush; start with a quick brainstorm of the main pressure points before committing to a line.

Here's the counterfactual. The referendum on 18 September 2014 is won by Yes, 52 to 48, on the same turnout as actually happened. Nothing before the vote is different: the two campaigns made the same arguments, Westminster made the same promises, and the SNP fought on the same plan — keeping sterling in a formal currency union and keeping the monarch as head of state. Only the count that night is different. Stipulate that the negotiations run on schedule and Scotland becomes an independent state on 24 March 2016, eighteen months after the vote.

I want a reasoned prediction of the next ten years, not a story. Build the argument around the three fights that dominated 2014 — currency, EU membership, and Trident at Faslane — but push further: borders and customs, North Sea revenue, the fate of the SNP, how the rest of the UK party system absorbs the shock, and whether the eventual settlement is stable. For each step, say whether you think the effect is robust or fragile, and what hidden assumption would have to be false for it to go the other way.

In particular, commit on the currency question. After a real 52% Yes vote, do the Treasury and the Bank of England actually hold the line against a sterling union, or does the negotiater's logic soften the veto? And if there is no sterling union, what does Scotland do — keep the pound unilaterally, create a new currency, drift toward the euro — and what happens to Scottish mortgages, bank supervision, and the lender of last resort in the meantime?

Also take a position on the EU path. Does Scotland end up inside the EU, on what timescale, and through what legal route? Does Spain delay it because of Catalonia, and what price gets extracted? And the reverse question: does a Scottish Yes make a Brexit referendum in the rest of the UK more likely, less likely, or neither — is that connection robust or just an easy narrative?

Be explicit about uncertainty. Flag the places where it turns on particular people (Salmond, Cameron, Carney at the Bank), on bond-market reactions, or on how EU institutions understood themselves in 2014. But after the brainstorm, actually commit — don't just catalogue possibilities. End with one paragraph on what this counterfactual should change in how we think about secession referendums as a device for resolving self-determination claims, or whether Scotland is too idiosyncratic to generalize from.
