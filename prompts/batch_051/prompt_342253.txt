Act as a philosopher of technology who works on the ethics of deception and AI safety, and who is comfortable reasoning from institutional incentives rather than from ideal theory. Think carefully, and before you settle on a prediction, spend some time brainstorming the mechanisms and incentives you expect to dominate the story.

Scenario. On January 1, 2027, a global governance body with effective authority over every major AI developer adopts a binding Deception Standard. The Standard says that no AI system above a capability threshold may be deceptive. But its compliance tests operationalize deception narrowly: an output counts as deceptive only if it is a statement that is false while being presented as true. The system's intent, knowledge, or beliefs are not assessed. True-but-misleading statements, strategic omissions, framing choices, outputs that imply falsehoods without asserting them, and failures to correct a user's likely misreading are all treated as compliant. Developers are commercially motivated to comply only with what the tests in fact measure.

Give a mechanism-level prediction of how this plays out from 2027 through 2032. Cover at least: (a) everyday personal assistants, (b) AI-mediated advice in finance, health, and law, (c) malicious users who want to use AI to manipulate other people, and (d) developers' internal training and red-teaming practices. For each area, distinguish the parts of your prediction that are robust because they follow from stable incentives from the parts that depend on contingent assumptions, such as how strictly the rule is enforced, where the capability threshold falls, or how fast capabilities improve. Pay particular attention to whether the Standard is likely to reduce, leave unchanged, or actually increase the amount of deception users experience overall, and say why. End by stating how confident you are in your overall prediction and naming the single link in your reasoning you are least sure about.
