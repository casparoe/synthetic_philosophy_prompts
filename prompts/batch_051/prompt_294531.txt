In a reading group on suffering-focused ethics, a friend of mine produced an argument from a passage in Schopenhauer, and I have not been able to find the place where it overreaches. The passage is from Arthur Schopenhauer, “On the Sufferings of the World,” in The Essays of Arthur Schopenhauer; Studies in Pessimism, translated by T. Bailey Saunders:

“I know of no greater absurdity than that propounded by most systems of philosophy in declaring evil to be negative in its character. Evil is just what is positive; it makes its own existence felt. Leibnitz is particularly concerned to defend this absurdity; and he seeks to strengthen his position by using a palpable and paltry sophism. It is the good which is negative; in other words, happiness and satisfaction always imply some desire fulfilled, some state of pain brought to an end.”

The argument he built on it was this:

(1) Phenomenological asymmetry. Pain is positive in character: it is a felt addition, not the mere absence of anything. Every pleasant or satisfying state, by contrast, is negative: it consists wholly in the fulfillment of a desire or the ending of a pain.

(2) If a pleasant state consists wholly in the ending of a pain or want, then, taken apart from that pain or want, it has no positive felt character of its own; it is a recovery of a zero point, not an elevation above one.

(3) Therefore pleasant states add no positive value to a life over and above the badness of the pain or want they cancel. The value of a life is exhausted by its pains, suitably discounted by whatever relief they receive.

(4) Nor can we object that relief from pain is itself a distinct positive good. That would count the same change twice: once as the badness of the pain, and again as a new good in the pain’s stopping. The pain together with its complete relief can therefore contribute at most zero to a life’s value.

(5) Now compare a possible life L—say, ninety years containing love, work, beauty, and achievement, and exactly one hour of acute pain—with the situation N in which that person is never born. By (3)–(4), L’s goods contribute at most zero and its single hour of pain is a net negative, while N has value zero. So N is better than L.

(6) By combining such comparisons, a world containing no sentient lives at all is better than any world whose sentient lives contain even one hour of pain, however much happiness they otherwise contain.

(7) That conclusion is, to me, unacceptable: a single hour of pain cannot make a rich and vastly happy life worse than never having existed, and a theory of value that is forced to prefer the empty world to a mildly imperfect but thriving one has lost something essential.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would most like to keep (1)—the asymmetry itself—and with it the thought that suffering has some sort of normative priority; but I do not want to reach (7). Which step should I give up, why did it look safe, and what does giving it up cost someone who wants to remain a suffering-focused ethicist? If several steps could be resisted, I want to know what each resistance commits me to.
