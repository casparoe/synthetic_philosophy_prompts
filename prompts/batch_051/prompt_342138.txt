Last evening I had a long and unresolved argument with a friend about whether an unending life would inevitably become tedious. He defended a version of Bernard Williams's Makropulos argument: a meaningful human life is organised around categorical desires — attachments, projects and concerns that give a person reason to go on at all — and such desires cannot be replenished indefinitely. Over an unending life, either one's character and concerns remain coherent enough for the same person to endure, in which case experience eventually turns repetitive and flat, or they change enough to escape that, in which case the later person is no longer continuous with the one who started the life. I kept reaching for a counterexample, and I now want to think it through properly. Suppose a technology allowed one to attenuate or prune episodic memories in a controlled way, preserving skills, dispositions and attachments while particular experiences faded before they could satiate the person. A single agent could then approach even familiar goods with something like perennial freshness. Does Williams's argument survive this development? I am less interested in whether such a technology will arrive than in what the thought experiment reveals about the argument's load-bearing premises. Which premise does the memory-editing case put under pressure, and is there a repaired version of the argument that still gives reason to regard an unending life as bad for creatures like us?
