I am a journalist who covers both the replication crisis in the human sciences and the world of quantitative model-building, and I keep noticing a structural parallel between two diagnoses I hear in those separate rooms. The first is the standard frequentist account of why so many published findings fail to replicate: many research teams test many hypotheses against noisy samples; at a conventional threshold of p < 0.05, a null association will clear the bar by chance one time in twenty; journals and hiring committees select on that clearing; so the published literature gets systematically enriched with results that are, in effect, the luckiest noise. Bayesian critics add that the binary null test is the wrong filter even before selection is considered, because a small p-value can correspond to a negligible shift in posterior probability once base rates and the pressure to find something are taken into account, and they propose shrinkage, hierarchical modeling, and prediction or estimation instead of rejection. The second is the familiar story of overfitting in machine learning and in systematic backtesting: try many model specifications or trading rules on historical data, keep whichever clears an in-sample performance threshold, and the selected candidate will look better than it is, because the threshold partly rewards noise; the standard remedies are regularization, cross-validation, and out-of-sample testing.

I cannot tell whether this is a deep isomorphism or a flattering analogy. The mapping appears to be: hypothesis becomes model; the p-value cutoff becomes the in-sample performance cutoff; the file drawer becomes the discarded backtest; failed replication becomes out-of-sample decay; preregistration becomes the train/test split. But there are also reasons to expect the analogy to come apart: a scientific claim is supposed to travel to new settings and populations, while a backtest is evaluated on data from the process that generated it; the incentive structures of labs and trading desks differ; and the Bayesian critique of null-hypothesis testing does not obviously map onto regularization. I want to know where this parallel is load-bearing and where it misleads, and specifically whether the frequentist/Bayesian divide on replication has a genuine counterpart in the bias/variance or regularization discussion in machine learning. As you answer, please distinguish what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these topics, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
