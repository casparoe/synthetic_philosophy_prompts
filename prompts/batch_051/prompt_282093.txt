Read Plato's Symposium through his account of drink in the Laws. In the Laws, Plato treats the properly supervised drinking party as a discipline in which self-control can be tested and trained, while he condemns ordinary drunkenness as corrupting and shameful. The Symposium is itself such a gathering, but a conspicuously controlled one: the guests decide against heavy drinking and spend the night giving speeches—until Alcibiades arrives drunk and delivers the dialogue's last and most intimate speech. I would like a reading that stands in both directions: how the Laws' theory of wine illuminates what the dialogue is doing, and where the dialogue complicates or resists that theory. Does Alcibiades' drunkenness mark the failure of the very discipline the Laws tries to institute, or does his speech give us the unguarded truth that the sober speeches, for all their brilliance, withhold? And on your reading, is intoxication a threat to self-knowledge, a test of it, or both? End by saying what the Symposium as drama shows about drunkenness that the Laws as doctrine does not.
