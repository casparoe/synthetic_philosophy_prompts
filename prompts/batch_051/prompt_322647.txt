I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I want to stress-test functional decision theory by working through two adjacent cases rather than having jargon waved past me. Please play a committed functional decision theorist: sharp, not a strawman, willing to concede small points but never the core thesis that an agent should choose the output of its decision function that would produce the best expected outcome even when the consequences flow through predictions or logical correlations rather than through direct causal links. Stay in character until I say "stop." Keep replies to one or two paragraphs, and explain technical terms briefly as we go.

Here are the two cases.

Case one is counterfactual mugging. A perfectly accurate predictor flips a fair coin. If it lands tails, the predictor asks me for $100. If it lands heads, the predictor pays me $10,000 if and only if it predicted that I would have paid had the coin landed tails. I have made no precommitment. I now see that the coin actually landed tails, so any payment would leave me $100 poorer in the only branch I will ever observe. The heads branch is like a git branch that was never merged: nothing that happens there returns a value to my current process state.

Case two is a variant I want on the table from the start; call it XOR blackmail. A blackmailer has two exact copies of me, Left and Right, in separate rooms with no communication. Each copy has one choice: pay $100 or refuse. Before the choices, the blackmailer commits to a rule based on its predictions of each copy's decision, with pay coded as 1 and refuse as 0. If the two predicted decisions are equal, so their XOR is 0, the blackmailer releases damaging information about Left. If the two predicted decisions differ, so their XOR is 1, it releases damaging information about Right. The blackmailer predicts each copy's decision procedure and local inputs perfectly. Both copies know the setup but do not know whether they are Left or Right.

Here is my opening move. In the counterfactual mugging case, once I see tails, the rational posterior probability I assign to heads is zero, and refusing makes me strictly richer under that posterior. To get me to pay, you have to say I should evaluate the whole decision function against a distribution over branches I have already had the chance to condition on, not against the branch I actually observe. But that is not a neutral principle: it is exactly the updatelessness you are trying to justify, and it looks like a memory leak in the utility function. Worse, applying that same function-level standard to XOR blackmail seems wrong. Two deterministic copies running the same decision procedure will always produce the same output, so Left is exposed no matter what procedure is chosen, and the procedure cannot make the outputs differ without asymmetric local information. The total harm is fixed; the only variable left is whether each copy loses $100. So am I supposed to say that functional decision theory pays in counterfactual mugging but refuses in XOR blackmail? If so, convince me the distinction is not just causal decision theory with extra steps.
