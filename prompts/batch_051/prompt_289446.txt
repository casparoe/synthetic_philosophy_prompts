I host a small philosophy podcast, and next week's guest defends a Bayesian version of the doomsday argument. Her view, in outline: if one accepts the self-sampling assumption — that an agent should reason as if they were a random draw from all observers in their reference class — then, because one's birth rank lies within roughly the first hundred billion humans to have lived, futures in which trillions more humans are born would make one a surprisingly early draw. Conditionalizing on one's birth rank and applying Bayes's theorem therefore shifts the posterior toward a relatively modest total number of humans, and hence toward a shorter expected remaining lifespan for the species.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond asking her to restate the view. Include at least one question on each of the following, and for each include a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give: (1) the reference class problem, and in particular why the class should be all humans rather than all observers, all sentient beings, person-moments (time-slices of persons), or a civilization-level class, and whether the argument survives changing the class; (2) the self-indication assumption — the rival principle that one's own existence is itself evidence that many observers exist — and whether it cancels or reverses the doomsday conclusion; (3) whether the argument yields an unconditional prediction about humanity's future or only a shift conditional on a prior over total births; (4) whether the argument has any empirically distinguishable content before the end comes, or whether it is purely an anthropic update.

As you draft the questions, separate what is settled in this debate from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it. Do not smuggle a contested premise, such as the self-sampling assumption or a particular choice of reference class, into a question as agreed background, and where a question presupposes something about the argument or the surrounding debate, flag in a brief bracketed note whether that presupposition is widely shared or disputed.
