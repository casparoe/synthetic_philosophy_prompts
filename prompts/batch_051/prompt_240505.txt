I keep having the same conversation with students who've had one of those impossible-seeming coincidences—a dream about a stranger, then meeting them the next week; a song in their head right before it comes on the radio—and who take it as evidence that something deeper is going on. So this term I wrote out the argument I give them, numbered and everything, and now I'm not sure it's as strong as I've been pretending.

(1) Over a lifetime, and across a whole population, a person is exposed to an enormous number of candidate pairings of events—songs, names, thoughts, places, dates. Call these 'coincidence opportunities.'

(2) For any particular one of these opportunities, the probability that the two events match in a striking way is tiny.

(3) But if you run enough opportunities, it becomes overwhelmingly likely that at least one of them will match. This is just the birthday paradox: 23 people and a better-than-even chance that some pair shares a birthday, even though any particular pair almost certainly doesn't.

(4) And the coincidences we hear about are not a nuetral sample of opportunities; they're exactly the ones where a match happened—we never hear about the millions of misses. It's the lottery winner in the newspaper.

(5) So an anecdote about an amazing coincidence is exactly what chance would have us hear about, and it gives no real evidence of anything beyond chance.

(6) Therefore, a rational person should never treat a coincidence anecdote as a reason to take seriously any 'deeper' explanation—telepathy, providence, the universe sending signs, whatever.

Go through this step by step. Is it valid? If a step fails, say precisely which one and what the corrected principle is; if it's valid, say exactly what it does and doesn't license. I keep wobbling between thinking this is just the right lesson about base rates and thinking one of the steps is doing more work than I can defend.
