In a seminar on Aristotle's four causes, the discussion turned to whether anything like the final cause survives in contemporary biology, or whether Darwinian explanations in terms of efficient causes and natural selection have made it redundant. A fellow student kept cutting this debate short with the slogan 'nature does nothing in vain.' Most recently, when someone proposed that a given trait — the human appendix was the example — might be a functionless byproduct or a vestige, he replied that it had to serve some purpose, because nature does nothing in vain. I suspect the sentence can express anything from a cautious methodological rule to a very strong claim about organisms, and I do not want to object to a claim he may not actually hold. Could you lay out the precise readings 'nature does nothing in vain' might have, as a menu ordered from the most trivial or merely methodological to the most radical? For each reading, indicate whether it is true, false, or defensible only as a rule of thumb; whether it is committed to something like Aristotle's final cause; and whether it can survive a Darwinian account of adaptation. Then say which reading or readings would actually underwrite the move from 'this trait exists' to 'this trait has a purpose,' and what additional assumptions even the strongest reading would need for that inference to go through.
