There's this long-running argument about whether "better than" is transitive that seems to go nowhere, and I want a diagnosis of why rather than a verdict. Here's the shape of it: you start at something clearly good, move along a spectrum in steps that are each clearly better than the one before, and end at something obviously worse than the start. One side says fine, so at least one of the pairwise judgments must be wrong—we just cant tell which one. The other side says no, the pairwise judgments are the data, and transitivity is the thing we should give up; betterness can be nontransitive and that's just a fact about value. The first side counters that dropping transitivity means you can't form a coherent ranking and you're open to money pumps; the second side says that's a problem for decision theory, not for axiology, and anyway the pump needs a sequence of trades you'd never actually make. My working theory is that this isnt really a dispute about value at all: it's about what "better than" is supposed to do. If its job is to guide choice, then it had better be transitive; if its job is just to report pairwise comparisons, intransitivity is weird but not automatically fatal. Is my diagnosis right, or is there a real formal or empirical disagreement hiding underneath? If there is, what could actually move it—a distinction, a theorem, better cases, dropping the word entirely—or should we expect it to stay stuck, and why?
