Two friends of mine got into it last weekend after one of them read some preprint. A lab had reconstructed a mouse's pain circuitry and got it running as a simulation on an ordinary laptop, hooked up to a virtual body that flinches and avoids things. They started arguing about whether the simulation could be in pain, and whether it would be wrong to leave it running all day. I stayed out of it but I want a proper point-by-point verdict.

(1) Lena said: if you think pain is a computation the nervous system performs, and the sim runs the same computation, then the sim is in pain. We can't say minds are substrate independent and then suddenly care about the wetware. If the mouse's pain is in the pattern, the pattern is in the laptop, so we should treat the laptop like we'd treat the mouse.

(2) Marcus said that's the exact slip-up. A computation isn't intrinsicly in the pattern. Whether the laptop is "running the pain computation" depends on us choosing to read voltages as spikes and the virtual paw as a paw. The sim is to pain what a weather forecast is to rain. The laptop has no nociceptors, no drives, no single system that the pain is for.

(3) Lena replied that "for" is doing a lot of unexammed work there. The sim does route the signal into withdrawal and learning, just inside the virtual world. If he demands that the causal connections be physical, he's made neurons special and given up functionalism. And if he says an interpreter has to attribute the computation, then a clever encoding lets you attribute pain to a stone, so his boundary is totally arbitrary.

(4) Marcus answered that the stone example is exactly why "same computation" can't do the moral work. The attribution has to be done by the system itself, not by us. A mouse's brain uses its own pain signals—the signal is there to make the mouse do something. The laptop just sits there. So the question isn't which computations exist but which ones are had by a system for itself.

(5) Lena came back: fine, so if I wire the sim to a little robot with batteries, reflexes, and a drive to avoid damage, does it switch on? The only thing I added is a body and a motor. If yes, then suffering depends on peripherals, not on the computation you said you were neutral about. If no, then your "for itself" test fails, because the robot's own survival behavior is now controlled by the sim.

(6) Marcus said he doesn't think adding a body automatically switches anything on, but he also thinks "runs the same computation" was the premise in dispute and she never earned it. We don't have a settled fact about which computation a physical system is actually running, so the moral question has to include how and by whom the computation gets picked out. He said the honest answer might be that we don't know where the line is, but her line—the floating pattern—is too cheap.

So please adjudicate move by move: for each numbered move, rate it from clearly wrong to clearly right and give a brief justification. Who's more right overall, and what are both of them missing?
