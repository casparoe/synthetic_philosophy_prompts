I am about to read the main philosophical accounts of the rule of law—Lon L. Fuller's The Morality of Law, Joseph Raz's formal conception, and Jeremy Waldron's procedural account—and I need the standard of evaluation before I start. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of the rule of law must deliver, with the case or consideration behind each, and mark which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are preferences of one camp.

Pressure points to include, though not to limit yourself to: (1) the apartheid or Nazi legal order—must a theory deny these the status of law, or can it treat rule-of-law as scalar? (2) Fuller's eight routes of failure in The Morality of Law, especially his treatment of rules that are unintelligible or contradictory; does formal legality have moral content or is it just efficacy in guiding conduct? (3) broad, unreviewable discretionary power that is nevertheless exercised predictably; (4) retroactive adjudication in common-law development; (5) Waldron's argument that the rule of law is essentially about argumentative procedure rather than the form of norms, which might leave a procedurally legitimate but substantively cruel statute fully compliant.

For each desideratum, say what the case shows and whether any major view must reject it. Then flag which pair of desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, and conclude with a brief scorecard for Fuller, Raz, and Waldron, identifying the single desideratum that most sharply separates them.
