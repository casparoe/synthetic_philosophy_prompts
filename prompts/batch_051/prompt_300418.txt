In my history class last week we were doing ancient math, and the teacher said the Pythagorean theorem was already known in Babylon before Pythagoras, then she mentioned Stigler's law — the saying that no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer, which is itself sort of a joke. Anyway, two people in my class, Maya and Devin, got into it at lunch about whether it even matters that the theorem is named after the wrong person. I just listened, and I honestly couldnt tell who was winning. Here's roughly the back-and-forth.

(1) Maya said the name actually bothers her. Calling it the Pythagorean theorem credits Pythagoras with something he didn't do, and most people really do think he discovered it, so the label keeps a false story going.

(2) Devin said she's putting way too much weight on a label. Names are basically arbitrary handles; nobody is making a historical claim when they say 'Pythagorean theorem,' and if the history is wrong, the fix is to teach the history better, not to rename the thing.

(3) Maya replied that most people won't get that extra history lesson, so in practice the name does the teaching. She also said if names were really just neutral handles, people wouldn't fight so hard to keep them.

(4) Devin said we still shouldn't rename, because by Stigler's law almost every scientific name is 'wrong,' so renaming would never end, and we'd lose all the stability that textbooks, tests, and search terms depend on. He said names are like usernames: stability matters more than accuracy.

(5) Maya said that logic would make it impossible to ever fix a bad label, since any widely used vocabulary has legacy uses. And Stigler's law isn't a law of nature — it's a pattern about who got credited in the past, which is exactly the pattern she wants us to stop backing. She also said the name isn't even stable across languages: different countries call the theorem different things, so 'stability' just means stability inside one tradition.

(6) Devin answered that an honorific name doesn't have to be read as credit forever. Once a name settles in, it just refers; saying 'the Pythagorean theorem' is no more an endorsement of Pythagoras than saying 'Thursday' is an endorsement of Thor.

(7) Maya said that might work for old mythology, but a person's name on a discovery is different: it looks exactly like an attribution, and the whole point of the label is to tell you who to associate with the result. If that association is false, the label is failing, no matter how established it is.

(8) Devin came back and said we should be careful, because Pythagoras or his school might have proved or spread the theorem, so the name might not even be as unfair as she's assuming. The safe move is to keep the label and stay neutral about the actual credit.

(9) Maya said that's backwards — the name is what makes neutrality impossible, because it makes Pythagoras the default answer while the evidence is murky.

Then say who you think is more right overall, or if the real answer is something neither of them said. I'm less interested in the actual history of the theorem and more in whether their arguments about naming, credit, and stability hold up.
