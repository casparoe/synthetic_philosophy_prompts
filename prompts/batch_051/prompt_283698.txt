I keep returning to an asymmetry in how people judge collective action on opposite sides of a wage bargain. Suppose a dozen independent cabinetmakers in my town agree with one another not to accept any order below a stated price. Most people call this price-fixing and think the state should step in. Now suppose a dozen cabinetmakers are employed in one large workshop, and they jointly tell the owner that none of them will work until their wages are raised. Most people call this a strike and think it should be protected.

To me the two acts are structurally similar: in both, people who earn their living making cabinets coordinate to withhold their work until they are paid more. The usual differences—whether the workers are independent sellers or employees, whether the buyer is one company or the public—are real, but I am not persuaded they can carry the moral weight they would need to carry, because each can be blurred. So I want to know: is there a principle that legitimately puts these cases on opposite sides? What is the strongest justification you can build for the asymmetry? Test it against one of the blurring cases—say, independent cabinetmakers with only one realistic buyer, or highly skilled employees who already earn more than their employer—and give an honest verdict on whether the distinction should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned.
