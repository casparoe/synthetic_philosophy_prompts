I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas for a popular magazine, and I'm working on a piece about why everyone keeps invoking the is-ought gap. The received story is that Hume warned against deriving normative conclusions from descriptive facts, and then G. E. Moore came along and called it the naturalistic fallacy, and now people toss that phrase around when someone says something like "humans evolved to be altruistic, so altruism is good." But while reading the actual Hume, I got stuck on a passage in An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals, Section I. He writes:

"whether they be derived from REASON, or from SENTIMENT; whether we attain the knowledge of them by a chain of argument and induction, or by an immediate feeling and finer internal sense; whether, like all sound judgement of truth and falsehood, they should be the same to every rational intelligent being; or whether, like the perception of beauty and deformity, they be founded entirely on the particular fabric and constitution of the human species."

That's not the usual is-ought quote. He seems to be doing something else: framing the core of ethics as a debate about reason versus sentiment, and asking whether moral judgements are more like mathematical truths or more like taste. So here's my question. I want you to act as an expert on Hume's moral philosophy and also someone who knows the later naturalistic fallacy literature. Work carefully and brainstorm before settling on an answer.

Suppose Hume could be shown the way the naturalistic fallacy is deployed today, especially against evolutionary psychology and moral nativism. Would he think the modern debate about the is-ought gap has actually advanced, or would he think it's been misframed as a logic problem when he thought it was fundamentally a faculty distinction? Would he be comfortable with Moore's version of the objection, or would he think it commits exactly the confusion he was diagnosing? And most speculatively: how would he respond to the idea that moral judgements are shaped by evolution but still can't be justified by evolution alone? I'm definately less interested in what the standard view of Hume says, and more in a precise, defended prediction based on the text.
