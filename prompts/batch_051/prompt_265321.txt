I'm a software engineer, mostly self-taught on this stuff, and I've been trying to get a handle on why Bayesian methods are justified — not just why they work well in practice, but whether there's something deeper that makes probability "the right" framework for reasoning under uncertainty. I fell down a rabbit hole reading about Cox's theorem, and a blog post I can't remember the name of summed it up in a way that's stuck in my head. It said something like: Cox proved that probability theory is the unique logic of uncertain reasoning. He started from three axioms that are hard to disagree with — first, that degrees of belief can be ordered and represented as numbers; second, that if A logically implies B, then you can't be more confident in A than in B; and third, that your confidence in A-and-B is a function of your confidence in A and your confidence in B given A. From those, plus some consistency conditions, he derived the sum and product rules of probability. So the rules of probability aren't just one arbitrary choice among many — they're forced on any rational agent. The post then said this means alternative approaches like fuzzy logic are either secretly equivalent to probability or internally incoherent, and anyone whose beliefs don't satisfy the probability axioms is being irrational.

As an engineer, this sets off my alarm bells: the theorem sounds real, but the leap from a derivation to "everyone else is irrational" has that smell of extra assumptions being quietly imported. But I dont have the background to say whether that instinct is right. I'd like to know how much of this summary is fair — which parts someone working on the foundations of probabilistic reasoning would accept, where the summary slides past technical details without saying so, and whether the closing claim about irrationality actually follows from the theorem or is just rhetorical. I've also seen hints that Cox's original proof had holes that later people patched; I'd like to know whether that matters for how much of the summary I can trust.
