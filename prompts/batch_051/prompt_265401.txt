I'm an undergraduate taking a decision theory course in which we've been working through the debate over expected-value fanaticism: the claim that expected utility theory, combined with an unbounded utility function, can require sacrificing a guaranteed but modest good for a vanishingly small chance at an enormous payoff, in the style of the Pascal's mugging case. For my term paper I want to get at a more specific question: whether the fanatical verdict depends essentially on unboundedness of utility, or whether a bounded utility function with a very high but finite upper bound produces the same behavior whenever the secure option sits far enough below that bound.

Could you construct a thought experiment that isolates this issue to the following specifications? The centerpiece should be a sequence of eight to twelve successive offers made to a single agent by an ordinary institution — a foundation, say, that asks the agent to redirect a fixed, morally significant guaranteed benefit toward increasingly speculative disaster-prevention projects. The agent is first offered a choice between keeping that certain benefit and exchanging it for the first speculative project; each later offer is a choice between keeping whatever project the agent currently holds and exchanging it for the next project in the sequence, where each successive project halves the probability of success while multiplying the payoff by enough that its expected value strictly exceeds that of its predecessor. Keep the scenario realistic: no perfect predictors, no infinite or exotic payoffs, just concrete long-shot projects with stipulated probabilities. After the scenario, walk through the reasoning of three agents and say which offers each accepts and why: (i) an unbounded expected utility maximizer; (ii) a bounded expected utility maximizer whose utility function has a finite but unspecified upper bound; and (iii) an agent who avoids fanaticism by systematically discounting very small probabilities rather than capping utility. Then tell me which structural feature is doing the work — the unboundedness of the payoff sequence, the gap between the guaranteed benefit and the utility ceiling, the linear treatment of probabilities, or something else — and what happens to the case if that feature is removed. Finally, explain how a defender of whichever theory ends up looking least reasonable in the case would attack the setup. The judgments should fall out of the stated utility functions and probabilities; the case should not stipulate the verdicts.
