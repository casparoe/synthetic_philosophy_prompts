Question: "Explain Descartes's view that nonhuman animals are machines (the 'beast-machine' doctrine). Your answer should (a) reconstruct the two main arguments Descartes gives for this view, and (b) explain what the doctrine implies for the question whether animals have sensations such as pain."

Student's answer: "Descartes holds that animals are bêtes-machines, or beast-machines: their bodies run entirely on the disposition of their organs, like a clock on its springs and wheels, without any soul or mind directing them. He gives two main arguments for this. The first is the argument from language. Parrots and other animals can produce words, but no animal arranges words so as to answer a question or report what it is thinking. Since language is the only outward sign of thought, its absence shows that brutes have no thought at all. The second is the argument from the scope of behavior. Some animals act more skillfully than humans within one narrow domain, yet they fail at everything outside it, whereas human reason is a universal instrument that adapts to all kinds of situations. Descartes takes this to show that animal skill comes not from reason but from the mechanical arrangement of the organs. It follows directly that animals, lacking minds, also lack sensations: when a dog yelps after being struck, the yelp is only a sound produced by matter in motion, and nothing is there to feel the pain."
