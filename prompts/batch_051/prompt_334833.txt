I came across a friend's summary of what Gödel's incompleteness theorems supposedly show, and I want to know how hard to push back on it. Here's the summary:

"Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that any consistent formal system rich enough to do elementary arithmetic will contain sentences that are true but can't be proved or refuted inside the system. The second theorem says that no such system can prove its own consistency, so Hilbert's dream of securing mathematics from within was dead. The standard upshot people draw is that no fixed formal system can capture all of arithmetic truth: for any system you write down, there will be true arithmetic statements it misses. And since a human can stand outside any given system, see that its Gödel sentence is true, and add it as a new axiom, Gödel also showed that human mathematical insight outruns any mechanical procedure — we can do something no machine running a fixed formal system could do."

How fair is that? Go roughlly sentence by sentence: what would a logician accept as stated, what would they want to hedge or precisify, and what is just overreach? In particular, does the last move — from our ability to recognize the truth of a Gödel sentence to the conclusion about minds and machines — actually follow from anything Gödel proved? And is the claim "for any system you write down, there are true arithmetic statements it misses" the same claim as "no mechanical procedure can capture all of arithmetic truth," or is something being smuggled across that gap?
