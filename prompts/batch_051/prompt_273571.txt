I am a novelist, and I have settled on a view about the things I write. I hold that Sherlock Holmes does not refer — not to a flesh-and-blood detective and not to the shadowy abstract object some theorists invoke — and that the sentence Sherlock Holmes is a detective is not literally true of anything, though it is correct in the distinct register of what is true in the fiction. I also hold that what is true in a story is not fixed by the author's private intentions but by what the text, the genre's conventions, and the background a competent reader brings to it authorize us to imagine. I want an honest map of what would count against this package of views. Give me the objections, cases, and neighbouring results that should genuinely move me, sorted from fatal to merely awkward; say which component of the view is most exposed and what a committed defender ought to concede counts against it; address at least true negative existentials, metafictional statements such as Holmes is a fictional character, cross-fictional comparisons, and inconsistent sequels; and tell me whether the position, or any part of it, has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and whether that matters less for a view about fiction than it would for a view about the physical world.
