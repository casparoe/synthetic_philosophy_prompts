Consider two ways of explaining the same workplace event. A manager says that an employee left because "Gen Z doesn't do loyalty," and the room treats this as a substantive insight about a real social kind. A second manager says that the employee left because she was born in 2001, and the room treats this as either a joke or a confused way of talking — even though the two explanations differ only in whether the birth-year information is packaged as a named generational cohort. The named cohort is granted a license to explain individual behavior that the identical year-range is denied. This asymmetry persists even though the boundaries of the named cohorts are contested, the within-cohort variation is enormous, and the apparent differences are often exactly what demographers call the age–period–cohort problem: cohort effects cannot be cleanly separated from age effects and period effects without strong assumptions.

What, if anything, justifies this asymmetry? Defend the strongest candidate justification you can find — presumably something about a named generation picking out a shared formative environment, demographic position, or collective trajectory that a bare range of years does not — and then test it against hard cases: two people born a minute apart on the conventional but contested Gen X/Millennial boundary; a would-be "pandemic generation" defined solely by school closures; and an individual whose cohort label predicts nothing that age and life stage do not already predict. End by saying whether the asymmetry should be kept as it is, revised (for example, by treating generations as vague but real kinds), or abandoned as a reification that survives mostly because it is useful to marketers.
