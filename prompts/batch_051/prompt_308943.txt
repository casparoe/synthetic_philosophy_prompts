I'm a software engineer, not a philosophy person at all, but I keep running into this cluster of questions — I think it's what people call expected value fanaticism? — and I'd like someone to tell me whether my gut hangs together. I do expected-value-style reasoning at work all the time (is a one-in-a-million crash that costs us a fortune worth fixing?), but when the probabilities get really small and the stakes get huge, my intuitions stop feeling principled. Here are my actual calls on some cases:

1. A stranger on the street says that unless I venmo him $50 he'll press a button that creates 10^30 simulated minds and tortures them for a billion years. I estimate the chance he can actually do that at maybe 1 in 10^30; he won't let me inspect anything. I don't pay him, and that feels right.

2. An asteroid-survey program says a particular rock has about a 1 in 10,000 chance of hitting Earth in 90 years and killing something like a billion people. Spending a few billion dollars a year on a deflection mission seems obviously worth it to me.

3. The St. Petersburg coin-flip game: the pot starts at $1 and doubles every time tails comes up before the first heads, so its expected value is infinite. I'd pay maybe $20 to play, not much more.

4. I won't buy a $2 lottery ticket with a 1 in 300 million chance at $100M except as pure fun. I don't think this one is really in tension with anything, but I'm including it in case it is.

5. At work we rely on a third-party auth library. Based on industry reporting about supply-chain attacks, there's maybe a 1 in 10,000 chance that this kind of cached dependency has a targeted backdoor. I'd spend real money and several engineer-weeks auditing or replacing it. If the chance were more like 1 in 10^9, I'd shrug and move on.

6. AGI risk: I dont have solid numbers, but I'd put at least a few percent on transformative AI arriving in my lifetime, and conditional on that maybe a few percent on things going very badly. I think society should spend a serious fraction of GDP on that risk, even though those probabilities are soft and the mechanism is nothing like asteroid physics.

7. A physics collaboration proposes a collider run with a 1 in 10^9 chance of triggering vacuum decay and ending everything. I wouldn't want them to run it until the mechanism is much better understood, even though the expected number of present-day deaths works out small if you don't count all future generations.

My half-baked principle is: tiny probabilities should only drive big decisions when they come from a mechanism I can model and check, not from bare possibilities or claims I can't interrogate. But I can already see that makes my AGI verdict shaky, since that risk is mostly speculative. And it doesn't explain why the asteroid case and the vacuum-decay case feel different when both are physics mechanisms; maybe the answer is just that 10^-4 and 10^-9 sit on opposite sides of some line, but I doubt the line is a clean number.

So: is there a coherent principle that captures most of my verdicts, what does it say about cases I haven't considered, and which of my calls does it force me to abandon? Be honest about which ones it can't swallow. One case I genuinely can't settle: a startup with a 1 in 10,000 chance of becoming enormous, versus a steady job that pays fine. What does your principle say there?
