I'm a philosophy PhD student working on relational accounts of agency and responsibility. In a seminar on collective responsibility, a colleague deflected a largely individualist account of responsibility by invoking the Ubuntu maxim that a person is a person through other persons (also rendered "I am because we are"). The line was clearly doing argumentative work, but I couldn't pin down what claim it was asserting: a developmental fact about how selves mature, a constitutive thesis that personhood is a relational property, a normative thesis that one becomes a person only by discharging duties to others, a political thesis that individual rights derive from communal membership, or something else again. Some of those readings look like near-tautologies; others look like strong, contested claims in the metaphysics of personhood. I'd like you to lay out the distinct precise theses this one sentence could be expressing, say which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false or simply unsupported by the proverb itself; then, rather than leaving the matter open, take a committed position on which reading or readings actually support the use my colleague put it to — grounding collective responsibility — and which would not.
