I am a novelist, and I have been turning over a question about how characters stay recognizably themselves while their traits shift across scenes. My craft vocabulary has served me well here: I think in terms of consistency and through-lines, motivation, subtext and register, scene pressure, and the difference between a character changing (an arc) and a character merely failing to cohere. But I am beginning to suspect that this vocabulary quietly assumes something I no longer quite believe—that a character is a stable bundle of traits that a scene reveals, bends, or breaks. I have no trouble writing, and defending, a character who is generous in one setting and withholding in another, because I do not feel that the two scenes contradict each other; I feel that the trait itself has somehow changed shape under pressure.

Recently I came across the Copycat model of analogy-making and the accompanying account of fluid concepts. As I understand it, the system works in an artificial microdomain of letter-string puzzles: given a problem like if abc changes to abd, how should ijk change, it does not apply a fixed table of rules but constructs temporary perceptual structures out of small stochastic pieces called codelets, under a kind of temperature that controls how far those structures may drift. The central claim is that concepts such as successor, group, or left-to-right sequence are not fixed definitions but are built and rebuilt to fit the context, and that analogy works by letting high-level concepts slip from their canonical roles under pressure from the problem. The more general philosophical position seems to be that concepts in general are fluid, context-built, and held together less by definitions than by the slippages that context permits.

Restate that position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of my craft: consistency, motivation, scene pressure, subtext, register, through-line, arc. Treat nothing as a mere analogy; I want a systematic translation. Then give me the residue report. Which claims map cleanly onto how a novelist thinks about character, which could only be translated by distortion, and which cannot be expressed at all? And do the failures cut one way or the other—is the fluid-concepts account missing the normative and thematic facts I care about, such as the fact that a character can fail, that a reader will not accept every slippage, and that the same drift can be a discovery in one novel and a betrayal in another, or is my craft vocabulary smuggling in a fixed, trait-based picture of persons that the fluid-concepts view should lead me to abandon? I want to know whether my intuition—that character consistency is just fluid concepts—is right, or is missing something.
