I'm in a comparitive political economy course and we've been going back and forth on why some countries are rich and others aren't—the institutions vs. geography vs. culture fight. I wrote up an argument that we can't ever really know the answer because of how messy historical causal inference is. I half like it, but I also worry theres a trick in it. Here it is:

(1) A causal claim about why nations are rich is only as good as the counterfactual comparison supporting it.
(2) For any historical comparison people actually use—border studies, settler mortality, natural experiments, whatever—there's always some possible unobserved factor that could confound the result.
(3) If a comparison can be confounded by some possible unobserved factor, then it doesn't actually establish its causal claim.
(4) So every historical comparison available to us fails to establish its causal claim.
(5) We can know a causal claim about history only if some historical comparison establishes it.
(6) Therefore, we can't know any causal claim about why some nations are rich.

Is this valid? If not, which step is the problem? I want a committed answer—not a menu of "here are five places you could maybe push back." If you think a step fails, say which one and how it should be read. If you think it's sound, defend that and don't wobble.
