I am a communications engineer with a solid working knowledge of information theory and coding theory — entropy, source and channel coding, redundancy, noise, mutual information, error-correcting codes — but no formal background in linguistics. Restate, in that vocabulary, the debate over how natural languages live with pervasive homonymy and polysemy: is ambiguity chiefly a costly defect that languages tolerate, or an efficient design feature that languages exploit, with pitch, lexical tone, and conversational context doing much of the disambiguation? Translate each side's central claims one by one, using notions such as channel, codebook, prior, redundancy, and error. For each claim, say what carries over cleanly, what must be distorted to fit the formalism, and what has no expression at all in the coding-theoretic vocabulary. Then assess the residue: does it show that the information-theoretic framework is too impoverished to capture the phenomenon, or that the linguistic claims were less precise than they looked?
