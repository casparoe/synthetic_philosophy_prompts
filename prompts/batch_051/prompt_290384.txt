I am an undergraduate in a historical linguistics course on language change, and we have been discussing when the spoken Latin of the Roman world became the Romance languages — for simplicity, when it became Italian. I have been trying to construct a criterion of my own, and I would like it assessed honestly before I build a paper around it.

I call it the chain-of-acquisition criterion. Two ways of speaking are the same language in my sense just in case they are connected by an unbroken sequence of normal first-language acquisitions: each link is a case in which a child acquires the way of speaking around them without formal instruction, and the result counts locally as a continuation of what their caregivers spoke, even though the child alters it a little. Applied to the case at hand, the colloquial Latin of the second century and modern standard Italian satisfy the criterion, because no single generation in between failed to acquire the speech around it, however large the accumulated change may be. I value the criterion for exactly that reason: it lets me say that Latin did not turn into Italian on any particular date, while still excluding transmission that was actually broken, as when a community shifted within a generation to an unrelated language.

The difficulty is that the criterion appears to be transitive, and the historical situation branches. Latin is connected by such a chain to Italian, but also to French and to Sicilian. If each of these is the same language as Latin, and same language here expresses identity, then French and Sicilian turn out to be the same language, which seems untenable. I have been considering two repairs. The first relativizes sameness to a path: relative to the chain running through the speech of central Italy, modern Italian is the same language as Latin, whereas French is the same as Latin only relative to a different path; two present-day varieties are then the same language at a given time only if some present-day chain links them. The second denies that this use of same language expresses strict identity at all, treating it instead as a vague, non-transitive similarity relation of the sort found in predicates like is the same colour as.

I would be grateful for a candid appraisal. Is the initial criterion coherent as stated, or do the proposed repairs smuggle in what the criterion was supposed to explain? What is the strongest objection a linguist or a philosopher of language would press against it, and is there a version that survives? If I have inadvertently reinvented a recognized position, I would rather engage with it under its proper name than present it as original.
