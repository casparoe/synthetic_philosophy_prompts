I work in the research unit of a finance ministry, and I have been asked to brief a committee on whether a legislative proposal to make a privately issued cryptocurrency legal tender is consistent with a long tradition of thinking about money. I want the brief built around one specific passage rather than modern macroeconomics. George Berkeley's The Querist (first published in 1735) presses hundreds of questions about banking and credit instead of asserting theses, but the drift is clear enough. Query 23 runs: "Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?" And Query 25 adds: "And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?"

The proposal concerns a coin with no issuer, no redemption promise, and no built-in claim on anything; its supply is capped by protocol; its price is set by scarcity and sentiment; and its holders overwhelmingly buy it to hold as a store of value rather than to spend. Merchant acceptance is negligible and would likely remain so even after the legal-tender provision takes effect.

Does Berkeley's ticket-or-counter view, read as he meant it in the Querist, count this instrument as money? Would making it legal tender satisfy the view, or does the view imply that the statute mistakes what money is? Argue it from the wording, from the purpose of Berkeley's queries about credit and industry, from what Berkeley would most plausibly have said if asked about an issuerless digital instrument whose price is driven by scarcity, and from how a careful reader of the Querist would take the remark. Then say which approach should govern the brief and why, and identify the one further fact about the coin that would most change the analysis.
