In my government class we have been studying Locke's account of how a person comes to own previously unowned resources: by mixing one's labour with them, subject to the conditions that enough of comparable quality is left for others and that one does not take more than can be used before it spoils. Our school owns an unused half-acre lot, and the student government has proposed turning it into garden plots under rules explicitly modeled on that account.

Any student may claim a ten-by-ten-foot plot by working it—clearing debris, turning the soil, and planting. The claim lasts the entire growing season, but it lapses if the plot goes unworked for two consecutive weeks. No student may hold more than two plots. Plots may be given away or bartered, but not sold. Before a new claim is approved, the student government must verify that every student who wants a plot and has none could still be offered a plot of comparable soil and sunlight; if not, the claim is denied. The student government may also revoke a plot if its holder allows harvested produce to rot on school grounds.

I would like you to analyze how this system would actually behave rather than whether it is a good idea in the abstract. Who would end up with plots, what would be planted, and how much work would people put in? How would students game the rules, and what second-order effects should we expect? In particular, would the two Lockean restrictions—the anti-spoilage rule and the requirement to leave enough of comparable quality for others—do the work Locke intended, or would they be quietly defeated? Where would the scheme fail first, and could it be repaired without abandoning the Lockean framework?
