Write a dialogue between a tranquilist and a eudaimonist. The tranquilist holds that only the absence of craving and suffering has final value; the eudaimonist holds that positive states such as joy, understanding, and achievement have final value over and above whatever craving or suffering they involve or remove. Give them a single question to work on: once someone has reached complete tranquility, could there be any reason to trade some of it for a life of striving, occasional distress, and real accomplishment or understanding? Make both positions equally strong, and make the speakers answer each other's actual arguments rather than talking past each other. At some point let them take up these difficulties: whether tranquility is best understood as a positive felt state or as a mere absence, and whether the tranquilist can admit a felt quality of peace without surrendering the view; whether the tranquilist is committed to saying that a permanently unconscious being is just as well off as an untroubled sage; and whether the eudaimonist can explain why joy is more than the ending of a prior dissatisfaction. Each speaker should concede the other's strongest point at least once and then develop a reply to it, so the dialogue has genuine movement. Write in continuous prose, as in an email or letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; indicate speakers only through ordinary prose attribution.
