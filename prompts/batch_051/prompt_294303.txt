You are a senior philosopher of mind who also works in applied ethics, and you have been asked to facilitate an adversarial collaboration between two research programs on the ethical implications of panpsychism. Work in stages and think carefully about each formulation before you write. Begin by privately reconstructing each side's view in its strongest, most charitable form; then draft the shared document.

The two sides are:

1. Micro-sentience extensionism. If panpsychism—or its stronger cousin, pan-sentience—is true, then at least some fundamental physical systems have sentient or proto-sentient states. Extensionists hold that this is morally revisionary: the class of morally considerable things extends at least partway down to fundamental constituents, and widely distributed micro-suffering can matter in moral accounting even when it is not unified into a single organism's pain.

2. Micro-sentience separatism. Separatists grant panpsychism for the sake of argument and still deny that microphysical experience carries direct moral weight. They hold that moral considerability attaches only to a unified subject whose states are valenced and temporally integrated, so that there is something it is like for that subject and something it is like for states to be better or worse for it. Particles, fields, and un-integrated micro-subjects fail this test, so panpsychism, even if true, leaves practical morality roughly where it was.

Write the document the two sides should co-sign, containing:

A. A numbered agreed-claims section with at least eight substantive claims both sides can affirm without hedging. Include at least one claim about the distinction between mere phenomenal character and valenced suffering; one about the combination problem and its relevance to moral considerability; and one about what both sides would say if micro-sentience turned out to be both valenced and un-integrated. Avoid vacuous or merely definitional agreements; if the two sides use "suffering" or "subject" differently, say so explicitly and give each side its own precise term.

B. A disagreements table with columns: topic; the extensionist formulation; the separatist formulation; and a neutral statement of what is genuinely at issue. Each formulation must be worded so that the camp whose view it states would sign it without complaint.

C. A crux section. For each genuine disagreement, state one or two cruxes—sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. I suspect, though I do not insist, that the cruxes will include (i) whether there is an integration threshold below which valenced states, if any, have zero moral weight, and (ii) whether summing tiny harms across disjoint un-integrated subjects is ethically coherent. For the integration-threshold crux, one concrete case both camps have already accepted as relevant is a graded spectrum from a single isolated microphysical system, through increasingly coupled and integrated systems, up to a unified nervous system; use that case or replace it only with something both camps could sign. For each crux, give the concrete case or kind of evidence both sides agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts it will show.

Target three to four genuine disagreements rather than a long list, and separate disagreements that are substantive from ones that were only about words. Do not include citations, footnotes, or reading lists; refer to positions and arguments only by their content. The finished text should read as if the two camps' most reasonable representatives had written it together under pressure to stop talking past one another.
