I recently spent an evening arguing with a friend about the possibility of recursive interpretability in AI. The motivating question was whether one AI system could ever give a complete, well-grounded explanation of another AI system's internal representations. The argument kept returning to a single sticking point. If an interpreter model produces an explanation of a target model, then to trust that explanation we need some grip on the interpreter's own reliability—its concepts, simplifications, and blind spots—and this seems to demand interpreting the interpreter. My friend argued that the regress is not vicious because each level of interpretation can be simpler than the one below it, so the chain bottoms out in components simple enough to inspect directly. I replied that this only moves the opacity to the stopping rule: whatever we treat as directly inspectable is itself a mechanism whose reliability we are taking on faith, so the chain ends by stipulation rather than by explanation. We also split on whether interpretability is the sort of thing that can be iterated at all, since it may be an engineering goal rather than a property with a well-defined transitive notion. The argument ended unresolved, but it felt like an instance of a wider impasse: some researchers treat the regress as a practical problem to be managed through better tools, while others treat it as a principled limit on self-explanation.

I am not asking for a verdict about which side is right. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is this a factual disagreement about the structure of AI systems, a disagreement about standards of evidence for what counts as an explanation, a verbal disagreement about the word interpretability, or a disagreement about when we are entitled to stop asking for further grounds? My working hypothesis is that the real dispute is about the terminus of explanation: the two sides have different views about what an acceptable stopping point must be like, and no amount of empirical work on model internals will move them until that normative question is surfaced. But I am not confident. Could a formal result—for instance, a theorem about chains of approximate interpreters—actually settle the issue, or would both sides simply reinterpret the result through their existing criteria? If progress is possible, what would it look like; if not, what exactly makes the question ill-posed?
