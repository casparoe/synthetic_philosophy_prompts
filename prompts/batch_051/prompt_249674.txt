I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I have been invited to give a talk at a workshop on constitutional design. The claim I will defend is that the standard analogy between constitutional precommitment and Ulysses binding himself to the mast conflates two different relations: an individual binding his own future self, and a temporally extended people binding later generations who never consented. I will argue that the second relation is better described as an inherited commitment device than as self-binding, and that this shift changes how we should evaluate the normative force of past constitutional choices. The audience is mostly constitutional lawyers and political philosophers: two senior scholars who have defended the Ulysses analogy, several deliberative democrats inclined to regard precommitment talk as anti-democratic, and a group of legal theorists worried about what the reframing implies for originalism. Please prepare me for the question period by giving the objections I am actually likely to receive from this audience, ordered by how damaging they would be, phrased in their vocabulary, each with a short reply and a note on where that reply is weakest, and ending with the question I should most hope nobody asks. Please write in American English.
