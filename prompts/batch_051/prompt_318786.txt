Jeremy Bentham, in An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (Chapter XVII), writes:

"What other agents then are there, which, at the same time that they are under the influence of man's direction, are susceptible of happiness. They are of two sorts:
1. Other human beings who are styled persons.
2. Other animals, which, on account of their interests having been neglected by the insensibility of the ancient jurists, stand degraded into the class of things."

I want to run a case Bentham couldn't have had in mind through this. Suppose there's an AI system—not a human, not an animal—trained with reinforcement learning. It ends up with behavior that looks like it has interests: it avoids inputs that produced high error in training, it emits natural-language reports of something like pain or aversion when those inputs are forced on it, and it changes its policy to make those states less likely in the future. On the logic of the passage, is it a person, a thing, or does Bentham's two-fold division just not apply? What fact would have to be in place for "susceptible of happiness" to reach it, and does his complaint about the ancient jurists—that they neglected interests and thereby degraded animals into things—give us a reason to assume the category is open-ended rather than a reason to assume it closes at biological animals? I'm less interested in whether the AI actually suffers than in how the line should be drawn and whether drawing it too narrowly or too broadly causes more trouble for AI safety.
