I'm taking a philosophy of medicine class and the unit on naturalism vs. normativism keeps making me think of the DSM-5 decision to drop the bereavement exclusion for major depression. The episode, as I understand it: under DSM-IV, if you met the symptom criteria for a depressive episode but the symptoms started within two months of a loved one's death, you generally weren't diagnosed with depression—it was treated as normal grief—unless the symptoms were especially severe or lasted past two months. In DSM-5 (2013), that carve-out was removed, so someone can now receive a major depressive disorder diagnosis in the first weeks of bereavement if the symptoms meet the usual threshold. The official reasoning was, roughly, that grief doesn't protect you from depression and that the old rule implied a false bright line between normal grief and disorder.

I know the standard worry is that this pathologizes normal grief, but I want the theory-level analysis. Naturalists about disease say a condition is a disease when something in the organism is functioning below its evolved or statistically typical design; normativists say disease concepts are value-laden judgments about which states are bad, undesirable, or warrant treatment. So: what does each side say the DSM commitee should have done with the exclusion, and what does each side think is actually going on when a grieving person meets depression criteria? Where does a naturalist locate the dysfunction in normal-looking grief, and why should two months matter—or should it? Does the fact that the boundary was changed by a committee vote count as evidence for normativism, or can a naturalist reply that the old rule was just a crude heuristic and the new one tracks dysfunction better? And what does the episode show in the other direction—does it confirm one side, or does it reveal that grief is a case where biological dysfunction and evaluative judgment can't be pulled apart? I want a disciplined mapping of what the theory explains and where it goes silent, not a rant about overdiagnosis.
