I recently joined the worship committee of a church in a Reformed denomination, and the chairperson has closed two debates by invoking the regulative principle as "whatever is not commanded is forbidden." I cannot tell whether that one-liner is supposed to be a hermeneutical rule, a burden-of-proof claim, or a substantive principle of worship, and I suspect the same sentence is doing several jobs at once. Take the slogan and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be expressing. For each reading, say whether it is defensible as a principle of worship, what it would actually rule in or out (for example: singing only Psalms, choosing a time of meeting, using a Bible translation, following a printed liturgy, observing a church calendar), and whether it is a reading anyone consistently holds. Please explicitly distinguish at least these axes: whether "commanded" covers only what Scripture explicitly commands or also what follows by good and necessary consequence; whether the principle governs the elements of worship alone or also the circumstances and forms; and whether it asserts that Scripture alone authorizes worship or the stronger claim that every practice not prescribed is forbidden. I also want to know which reading the chairperson actually needed for his arguments to go through, and whether that is a reading he would likely accept if it were spelled out.
