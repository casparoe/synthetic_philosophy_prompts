I'm taking a criminal-law-ish class this term and we spent most of a week on void for vagueness, and it has made me mildly insufferable about reading old local ordinances. My uncle was on a small city council years ago, and he's convinced the doctrine is just a way for judges to cancel a law they personally find annoying. So I pulled up an old ordinance from his city's code, passed in the early sixties, that's still on the books and reads:

"No person shall make, continue, or cause to be made or continued, any unnecessarily loud, excessive, or unusual noise, and no person having charge of any premises shall allow any such noise to come from those premises, when the noise by reason of its loudness, pitch, duration, or character is likely to disturb the quiet, comfort, or repose of a reasonable occupant of any neighboring property. Violation of this section is a misdemeanor."

The thing that started the fight is this. My uncle's next-door neighbor installed one of those motion-sensor ultrasonic boxes meant to stop dogs from barking; it fires off a high-pitched pulsed tone whenever a squirrel or a delivery truck trips it. My brother, who's sixteen, can hear it clearly from our own front yard (he says it's like a dental drill that follows you around) and it gives him headaches. Most adults hear nothing. The city sent out a code enforcement officer, who was about sixty, and he stood under the thing for five minutes and declared there was no "observable noise," so the city couldn't do much. But the ordinance doesn't say the noise has to be audible to the officer, or to most people, and my brother is plainly a "reasonable occupant" of a neighboring property in most other respects.

I don't really need a prediction about whether we'd win in practice. I'm trying to get clear on what the different interpretive approaches would each say about whether the ordinance reaches a sound only some people can hear — the words, the purpose (I assume it's about protecting the peace and quiet of residents, but back then nobody imagined a device designed to be audible to dogs and teenagers but not to adults), what the city council in 1963 would have said if you'd explained the device to them, and what a reasonable reader of the text would expect to be covered. And then the doctrine question: is the word "noise," or the phrase "reasonable occupant," too vague to do this work once hearing turns out to vary with age and earbuds and mild hearing loss? Or is this just a normal statutory-interpretation problem that a court can handle, with vagueness having nothing to say until the law is much worse? Where should the line between "imprecise but fixable" and "void for vagueness" fall in a case like this?
