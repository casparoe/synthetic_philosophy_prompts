I'm working on a novel where the main character is a 19th-century clergyman-naturalist who starts out a fairly conventional Christian and then slowly comes apart while studying parasitic wasps — months of watching ichneumon larvae eat a caterpillar from the inside, that sort of thing — because he can't square it with the idea that creation is good. I've sketched some notes for an essay I might pull from the same material, more like me thinking through the theology before I let it steer the book too much. I'd like your comments on it, and I don't need you to be nice.

The notes:

The problem of evil for humans is old news, but what keeps bothering me is that pain and predation are vastly older than us. Whatever you make of the Fall, animals were tearing each other apart, starving, dying of infection for hundreds of millions of years before any human existed to sin. So either the Fall reached backward into deep time somehow, or the world was already — not exactly fallen, but not something you could call very good and mean it the way you mean it about a loaf of bread. My character's vicar friend insists death is just how creation works, the engine of new forms; my character can't get past the specific cruelty of it: the wasp that does not kill the caterpillar quickly, the cat that plays with the mouse, the wasting disease. Not death in the abstract. Death as duration.

The traditional line, as I understand it, is that there was no death before the Fall, and some people held that literally: all animals vegetarian, nothing died, everything peaceable. Once fossils and geology made that impossible to hold literally, the options shrank. People seem to have quietly moved to one of a few positions: (a) it all still traces to human sin but through some cosmic or retroactive causality; (b) there was an earlier fall, maybe of angels, that broke nature before we got here; (c) only human sin matters and animal pain simply isn't on the ledger; (d) the six days were never about ordinary chronology, so the whole problem doesn't arise; or (e) God values the ecosystemic drama as a whole more than the subjective states of individual creatures, so predation isn't really a defect once you look at it from the right level. My character wants to beleive something like (d), but he's a naturalist, and (d) feels like a dodge to him.

What I'd actually like your comments on: First, is the distinction I'm leaning on between animal death as a natural process and animal pain as a concrete, extended thing doing any real work for a theology of creation? Does it matter, theologically, that an ichneumon larva eats a caterpillar alive over weeks rather than a lion killing a gazelle in thirty seconds, or is that just my own squeamishness? Second, of the five positions I sketched, which one gets closest to a coherant doctrine of a good creation without pretending the natural history isn't real? Third — the writerly part — would a reader find the vicar's line that death is the engine of creation plausible as something a nineteenth-century clergyman-naturalist would actually say in, say, the 1830s, or does it ring too post-Darwin? Feel free to flag anything else in the notes that would embarrass me if I published them.
