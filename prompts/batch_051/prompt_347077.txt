At a reading group I attend, two members argued about Frederick Douglass's later view — defended in his Glasgow address of 1860 — that the U.S. Constitution, read strictly according to its text, does not guarantee a right to hold property in human beings and can be wielded as an antislavery instrument rather than treated as the pro-slavery covenant the Garrisonians took it to be. I tried to steelman both sides and could not settle it on the spot, so I am handing it to you for a move-by-move verdict. The exchange went approximately like this.

(1) Mina said Douglass's central move is a serious piece of textualism. The Constitution never says slave or slavery; where it touches the institution it speaks obliquely about persons held to service or labour. A reader bound to what the document actually says would therefore never find in it a guarantee of property in man. The pro-slavery reading has to be imported, by treating the instrument as a cipher for the framers' privately held aims. But Douglass's point was that the people ratified words, not unspoken intentions, and that the convention had deliberately sat behind closed doors so the public would judge the text rather than the debates.

(2) Julian replied that this mistakes euphemism for absence. A legal instrument can entrench an institution without ever naming it, and the apportionment and fugitive-labor clauses are obvious examples: one gives slaveholding states extra representation in the House by counting a portion of the people they hold in bondage, the other obliges free states to deliver up escaped persons held to service. Calling the language oblique does not make it neutral; if anything, the studied circumlocution shows the drafters knew exactly what they were embedding and did not want to say it. Douglass, Julian said, is treating linguistic reticence as if it were substantive liberty.

(3) Mina answered that this misses the actual claim. Douglass did not deny that slavery existed under the constitutionally established government, or that many framers intended to protect it. He said those facts do not settle what the instrument as adopted means. And the clauses Julian cites are exactly the ones Douglass means when he says a pro-slavery gloss has to be read into the words, since the instrument itself confers no express authority to treat any person as chattel. If we may bind the text by whatever the drafters were reluctant to say in it, a written constitution cannot constrain its own government.

(4) Julian pushed back with two points. First, plain meaning is not dictionary abstraction: the euphemisms got their force from the legal context of the 1780s, where a person held to service or labour was understood by everyone to include a slave. Reading the instrument as a Martian would, precisely by ignoring that context, manufactures an antislavery document out of silence. Second, there is something suspicious about the procedure. Douglass's text-only rule arrives at the conclusion his political program needed — that conscientious people may vote and hold office under the Constitution — just as the Garrisonian reading arrived at the conclusion their disunionist program needed. That symmetry does not make Douglass wrong, but it is reason to suspect the text is being recruited rather than merely read.

(5) Mina said those two objections are very different. The context objection assumes the very thing to be shown, namely that the euphemistic clauses carried an antislavery-excluding meaning simply because pro-slavery readers said so; by Douglass's lights, the pro-slavery interpretation was itself one more unofficial commentary that the ratified text never adopted. And the political-convenience objection is a wash, since everyone in the argument had policy preferences lined up with their reading; it therefore cannot decide between them.

I could not separate, in real time, which of these moves are fair to Douglass, which are independent criticisms of textualism, and which are merely rhetorical.

You are a constitutional historian and a professional philosopher who specializes in argument analysis. Please think carefully, and before judging, reconstruct the strongest version of each side's position. Then go through moves (1) through (5) in order, rating each on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. Finally, give an overall verdict: who is more right about Douglass's argument, and what, if anything, are both of them missing?
