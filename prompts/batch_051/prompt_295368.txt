ok so in my animal minds class the prof gave us a checklist for deciding when an animal is actually feeling pain rather than just doing damage-avoidance. the four criteria are: (1) it has nociceptors that feed into some kind of centralized nervous system; (2) its pain behavior is changed by analgesics, so morphine makes it stop caring about the injury; (3) it treats the noxious stimulus as one motivation among others, meaning it will suppress the escape response when something more important is going on, and pain can interupt its other drives; and (4) it can learn to predict and then avoid the stimulus before it even happens.

the case on the problem set is a made-up tide-pool worm. it has real nociceptors wired into a simple ganglion, and if you poke it with a hot probe it does this fast all-or-nothing curl, every single time. give it morphine and, weirdly, the curl gets slightly more vigorous instead of weaker. and it can be conditioned: flash a light before the heat, and after a while the worm curls to the light alone, so there's real learning. but the curl is completely ballistic—same latency, same sequence, whether the worm is feeding, mating, whatever. if you lesion the circuit the curl disappears and literally nothing else about the animal changes; it keeps feeding, keeps mating, doesn't groom the damaged site.

so, going criterion by criterion, is this worm feeling pain? where do the criteria pull in different directions or just refuse to settle it? and tell me whether the problem is with the worm or with the list—like, is criterion (2) or (3) carrying too much weight, and what would a better criterion even look like.
