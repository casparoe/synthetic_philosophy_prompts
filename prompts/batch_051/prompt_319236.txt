I'm trying to pin down what I actually mean by "understands a proof," and I've got a pile of case-verdicts that I want a principle for, if there is one.

My gut calls:

1. A student can reproduce the standard proof that sqrt(2) is irrational flawlessly, including the bit where p and q are assumed coprime. But when I ask "where does the proof actually use coprimeness?" or "what would go wrong if p and q weren't coprime?", they've got nothing. Verdict: doesn't understand the proof, even though they can produce it.

2. A different student can't carry out the proof at all. They can say: it's a reductio; you assume sqrt(2) = p/q in lowest terms; squaring gives 2q² = p²; parity forces p even; then q ends up even too; that contradicts lowest terms. But if you put a pencil in their hand they'd strugle with the algebra. Verdict: they understand the proof better than student 1, even though student 1 would ace the exam question.

3. Someone checks every step of a long, ugly classification proof—the kind that churns through dozens of cases—and is convinced each step is valid, but they have no idea why the theorem should be true or why the case split is the natural one. Verdict: they've verified it, but they don't understand it.

4. A student has memorized a proof and can also recite a correct answer to "what's the main idea?" because they memorized that too. But ask them to prove a mild variant where the same proof needs one new observation, and they're stuck. Verdict: no real understanding.

5. A working mathematician hasn't checked the details of a proof in years and couldn't reproduce them. But they can explain the strategy, say why each major move is there, tell you what would collapse if you removed a key lemma, and pinpoint where someone else's attempted proof has to fail. Verdict: they understand the proof, probably better than someone who can currently reproduce it line by line.

6. Now the one I keep going back and forth on. A student can answer every "why is this line true?" question with the right reason, and they pass a transfer test with two variants. But it's all slow and effortful, and they say they have no sense of the proof as a whole—no click, no "aha." I honestly don't know whether to say they understand the proof or just reliably reason their way through it.

Is there one principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that gets all of these, or nearly all? State it precisely, and say which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. Then tell me what it predicts for a seventh case: someone is given a correct, detailed proof, checks every step against the axioms with an automated proof assistant, finds no errors, but can't explain the proof in there own words afterward. Are they in the same boat as case 3, or a different one?

One thing I'd ask: separate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on mathematical understanding, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning—no preamble, no restating the question.
