An argument I keep tripping over in discussions of animal consciousness now seems too strong to be sound, and I want to know exactly which move is doing the damage. A friend pressed it on me to show that the whole practice of drawing a line between conscious and nonconscious animals is question-begging. It runs as follows.

(1) The only evidential route we have to attributing phenomenal consciousness to a nonhuman animal goes through markers: flexible behavior, learning and memory, injury-related trade-offs, nociceptive physiology, responses to anesthetics and analgesics. Represent each species by its profile across these markers.

(2) Across species these profiles form a near-continuum. Whatever the endpoints, one can construct a chain of intermediate species from dog down to bee such that each adjacent pair is close on the markers, while the first and last are far apart.

(3) If a marker profile justifies judging a species conscious, then a sufficiently similar profile justifies judging the next species in the chain conscious. The difference between adjacent profiles is too small on its own to bear the weight of distinguishing a rational attribution from a rational withholding; any cut will sit between two profiles that are nearly equivalent evidentially.

(4) Therefore, by moving stepwise down the chain, the same marker-based warrant that makes dog-consciousness reasonable also makes bee-consciousness reasonable. Conversely, if the bee's profile is not enough to warrant the attribution, then by the same principle run in reverse, the dog's profile is not enough either.

(5) So the marker-based approach appears to yield exactly two options: bee-consciousness or dog-skepticism. But neither horn is acceptable—bee-consciousness looks like over-attribution, and dog-skepticism looks absurd.

(6) Conclusion: marker-based attributions cannot justify our ordinary conviction that dogs are conscious while bees are not. Anyone who keeps the dog-but-not-bee verdict is either following an unargued prejudice or covertly appealing to some further categorical feature, and no such feature has been produced whose absence in bees is established independently rather than assumed.

I most want to keep a marker-based epistemology in spirit, and I will not give up the dog verdict, but I have no candidate hidden feature up my sleeve. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost for the rest of the debate?
