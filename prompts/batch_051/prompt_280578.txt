In 1995, a mid-sized civil-law country—call it Vesperia—emerges from two decades of authoritarian rule and drafts a new constitution. Haunted by the fact that nearly everything objectionable under the old regime had been carried out under formally valid statutes, the drafters insert the following provision, Article 87: “No organ of the state shall apply a statute or decree when, in the reasoned judgment of the applying organ, doing so would produce a result that is manifestly unjust; such a refusal shall stand unless the Constitutional Court, by a two-thirds vote, holds that the statute may be applied notwithstanding the finding of injustice.” The drafters deliberately leave “manifestly unjust” undefined and direct judges to answer that question by considering what a morally serious observer would find intolerable, not by consulting the constitution’s own text.

I’d like you to reason through how Vesperia’s legal order is likely to develop over the next forty years, and to use the resulting trajectory to test the Hart–Fuller debate. If you take Hart’s side, the worry is that collapsing the question of what law is into the question of what law ought to be will sacrifice the predictability and settled expectations that make law capable of guiding conduct; if you take Fuller’s side, the reply is that a legal order that treats moral appraisal as foreign to legality has already lost its claim to obedience. Work this out at the level of mechanisms, not slogans. What happens to how the legislature drafts statutes, and does it begin to avoid morally contested fields altogether? What happens to litigants’ strategies, to the market for legal advice, and to the pricing of legal risk in contracts and regulation? Do the courts converge over time on a stable, unwritten set of moral criteria, or does the undefined standard fragment and become politicized? And does Article 87 effectively embed a moral test in Hart’s rule of recognition, or would a Hartian insist that the article is just one more source-based rule that courts apply non-morally? Distinguish the effects you regard as robust from those that are guesses, and identify which assumptions about the legal profession, political parties, or public opinion would have to change to reverse your prediction. Finally, say whether the forty-year simulation strikes you as a vindication of Hart, of Fuller, or of the suspicion that the entire debate was misposed.
