In Robert Rosenthal's centipede game, the standard backward-induction argument says that a rational first mover should stop immediately, because each later player, facing a similar choice, would prefer a slightly larger immediate payoff over a slightly larger payoff one step later, and this reasoning is common knowledge. Now suppose that before the game begins, each player may publish a conditional policy on a publicly verifiable, automatically enforced ledger: for example, "I will pass at my second decision node if and only if you have passed at your previous node." Any attempt to deviate from a published policy triggers an automatic penalty paid from funds the player has placed in escrow. The players cannot sign a single joint contract, but each can unilaterally bind itself in this way, and both know which policies the other has published before choosing whether to enter the game. Does the backward-induction argument still rule out reaching the large final payoff, or has the changed assumption—that unilateral commitment is cheap and perfectly credible—broken the argument at one of its premises? If it still rules it out, explain why the ability to publish unconditional and conditional commitments does not help. If it does not, say precisely which premise no longer applies and whether the remaining phenomenon should still be called the backward-induction paradox or something else.
