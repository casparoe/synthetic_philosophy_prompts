I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I keep coming back to the old question of whether market society corrupts or civilizes us — the doux-commerce idea that trade polishes manners and teaches honesty, versus the suspicion that commerce rewards the calculating and the shameless and quietly turns every relationship into a transaction. The historical evidence is just too confounded to settle anything for me, so I've been trying to work through a clean counterfactual instead, and I'd like to hear your reasoning on it.

Imagine that, from tomorrow, a country much like Denmark today somehow gets the following arrangement. Before you enter any market transaction — as buyer, seller, employer, or employee — you can see, at zero cost, a complete and accurate record of the other party's entire market history: every promise kept or broken, every time they exploited an information asymmetry, every dispute and how they handled it. You also know that your own record is visible to anyone you might deal with. Nothing else changes: contracts are enforced exactly as before, property law is the same, there is no extra legal punishment for a bad record, and the record only covers market conduct, not private life.

Now tell me how the commercial culture of that country looks fifty years in. I don't just want a story: I want the chain of mechanisms. Which traits does this transparency select for among people who thrive, what happens to genuine trust when everyone can look everyone up, do people get better at honest dealing or just better at gaming the record, and does the market end up more or less civilizing in the sense the old debate cared about? I keep flip-flopping between two stories — one where this is the best school of virtue ever built, one where it produces a society of performatively clean ledgers and private cynicism — and I can't settle wether either is robust or what would have to be true for one to win.

Please start your answer with the bottom line — the single claim you'd most confidently defend about where this ends up — in the very first sentence, and then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating the question.
