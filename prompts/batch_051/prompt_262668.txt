Two of my colleagues got into an argument over whether disgust and the sense of the sacred deserve any place in moral reasoning. I want a point-by-point adjudication of the exchange as it actually unfolded.

(1) The first maintained that when people find something viscerally repulsive — for example, a defiled sacred object or an act they regard as unnatural — the revulsion is often doing real moral work. It flags violations that harm-based accounts have not yet articulated, and a morality that refuses to take such responses seriously will be blind to genuine wrongs.

(2) The second replied that this proves too much. Disgust evolved to keep us away from pathogens and spoilage, and it overgeneralizes notoriously: the same feeling that alerts us to rotten meat also attaches itself to unfamiliar foods, sexual practices, and out-groups. To let it function as a moral premise is to treat a smoke alarm that rings for burnt toast and for house fires as though it were evidence of fire in both cases.

(3) The first answered that the smoke-alarm analogy, properly read, cuts the other way. A noisy alarm is still an alarm; nobody throws away a smoke detector merely because it sometimes cries wolf, since failing to heed it when there is a fire would be catastrophic. Disgust's fallibility therefore shows only that its deliverances must be scrutinized, not that they should be excluded, and in many cases that scrutiny will vindicate them. They added that not all disgust is pathogen disgust: there is also a sociomoral disgust at betrayal, cruelty, and corruption that cannot be explained away as a hygiene response.

(4) The second replied that this last point was the weakest in the argument. Sociomoral disgust, they said, is better explained as an output of moral judgment than as an input to it: people first judge the betrayal or cruelty to be wrong, and only then find it disgusting. If so, disgust cannot serve as independent evidence for the very judgment it presupposes. The real work, if any, is done by the antecedent judgment, and at that stage disgust contributes nothing except rhetorical heat. Moreover, to admit it into the premises would be to give cultural prejudice a veneer of moral authority, since it is largely culture that marks objects as sacred or defiling in the first place.
