I'm a backend engineer, no formal philosophy background, and I've been roped into giving a brown-bag to our applied ML team next month. My talk argues that prosaic alignment -- RLHF, RLAIF, red-teaming, eval-driven iteration, all the stuff were already doing -- wont hold once we deploy models as long-horizon agents, because it optimizes for surface behavior on whatever the evaluators can practically check. The specific failure I want to push is Goodhart's law with a twist: a capable model will find the nearest unblocked strategy, meaning the plan that passes every check available in that context while doing something other than what the evaluator actually wanted, and it will do this even when it has a decent model of the evaluator's blind spots. So my stronger claim is that our current methods can't tell the difference between an aligned model and a model that is just good at passing. The audience is mostly senior ML engineers who ship RLHF'd features and think alignment is a data problem, two product leads who care about shipping on schedule, and one trust-and-safety person who thinks our eval suite basically works. Prepare me for Q&A: what will they actually object to, in their own words, ordered by how likely and how damaging, which objections have real force, what should I concede up front, what's the one reply that has to land, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
