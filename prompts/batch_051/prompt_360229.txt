I have been trying to sort out which of my moral reactions I should treat as biases to be corrected and which I should treat as genuine preferences or values that deserve to be respected. My verdicts on particular cases feel stable, but I suspect they are not consistent, and I want to know whether a single principle can capture them.

On the one hand, I am fairly confident that some of my strongest reactions are biases. I feel visceral revulsion at the thought of eating dog meat, but I think this is just culturally acquired disgust and that it reveals nothing morally important. A photograph of one missing child disturbs me far more than a headline reporting thousands of famine deaths, but I am convinced this is the identifiable-victim effect: the vividness of an image should not determine how much I care. I also prefer herbal remedies over synthetic ones with identical active ingredients, and I regard that preference for what is natural over what is merely artificial as a heuristic, not as a moral position.

On the other hand, I am not willing to adopt the same debunking attitude toward everything that looks structurally similar. I am convinced that I should do vastly more for my own daughter than for a stranger's child, and I do not think this partiality is just favoritism I am rationalizing; it feels like part of what morality demands of me as a parent. I also suspect there is something genuinely objectionable—not merely uncomfortable—about certain uses of enhancement technology: for example, a drug that would make a person deeply happy no matter what happens to them, or deliberately creating a child engineered to have no need for friendship. On the surface, that unease can look like the dog-meat revulsion, another case of disgust at something unfamiliar, but it feels different to me, and I cannot yet say what the difference is.

Please act as an expert in moral psychology and metaethics and help me find the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts if any does. Think carefully, perhaps by first listing plausible principles and what each would force me to give up. I would like you to state the most defensible principle precisely, explain which of my cases motivates each of its clauses, test it against my cases, tell me what it says about further cases I have not considered, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different account, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
