I am a novelist writing a contemporary political novel in which the monarch of a constitutional monarchy privately tells the prime minister, over three weekly audiences, that a proposed bill would harm the country and that the palace may have to make its unease known if the government proceeds; the conversations leak and become a social media controversy. I want the novel to be intellectually defensible rather than merely dramatic. In The English Constitution, Bagehot separates the dignified parts of the constitution, which win the people's loyalty, from the efficient parts, which actually govern, and he treats the Crown as the chief dignified element whose influence depends on staying above visible political combat; at the same time, he grants the sovereign a standing right to advise, caution, and encourage ministers. What would Bagehot make of this present-day case? Give the strongest Bagehotean argument for treating the monarch's repeated private pressure as a legitimate exercise of that advisory role, the strongest for treating it as an unconstitutional intrusion of the dignified Crown into efficient politics once it becomes public, and then say which reading you think is closer to his actual position. If the text underdetermines the case, identify the further feature of the scenario that would most clearly settle it.
