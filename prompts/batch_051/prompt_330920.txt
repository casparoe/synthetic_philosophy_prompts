I'm not a philosopher — I do land-use work for a county, so I spend a lot of time in rooms where people argue about flood maps and highway widenings — and I keep tripping over a distinction I can't name. It's about when we say someone "accepted" a risk versus when we say the risk was imposed on them without their consent, and the thing that bothers me is that the line seems to track whether the risk gets filed under "background" or "natural," not anything about actual consent.

First case. A family buys a house on a floodplain. The maps are public, the previous owner had water in the basement twice, everyone in town knows the river comes up every couple of decades. The house floods. The near-universal recation is: they knew what they were getting into, they accepted the risk, end of story.

Second case. A developer builds on a slope that drains toward some older homes. The drainage changes slowly as the hillside is built out, so by the time the older homes flood, everyone there knew the runoff was getting worse. But the reaction is the opposite: people say the risk was imposed on them, that they never consented to being the drainage catch for somebody else's project. Same knowledge, same staying put, totally different moral verdict.

The more I look, the less the difference seems to be about what anybody actually knew or consented to. It's about whether the risky condition gets classified as "that's just how this place is" versus "a specific person's choice made it worse." That filing decision is doing huge moral work — it decides whether your complaint is legitimate or whether you're just a sore loser — and yet the filing itself seems to follow whatever counts as normal for the area, not any principle. A river that floods partly because of a century of upstream dam and levee decisions is a hybrid, and in practice everybody just picks the category that flatters their side.

The weirdest part is how retroactive it is: the couple whose farm ends up next to the new highway exit did not consent to it and was treated, at the time, as having a serious risk imposed on them; twenty years later, if they complain about the noise, people say they've accepted it by staying put. Consent gets backdated out of ordinary inertia.

Does this phenomenon have a name? I know tort law has the doctrine of assumption of risk, and I know there's philosophical writing about imposing risk on others, but I'm after this specific thing: the way the imposed/accepted line seperates along a background-vs-chosen classification that is itself socially maintained and applied after the fact. If there is a name, I want its boundaries — what counts, what doesn't, and whether the natural/artificial split is doing the real work or just standing in for something else. A bare label won't settle it for me; if there's no established name, how would you carve it and what would you call it?
