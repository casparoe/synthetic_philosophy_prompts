I keep running into this tension and can't settle it. As a novelist I spend my days watching imaginary people chase things, and the characters who are happiest in a scene are almost never the ones trying to be happy. There the ones lunging at something else — a person, a manuscript, a child, a garden, a grudge — and the happiness shows up as a side effect. The moment a character treats happiness itself as the target and starts optimizing his life for pleasant experiences, he goes dead on the page, and I suspect the same is true off the page: you can't reach happiness by aiming straight at it.

But abstractly I still find hedonism the cleanest theory of well-being going. It says an experience is good for you exactly insofar as it feels good from the inside, bad exactly insofar as it feels bad, and a life goes well when the first outweighs the second. It's simple, it doesn't smuggle in a list of supposedly worthwhile activities, and it gives a tidy method: to make someone better off, improve how their experience feels. I'd hate to give up that tidiness because my characters misbehave.

So which yields? Is the novelist's intuition a quirk of human psychology — good for fiction but irrelevant to what well-being is — and should be discounted? Or is it tracking something hedonism leaves out: that happiness is only ever had indirectly, or that the value lies in the activity and its object rather than in the feeling? Give me the strongest version of both sides, then a negotiated verdict — debunk the intuition, amend the theory (two-level hedonism or something like it), or show they're compatible — and say what would justify each move, not just pick one.
