I've started noticing that a lot of the reasons people give aren't actually reasons the piece is good—they're reasons to want to take it. Like, the author's mom is the vice principal, or if we reject him he's going to be awful to us all year. Those are easy to spot. But then there's stuff like we haven't published any freshmen this season, or readers will think we're elitist if we say no, or the poem is pretty bad but she's trying really hard and it would mean a lot to her. I can feel that those are different from it develops the image badly line by line or the ending undercuts the whole point, but I can't articulate the sorting rule.

Can you write me a decision procedure, based on whatever the philosophy of reasons actually knows about this, that the whole staff can use to separate reasons that bear on whether the piece is actually good from reasons that are really about us, or about the consequences of accepting it? It has to be runnable by a sophomore in five minutes before a meeting, explainable to our faculty adviser who signs off, and it should say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it kicks the decision to a person because its a values call. It should also flag cases where a reason is genuinely both kinds at once. Then run it on three examples: the vice principal thing, the first time we reject a piece by a senior who's about to graduate, and a poem with one genuinely great image but a bunch of filler. Include at least one case where your procedure tells us to do something we won't like. I don't want "just consider each piece on its merits"; I want the actual rule and where it breaks down.
