I'm a PhD student in archaeology and something about the way we talk about evidence has been nagging me. This summer I worked at a site with a small Hellenistic sanctuary and, a few hundred meters away, a stretch of irrigation channel that had been carefully graded, repaired twice, and laid out with a kink that made no hydraulic sense. The difference in how the two got written up was stark. The sanctuary produced a couple of broken figurines and an ash layer, and the report confidently reconstructed dedicatory practice, vows, the community's sense of the god's presence. The irrigation works got written up almost entirely as subsistence infrastructure; the kink was shrugged off as a later property line or a mistake, and nobody even considered that the alignment might express a norm — a calendrical orientation, a rule about the proper direction water should flow, a restriction on who could tap the channel. This looks like a general asymetry. We let special, expensive, or representational objects (tombs, figurines, temples, caches) speak to beliefs and institutions, but we treat mundane infrastructure (ditches, middens, latrines, field systems) as evidence only of economy and technology, as if belief stopped at the shrine door. Is there a defensible principle behind that divide, or is it mostly a bias toward the spectacular and the representational? I want the strongest principle you can find, tested against hard cases — say, a latrine dug to avoid a ritually dangerous direction, which is cheap, functional, and expressive all at once, or a granary whose layout mirrors cosmology but also keeps the grain dry. If the best principle says we should mostly give up the asymmetry, tell me what a more even-handed inferential practice would look like on an actual excavation. And if you think I've misdescribed how archaeologists actually reason, or that the question rests on a confusion, say that plainly instead of playing along. However you come down, tell me how confident you are and what would change your mind.
