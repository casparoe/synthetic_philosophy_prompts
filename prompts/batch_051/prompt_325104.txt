Here's an argument a friend walked me into that seems to prove too much, but I can't find the exit. (1) If a life with zero novelty is boring, and boredom is bad, then a life with zero novelty is worse for the person living it than an otherwise identical life with some novelty. (2) So novelty isn't just useful for getting other goods; it's a basic part of well-being. (3) If novelty is a basic part of well-being, then adding any small bit of novelty to someone's life should make that life at least slightly better, as long as it doesn't cost anything else. (4) So a person who is perfectly happy with their fixed routine—same job, same meals, same friends, same walks—would still be better off if, once, they took a new route home. (5) But wait: if they only take the new route because they're afraid of a boring life, then novelty is just a tool for avoiding the bad feeling of boredom, not a basic good. (6) So either novelty is both a basic good and merely instrumental, which sounds incoherent, or premise (1) is false. He says the only consistent move is to reject (1), but then we have to say an endlessly repeated, entirely predictable life can be just as good as a varied one, which he says is absurd. I want to keep (1) and (2), but I suspect something slid between (4) and (5). Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Give me your answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
