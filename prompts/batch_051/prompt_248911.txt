Im a software engineer and I got curious about an old essay by T.H. Huxley called On the Hypothesis that Animals Are Automata, from 1874. In one part he compares consciousness to a steam whistle on a locomotive: the whistle is produced by the engine as a side effect, and it doesn't do anything to drive the wheels. He seems to be saying human consciousness is like that too—it goes along with some brain processes but has no causal influence on what the body says or does.

What I keep getting stuck on is this. If that picture is right, then when a philosopher writes or says the sentence 'consciousness exists but has no causal power,' the words he produces are caused entirely by neural machinery, not by consciousness. So the fact that the sentence is about consciousness, and that the person has accurate introspective access to the very thing that is supposed to be inert, starts to look like a massive coincidence. Its not like there's any feedback from consciousness to let the brain know the theory is right. So why would Huxley's brain produce the true sentence instead of gibberish? The theory seems to undercut the reliability of the evidence (introspection) that the person used to arrive at it.

I'm trying to figure out if this is a known problem with a name. Ive seen people call it something like the 'paradox of phenomenal judgment,' but I dont know if that's exactly the same thing or if that term means something more specific. Is that the standard name? What I want is someone to carve the problem precisely: is it fatal to the steam-whistle view, is it self-undermining, and where's the boundary between this and the normal problem any physicalist has about why introspection is reliable. If epiphenomenalists have standard ways out, I'd want to see whether they actually solve the puzzle or just push it down a level.
