In his Preface to *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge*, Berkeley writes: "For, as there are some passages that, taken by themselves, are very liable (nor could it be remedied) to gross misinterpretation, and to be charged with most absurd consequences, which, nevertheless, upon an entire perusal will appear not to follow from them; so likewise, though the whole should be read over, yet, if this be done transiently, it is very probable my sense may be mistaken; but to a thinking reader, I flatter myself it will be throughout clear and obvious."

I have been in a reading group on Berkeley's idealism for months, and the argument about the so-called Master Argument in sections 22–23 of the *Principles* has gone nowhere. One side says the argument is simply invalid because it illicitly moves from "I cannot conceive of an unconceived tree" to "an unconceived tree is impossible"; the other side says that reading ignores Berkeley's own warning that the relevant act of conception is the object of the argument and that he is not reporting a merely psychological limitation. Each side accuses the other of doing exactly what Berkeley describes in this passage: taking the argument by itself and charging it with absurd consequences. I suspect the stalemate is not really about the validity of a single inference but about what kind of argument Berkeley took himself to be giving, and how much weight the surrounding text should carry. Is that a plausible diagnosis? If so, what would actually count as progress: a formal reconstruction, a distinction among kinds of impossibility, a better account of Berkeley's rhetorical aims, or something else?
