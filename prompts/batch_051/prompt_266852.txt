I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I also do curriculum design part-time for a small company that makes middle-school math lessons. We've been going back and forth about when a visual or an animation actually helps kids grasp a concept and when it just gives them a vivid picture that they then mistake for the idea itself. Lately I've been rereading Aristotle's On the Soul, especially Book III chapter 3, where he says that imagination is neither perception nor belief, and that it's up to us when we wish, but belief isn't up to us in that way. That distinction seems directly relevant to what we do: we have to decide, for each concept in the curriculum, wether to build a visual scaffold or to withhold one, and if we do build one, how to keep it from over-committing kids to a picture. We also have the constraint that most of the team hasn't read Aristotle and I have about 15 minutes before each design meeting to run whatever we come up with.

So I'd like you, as an expert on Aristotle's philosophy of mind, to help me turn this into a reusable design procedure. Write a step-by-step checklist or decision procedure my team could actually run on a proposed visual for a concept. It should include: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of pedagogical judgment rather than anything Aristotle can settle. Please be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about where the procedure will misfire. Then apply the procedure to two concepts we're currently working on: negative numbers and the idea of a variable. For each one, show where the procedure says yes to an image, where it cautions, or where it refuses. And please include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like. Before you write anything, maybe brainstorm the distinctions Aristotle actually draws, then use those to build the checklist.
