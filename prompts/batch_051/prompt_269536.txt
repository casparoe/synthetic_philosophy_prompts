I'm a retired chemist who's recently gotten into philosophy, and my local maritime-history society has to make a ruling. I've been going back to Plutarch's Life of Theseus because it seems to be the original source of the puzzle. Here's the passage (Dryden translation):

"The ship wherein Theseus and the youth of Athens returned from Crete had thirty oars, and was preserved by the Athenians down even to the time of Demetrius Phalereus, for they took away the old planks as they decayed, putting in new and stronger timber in their place, insomuch that this ship became a standing example among the philosophers, for the logical question of things that grow; one side holding that the ship remained the same, and the other contending that it was not the same."

The society has three precedents. (1) The Daedalus has been under continuous care since 1810, and over that time every timber has been replaced during ordinary repairs; the society ruled she is still the same vessel. (2) The Calliope sank in 1912, after which all recoverable original timbers were reassembled into the same hull shape with new fastenings; the society ruled she is still the same vessel. (3) The Hermione was too rotten to restore, and a replica built from new wood and the original plans was ruled not to be the same vessel.

Now the Proteus is being repaired like the Daedalus, but the old planks are being saved as they come out. A donor proposes to use those saved original planks to reassemble a second hull while the continuously maintained ship remains afloat. Which hull should the society recognize as the original Proteus if it wants to be consistent with its precedents? Which precedent is the closest analog, and if the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, what principle would the society be adopting with each choice? Take a stand.
