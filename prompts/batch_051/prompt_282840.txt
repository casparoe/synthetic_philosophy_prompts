During my career as a natural scientist I noticed, but never really questioned, an asymmetry in how intellectual credit is assigned. A mathematician who proves a theorem gets the prize, the named chair, the theorem's name; a referee who spends months verifying the proof and catches subtle errors gets at most an acknowledgment. Finding is called creative; checking is called routine. My recent reading in computational complexity has undermined my confidence that this is a distinction of kind rather than of circumstance. For a large class of problems, checking a proposed solution is computationally easy while finding one appears hard; that is the P versus NP question. If P were to equal NP, then for those problems finding and checking would, in principle, differ only by polynomial overhead—perhaps with impractically large constants, but no longer in kind. Supposing such a proof were announced tomorrow, should our credit practices change? Is there a defensible principle that would still justify valuing discovery over verification, or would the asymmetry be exposed as a contingent feature of our computational predicament that we mistook for a fact about intellectual merit?
