For the novel I am drafting, the central polity is a republic governed largely by allotted citizens' assemblies, and I have worked out a set of theoretical commitments that its founders are meant to defend in earnest. Before I let the founders argue for this system, I would like an audit of whether my own commitments hang together. The commitments are as follows.

(1) The democratic legitimacy of a legislature consists in its descriptively representing the citizenry and in every citizen's having an equal opportunity to take part in binding decisions; whether particular officeholders are authorized by the votes of particular citizens is not part of what makes a legislature democratic.

(2) Elections are aristocratic in operation: repeated contests for office systematically favour the ambitious, the wealthy, and the articulate, so an elected chamber cannot supply the legitimacy described in (1).

(3) Selection by lot from the full roll of citizens is more democratic than election, because only a lottery gives every citizen an equal chance to serve and yields, in expectation, a chamber that mirrors the governed.

(4) Nevertheless, a citizen drawn by lot who never sought office is authorized by no one in particular to make law for others, and this seems to me a real defect of sortition, even if not an insurmountable one.

(5) The defect in (4) is supplied collectively: by adopting a constitution that institutes the lottery, the demos authorizes in advance whoever the lot later selects, just as a jury system authorizes the particular twelve who are eventually drawn.

(6) Service in the allotted chamber ought to be compulsory rather than voluntary, because a lottery over self-selected volunteers would be skewed toward the politically eager, the highly educated, and the economically secure, recreating the same aristocratic skew that (2) condemns in elections.

(7) Compulsory service is not an infringement of liberty in any sense in which compulsory jury service is not; the two differ only in the weight of the burden, not in kind.

(8) Once seated, members of the allotted chamber should be insulated from the pressure of re-selection, since pressure to please future majorities is the main channel through which organized interests capture elected assemblies.

(9) Even so, the chamber should remain responsive to shifts in the public's settled convictions over the course of its term, because a democratic assembly that governs against the considered views of the governed is defective.

(10) I do not regard the competence objection to sortition as serious: elections are not a competence filter either, and both bodies acquire whatever expertise they possess after selection, through staff, briefings, and structured deliberation.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs, or larger subsets, are in genuine tension and how severe each tension is. Distinguish the pairs that only appear to be in tension from those that really are, and for each real tension say what would have to give and what the least costly revision would be. I would also like to know which single revision would do the most to make the set coherent, and what that revision would cost the overall view. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious.
