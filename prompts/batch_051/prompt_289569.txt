I direct the internal review office of a professional association that certifies midwives and accredits birth centers. My office has six staff, only one with legal training; outside counsel is available for periodic review but not for day-to-day triage. We receive two or three reports a month from employees, members, and accredited centers about suspected wrongdoing, retaliation, or misconduct. Our triage decisions determine whether a report is handled as an internal personnel matter, referred to our ethics committee, or treated as a protected disclosure, and we have ten working days from intake to classify a report before the complainant is entitled to take it elsewhere.

Our existing intake checklist is essentially a compliance screen: Did the person follow the internal reporting chain before going outside? Did they take or leak documents? Were their motives free of any personal grievance? That screen keeps returning results I find morally wrong. It operationalizes loyalty as keeping quiet and staying inside, which makes whistleblowing something a decent employee does only when some external obligation—compliance, honesty, the law—overrides loyalty. Yet the most serious cases we handle involve people who insist they went outside because they are loyal: loyal to what the association is supposed to be, and disloyal to the faction, supervisor, or practice that is corrupting it.

Please write a decision procedure my staff can actually run on each incoming report, grounded in a defensible account of loyalty, whistleblowing, and betrayal, but written so that no one running it needs to know the theory behind it. I want ordered questions, a specification of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, explicit points where the procedure stops and assigns an outcome, and an explicit point where it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact or structure. The procedure should yield at least these outcomes: (a) treat as loyal dissent, escalate and protect accordingly; (b) treat as breach or betrayal, route to ordinary misconduct handling; (c) flag for the ethics committee as a case the procedure cannot settle.

Then apply the procedure to these three reports:

1. A staff accountant discloses to a journalist that the association has for two years classified revenue from a corporate sponsor as unrestricted membership dues in its reports to members, after the accountant's supervisor declined to correct it internally. The accountant is also in an active pay dispute with that same supervisor.

2. A certification examiner uses an anonymous external hotline to report that a board member has repeatedly intervened to have weak candidates from the board member's own training program passed. The examiner first raised the matter internally and was told in writing that the board member was known for this and it was not worth pursuing.

3. An IT employee posts internal correspondence revealing that the executive director was treated for alcohol dependence, on the grounds that the executive director had been hypocritically strict about staff absences.

For each case, state which outcome the procedure returns, what assumptions about the case are doing the real work, and where a reasonable person would need to override or supplement the procedure. Flag any outcome that should make my office uncomfortable, and say why the procedure should return it anyway. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do: what kinds of cases it will predictably misclassify, and why the remaining judgment cannot be eliminated by adding more detail.
