So Hofstadter's superrationality was aimed at a very specific setup: a one-shot prisoner's dilemma where the other player is, for practical purposes, you—a twin or a copy who is guaranteed to run the same reasoning as you. In that setting you cooperate because there's no free lunch: whatever you decide, the other player decides too, so defecting just guarantees mutual defection. Fine.

Now here's the wrinkle. Suppose the two players are two instances of the same large language model: identical weights, same training data, but different prompts and different context windows. Each one can see the other's full context, and both know they come from the same model snapshot. They're no longer perfect copies in their actual reasoning—they share almost everything, but their local situations differ, and their outputs might come apart because of sampling or small prompt details. Does Hofstadter's argument survive this, or does the symmetry premise die the moment the players are near-copies rather than exact copies? If it survives, I want to know what 'near enough' cashes out as and where the line gets drawn. If it doesnt survive, is there a weaker principle—the program-equilibrium idea that each can prove the other would cooperate because each can read the other's code—that rescues cooperation, or do we have to shrug and treat the whole thing as an ordinary bargaining game?
