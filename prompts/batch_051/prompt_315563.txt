I've been reading the epistemology stuff on the Meno problem — why should knowledge be more valuable than mere true belief? — and I keep noticing the debate just goes in circles. In the Meno, Socrates himself points out that a true opinion about the road to Larissa gets you there just as reliably as knowledge, so the advantage can't be purely practical. Reliabilists then say knowledge is true belief produced by a reliable process, but critics reply that the reliability only adds value if the believer actually cares about it, which they often don't, so it's hard to see why knowledge is worth more than the true belief plus the process. Virtue epistemologists say knowledge is a kind of achievement (true belief because of ability), and achievements are valuable for their own sake. Then critics produce cases where knowledge doesn't look like an achievement — easy perceptual knowledge, testimony, chicken-sexer cases — and say the virtue people are smuggling in an outside value claim, not an epistemic one. My working theory is that the two sides are mostly answering different questions under the same label — one about distinctively epistemic value, one about all-things-considered value — and that underneath that theres a bedrock disagreement about whether achievement-talk is doing real explanatory work or is just a metaphor.
