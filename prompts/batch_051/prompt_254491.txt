I spent my career as a geochemist, and when I first encountered Garrett Hardin's lifeboat metaphor — a rich nation represented as a boat carrying 50 people with a capacity of 60 while 100 poor swimmers struggle in the water — I found it bracing, because it treated carrying capacity the way I was trained to treat a finite aquifer. But I now suspect the argument gets much of its moral force from a modeling choice rather than from any principle: the boat's capacity is fixed, and the swimmers can contribute nothing but additional weight. I would like you to work through a controlled variation of the metaphor for me.

Keep the starting numbers: 50 aboard, a safety line at 60, and 100 in the water. Now make two changes. First, capacity is not fixed. Among the 100 swimmers are 12 shipwrights and 8 of their family members. If taken aboard, the 12 can, using the boat's spare timber and tools and consuming only reserve rations, build a second boat within four weeks; the second boat would hold 40 people and would make the original boat's crowding problem largely disappear. Taking the 12 aboard raises the number aboard to 62; taking them plus the 8 family members raises it to 70. Second, safety is probabilistic rather than a sharp threshold. A severe squall may strike during the four-week building period; let p be the probability, known to everyone aboard, that such a squall arrives before the second boat is launched. If it arrives while 60 or fewer are aboard, everyone survives. If it arrives while 61 to 70 are aboard, the boat survives with probability 3/5. If it arrives while more than 70 are aboard, the boat is lost. If no squall arrives during the building period, the second boat is completed, enough people transfer to it, and the original boat drops back below its safety line, so the temporary pressure on the margin is over.

On the classic lifeboat analysis, the prudent and just policy is to take no one: the six unused berths are not real surplus but a safety margin against bad luck, and spending that margin on any swimmer would be like depleting a commons that everyone aboard needs. In my variant, the margin can be spent on something that will make the margin unnecessary later. I want you to reason about this as a professional philosopher specializing in international ethics. Start by brainstorming the possible policies and by reconstructing the strongest version of the take-no-one argument in this new setting. Then tell me: does the classic conclusion survive the change? For what values of p does it fail, and which policy should the boat adopt — take no one, take the 12 only, take all 20, or something else? Does the classic style of analysis justify distinguishing the 12 useful swimmers from the other 88, and if it does, is that distinction a moral one or merely an artifact of the model? Finally, draw the lesson for foreign aid: when, if ever, should a rich country treat a poor country's population not as a demand on a fixed stock of resources but as potential co-producers whose contribution can only be unlocked if the rich first accept a temporary reduction in their own security?
