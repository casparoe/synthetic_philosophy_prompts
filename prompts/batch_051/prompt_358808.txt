I'm a software engineer, so if I've got the doomsday argument wrong, that's on me, but here's the version I'm working from. The argument says you should treat yourself as a random sample from all the humans who will ever be born. If our species dies out relatively soon, the total number of humans is small, and it's not weird that my birth rank—around the hundred-billion mark, give or take—is a big chunk of the total. If we instead go on to produce trillions or quadrillions of future people, then I'd be sitting in the first tiny sliver of all humans, which would be a suspicious coincidence. So my existence right now is evidence that the species ends sooner rather than later.

The thing I cant stop thinking about, probably because of my job, is digital minds. A lot of people in AI expect that we'll eventually build sentient systems, or at least systems that carry civilization forward after biological humans are gone—populations of them, maybe running on server farms. It seems like the argument depends entirely on what gets counted in the sampling step. If digital minds count as 'people,' then the future population could be enormous even if biological humans go extinct soon, and the argument looks very different. If they don't count, I cant see what justifies restricting the sample to biological humans instead of 'minds' or 'observers' or 'moral patients.'

So does the doomsday argument survive this, does it need a repaired version, or is it dead once you take digital minds seriously? If digital minds count, does the argument just become a prediction that there probably won't be trillions of them after all? If only biological humans count, what stops the reference class from being arbitrary? I want to know which premise the digital-minds possibility actually bites on, and what the strongest version of the argument left standing looks like.
