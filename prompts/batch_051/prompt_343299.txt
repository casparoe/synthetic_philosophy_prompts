I keep running into a version of this in a reading group, and none of the fixes I try feels like it actually costs nothing. It's an argument that the Incarnation -- one person with two natures -- is just flatly inconsistent, and I can't find the bad step.

(1) Christ is one single person, the Son, who has two natures: a human one and a divine one. I'm not moving on this.
(2) Necessarily, whatever has the divine nature is omniscient and cannot suffer.
(3) Necessarily, whatever has the human nature is not omniscient (the gospels have him saying he doesn't know the day and hour) and can suffer.
(4) If a subject has a nature, then the properties essential to that nature are properties of the subject itself, not merely properties of some abstraction.
(5) So the one person is both omniscient and not omniscient, both passible and impassible. Contradiction; hence the doctrine is false.

The obvious move is that the Son is omniscient qua divine and non-omniscient qua human, so there's no real contradiction, just different respects. But if that qua-maneuver is legitimate here, it seems like I can make any pair of contradictories compossible by finding two respects -- "qua this, F; qua that, not-F" -- which is itself absurd, so the fix proves too much. So either I reject (4) and end up saying the natures aren't really the Son's own the way properties usually are, which starts to sound like two subjects; or I accept the qua-fix and worry I've dissolved all contradiction everywhere. I'd most like to keep (1), (2), and (3). Which step goes, and what exactly does it cost?
