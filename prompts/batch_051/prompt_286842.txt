I'm a philosopher who got roped into co-teaching this year's historiography seminar, and the historian I'm paired with wants me to circulate a one-page statement of where I stand on the objectivity question so the grad students know what their arguing with. I'm not a historian myself, so I want to be sure I'm not about to hand out something incoherant to thirty people who've actually spent time in archives. Here's the list I've ended up with:

(1) The past is real and fixed: there is a determinate set of things that happened, even where no record survives and no historian will ever recover them. (2) Ranke was basically right as a regulative ideal: the historian should try to set aside present concerns, avoid anachronism, and get at the past on its own terms rather than ours. (3) Carr, though, was on to something: facts only become historical facts through being selected and framed by a historian; significance isn't lying around in the archive pre-packaged, it is assigned from some standpoint or other. (4) Following Hayden White, I also think the literary side of historiography is real rather than decorative: a full-scale historical account gets emplotted, and the plot forms (tragedy, romance, comedy, satire) are brought to the sources rather than found in them. (5) All the same, I think some histories are just better than others in a non-aesthetic sense — more accurate, more responsive to the evidence, more adequate to what happened — and that this isn't merely a matter of audience persuasion or fidelity to present politics. (6) History is answerable to the evidence in a way historical fiction isn't, so a historian can be flat-out wrong about the past even when the narrative is rhetorically effective.

I keep thinking these six hang together, but the more I reread them the less sure I am that I have a position rather than a list of six claims I happen to like. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look that way, and what's the cheapest revision to make the set consistent? Rank the problems by severity rather than listing them all as equal. And since I'll have to defend your diagnosis to my co-teacher, tell me what would change your mind about it: which argument or consideration would push you to a different verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
