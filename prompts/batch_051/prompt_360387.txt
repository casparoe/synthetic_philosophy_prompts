Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz defended the principle of the identity of indiscernibles: no two distinct things can be exactly alike in every respect, because numerical distinctness must always be grounded in some qualitative difference. In the twentieth century, Max Black offered a famous counterexample: a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, alike in every qualitative respect, situated at some distance from one another. If Leibniz could be given a complete and sympathetic presentation of Black's example, how would he respond?

Explain which of his deeper commitments—about space, relations, the principle of sufficient reason, or the nature of substance—would do the main work, and consider whether the spatial relation between the spheres gives Leibniz a way to distinguish them without abandoning his principle.
