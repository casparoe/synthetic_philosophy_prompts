I'm taking a course on collective decision-making, and we've just covered the discursive dilemma. A classmate sketched the following argument to show that majority voting on each proposition can't really produce an inconsistent collective position, and I can't tell whether to buy it. Could you assess it step by step: is the argument valid and sound, and if not, exactly which step fails and why?

Set-up: a three-member panel votes separately on three propositions, P, Q, and R, where R follows logically from the conjunction P ∧ Q. Each judge individually holds a logically consistent and implication-closed set of judgments. The panel accepts a proposition just in case at least two of the three judges accept it.

The argument:

(1) Suppose the panel accepts P and accepts Q.
(2) Then P has a two-judge majority, and Q has a two-judge majority.
(3) Any two two-member majorities among three judges must share at least one member, so at least one judge accepts both P and Q; therefore a majority accepts P ∧ Q.
(4) By consistency, any judge who accepts P ∧ Q accepts R.
(5) So R, too, has majority support, and the panel accepts R.
(6) Therefore, whenever the panel accepts the premises, it also accepts the conclusion; majority voting cannot produce a discursive dilemma.

If the argument goes wrong, please illustrate with a concrete distribution of votes. Also, say how confident you are in your diagnosis and which point you are least sure of.
