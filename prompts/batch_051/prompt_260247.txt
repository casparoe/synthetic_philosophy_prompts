In the philosophy unit of my high school class we read a piece about the self that I keep returning to. The author's idea was that the self is like an object's center of gravity. A center of gravity is not a hidden part of the object: you could take the object apart and never find it, yet it is real enough to do respectable work, since it lets you predict how the object will move. In the same way, the author argued, the self is not a soul, not a separate region of the brain, and not a little person inside the head; it is a real abstraction over the story of a whole life, or what she called a center of narrative gravity. The analogy is doing heavy lifting in the argument: it is meant to let us say that the self is perfectly real while denying that it is a thing or a substance, so we can stop looking for the item inside us that has the experiences without concluding that the self is nothing at all.

I want to audit this analogy instead of just accepting or rejecting it. First, the correspondence: what maps to what? The distribution of mass in the object corresponds to what in a person, the point itself corresponds to what, and the fact that the center does not push or pull but only summarizes the mass corresponds to what in the case of the self? Second, where does the mapping break? For instance, a center of gravity is passive — it does not cause the object's motion — while the self seems, at first glance, to be exactly the thing that deliberates and decides; is that a genuine break that damages the intended conclusion, or only a surface mismatch? Third, what does the analogy implicitly import from physics that may not hold for selves — uniqueness, objectivity, neutrality — and which of those imports matter for the conclusion and which are merely decorative? Finally, does the conclusion the author wanted (the self is real without being a thing inside us) survive once the disanalogies are on the table? If you think the analogy cannot carry the conclusion, say whether some better analogy could carry it.
