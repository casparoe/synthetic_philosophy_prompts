You are a professional philosopher of religion with specialist command of classical Islamic theology, in particular the dispute between the Mu'tazila and the Ash'arites over the grounds of moral value and obligation. Please think carefully and work through the strongest available positions before you commit to a verdict; I want a rigorous, self-contained analysis, not a survey of the historical literature.

Here is my situation. I find myself attracted to the Ash'arite position because of its systematic virtues. It offers a single, unified account of obligation: what makes an act wrong is that God forbids it, and more broadly, moral properties are constituted by divine command and prohibition. This preserves God's absolute sovereignty, since it sets up no standard of goodness or justice to which God would be answerable. It also gives a clean epistemic story: to know what I ought to do, I do not need a free-standing moral epistemology that operates independently of revelation. The theory strikes me as tidy, parsimonious, and theologically careful in exactly the ways a good theory should be.

But I keep running into a firm intuition that I have tried, honestly, to give up and cannot. If God were to command the torture of children as an act of worship, I still want to say that something would be deeply wrong with what was commanded — not merely that I would dislike it, and not merely that it contradicts the revealed law of this world, but that the act itself would be wrong. Likewise, on the Ash'arite analysis it seems that saying "God is just" or "God is good" collapses into saying that God does and commands whatever God does and commands, which leaves me unable to locate what praise or gratitude is praise of. The Mu'tazila have a diagnosis of exactly this problem: they argue that unless acts have a goodness or badness that makes them fitting or unfitting objects of command prior to and independent of the command itself — what their later formulations call rational taḥsīn and taqbīḥ — divine justice and God's praiseworthiness become empty. But their remedy seems to purchase intelligibility by positing a moral order that stands above or alongside God, and I understand why the Ash'arites regarded that as idolatrous in its own way, or at least as a violation of divine uniqueness and freedom.

I want you to do three things. First, present both sides at their strongest: give me the best Ash'arite defense against the charge that their view empties divine justice of content, and the best Mu'tazili account of why a God who is essentially and recognizably just must be constrained by reasons that are not simply produced by his will. Second, work through the possible resolutions explicitly. Is my intuition something that should be debunked — a piece of anthropomorphism, or an artifact of human moral sentiment that has no license to legislate for God? Should the Ash'arite theory be amended or the Mu'tazili alternative adopted? Or is there a reading of the Ash'arite view on which it never contradicted the intuition in the first place, perhaps because the intuition tracks something about our concepts of justice and worship rather than about the metaphysics of value? Third, issue a negotiated verdict: state which of these moves you would make, and lay out clearly what would justify each kind of move and why you think your chosen one is best. Do not merely pick a side; I want to understand why the debunking, the revision, and the compatibilist readings each succeed or fail under pressure.

I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; keep the discussion self-contained and put the historical material to work only insofar as it sharpens the underlying philosophical options.
