I've had a distinction kicking around in my notes for months and I want someone to kick it's tires before I use it in anything public. I keep splitting changes to our concepts into two kinds: mending a concept and swapping a concept. Mending keeps the old word, keeps most of its obvious paradigm cases, and just retunes the edges — add a condition, tighten a boundary, demote a borderline case — so the concept does better at what it was already trying to do. Swapping is when you actually want a different tool: new word, new inferential role, new paradigm cases, and you don't care that the old central cases mostly don't count anymore. My hunch is that a lot of what gets called conceptual engineering or ameliorative analysis is really swapping presenting itself as mending, because mending sounds like respectful repair and swapping sounds like changing the subject on purpose. And I think the disguise does real damage: people test the new concept against the old paradigm cases and call it a failure, when trashing those paradigm cases was exactly the point.

Here's my test: after the change, would a competent ordinary speaker who never heard of the theory still say it's the same rough concept — same word, same paradigm examples, just cleaner edges? Then it's mending. Would they say you've changed the subject, or would you have to teach them the theory before the new use even makes sense? Then it's swapping. The example I keep coming back to is addiction, which is usually narrated as better science fixing up the same old concept, but it went from a will-based concept (paradigm: the habitual drunk) to a disease-based one (paradigm: the dependent patient with tolerance and cravings). That looks to me like swapping sold as mending. But then I worry the whole cut collapses once you ask what it is for a concept to stay the same concept through a revision — maybe mending and swapping are just points on a continuum of how much I choose to advertise the change.

You're a professional philosopher working on conceptual ethics and social concepts. Think it through carefully, and before you settle on a verdict, spend a paragraph trying to make the distinction work better than I've made it work. Then tell me: the three strongest objections, the cases where mending and swapping blur together, what this is closest to in the existing literature (I know a bit of Haslanger and Carnap — use them as landmarks, not as a reading list), and whether the main claim, that amelioration is usually disguised replacement, survives or is just a catchy way of restating the obvious. Be candid; I'd rather find out now than from a reviewer.
