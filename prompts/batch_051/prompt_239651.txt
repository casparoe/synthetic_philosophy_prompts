Draft the joint statement that should come out of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between two members of my seminar on the epistemology of religious authority: a Protestant theologian committed to sola scriptura and the perspicuity of Scripture, and a Catholic theologian committed to the position that private interpretation of Scripture terminates in an endless regress unless a divinely authorized interpretive authority, the Church's magisterium, can settle what the text means. They have spent the term arguing past one another; the statement should be one each could actually sign, not a compromise document that blurs their positions beyond recognition.

The statement should begin with a numbered list of agreed claims. I expect it to include precise claims about the text of Scripture itself, the fact that interpretation is a fallible cognitive activity, the existence and scale of real disagreement among sincere and competent readers, and the fact that neither sincerity nor numbers settles the question of meaning. The list should also separate what are often run together: the material sufficiency of Scripture (the claim that Scripture contains all that is necessary for salvation), the formal sufficiency or perspicuity of Scripture (the claim that what is necessary for salvation is clear enough to be known from Scripture without an infallible external interpreter), and a doctrine about readers' reliable access to the text. Getting that separation right is part of the exercise.

Then include a disagreements table of three or four rows. Each row should state a single point of disagreement in wording that each side would accept as a fair formulation of its own position, and should identify the crux behind it—the sub-question whose settlement would move at least one side. The table should capture the regress worry rather than a caricature of it. The Catholic side should press the point that the authority of a private reader's judgment cannot be its own guarantee: appealing to a further private judgment to authenticate the first yields a regress, while stopping the regress at one's own reading is arbitrary. The Protestant side should reply that this is a generic skeptical argument that would equally undermine the magisterium's self-interpretation, and that perspicuity is restricted to matters necessary for salvation, on which competent readers making due use of ordinary interpretive aids converge. The residual disagreement should sit in that exact place.

For each crux, specify at least one case, argument, or piece of evidence that both parties agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Candidate cases include the Arian controversy, in which a dispute over the interpretation of a wide range of Scriptural texts was pressed to a settlement at an ecumenical council, and the Reformation-era conflict over justification, in which a claim that Scripture clearly teaches one thing was pressed against the teaching office of the day. Neither side should be permitted to treat its favored case as decisive on its own; each should say in advance what scrutiny of that case will show and what would count as the other side's best response.

If the collaboration uncovers part of the dispute as verbal, the statement should identify which part and why, without reducing the entire disagreement to a verbal one. It should close with a short paragraph in which each party states the single most important consideration it has been forced to take seriously from the other side. Write in American English.
