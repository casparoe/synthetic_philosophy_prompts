I have a standing rule for arguments in which someone tells me that I cannot really understand or judge a matter from the outside. When the person's case depends on my not having had the relevant experience—when the implied premise is that I would have to have been there, gone through it, or lived it myself before my judgement could count—I discount that appeal almost to zero unless they can name at least one checkable difference: something I would predict, expect, or do differently if I accepted that the experience supplies information beyond the facts I already have. If they cannot produce one, I treat the appeal as a conversational move rather than as evidence, and I keep my original view intact.

The case that made me reconsider is this. A friend recently defended her decision to leave a secure job for freelance work in a volatile field by saying, in effect, that I could not judge whether she had been reckless because I had never known the particular insecurity of living without a salary. I applied the rule. I asked what I should now believe about her, or about the decision, that I am currently wrong about, and when she could not say, I did not change my view.

I have always thought of this as a defence against unarguable appeals to private experience. But I have been reading about the knowledge argument, and it disturbs me. In the thought experiment usually called Mary's room, a scientist called Mary has complete physical and functional knowledge of colour vision but has never herself seen colour; the question is whether she learns something new on first seeing red. If she does, then there seem to be facts that can be possessed only through the experience itself, facts that no amount of outside information would have let her state in advance. That would mean my rule screens out a genuine kind of evidence, not just rhetorical padding.

I am asking you to assess the rule as a policy rather than as a logical inference. Is this shortcut defensible given what is at stake in cases like Mary's? Where does it go wrong, and is there a restatement of the rule that keeps its benefits—protecting me from unfalsifiable conversation-stoppers—without quietly assuming that the physical and functional facts are all there is, and without dismissing real first-person knowledge? I would also like your view on whether someone in my position, unable to acquire every relevant experience, is entitled to a policy of this kind at all. Please also say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict about whether the rule is defensible, and where your overall reasoning is most likely to fail.
