I'm from the American South but just got back from a semester in Berlin, and I can't stop thinking about how the two places handle the past differently. In Berlin I kept nearly tripping over the Stolpersteine: little brass squares set into the sidewalk outside apartment buildings, each one engraved with a name and dates and the bare fact that this person was deported or murdered by the Nazis, placed at the last address where they had lived before being taken. You have to stop and bend down to read them, and most of the time people just walk over them. Then there's the enormous abstract field of grey slabs near the Brandenburg Gate, teenagers doing school trips at concentration camp memorials, and laws that make it a crime to wave a swastika flag or deny the Holocaust. Meanwhile I come home to my Georgia hometown, where there's still a bronze Confederate general on the courthouse lawn and the local Sons of Confederate Veterans chapter says taking it down would erase history. I've got strong gut feelings about which of these is better, but I want to know what each one actualy assumes.

Treat this as a philosophy puzzle rather than a history essay. What theory of memory — or collective responsibility, or political identity — does each of these practices presuppose? Does the decentralized, easy-to-miss Stolperstein practice presuppose a different view of remembrance than the big official memorial in Berlin, or are they two expressions of one view? Does either one actually require thinking that people today inherit guilt for what past generations did, or can they be justified without that? And where are these practices underdetermined, meaning several different theories could all license the same practice? Act as a philosopher working on collective memory and responsibility: start by brainstorming the possible commitments each practice could have, then reconstruct the most plausible implicit theory for each and say which parts are genuinely forced and which are optional.
