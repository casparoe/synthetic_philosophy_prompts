I'm retired now from climate science — for decades I built integrated assessment models of carbon policy, so I think in state variables, forcing, discount rates, ensemble spread, deep uncertainty, tip points, and long-horizon optimization. Lately I've been reading the longtermism debate on my own. As I understand it: one side says the far future could contain a staggering number of people, so steering the long-run trajectory now dwarfs ordinary near-term politics; the other side says that's a recipe for a self-appointed vanguard overriding democratic checks because it claims to know where history should go. Restate that argument — including any longtermist reply that tries to head off the authoritarian worry, e.g. by treating robust institutions as part of the trajectory worth preserving — in the vocabulary I actually trust: objective functionals over long horizons, pure time preference versus intergenerational neutrality, model ensembles, tail risk, controllability, and error that grows with the horizon. Be faithful; don't just rename things. Then give me the residue report: what translated cleanly, what you had to fudge, and what has no counterpart in that vocabulary at all. In particular, is the critics' worry about optimization per se (too much control over a long horizon), or about who picks the objective function and whose model gets run — and is the fact that my toolkit can't express legitimacy or consent a hole in the longtermist picture or just a hole in how climate-policy people talk?
