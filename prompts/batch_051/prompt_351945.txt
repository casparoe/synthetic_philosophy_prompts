Lately I've been doing some local history research on people who don't show up much in official documents: tenant farmers, domestic workers, enslaved people, women whose names only appear as "wife of." I've developed a rule and I don't know if it's defensible. My rule is: if there's no documentary trace, I don't count it as a historical claim at all. I'll say "there's no evidence that X" rather than "X happened" or "X didn't happen," and in practice I treat no evidence as a reason not to believe the claim. A friend who's a historian tells me this is a pretty serious bias, because the archive itself is a product of power: courts, tax collectors, census takers, slaveholders, and churches decided what got written down and what got kept. So my rule quietly converts "the people who controlled the records didn't bother to record it" into "it didn't happen." I see her point. But the alternative seems worse to me: if I lower my standards and start admitting oral tradition, family stories, and local legend, then I'm accepting evidence with all kinds of error, motivated memory, telephone-game distortion, people repeating what they half-remember. I can't fact-check any of it. So is my rule defensible as a shortcut for someone with limited time, or is it just a bias dressed up as rigor? Is there a version that keeps some discipline without re-enacting the silences? And how would I tell, from the inside, whether a particular silence is evidence of absence or just evidence of who held the pen?
