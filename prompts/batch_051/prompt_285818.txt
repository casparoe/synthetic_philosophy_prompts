A local land trust holds a seventy-acre property under a deed restriction from 1961 requiring that the land "shall be kept forever in its natural wild condition, without cutting, planting, draining, or the introduction of any foreign plant or animal, so that future generations may find it as it has always been." The donor's clear aim was to stop the place being logged or developed. Last year an insect from Asia reached the tract and is expected to kill most of the old-growth hemlocks within a decade; once the canopy opens, the understory is predicted to fill with invasive shrubs. Our board has three options: release a parasitic wasp from the insect's home range every few years to hold the hemlocks, cut the dying trees and plant species that can survive a warming climate, or do nothing and let whatever comes replace the hemlock grove. The words plainly forbid the first two, yet doing nothing seems to defeat the stated aim that future generations find the land as it has always been. I want to understand how the plain words, the original purpose, what the donor would likely have said if asked about insect outbreaks and a warming climate, and what a reasonable reader of the clause today would take it to require, each come out. And beneath that: once "natural condition" can only be kept by intervening, what is the clause protecting—the particular species on the land, the process of non-interference, the absence of human fingerprints, or the visitor's experience of a place that looks untouched? If the ordinary ways of reading it now pull in opposite directions, which should guide us, and why?
