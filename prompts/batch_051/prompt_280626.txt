I write about science and ideas, and I'm drafting an essay that reads the history of political thought through a question in modern institutional design: should we build systems around incentives, or around the cultivation of character? I plan to present Han Feizi's Legalism as the classic case for the incentive route. As I understand him, Han Feizi argues that a ruler cannot govern by relying on the virtue of ministers and subjects — loyalty and benevolence are too rare and too easily counterfeited — so the state must rest on a dependable machinery of reward and punishment. My working assumption, and the line I'd like to take, is that this commits him to a stringent view of human nature: people are at bottom self-interested calculators, and the virtues a Confucian ruler tries to cultivate are largely illusory, displayed only because the incentives happen to reward them. I want to be sure I'm not handing my readers a distortion. If Han Feizi really is committed to psychological egoism of that sort, what separates his well-ordered state from a criminal syndicate run competently on fear and payoffs? And if he is not so committed, what is left of his objection to the Confucian alternative, which holds that government should be entrusted to rulers and ministers of cultivated moral character?
