my reading group keeps ending up at the same place. someone cites the Wang Yangming line usually translated as the unity of knowledge and action (zhixing heyi), everyone nods, and the argument stops. but the sentence is quietly doing different work each time. one week it was used to say that someone who could reconstruct the argument perfectly and then acted against it "didn't really know it." another week it was used to dismiss careful armchair analysis as just guessing, on the grounds that there is no purely intellectual grasp of a thing separate from the activity of applying it. a third week someone used it to say self-deception about your own motives is impossible, since what you do simply is what you know and there is no hidden inner state left over to be wrong about.

those feel like three different claims wearing the same slogan, and i want them pulled apart (i keep trying to seperate them myself and ending up with a mush). what are the distinct precise theses the line could be asserting, from the almost-trivial to the radical? for each reading: state the exact claim (who knows what, what counts as knowledge, what counts as action, and which way the dependence runs), say whether it's a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or false, and say which of the three moves above it actually licenses — including the possibility that one move needs two readings taped together, and that some readings license none of them.

give me a table, one row per reading, with these columns:

- reading: short label for the thesis
- precise_claim: the thesis stated as exactly as possible
- terms: what knowledge and action each cover on this reading
- verdict: platitude / substantive and defensible / false / needs qualifications (say which)

then after the table a short paragraph: which reading is doing the real work in those arguments, and which readings the people quoting the line probably don't mean but keep implying anyway.
