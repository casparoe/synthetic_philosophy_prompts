A short essay exam question and a student's response are below.

Exam question: Consciousness is often described as unified: a person's simultaneous experiences are supposed to belong to a single conscious point of view. What do split-brain cases show about this unity? In your answer, be precise about what the behavioral evidence establishes and what it does not, and about what further assumptions are needed to move from that evidence to a conclusion about how many streams of consciousness or subjects a split-brain patient has.

Student answer: Split-brain patients have had their corpus callosum severed, which blocks direct communication between the left and right cerebral hemispheres. Because the visual system is cross-wired, a stimulus flashed to the right visual field is projected to the left hemisphere, and a stimulus flashed to the left visual field is projected to the right hemisphere. Since language is usually in the left hemisphere, a patient shown a word in the right visual field can say it; but if the word is shown in the left visual field, the patient will typically say they saw nothing, while the left hand, which the right hemisphere controls, can still select the matching object from an array. That much is well-established behavioral data.

The relevant datum for the unity of consciousness is the dissociation between what the patient reports and what the patient's hands can do. The natural conclusion is that the two hemispheres were carrying on separate processing, and that the right hemisphere's contents were inaccessible to the part of the brain that produced the patient's verbal answer. This is enough to show that the ordinary assumption of a fully unified consciousness cannot simply be taken for granted in these patients. Strictly, the behavioral evidence alone does not force us to posit exactly two streams of consciousness; it could in principle be one stream with rapidly shifting access. So the strongest unqualified conclusion is that interhemispheric integration has broken down, not that we have counted two distinct minds.

There is, however, a decisive further consideration. The right hemisphere never verbally reports the stimulus. If the right hemisphere were conscious of the word, the patient would be able to say so, since saying what one is conscious of is precisely the mark of consciousness. The right hemisphere's correct manual choices are therefore best explained as unconscious sensorimotor processing of the kind found in blindsight. It follows that the split-brain patient has a single stream of consciousness, located in the left hemisphere, together with a set of unconscious right-hemisphere capacities. This in turn reveals the true nature of unity: unity of consciousness just is the availability of one's states for verbal report. So split-brain cases do not reveal a divided consciousness; they reveal only a modest reduction in reportable access. Ordinary cases of driving on autopilot illustrate the same point in miniature—the driver cannot report the route afterward, but we would not say a second subject was involved.
