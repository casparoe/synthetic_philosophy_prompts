Question:

Explain what an information cascade is, and distinguish it from simple conformism. Using a small sequential-choice example, show how a cascade can arise among rational agents. Then address the following: does the possibility of information cascades show that market or group outcomes are irrational? Why or why not?

Student answer:

An information cascade occurs when a person observes the choices of earlier decision-makers and rationally decides to ignore her own private information because the public history of choices is more informative than her single private signal. This is not the same as conformism. Conformism involves a preference for fitting in, matching the group, or avoiding social disapproval. In a cascade, by contrast, the person only cares about being correct, and she follows the crowd because the crowd's past choices contain genuine evidence.

Consider a simple example. Ann and Ben must each choose restaurant A or restaurant B. Each receives an independent private signal about which restaurant is better. The signals are equally reliable, and the two restaurants start out equally likely to be best. If Ann's signal says A, she chooses A. If Ben's signal says B while Ann chose A, then the evidence is balanced, so Ben follows his own signal and chooses B; if Ben's signal says A, he chooses A. So when Ann and Ben both choose A, their choices reveal two independent pieces of evidence favouring A. Now Carol arrives with a private signal saying B. The public record contains two signals favouring A, while Carol's own signal is one favouring B. Since the signals are equally weighted, the balance of evidence favours A, so Carol rationally chooses A even though her private information points to B. From Carol onward, each later person is in the same position as Carol and rationally chooses A. Carol's choice, and all later choices, no longer reveal the decision-makers' private signals, so the cascade continues.

The same argument applies unchanged if there are many alternatives rather than just two. Early choices simply accumulate, and later decision-makers are equally forced to follow, which is why markets get locked into particular technological standards and why so many choices are path-dependent.

The central lesson is that information cascades show that market and group outcomes can be irrational. Each individual is rational, but the group is not: the group throws away the private information of everyone who arrives after the cascade starts. A crowd can therefore settle on the wrong restaurant, and even a market can get stuck on an inferior product, not because anyone is stupid or careless, but because the group-level decision procedure is rationally defective. The answer, then, is yes: information cascades are a case in which individual rationality adds up to collective irrationality.
