I am the collections registrar at a mid-sized university art museum with a teaching collection of roughly 15,000 paintings, works on paper, and decorative arts, and I need a standing decision procedure, a reusable flowchart in words rather than a memo about one case, for the four or five times a year when an object's authenticity, attribution, or identity becomes disputed or changes. The stakes are high in several ways at once: a wrong call misleads students and scholars, embarrasses the museum with donors and lenders, can expose us to legal claims, and distorts the values we report to insurers. The people who will run the procedure are registrars and curators, not philosophers, and they will usually have less than an hour per case. The director signs off on the final disposition, but wants a written reasoning trail that can survive outside scrutiny.

Ground the procedure in whatever the philosophy of art actually knows about fakes, forgeries, copies, and the value of originals, translated into working rules a non-philosopher can apply. The procedure should explicitly separate three kinds of question, to be asked in order: attribution, meaning who made the object; authenticity, meaning whether it is what it is presented as being, for example an 18th-century drawing rather than an early 20th-century imitation; and identity, meaning whether the surviving object is still the original work after extensive restoration, loss, or replacement of parts, and at what threshold it stops being so. It should also build on two conceptual distinctions from the philosophy of art, translated into tests a curator can apply. First, distinguish works whose identity is tied to a particular object's history of production, such as a painting or a carved sculpture, from works for which a competent copy can itself count as the same work, such as a novel, a musical composition, or a print pulled from the artist's own matrix. Second, distinguish the observable surface properties of an object from its history-dependent properties, so the procedure can say whether a work's status can change when we learn something new about how it was made even though nothing visible changes. Treat forgery, workshop production, later imitation, copy, and restoration as distinct categories; a work can be genuine but not by the master's own hand, or authentic but heavily restored, and the procedure should not run these together.

Some philosophers of art hold that a perfect fake is aesthetically just as good as the original and loses only market or sentimental value; others hold that history of production partly constitutes what a work is and what it achieves, so an original and a perfect fake are not the same kind of artifact at all. Your procedure must not silently adopt one side of that dispute. For each branch in the procedure, say which working rule follows from which side, and mark where the museum is making a philosophical choice rather than applying a settled fact.

For each question in the sequence, specify what counts as a sufficient answer for museum purposes, for example what kind of technical, documentary, or curatorial evidence would settle an attribution question, and where the procedure stops with a definite no rather than a maybe. The procedure should issue a disposition for each case: retain and relabel, withdraw from display, return to a lender under stated conditions, keep as a teaching specimen on fakes, or, in an extreme case, destroy. It should say which dispositions follow from the taxonomy and which are value judgments reserved for the director or the collections committee.

Run the procedure on these three cases and show the step-by-step reasoning:

(i) A drawing long catalogued as by a major 18th-century draughtsman is shown by paper analysis to be on 20th-century paper whose watermark is associated with a known 20th-century forger; it is probably a deliberate fake.
(ii) A contemporary painting long exhibited as by a named artist is confirmed to have been executed largely under the artist's close supervision by studio assistants; the artist planned the composition, corrected the canvas at several stages, and signed it.
(iii) A polychrome wooden sculpture of a saint has been restored so extensively over three centuries that roughly 30 percent of the original carved wood and 15 percent of the original paint surface remain; the museum has always treated and labelled it as the original devotional object.

Make the third case, or another case if the procedure forces it, produce an answer the museum will not like, such as the conclusion that our long-standing label is false or that the object can no longer be described as the original work; say that plainly rather than softening it.

Finally, state what the procedure cannot do: the cases it cannot settle without a direct value choice, the borderline it will always have trouble with, and the ways it will misfire if we treat it as a substitute for curatorial judgment instead of a way of surfacing the judgment calls that were already being made.
