I am a journalist who covers the intersection of culture and ideas, and I am planning a long-form piece on political commitment in American fiction. I want to frame it through W. E. B. Du Bois's argument in the 1926 Crisis essay Criteria of Negro Art that art is always propaganda, and that Black artists should therefore openly use their work for racial advancement rather than let it drift toward the tastes of white readers or of a self-appointed art world. I would like to read Ralph Ellison's 1952 novel Invisible Man against that claim. The novel is often placed in opposition to the protest-novel tradition, but I do not want to reduce it to a simple counterexample. I want a critical reading that lets Du Bois's position illuminate specific scenes and choices, while also letting the novel complicate or resist the position. Which episodes in Invisible Man most pressure Du Bois's thesis, and which episodes arguably bear it out—for instance, the narrator's Brotherhood speeches, the Liberty Paints episode, the death of Tod Clifton, the final riot, or the frame narrative? Use whichever scenes you think carry the most weight, and do not treat the novel as a mere allegory for an intellectual position. At the end, I want your honest assessment of what Ellison's novel adds to the debate about art and propaganda that Du Bois's essay alone cannot. As a journalist, I also need to separate what is settled about Du Bois's view and about the novel from what is genuinely contested among critics. Where your reading depends on a contested interpretation, say so plainly, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
