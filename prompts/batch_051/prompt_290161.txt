I am a student currently enrolled in a course on decision theory and the foundations of probability, and I have been unable to settle my view of the two-envelope problem. I want you to treat it not as a puzzle to be explained away but as a direct conflict between a principle I find compelling and an intuition I find impossible to surrender, and to help me decide which one should give way.

The setup is the familiar one. Two sealed envelopes contain sums of money; one contains exactly twice as much as the other, and I do not know which is which. I select one at random. Before I open it, the symmetry argument seems decisive: neither envelope can be the better choice, and no decision rule can reliably extract a gain from a pair of indistinguishable, symmetrical envelopes.

Now suppose I open my envelope and find some amount, which I will call x. The other envelope must contain either x/2 or 2x. The expected-value argument proceeds by treating these two possibilities as equally probable, giving an expected value to switching of (x/2 + 2x)/2 = 1.25x, strictly greater than x. Since the argument would deliver the same verdict for any value of x I might find, it appears to recommend committing to switch before opening — and, once I have switched, the identical argument appears to recommend switching back. This is flatly incompatible with the symmetry intuition.

I can see three possible rulings, and I want the strongest version of each. First, that expected-value reasoning is the correct arbiter and the symmetry intuition should be debunked as the product of ignoring conditional information. Second, that the symmetry intuition is tracking a real constraint on rational choice, and the expected-value argument must contain a false step — most plausibly the equally-probable move — so the argument should be amended or abandoned. Third, that the two are compatible once the implicit prior over the amounts is made explicit, or once conditional expected value is carefully separated from ex ante expected value; the appearance of contradiction would then come from sliding between them.

Tell me which ruling you would adopt and what would justify each of the other two. In doing so, address the role of the prior: what happens to the argument if the smaller amount is drawn from a proper distribution with finite expectation, and what happens in a bounded variant where the stakes are drawn uniformly from a finite set? My underlying question is whether the apparent advantage of switching tells me something about the envelopes or only about an improperly specified probability model.
