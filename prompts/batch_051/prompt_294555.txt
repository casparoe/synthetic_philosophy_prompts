You are a moral philosopher whose specialty is the ethics of everyday interpersonal commitment. I want you to produce a full specification of what an adequate theory in this area must get right, before I read any more particular theories. The area is the ethics of small promises: keeping the casual plan, arriving when you said you would, doing the small task you agreed to do, sending the message or making the call you said you would make, and warning someone when you can see you will be late. Treat punctuality as one central case, not the whole topic. Assume the purpose is a theory adequate for guiding and assessing ordinary interpersonal conduct, not for legal codes or institutional policy, though you may note where those differ in kind.

Write the desiderata for such a theory: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, and the phenomena it must explain, with the case or consideration behind each item. I want the full list, not an argument for one theory. In particular, make sure the list addresses at least the following, correcting or discarding any item if you think it is not a genuine requirement: (1) the difference between lateness and failure to notify, and between breach, excuse, blame, and owing repair; (2) the apparent mismatch between the triviality of any single small breach and the seriousness of being the person who reliably breaches; (3) the line between voluntary incurring and background expectation, including the difference between "I'll be there," "I'll try to be there," and merely having been invited, and whether silence or delay can itself create a commitment; (4) the relation between moral obligation and social norm, where a theory must neither collapse the two nor simply identify them, and must explain why not every expectation someone feels, such as instant replies or permanent availability, is an obligation merely because it is felt; (5) the directed character of these commitments, in that they can be owed to a particular person, and what that entails about standing to forgive, waive, or complain; (6) the contextual variability of expectations across relationships, settings, and cultures, and how a standard can be context-sensitive without making everything arbitrary; (7) the role of repair, including apology, updating, and making good, on pain of these being mere ritual; and (8) consistency with what we know about how people actually sustain trust and regard one another in recurring low-stakes interactions.

For each item, say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp, and state which requirement does the most work in eliminating otherwise popular theories. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. I am especially interested in whether the triviality of any single small breach and the moral seriousness of a settled pattern of small breaches can both be preserved without positing a group-level agent or treating habits as a black box; if you think reconciling them forces a choice between act-based and character-based pictures, say so explicitly.

Then, briefly and only after the list, indicate how the main families of view, consequentialist, Kantian or generalizing, virtue-ethical, and care- or relationship-based, would score against the list, keeping the emphasis on the criteria rather than the scorecard.

Think carefully. Begin by brainstorming candidate requirements, then prune and defend the final list. Write in a formal tone. End by stating which argument or consideration would most likely change your mind about the list as a whole, and where your reasoning in constructing it is most likely to fail.
