I keep noticing a structural parallel between two problems that are usually discussed in isolation, and I would like help deciding whether it is deep or superficial.

The first is the puzzle of petitionary prayer to an omniscient and immutable God. If God already knows what I need and has eternally and unchangeably willed what will happen, then my asking cannot supply God with new information or move God to do something God was not already going to do. Petitionary prayer can therefore look pointless or even incoherent: a request addressed to a being who, by definition, cannot be swayed by it. The standard replies are that prayer changes the one who prays, or that God eternally wills to grant certain goods only as responses to prayer, so that the prayer is part of the decree rather than an attempt to revise it.

The second is Newcomb's problem in decision theory. A reliable predictor has already placed a large sum in an opaque box on the basis of a prediction about whether I will take both boxes or only the opaque one. When I later choose, my choice cannot causally change what is already in the box. Causal decision theorists say I should take both boxes, since only causal consequences should matter and taking both dominates. Evidential decision theorists say I should take only the opaque box, because doing so is strong evidence that the box is full, and expected utility tracks evidence, not just causation.

The parallel I keep seeing is this. Both cases involve an act that cannot causally produce the state of affairs one cares about, because that state is settled independently of the act. The only relation between act and state is non-causal: in Newcomb's problem it is the predictor's reliability, and in the theological case it would be the fit between the prayer and God's eternal decree or foreknowledge. Both cases also seem to generate the same split between a causal stance, on which the act is pointless, and an evidential or participatory stance, on which the act can still be the right thing to do because it is appropriately linked to a state that has already been settled.

Is this mapping sound? In particular, is the role played by the predictor in Newcomb's problem the same kind of thing as the role played by God's decree or foreknowledge in the theology of prayer, or does importing decision-theoretic categories distort the theological case? What would have to be true on both sides for the debate between causal and evidential decision theory to transfer cleanly to petitionary prayer? And can you identify something this parallel would predict that turns out false, or a precise place where the two cases come apart? If the comparison rests on a misunderstanding of either problem, or if the question is badly formed as posed, tell me that directly rather than forcing a verdict from a flawed analogy.
