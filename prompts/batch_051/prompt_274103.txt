I spent fourty years in a molecular biology lab before retiring, and I've only recently started reading philosophy seriously. Something about the conflict-of-interest system has been bothering me since I stopped working, and I'd like a philosopher's take on it.

When I reviewed papers or sat on grant committees, the COI forms asked about money: consulting fees, patents, stock, a spouse working for the drug company. They never asked me to declare strong moral or political commitments. A reviewer can be a lifelong animal-rights activist judging a paper on animal experiments, or a devout Catholic judging embryonic stem-cell work, or an anti-nuclear campaigner judging radiation-risk research, and the form doesn't treat that as a conflict. The implicit assumption seems to be that money can corrupt your judgement but moral commitments are somehow fine—or that you can set them aside when you can't set aside a consulting fee, which seems backwards to me, since a religious or political identity is usually much deeper and more central than a few thousand dollars in stock.

What view of the difference—if there really is one—between a bias and a value does this practice presuppose? Is it coherent? Could the system be after something else entirely, like tractability, rather than a real distinction? And what would conflict-of-interest policy have to look like if we took money and moral commitments to be equally capable of biasing someone? I'm not after a reading list; I want to know what the practice is quietly committing itself to.
