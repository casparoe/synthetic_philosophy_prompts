I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing an essay on a curious feature of scientific evaluation: several of our best theories are, strictly speaking, false, and yet we reasonably regard some as vast improvements over their rivals and over "safe" truths that no one doubts. The philosophers' word for this is verisimilitude, or truthlikeness: closeness to the whole truth about a domain, understood as something that admits of degrees and can be used to grade false theories rather than merely sorting claims into true and false. Karl Popper introduced the term because he wanted a non-inductive way of saying that one false theory may be objectively closer to the truth than another; his own attempts to formalize the idea ran into a famous technical objection, and the discussion since has splintered rather than settled. I am not looking for a survey of that literature. I am looking for a working definition I can actually apply, and I want you to propose one from scratch.

Here are the cases I want it to handle. (a) A complete and exactly true description of a bounded subject matter — the trajectories and final positions of three billiard balls after a particular collision — should receive the maximum score. (b) A statement of a physical constant that is correct to nine decimal places and wrong in the tenth should score very high but not maximal. (c) A true but heavily hedged assertion, such as a forecast that tomorrow's temperature in a given city will fall between −30 and 50 degrees Celsius, should not score high merely because it is true: vacuous safety is not closeness to the truth. (d) A theory that gets a system's central causal mechanism right while erring in several low-level details, and a rival that gets the details right while misidentifying the central mechanism — both are false, and I have a strong sense that the first should outrank the second, but I cannot yet say what principle explains this. (e) Two astronomical theories that fit the same positional data equally well but posit different structures, one geocentric with epicycles and one heliocentric with ellipses; if one is structurally closer to how the system actually works, a definition ought to be able to say so even though both are imperfect. (f) A false theory inflated by conjoining many true but irrelevant claims, such as a flawed model of memory plus an accurate list of state capitals; I am not sure whether the extra true content should add anything, and I suspect the definition will have to explain why irrelevant truths should not count. (g) A theory that is wholly accurate in what it asserts but radically incomplete — a causal map that leaves out half the known relevant factors. It is true of a fragment, but I am unsure whether incompleteness of this kind depresses truthlikeness or is a separate failing entirely.

Propose a definition of verisimilitude, run it through all seven cases, and say where it delivers the wrong verdict or none. Then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts it forces. Finally, tell me which of its verdicts you would be least willing to defend in print, and why.
