I have been working on a paper about whether the endowment effect—the familiar gap between what people demand to part with an object they own and what they would pay to acquire it—is best understood as a cognitive bias or as a legitimate expression of the way preferences attach to what is theirs. While preparing for my philosophy of law course, I noticed a parallel I cannot decide is deep or superficial. A striking amount of legal doctrine seems to institutionalize the same asymmetry between the party who already holds something and the party who merely aspires to it. Possession is treated as presumptively legitimate; the burden of displacing an established possessor is substantial; adverse possession can allow a long-established de facto holder to defeat the paper owner; and courts often value repose and settled expectations more than corrective reallocation after the fact. This looks, at the level of institutions, like loss aversion written into the rules.

But I can also see reasons to think the analogy breaks down. The law's preference for possession may be a response not to the value of holding as such, but to the epistemic and administrative costs of reopening settled questions—costs that a single experimental subject choosing between keeping and buying a mug does not face in the same way. Conversely, if the parallel is deep, arguments might transfer in both directions: a critique of legal status-quo preference could undermine the view that the endowment effect is a legitimate preference, or a defense of loss aversion as rationally tracking attachment and uncertainty might vindicate the law's solicitude for established holdings. Could you help me work out whether this parallel is genuine, loose, or merely metaphorical? I am looking for a precise statement of the mapping between the two domains, an account of what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer, and at least one place where the analogy predicts something that turns out false.
