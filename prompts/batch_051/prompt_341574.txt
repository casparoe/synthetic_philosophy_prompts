I keep going back and forth about whether a translated text can be inspired. In the preface to the 1611 King James Version, “The Translators to the Reader,” the translators open with this: “Zeal to promote the common good, whether it be by devising anything ourselves, or revising that which hath been labour by others, deserveth certainly much respect and esteem, but yet findeth but cold entertainment in the world.” That sounds like they saw translation as ongoing human revision, not as a once-for-all inspired product. But here’s the rub: most KJV-onlyists treat that same English text as Scripture in the full sense, and the New Testament writers themselves quote the Septuagint as authoritative even where it departs from the Hebrew. My working theory is that inspiration attaches strictly to the original-language texts, and a translation is at most a fallible pointer to what God said. My intuition won’t fall in line: when I read John 1 in English, I feel like I’m encountering the word of God directly, not hearing a pointer to it. Which should yield? Is this a case where the intuition is a product of liturgical habit and should be dropped, does the theory need to distinguish inspiration from authority or from “word of God” in a way I’m missing, or can both be saved? Give me the strongest version of each move and tell me which costs most.
