I've been reading around Locke's theory of property for a course on the history of political thought, and a friend sent me a passage from an introductory piece (I've lost the link) that summarises his view roughly as follows: 'Locke's idea is that you come to own a thing by mixing your labour with it. When I pick an apple or till a field, my effort is the source of most of the thing's value, and since I deserve the fruit of my own exertion, the apple or the field becomes mine. The only real limit Locke places on this is the proviso: I may take from the common stock only if others are still left with enough of comparable quality. In the state of nature, when land was plentiful, this condition was met automatically; once money was introduced and goods could be stored without spoiling, land became scarce and the proviso could no longer be satisfied, but by then everyone had tacitly consented to money, so the resulting inequality was legitimate anyway.' The passage is written with real confidence, but I have a nagging sense that the argument moves too quickly, especially at the end. I am inclined to believe the value-and-desert part and the tacit-consent part; it is the middle I cannot get a grip on.
