I've ended up with a cluster of views about dirty-hands cases, and I'd like an audit of whether they fit together before I keep defending the package. Here is where I've landed:

(1) In at least some political decisions there are genuine moral remainders: a leader can be all-things-considered required to authorize an action — for instance, an operation that will certainly kill one innocent person as collateral to prevent a massacre of many — even while that action is morally wrong in itself, and this wrongness is not canceled by the fact that the alternative would have been worse.

(2) If someone does exactly what they were all-things-considered morally required to do, and they judged the situation correctly, then they have no reason for guilt or self-reproach. Guilt is apt only for culpable wrongdoing, and there is no culpable wrongdoing when one acts as one ought.

(3) The residue in such cases is not merely tragic or aesthetic. It grounds a continuing obligation of a special kind: the leader still owes acknowledgment to the victims and some form of repair, even though the decision was the right one and would be made again.

(4) I reject the idea that duties conflict in a way that makes both alternatives forbidden. When moral considerations of different strength conflict, the stronger consideration determines what one ought to do; the weaker one is outweighed and drops out of the picture of what there is most reason to do.

(5) I do not think the residue requires a distinctive property of being morally dirty or tainted. Ordinary wrongness, justification, and repair should be enough to capture everything that is true in the dirty-hands literature.

Does this package hang together? I can feel that some of these pull against one another, but I am not sure which joints are load-bearing, which tensions are merely apparent, and which belief is doing the most work. Give me the audit: order the tensions by severity, say for each which commitment would have to give and what I would lose by giving it up.
