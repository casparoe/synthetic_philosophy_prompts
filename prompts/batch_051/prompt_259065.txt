I chair the coverage committee of a large public health system. Fourteen times a year we meet for ninety minutes and decide which treatments, vaccines, and services the system will cover from a fixed annual budget, and every no means someone on the margin goes without. None of us is a trained philosopher. The decisions are reviewed afterward against a standard of being procedurally fair and reasonable, and the finance director has to sign off on a written justification each time. I have spent enough years reading rationalist-adjacent material to distrust any procedure that works only when the people applying it already share an intuition, so I want something we can actually run in the meeting, not a restatement of our values.

The framework I keep returning to is Scanlon's contractualism. As I understand it, the view is that an act or policy is wrong if it could be rejected under the principles a person could reasonably reject, which means each person's complaint has to be answered; and the literature around it suggests it is hostile to simply aggregating many small benefits so that they outweigh one person's serious harm. But a real coverage committee cannot avoid aggregation entirely, because almost every budget decision spreads small gains over thousands and concentrates large losses on a few. I want a reusable procedure that operationalizes the difference between legitimate and illegitimate aggregation under contractualist reasoning, rather than just asserting the distinction.

Please write the procedure as:

- an ordered list of questions we work through for each proposal, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each;
- explicit stopping rules: which answers end in a no, which in a conditional yes, and which questions are genuinely value judgments the procedure must hand to the committee instead of resolving;
- a small set of standard cases or probes to apply at each step, so a clinician and a financial analyst run the same rule and converge most of the time; and
- clear warnings about which steps look mechanical but are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then apply the procedure to three realistic proposals:

1. A drug that extends life by a median of six weeks for advanced cancer, with roughly four thousand eligible patients per year, giving a large aggregate benefit.
2. An enzyme replacement therapy that is the most expensive line item in the system's history and durably prevents severe neurological disability in about six children per year.
3. A vaccine that prevents serious illness in most of several million recipients but is expected to cause a rare, severe adverse reaction in a small number of predictable cases each year.

Include at least one case in which the procedure returns an answer the committee will not like, one we would have approved under our current cost-per-quality-adjusted-life-year habit, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do, especially where it cannot settle the one-versus-many question by computation.
