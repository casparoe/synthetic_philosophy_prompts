I maintain a small open-source neural-network inference engine, and I am trying to resolve a dispute over a license clause I drafted myself. The engine is a general transformer runtime: given a file of tensor weights and a short architecture description, it executes the model. On its own the engine does nothing useful — with random weights it emits noise — and the weights are inert arrays of floating-point numbers without an engine to run them. A company has now trained a medical-transcription model using a standard architecture and wants to distribute the trained weights, a file of several billion parameters, under a proprietary license, while instructing users to obtain my engine separately. The weights contain none of my source code and were not produced by editing it, but they have no substantial use apart from my engine, and it is only the combination of engine plus weights that performs transcription.

The license I wrote years ago contains this clause, which I am quoting exactly:

"Article 4 (Copyleft). You may copy, modify, and distribute the Program, provided that any work which you distribute and which is based on the Program is distributed, as a whole, under the terms of this License. A work is based on the Program if it (a) contains the Program or any part of it, (b) is a modification or adaptation of the Program, or (c) is designed to be linked or combined with the Program to form a larger work and has no substantial use apart from the Program."

When I drafted Article 4, I was thinking about someone forking the code or statically linking a proprietary module into the engine. I did not anticipate a case in which most of a functioning system's behavior lives in a data file that is not source code, is not compiled, and yet determines what the program does. So I cannot tell whether the clause reaches these weights, and I do not trust my own instincts because I have a stake in the question.

Please approach this as an expert on software licensing and legal interpretation. Work through what each of the four standard approaches to interpretation gives here: (1) the plain words of the clause, (2) the purpose of the copyleft requirement, (3) what I, as drafter, would probably have said if this case had been described to me when I wrote it, and (4) what a reasonable recipient of the weights would understand the clause to cover. Then tell me which approach should control and why. I care less about the immediate practical outcome than about the line between a program and its data, and whether the distinction "program vs. data" can bear the weight the company is putting on it. Where the matter is genuinely unsettled, say so, and separate what is settled from what is contested among people who know this area.
