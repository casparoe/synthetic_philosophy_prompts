I am writing the take-home final for an upper-level seminar in the history and philosophy of logic, and I want one essay question on the shift from syllogistic to modern, post-Fregean logic. The question should be answerable in 90 minutes, and it should be impossible to answer well by reciting the usual slogans—that Frege invented quantifiers, that the syllogism could not handle relations, or that modern logic is more expressive. I want students to be forced to say precisely what feature of syllogistic form produces those limits, and what in Frege's analysis actually removes them. Please provide: (1) the essay question; (2) a model answer of about 700 words; and (3) a marking scheme out of 25 marks, built around five must-hit moves with the marks each is worth and a note on how candidates typically get each one wrong. The marking scheme should make explicit what separates an A from a B answer.

Question (90 minutes, upper-level seminar):

Assess the claim that the difference between Aristotle's logic and Frege's is mostly one of notation: Aristotle gave a catalog of valid forms, and modern logic merely translates them into a more compact symbolism. To assess it, you must do the following. (i) State precisely what form syllogistic assigns to a proposition, and what kind of inference that form directly supports. (ii) Exhibit the kinds of inference it cannot represent, using at least one genuinely relational example and one example of multiple generality; in each case, explain why the obstacle lies in the form, not in the particular subject matter. (iii) Explain how Frege's function–argument analysis, together with variables and quantifiers, handles those examples. (iv) Say what happens to the traditional Square of Opposition once All S are P and Some S are P are analyzed in the Fregean way, and explain whether the result is a loss or a correction.

Model answer (about 700 words):

A full answer begins by rejecting the notational-upgrade thesis and locating the difference in what each logic takes as its basic unit. Syllogistic takes the term as basic and forces every proposition into a fixed two-term template: a subject and a predicate joined by a copula, marked for quantity and quality. Syllogistic inference then works by comparing terms through a shared middle term—determining inclusion, exclusion, or partial overlap among terms. This is not a mere choice of presentation; it is a restriction built into the form of every proposition the logic recognizes.

The first restriction is relational. Because every categorical proposition contains exactly two terms, a relational verb cannot be represented with its full structure. In a sentence like Achilles is faster than the tortoise, the relation faster than is not an additional term or a property attaching to a single object; it relates two things. Syllogistic can flatten it only by building the relation into a compound predicate, faster than the tortoise, applied to the subject Achilles. That flattening severs the logical connection between the two relata and hides what the relation does. Consider the inference from Every horse is an animal to Every head of a horse is a head of an animal. This inference is plainly valid, but its validity depends on one and the same occurrence of the relation x is a head of y doing double duty—connecting horse to head of a horse and animal to head of an animal. A syllogism cannot represent that shared structure. If head of a horse and head of an animal are treated as self-standing terms, the internal connection between y and head of y disappears, and the inference becomes unavailable to the logic. The problem is not subject matter; it is the fixed arity of the two-term form.

The second restriction is multiple generality. A sentence like Every boy loves some girl is ambiguous: it can mean that for each boy there is some girl whom he loves, or that there is some girl whom every boy loves. These two readings differ in which quantifier has scope over which, and the difference is one of logical structure, not emphasis. Syllogistic has no way to mark the difference. It can only classify the sentence as, say, a universal proposition with a compound predicate loves some girl—but that same classification fits both readings. There are no variables to cross-reference the boy and the girl within a single proposition, and no syntax for nesting one quantifier inside the scope of another.

Frege's analysis removes both restrictions at once, because it changes the analysis of the proposition itself. Instead of beginning with terms joined by a copula, Frege begins with function application. A concept is an unsaturated function that yields a truth-value when completed by an object; a relation is a function with more than one argument. Quantifiers are second-level functions over concepts: the universal quantifier says that a concept maps every object to the True, and the existential quantifier says that it maps at least one object to the True. Variables and binding then allow composition without a fixed template. From a two-place relation, say loves, one forms loves(x, y), then there exists a y such that loves(x, y), then for every x, if x is a boy then there exists a y such that y is a girl and loves(x, y). Because the quantifiers nest, the two readings of Every boy loves some girl become distinct formulas; because relations retain their argument places, the head-of inference can be represented as an inference about the relation x is a head of y, and the step from the premise to the conclusion can be carried out explicitly.

Frege's analysis also separates two things syllogistic merges. Socrates is mortal has the form of an object's falling under a first-level concept. All humans are mortal has the form of a relation between concepts, expressed with a universal quantifier over a conditional. Syllogistic, by contrast, must force both sentences into the same two-term subject–predicate mold, which is why singular propositions sit awkwardly in the tradition.

This yields the fourth point, about the Square of Opposition. On the Fregean reading, All S are P is a quantified conditional: it says of everything that if it is S then it is P. It is therefore true whenever there are no S. But Some S are P is an existential conjunction: it says there is something that is both S and P. Consequently subalternation fails—all unicorns are white does not imply that some unicorns are white. Contrariety fails as well: All S are P and No S are P can both be true when there are no S. And I and O are no longer subcontraries, since both can be false when S is empty. Only the two contradictory pairs survive without an additional premise that S is nonempty. This is not a loss but a clarification: it separates the conditional content of a universal from the existential content of a particular, and it makes visible an assumption the old Square had to build in silently. The traditional Square can be recovered by explicitly conjoining the universal with the claim that some S exists.

The bottom line is that the gap is philosophical, not typographical. Syllogistic fixes the shape of every proposition in advance; Frege lets propositional form be generated by the recursive application of functions, with quantifiers as higher-level functions over concepts. That is what buys relational and multiply general inference, and it is also why the old Square's existential commitments become explicit and revisable.

Marking scheme (25 marks total):

1. Precise characterization of the syllogistic form (5 marks). The answer must state that a categorical proposition is a two-term subject–predicate structure marked for quantity and quality, and that inference proceeds by term comparison through a middle term. It must connect this to the thesis being assessed, not merely call syllogistic less expressive. Typical error: dismissing syllogistic as old or cumbersome without isolating the fixed two-term template as the source of the limits.

2. Relational limitation shown through a working example (5 marks). The candidate must use a genuinely relational inference, such as the head-of-a-horse example, and explain why the relation cannot be reified into a compound term without destroying the logical connection the inference needs. Typical error: citing John loves Mary and saying that relations are missing, without exhibiting an inference that is valid but syllogistically blocked.

3. Multiple generality and scope (5 marks). The candidate must give the two readings of a sentence such as Every boy loves some girl and explain why syllogistic cannot distinguish them: there are no bound variables and no mechanism for nesting one quantifier inside another. Typical error: giving only one reading, or treating some girl as if it were a name, or claiming the ambiguity is merely stylistic.

4. Accurate account of Frege's machinery (5 marks). The candidate must explain function–argument analysis, concepts as functions to truth-values, relations as multi-argument functions, and quantifiers as second-level functions, and must show how variables and binding compose a nested formula. Typical error: saying only that Frege introduced quantifiers and variables, without connecting them to function application and composition, or treating the quantifier as an abbreviation over a list of terms.

5. The Square of Opposition and existential import (5 marks). The candidate must explain the conditional reading of All S are P and the existential-conjunction reading of Some S are P, identify which Square relations fail without nonempty S (subalternation, contrariety, subcontrariety) and which survive (contradiction), and explain why this separates quantity from existence. This point should also note the contrast between singular propositions and general ones. Typical error: saying that modern logic rejects the Square, without specifying which relations break and what extra assumption would restore them, or wrongly claiming that the Fregean universal still implies existence.

Band descriptors: An A answer scores 20–25 and makes all five moves under a single diagnosis—that the fixed arity of the subject–predicate form explains every limit—and treats the new notation as downstream of a change in the analysis of the proposition. A B answer scores 15–19 and makes most of the moves, but presents them as a list of defects and under-explains function-argument analysis. A C answer scores 10–14 and shows familiarity with the vocabulary—quantifier, relation, existential import—but cannot deploy it to exhibit a syllogistically blocked inference or to show how Fregean composition works. Below 10, the answer is a historical summary that asserts the old logic was limited without diagnosing where the limitation came from.
