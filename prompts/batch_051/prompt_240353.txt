My sister lives in a tourist-heavy city and has way too many opinions about whether visitors have to follow local everyday conventions, and I genuinely can't tell what principle she's actually running. Her track record, as far as I've reconstructed it from years of arguments: she thinks jaywalking is fine on an empty street in a town where everyone crosses against the light, even at 3am, but says it's wrong to jaywalk across a busy road in a city where pedestrians reliably wait for the signal, because you're startling drivers and making them brake. She says a tourist who skips the tip at an American diner where servers make a tipped wage is doing something wrong, even if the tourist comes from a no-tipping country and had no idea about the custom. But she also says not tipping in Japan is perfectly fine, and that insisting on tipping there would actually be obnoxious. She says you have to join the back of an orderly queue at a packed London bus stop, but she also says there's nothing wrong with the shoving scrum at a stop where nobody queues and the bus just opens its doors. She says a guest should take their shoes off in a home that has a obvious shoe rack and a no-shoes rule at the entrance, even if nobody mentions it.

Here's the case we're now arguing about. An almost empty London Underground station at six in the morning: there's an escalator, nobody behind you, nobody coming up the other side, and you stand on the left, the side everyone walks on. Assuming she'd have some verdict, what do you think she says, and why? I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in the rule it reveals. What are the plausible rules that fit everything she's already said, what would each one predict about the empty-escalator case, which earlier case is the closest analog, and what's the one further case you'd engineer to cleanly split the rules she might be running?
