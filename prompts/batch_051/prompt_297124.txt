I'm taking a philosophy of probability course right now and I've basically convinced myself that the principle of indifference is the right way to handle complete ignorance: if you've got n mutually exclusive and exhaustive possibilities and no evidence that distinguishes any of them, assign 1/n to each. I know Bertrand's paradox is the obvious objection — the random chords thing where you get 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4 depending on how you set up the randomization — but I've been dismissing that as a problem about underspecified randomization procedures rather than about the principle itself. My professor made me less sure, so I want an honest map of where the position is actually exposed.

Think of yourself as an expert in formal epistemology and the foundations of Bayesian probability, and take your time — maybe brainstorm the main lines of attack first and then organize them. What I want is: what would have to be true, or what cases, arguments, or formal results, would count against the principle of indifference? A few things I specifically want covered:

- Sorting: which objections are fatal, which are just awkward, and which are more about presentation than substance? I don't just want a list of criticisms; I want to know how decisive each is.
- The reparameterization problem. If a uniform prior over a length and a uniform prior over the same quantity squared (or inverted) give different answers, it looks like the principle doesn't just fail to give a unique answer — it actively contradicts itself depending on how you describe the variable. Is that a genuine contradiction, and can the move to Jeffreys-ish 'uninformative' priors or symmetry-based rules actually fix it, or is that just relabeling the problem?
- The partition problem: any situation where you can split the possibility space in two genuinely different but equally natural ways and get different probabilities out. Does a defender of the principle owe us a non-circular account of which partitions are 'natural' or 'basic'? What would that account even look like?
- Continuouos spaces. I've started to suspect the principle only makes sense for finite lottery-style cases and breaks down for continuous quantities. Is that fair, and if so is it still useful in the finite cases, or does the continuous failure undermine it everywhere?
- What would a committed objective Bayesian — someone who still wants an indifference-style rule — themselves concede counts against the view, as opposed to what they'd dismiss as demanding too much? I want to know which escape routes are internal fixes and which are real concessions.
- In the end: has the principle been patched and hedged so much that it's unfalsifiable? And if it has, is that actually bad for a principle of this kind, or just appropriate for a normative constraint on reasoning?

I'd rather you reason this out than cite papers at me — I care about the arguments and the cases themselves. And if the honest conclusion is that the view is in worse (or better) shape than I think, just say so.
