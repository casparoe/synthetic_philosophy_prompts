I've ended my philosophy of cognitive science seminar with the imagery debate for years now, and every time I reread the exchanges I end up with a different diagnosis of why its stuck. The strange thing is how much the two sides agree on. Kosslyn and Pylyshyn both accept the scanning results—people take longer to scan across larger distances on a memorized map—and the rotation results, where response time tracks angular disparity, and the neuroimaging stuff showing early visual cortex lighting up during imagery. Kosslyn says the natural explanation is that the underlying representations are depictive: they have something like spatial extent, and the mind inspects them the way it inspects a picture. Pylyshyn says all of that can be handled by a discursive, propositional code plus the subject's tacit knowledge of what their own visual experience would be like; the picture is an effect of the task, not the format of the representation. So both sides accept the data and then diverge completely on what the data are data *for*. That's made me suspect the impasse isn't really an empirical one, or at least not empirical in the way the participants assumed. But I'd like a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute over what gets to count as "representational format"? A difference about what an explanation of a cognitive capacity has to do—does it have to specify a format, or can it stop once it's told you the content? A hidden empirical question that has just been hard to isolate? Or two different questions running under one name, "what is imagery?" And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question—or should we conclude the decades-long standoff is a sign the question was ill-posed and stop expecting movement? I keep assigning this debate because I can't tell whether it's a real argument or a pair of ships passing in the night, and I'd rather know which.
