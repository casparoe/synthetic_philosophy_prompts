Someone in our group chat posted a news story about a garment factory outside Dhaka paying the equivalent of $3.15 a day for twelve-hour shifts, and two of my friends, Maya and Jon, went back and forth about it for an hour. I saved the transcript because I wanted to think it through for an ethics class, but I can't sort out where the real disagreement is. Here's most of it.

Maya: That's exploitation, full stop.

Jon: The workers lined up for those jobs. Their next best option pays under a dollar a day. If the factory closed they'd be worse off, so you can't be wronging them by making them better off than their alternative.

Maya: You absolutely can. Taking advantage of someone's terrible alternatives is exactly what exploitation means. Making someone better off doesn't make the transaction fair.

Jon: Exploitation needs force, fraud, or harm. This is a voluntary exchange with no deception. You're basically just saying you don't like the price.

Maya: You're asserting the non-worseness claim: a voluntary transaction that makes both parties better off than no transaction can't be morally worse than no transaction. That claim is false on its face. Consider a rescuer who says "I'll pull you out of the well for your entire life savings." That's better than the alternative and obviously wrong to demand.

Jon: The rescuer case isn't about labor. The rescuer has a duty to aid and a monopoly over an immediate life-or-death need. The factory didn't cause the workers' poverty, it isn't their only employer, and the workers aren't in an emergency. You're comparing ordinary labor to duress.

Maya: You don't need to have caused the background injustice to benefit from it. The factory is profiting precisely because the workers' outside options are awful, and the owners know that. Benefiting from an injustice you could partially offset, and choosing not to, is a form of participation in it.

Jon: If the test is "could pay more", then every employer everywhere is guilty, because wages always reflect bargaining power. Your standard has no stopping point, which means "exploitation" just means "a wage I disapprove of."

Maya: It's a continuum, and this factory is far along it; that's not a reductio. And you've changed the subject: whether it's exploitative is one question, whether banning it would help the workers is another. I can think it's wrong while also thinking we shouldn't just make it illegal, because the employer could raise wages, cut hours, or let workers unionize.

Jon: If the employer pays above the local rate, they'll automate or hire fewer people. You'd leave some of the workers with their second-best option — that's worse, and it's the actual result of your view.

Maya: That's an empirical bet, and it treats job count as the only thing that matters. If the only way to help these workers is for the terms to stay just bad enough, I'd say the whole arrangement is less of a triumph than you think.

I keep flip-flopping on this. Can you adjudicate the specific moves? I want a scorecard, not a single winner: for each exchange, say who is right and why, where a term is being used in two different ways, and what both of them are leaving out. Especially, keep seperate the questions whether the deal is exploitative, whether it's wrong, whether the law should ban it or consumers should boycott it, and what an individual factory owner ought to do.
