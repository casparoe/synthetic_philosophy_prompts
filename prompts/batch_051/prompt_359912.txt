I'm TA-ing a philosophy of law section and the professor has me grading short-answer questions on the Nuremberg and Tokyo trials. Here's the question as written, and below it one student's answer. Give me a grade (letter, points out of 10, or both) and the point-by-point comments that justify it. I want to make sure my comments track the answer's actual strengths and weaknesses rather than just its general feel.

Question: "Explain the ex post facto objection to the Nuremberg and Tokyo war crimes trials, and assess two standard replies to it. Does the objection apply with equal force to all three categories of crime in the London Charter? Is the 'victors' justice' objection merely the retroactivity objection restated, or is it a separate complaint?"

Student answer: "The ex post facto objection is that the Allies convicted people under standards that were not binding criminal law at the time the acts were committed, and that violates nulla poena sine lege. The objection bites hardest for crimes against peace, since waging aggressive war had never before been treated as an individual crime. The Kellogg-Briand Pact renounced war as an instrument of national policy, but it bound states rather than individuals and had no penal provisions. Still, because states can act only through officials, the Pact must be read as criminalizing the individual decision to go to war; otherwise the Pact would have been pointless. Two replies are standard. The first is a natural-law reply: acts this egregious cannot claim the protection of legality, and that is basically what the tribunal meant when it said nullum crimen is a principle of justice, not an absolute limit on sovereignty. The second reply is usually called the positivist one: the Nazi authorization statutes were so iniquitous that they were never law at all, so there is nothing retroactive about applying the ordinary criminal law that was always in force. The objection is much weaker for war crimes and crimes against humanity, because murder, enslavement, and attacking civilians were already criminal under the laws of war and ordinary municipal law; only the international forum was new. It is most serious for crimes against peace. The victors' justice objection, by contrast, is separate: even if the rules were fine, only the defeated were tried, the victorious powers wrote the charter and supplied the judges, and the Tokyo trial is the sharper case of this, as Pal's dissent shows. So the two objections can come apart."
