I can't stop thinking about something since my sister cut off my dad. He wasn't abusive, just cold and selfish and exhausting for thirty years, and when she finally said "I'm done" I told her I was proud of her. But then I realized I've said the same thing to friends about draining friends and never once treated it as a big moral event. The asymmetry: when a friendship becomes all cost and no care, leaving is just what happens; when the person is a parent, leaving gets treated as this huge, weighty, last-resort decision, and "he's still your father" is supposed to count even when the actual relationship is dead. That's backwards on its face, because the friendship was chosen and the parent-child tie wasn't — you'd think the chosen relationship would be the one with more strings. There's a view I keep coming back to that says grown children owe their parents whatever they'd owe a friend: not a debt for being raised, just whatever the current relationship actually supports. If that's right, the thing needing defense isn't the estrangement, it's the "still your father" move. So is there a principle that makes the unchosen tie more binding then the chosen one, or is this just kin-bias dressed up as principle? I want the strongest case for both sides, tested on the hard cases.
