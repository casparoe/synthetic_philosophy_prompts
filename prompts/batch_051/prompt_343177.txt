I'm a novelist, and this may sound like a craft question but its become a philosophy question for me. There's a view running through Stoicism and Buddhism (and parts of Epicureanism) that the good life is freedom from disturbance — ataraxia, apatheia, equanimity. The theory behind it is elegant: suffering doesn't come from events themselves but from the judgments I add to them, the implicit belief that things outside my control — money, reputation, even other people staying alive — are genuinely good or bad. The only thing properly up to me is how I judge and respond. So if I stop assenting to the thought "this is a terrible loss," the loss stops being terrible to me. As a method it has exactly the virtues I usually want in a theory: it's simple, it unifies a huge range of emotional pain under one mechanism, and it gives a decisive procedure for any crisis — find the judgment, suspend it, return to calm. I'm honestly drawn to it.

But I have an intuition I can't shake: some disturbances are not noise, they're part of what it means to love and to see clearly. A parent who loses a child and regards it with full serenity doesn't strike me as having mastered something; they strike me as having lost something, as though the absence of grief were itself a defect. And as a novelist, the whole craft depends on treating the fact that I'm disturbed by imagined people as a feature, not a bug. If I actually achieved full apatheia I'm not sure I'd want to write another novel, or that I'd love anyone in the way I mean by love.

I don't want a pep talk for one side. Give me the strongest version of each: the case that my intuition is just my attachment talking — exactly what the theory predicts I'd feel from inside — and the case that the theory's ideal of freedom is a kind of flattening, even an evasion of the part of ethical life that matters. Is there a reading on which equanimity and the fittingness of grief or moral anger are compatible — say, by separating first-order disturbance from second-order turbulence, or by narrowing apatheia to something like freedom from self-inflicted rumination — or do I actually have to choose? What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, and what would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes? Which should yield, and what would justify each move?
