I am giving a philosophy department colloquium next month on why historical analogies mislead, and I want to prepare for the question period rather than only the talk itself. My thesis is that the Munich and Vietnam analogies do not fail primarily because the cases are disanalogous; they fail because they convert a vivid precedent into a tacit estimate of how often certain kinds of crisis end badly, and the historical record cannot support such a frequency claim. I argue that analogies earn their keep as hypothesis-generating devices but not as evidential warrants, and that the Thucydides trap is the hardest case for my position because it seems to supply a mechanism—structural fear between a rising and a ruling power—rather than a bare narrative. The audience is a mixed humanities colloquium: roughly half analytic philosophers of science and epistemologists, a quarter intellectual historians, and the rest political theorists. The epistemologists will want the logic of analogy formalized, the philosophers of science will resist any sharp distinction between lawlike and non-lawlike inference, the historians will distrust treating history as a sample of cases, and the political theorists will demand to know what a decision-maker should do instead under urgency. Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections in the order they are likely to arise and in the vocabulary each group would actually use, how damaging each objection is, a two-minute reply for each, where that reply is softest, what I should concede up front, and the question I most hope nobody asks.
