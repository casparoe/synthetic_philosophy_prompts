I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, and I keep thinking about metacognition through the profiler/debugger analogy: thinking about your own thinking is like running a profiler on yourself from the inside — you instrument the hot paths, see where the cycles are going, and refactor. That's led me to assume metacognition is almost always useful, maybe just with some runtime overhead. But I keep tripping over cases where it backfires: the centipede that stumbles once it thinks about its legs, the guitarist who starts noticing their fingerings mid-solo and fumbles, someone spiraling in rumination about whether they're thinking clearly at all. I want an audit of how far the analogy actually goes. Show what maps to what (a table is fine), where the mapping breaks, and which of those breaks actually matter for the conclusion that metacognition is generally helpful. I'm especially curious what the analogy quietly imports — a separate observer process that doesn't alter the code, access to full state, a known optimization target, the idea that the "program text" stays the same whether or not you instrument it. Then say whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether there's a better software analogy or whether this metaphor just gives out. American English, and no need for citations.
