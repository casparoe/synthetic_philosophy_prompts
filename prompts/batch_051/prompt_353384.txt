I've been down a rabbit hole about dispositions lately, becuase of a mildly annoying personal thing: I get palpitations that never show up when I'm actually at the cardiologist, so I got curious about why something can be "there" and yet never manifest under the very conditions that should trigger it. Anyway, that led me to finks and masks, and now I have the skeleton of a short essay I want to submit to a philosophy blog. I'd love comments before I commit to writing it out — mainly on whether I'm getting the standard distinction right and whether the main idea is original enough to be worth 2500 words.

Working title: Finks, masks, and where we draw the line.

The setup I'm working with: a disposition is a stimulus–manifestation pair, like fragility is struck → breaks. A fink is a disposition that vanishes exactly when the stimulus arrives — the classic glass that's fragile except that a guardian would render it unbreakable at the instant of impact. A mask is different: the disposition stays put, but something blocks the manifestation — the fragile glass packed in foam, or a poison that's still poisonous but the antidote stops it doing its thing.

My hunch is that the difference between a fink and a mask is less about the case itself and more about how we describe the stimulus and where we draw the boundary between the bearer and its environment. The bubble-wrap glass: if I describe the stimulus narrowly (struck by a hard object, not foam), the masked case kinda evaporates, because the precise stimulus never actually occurs. If I describe the disposition broadly enough to include the interferer as part of the bearer, then a fink starts looking like an internal mask. The clearest case I've found is a heater on a thermostat: ask whether it's disposed to overheat the room, and I can tell the story as a fink (the thermostat cuts the current, so the stimulus is removed right when it would cause trouble), as a mask (the heater keeps its disposition, the thermostat blocks the manifestation), or as no disposition at all (the heater-thermostat system as a whole never had it). Same circuit, three categories.

So the thesis I'm leaning toward: fink and mask aren't two natural kinds of interference; they're two ways of slicing up one situation, and the slices are in us, not in the wiring (or the glass, or the poison). My worry is that someone will object that a fink's interferer acts on the disposition itself while a mask's interferer acts only on the manifestation, and that this is a real, principled line. Is it? I can't tell whether that line is doing work or just restating the labels.

So, comments appreciated on three things: (1) Have I mischaracterized the standard fink/mask distinction in a way that would embarrass me publicly? (2) Does the thermostat example actually show what I want it to show, or is it just ambiguous in a boring way? (3) What's the strongest version of the objection that there's a principled fink/mask line, and can I answer it without the reply becoming question-begging? I'd rather hear this now than after I've drafted the thing.
