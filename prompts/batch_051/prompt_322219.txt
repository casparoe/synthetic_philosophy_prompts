I've been trying to break the stochastic parrots impasse by writing down the conditions under which I would actually say that a language model understands the words it produces, and then constructing a case that sits exactly on the boundary. My working list is:

(1) Grounding. Some of the system's basic symbols must be connected to their referents through channels other than further symbols — perception, action, or their traces — not just through definitions and co-occurrence statistics.
(2) Systematicity. The system must cope with novel combinations: parse sentences it has never seen and produce sentences it has never seen in ways that track the structure of what the sentences are about.
(3) Inferential responsiveness. The system must normally accept the immediate entailments of what it has just said, refuse pairs it recognizes as contradictory, and keep track of the difference between asserting a claim and merely mentioning or translating it.
(4) Truth-sensitivity. The system's disposition to produce a sentence should be sensitive, in ordinary cases, to whether that sentence is true. Concretely: when corrected on a false claim, it should change not just the stored item but the underlying pattern, and its errors should mostly trace to missing information rather than to indifference between P and not-P.

Now the case. Consider a system trained only by next-token prediction over a textual corpus: no vision, no audition, no motor channel, and no post-training rewards tied to facts in the world. In testing, it learns a novel term from a single definitional sentence and then uses it correctly in genuinely new combinations; it distinguishes entailments from mere co-occurrence; it handles counterfactual questions of the form whether a sentence would still be true if a named feature of the situation had been different; and when told a past answer was false, it revises the general way it uses the relevant words rather than memorizing the item. Mechanistically, though, it is doing nothing but sampling from a conditional distribution over text.

Its only contact with the world beyond text is derivative: the corpus was produced by speakers whose own words were grounded in perception and action. So whether condition (1) is met depends on whether grounding can be inherited through the training distribution, and whether conditions (2) through (4) are met depends on whether the right kind of mechanism matters or only the behavior.

Does this system understand the language it uses? Work through the four conditions one by one, and flag any condition that underdetermines the answer. Then tell me whether the case shows that grounding should be dropped, that the behavioral conditions need a mechanism clause, or that my list is really tracking two different questions. I would prefer to keep grounding, but I am not sure it survives contact with this case.
