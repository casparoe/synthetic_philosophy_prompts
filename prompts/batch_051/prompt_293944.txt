You are a philosopher of language who specializes in the status of sign languages. Think carefully and reason step by step; before committing yourself, brainstorm two or three principles that could underlie the data.

A linguist I know has been classifying communication systems as languages or not for years. Her running record: American Sign Language and British Sign Language, each a language and distinct from the other; Nicaraguan Sign Language, which emerged within a single school generation, a language; written English, not a separate language but only a notation for English; the honeybee waggle dance, not a language; an isolated deaf child's home sign — an idiosyncratic gesture system with no signing community — not a language; a pidgin with no native speakers, not a language; and a creole descended from a pidgin, with native speakers and full grammar, a language.

A new case has come up: a monastery with a centuries-old vow of silence uses a signed system with several thousand conventional signs, productive compounding, and flexible sign order. It is deliberately taught to novices, used for liturgy and for the full range of everyday communication, and has never been anyone's first language.

What should I expect her to say about the monastic signed system if she wants to stay consistent with her record? Identify the candidate principles that fit her earlier verdicts, say which earlier cases are the closest analogs, and state what principle she would be adopting with each possible answer; say where the verdicts genuinely underdetermine the new case. Give a best guess about her verdict, with reasons. Finally, specify one further case that would cleanly separate the candidate principles, and explain why it works.
