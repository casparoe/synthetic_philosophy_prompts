Since a bad fever last winter left me with several weeks of depersonalization—thoughts arriving with no felt owner, a running commentary but nobody behind it—I have come back to Descartes with a deflationary conviction I cannot shake. I now argue in my reading group that the cogito entitles us to far less than is standardly claimed: at most, that thinking is now going on, not that there exists a thinker, not that this thinker persists across time, and certainly not that there is a thinking substance. The claim that a first-person existence is secured by the very act of doubting seems to me to license only a thin conclusion—something like a bare occurrence of thought—and everything Descartes builds on top of it (an enduring thinking thing, the indivisibility of the mind, the distinctness of mind and body) looks like equipment imported after the fact rather than delivered by the argument. I know this is not the orthodox reading, and I know there are sophisticated defenses of a stronger conclusion that treat the cogito as a performance rather than as an inference.

I want you to play the role of a professional philosopher with expertise in Descartes and early modern philosophy, and to be candid with me rather than encouraging. Please think slowly and carefully. Begin by steelmanning my deflationary reading as rigorously as you can, so that I can see you have understood it before you criticize it. Then give me a systematic map of what would count against it: what would have to be true—about self-consciousness, about the structure of thought, about Descartes' own project—for the cogito to prove a thinker and not merely thinking? Which considerations would be decisive against my position, which would merely be awkward, and which of my available replies (I suspect I would retreat to reading the word thinking as ontologically neutral) are live options rather than ad hoc patches? I also want to know what a committed defender of my deflationary reading would themselves concede as counting against it, not only what the orthodox side says. To sharpen the assessment, I will say what I would personally find most persuasive: an argument showing that my reading covertly presupposes the very unified subject it claims to eliminate. But I am prepared to be told that my view is too insulated to be interesting.
