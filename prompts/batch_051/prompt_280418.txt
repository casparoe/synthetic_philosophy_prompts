I'm on the patient-safety review panel at my hospital, and I've ended up drafting next week's decision memo on a case that has us all arguing about what it even is for an omission to cause an injury. Our policy currently says an omission counts as a cause of a serious incident only if the injury probably would not have happened had the omission not occurred — a but-for test, basically. The case is this. A man with dementia got out of bed around 3 a.m. on a ward that's supposed to be locked down overnight. There are two sets of doors on opposite ends of the unit, each with an assigned night aide responsible for locking them; both forgot. He wandered out the east doors and fell down a stairwell, badly hurting himself. Here's the twist: he'd been found wandering toward the west doors on several previous nights, so pretty much everyone on the panel agrees that if the east doors had been locked, he probably would have kept wandering and gone out the west doors instead. That means under a strict but-for reading, neither unlocked door caused the fall — take away the east-door omission and the injury still happens, take away the west-door omission and it still happens. And then there's a third employee, a nurse who saw him shuffling toward the east doors a couple minutes before he got out, decided he was just pacing, and said nothing. If she'd redirected him or called it in, the fall almost certainly wouldn't have happened, so her omission is a clean but-for cause.

The panel has split into camps. One camp says the policy is too crude: the two door omissions were each part of a set of failures that, taken together, guaranteed the injury even though neither one was necessary on its own, so we should revise the policy to say an omission counts if it's part of a set of actual deficiencies that jointly made the bad outcome all but inevitable. Another camp says no, the but-for test is doing exactly what its supposed to do, and we only dislike the result because two people were careless; omissions are absences, and once you start calling a non-necessary absence a cause you have no principled place to stop (why not the staffing office that didn't add a third aide, why not the architect who put the stairwell there). A third camp wants to record the nurse's omission as the only cause, flag the door omissions as non-causal process failures, and not touch the policy at all.

What I need is a short memo the panel can actually circulate and vote on. It should make a concrete recommendation — apply the but-for test and record only the nurse, or revise the policy and count the door omissions too, or some third option — with reasons, and a genuinely fair statement of the strongest consideration on the side you're recommending against. The memo has to name whose omissions get recorded as causes in this case, and it has to be something our hospital could actually adopt, not just a survey of positions.
