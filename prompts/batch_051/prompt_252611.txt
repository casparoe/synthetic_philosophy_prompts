I'm putting together a syllabus section on the paradox of tragedy and I've been collecting the explanations people give for why we seek out art that makes us sad, scared, or miserable. The ones I already have are: (1) catharsis — tragedy purges or discharges pity and fear and leaves us relieved; (2) Hume's conversion story — the eloquence and artistry convert the painful passions into pleasure; (3) psychical distance — the aesthetic frame strips the emotions of their practical stakes so we can enjoy them; (4) the control account — we enjoy negative emotions in fiction because we know we can stop, leave, or redirect attention, so the threat is defused; (5) Feagin's meta-response view — the pleasure is not in the sadness itself but in our satisfaction at being the sort of person who responds with compassion; (6) benign masochism — in a safe context we enjoy the frisson of a negative response the way people enjoy chili heat or a roller-coaster drop; (7) the make-believe dissolution — we don't literally feel sadness for fictional characters, we only make-believe that we do, so the paradox evaporates; and (8) the rich-experience/compensation view — the sadness is compensated by the insight, beauty, or meaningfulness of the work, and we value the whole experience including the sad parts.

I'm fairly sure these are the standard moves, and I don't want a restatement of one of them or a blend of two. Is there another explanation for why we enjoy sad art that's genuinely differnt from all eight? If you think there is, give it, say what it explains that the eight don't, and tell me honestly whether it's any good. If you think the eight really do exhaust the space, say so and defend that instead.

Whatever you conclude, put the bottom line at the very end as \answer{...}: a short label for the new account if you give one, or \answer{none} if you argue the list is exhaustive.
