I am planning an essay and would welcome comments on the idea before I commit to writing it; I work as a software engineer, so please treat me as an outsider to philosophy who needs precise but patient criticism.

The idea grew out of an analogy that felt natural to me and then started to seem strained in an instructive way. People sometimes describe governance as a kind of software running on a population, with emigration, charter cities, and seasteading treated as versions of a user's right to stop using one service and switch to a competitor. The part of that analogy I want to examine is forking. In open-source software, when a project's maintainers make bad decisions, contributors and users can fork the codebase and run the fork on the same generic infrastructure: the same hardware, the same internet, the same compilers and runtimes. The substrate is cheap, copyable, and fungible, which is a large part of why exit is realistic and why forking disciplines maintainers. In the case of states, the substrate is different: dry land under a given jurisdiction is scarce, non-copyable, and not fungible, and its location relative to ports, markets, and infrastructure matters enormously. There is no `git clone` for territory.

My working thesis is this: charter cities and seasteading are best understood not primarily as legal innovations but as attempts to make the substrate of governance forkable. A charter city does this by leasing a slice of an existing state's territory under a semi-autonomous rule set, which is a fork that still runs on the parent project's hardware. Seasteading does it by manufacturing new substrate, buildable ocean surface, so that exit no longer requires persuading an existing state to surrender land. The deeper obstacle to opting out of the state, on this view, is not that consent to a state is conceptually confused, but that the physical resource on which any jurisdiction runs cannot be copied the way a repository can.

I would be grateful for comments on three points:

1. Whether the fork analogy actually tracks how charter cities and seasteading work, or whether it quietly imports a false picture of consent and exit. Most people never choose their first "host system" in any meaningful sense, and the costs of leaving a state are not merely technical or transactional. I suspect the analogy may overstate how much agency exit involves.

2. Where an essay built on this analogy would be weakest as seen by someone trained in political philosophy. I am probably conflating distinctions I barely know, such as legitimacy versus justice, or negative versus positive liberty, and I would rather be told now than after writing the piece.

3. Whether the central claim is strong enough to carry a moderate-length essay. If the fork/substrate framing is merely a vivid way of restating that scarce land constrains political exit, I am not sure it earns its keep; if it reveals something non-obvious about why exit is hard, I would want to know where the novelty actually lives.

I am less interested in being pointed to a literature than in whether the argument itself is sound and where it bends or breaks, so please do not worry about giving me references; say plainly where the reasoning goes wrong.
