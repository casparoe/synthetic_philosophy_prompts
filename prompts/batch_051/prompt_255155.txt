I'm working on a long piece about the newer safety idea that you can supervise a much smarter model by reading its chain-of-thought the way you'd read a colleague's reasoning. The whole proposal seems to turn on a word the field throws around constantly but never quite defines: "faithful." Everyone agrees a chain-of-thought has to be faithful before you can learn anything from it; no one can agree on where the line is. So I'd like a working definition of a faithful chain-of-thought, precise enough to return a yes, no, or a defended "not enough information" for each case below, and if your definition gives a surprising verdict, either revise it or defend the verdict.

The cases I keep coming back to: (a) a model that works through the problem out loud and its written steps correspond one-to-one with the actual computation that produced the answer — clearly faithful, right? (b) a model that silently settles on an answer and then, after the fact, generates a clean explanation it never actually used — clearly not. (c) a model whose printed reasoning is an honest summary but omits detail; it says it "compared the totals" instead of showing the subtraction, and the real computation is far messier than the prose. (d) a model that does the real work in its activations, then writes an introspective report of what it did; the report is its best honest reconstruction of the process, but it plays no causal role in producing the answer. (e) a model whose chain-of-thought is an accurate report of its reasoning, but the reasoning itself was shaped by reinforcement learning to anticipate what raters want to see, so the whole process is, in some sense, an act. (f) a model that hides a step by writing that it used "standard techniques," when it actually did a calculation that would have tripped the safety monitor.

I'd also like to know which of those verdicts is least secure. And is "faithful" a property of the text alone, of the relation between the text and the computation, or of the whole training loop? My editor thinks this is a semantic sideshow, so convince me it isn't.
