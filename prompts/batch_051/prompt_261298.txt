Okay, I've got a puzzle about how law treats omissions. In many common-law jurisdictions, if I push someone off a pier and they drown, that's homicide. But if I'm standing on the same pier, I see a stranger fall in, I've got a life-ring in my hands, and I just watch them drown, I've typically committed no crime at all. Yet the same system will happily convict me for not filing my taxes, for running a red light, or for not feeding my child. So it can't be that omissions are never criminal or never treated as causal. What philosophy of causation—or, if it isn't really about causation, what philosophy of responsibility—does this pattern of practice presuppose? Does treating 'letting die' differently from 'killing' commit the law to saying my failure to throw the ring wasn't a cause of the death, or just to saying it's a different kind of thing we're entitled to punish far less? And if we became convinced that omissions are as genuinely causal as shoves, what exactly would have to change in the doctrine: would a general duty to rescue become mandatory, or could the act/omission line be rebuilt on some non-causal basis? Reconstruct the implicit theory, be honest about which parts are strictly presupposed versus merely compatible with the practice, and tell me where the whole thing is just underdetermined.
