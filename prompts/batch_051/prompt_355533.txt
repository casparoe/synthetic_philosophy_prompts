I'm a software engineer on a team building an AI assistant for customer service. The product has an unusual constraint: for a designated class of outputs, the assistant may either state a proposition unhedged, as established fact, or not state it at all; there is no hedging mode. Every such statement must be approved by a small internal review board before launch, and the board's past rulings are treated as precedents. I have no formal philosophy background, but I need to turn the board's decisions into an enforceable policy in our assertion pipeline, so I care about the underlying principle more than the one-off verdict.

Dana chairs the board. She isn't a philosopher by training, but she has a consistent feel for when an unhedged assertion is acceptable. Here is the decision log so far.

Case 1. The assistant says, "Your payment failed," based on a live, synchronous failure code from the payment processor, confirmed in our own transaction log. The payment had in fact failed. Verdict: permitted.

Case 2. The assistant says, "You'll be in the first delivery window tomorrow," based on a route-classification model that is 93% accurate on historical deliveries. In this instance the prediction was wrong. Verdict: not permitted.

Case 3. The assistant says, "Your card was charged twice on March 4," based on two independently maintained records that matched exactly and are written under strict integrity controls. The statement was true. Verdict: permitted.

Case 4. The assistant says, "Your order has shipped," based on a cache that had failed to invalidate after a fulfillment update. The order had not shipped. Verdict: not permitted.

Now a new case is before us.

Case 5. The assistant wants to say, "You did not win the sweepstakes." The drawing took place last week, so the matter is settled, but our copy of the winner list has not synced and the release decision must be made today. The only information available is that there were 1,000,000 entries, there was exactly one winner, and this user had one entry; we can treat it as stipulated that, as it happens, the user did not win. Should the assistant be permitted to emit the unhedged sentence on that basis alone?

Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in epistemology and the norms of assertion. Think carefully, and before applying any rule, brainstorm the plausible general principles that could explain Dana's four earlier verdicts. I've heard labels such as knowledge norm, justified-belief norm, belief norm, and truth norm used in these debates, though only vaguely; map those onto the cases if they matter. Then:

- Identify which candidate principles are jointly consistent with the four earlier verdicts.
- Say what each serious candidate would predict about Case 5.
- Recommend the verdict that best coheres with the decision log, giving your best guess about which principle Dana is implicitly running. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the new case, say so clearly and explain which principle we would be adopting under each choice.
- Identify the closest analog among the four earlier cases and defend the comparison.
- Propose one further case that would most cleanly separate the candidate principles that remain live after Case 5.
