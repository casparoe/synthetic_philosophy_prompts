My school is considering changing the way it gives its community-service award, and I want the proposed rule analyzed as a mechanism rather than as a statement of good intentions. Under the proposal, the award would no longer go to the student whose volunteering had the largest or best-publicized effect. Each candidate would instead submit a private deliberation record: a note, ideally written before or during the project, laying out the reasons for which they acted, including motives they considered and rejected. A faculty panel would then identify, independently, which features of the action actually made it right (for example, that it met an urgent need, kept a promise, or respected someone's dignity). The candidate's score would be based only on the overlap between their stated motivating reasons and the panel's list of right-making features, with a point subtracted for each self-interested reason the panel finds in the record (college applications, popularity, winning the award itself). Outcomes would matter only as evidence for reconstructing what the student could have foreseen when they acted. A student who did the right thing for the wrong reasons would score zero, while a student who did something modest for the panel's right reasons could win. What would this select for, and how would students change their volunteering and their self-descriptions in response? Where does it get gamed first: coached records, choices made because their right-making features are legible to a panel, students with clean self-narratives winning over students with honest mixed motives? What happens when the panel's list of right-making features is contested or wrong? Does the scheme measure moral worth, or does it measure the ability to produce a certain kind of self-description that flatters the panel's morality? And could any patch, such as ignoring self-interested motives, accepting only contemporaneous records, or keeping the panel's criteria secret, fix the failures without creating worse ones?
