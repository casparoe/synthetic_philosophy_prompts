I cover language and ideas for a publication, and while reading up on presuppositions for a piece, I've been stuck on a wierd asymmetry in how we let new information into a conversation. Suppose a friend says to me, "Ever since my sister moved back, I've been sleeping terribly." Without any checking or even noticing, I now take it for granted that he has a sister, even though I had no idea before. The shared conversational record just updates. But suppose he'd said instead, "By the way, I have a sister." Then I'd treat it as an ordinary assertion: I might ask questions, wonder why he's telling me now, or hold back until it's supported. Same information, two very different routes into the common ground. It's not just siblings, either. A question like "Has your chronic fatigue stopped interfering with your work?" slips in the presupposition that you have chronic fatigue, and people sometimes absorb that too, unless they are already on guard.

So presuppositions seem to get added to the conversational scoreboard for free, whereas asserted content has to earn its way in. I'm trying to figure out whether this asymmetry is defensible, or whether it's a bug in how we run conversations that we've simply learned to live with—and possibly exploit. What's the strongest principle that could justify letting presuppositions update the scoreboard automatically while making assertions wait for acceptance? Test that principle against nearby cases (e.g., a stranger's loaded question, a presupposition that is new information, a presupposition that is contested) and then tell me honestly whether to keep the asymmetry, restrict it, or give it up.
