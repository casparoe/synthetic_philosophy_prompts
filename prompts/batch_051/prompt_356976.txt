In a seminar on long-run predictions in economics, I pressed the standard falsification objection to Malthus: his 1798 Essay argued that population tends to grow geometrically while the food supply can grow only arithmetically, so population is held in check by war, disease, and famine unless checked in advance by moral restraint, yet the centuries after him saw population and living standards rise together rather than the predicted catastrophe. The instructor waved this off with the remark that Malthus was not wrong, just early, and I keep meeting the same line whenever neo-Malthusian resource arguments come up. I want to know exactly what that slogan could be asserting so I can respond to it properly. Lay out the distinct precise claims that the phrase Malthus was right, just early might stand for, ordered from the most trivial to the most radical. For each reading, say whether it is independently plausible, whether it actually rescues Malthus's specific argument from the falsification objection or merely insulates that argument from testing, and what would count as evidence against it. Then say which reading, if any, would justify treating the falsification objection as unpersuasive rather than merely postponing the reckoning.
