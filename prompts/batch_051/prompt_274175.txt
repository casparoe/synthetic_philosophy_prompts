I have been trying on my own to understand what separates genuinely knowing something from merely being confident about it, and I have a friend, Elena, whose judgments about this are unusually stable. She has no formal training in philosophy, but over years of argument I have kept an informal record of her verdicts. I want to know how she should extend that record to a new case.

Her verdicts so far:

1. A traveler at a train station asks a local whether this is the platform for the airport line. The local rode that line a month ago and clearly remembers the airport trains leaving from this platform; if he is wrong, the traveler waits eight minutes for the next train. The local says he knows this is the platform. Elena says he knows.

2. In a dim bar, a man watches a coin spin to rest on a table some distance away. Nothing turns on the result, and he never got a clear look, but he announces that he knows it landed heads. Elena says he does not know.

3. An emergency-room doctor is about to give a patient penicillin and tells a nurse she knows the patient has no allergy. The chart lists no allergy and the patient said "no" when asked, but a mistake would be life-threatening. Elena says the doctor does not know and should run the quick allergy test first.

4. A bomb-disposal technician has just cut a wire. Her partner asks over the radio whether she knows the detonator is disarmed. She watched the cut happen on a clear live feed seconds earlier, and if she is wrong people will die. Elena says she knows — the evidence is overwhelming enough to count even with so much at stake.

5. Two friends bet five dollars on whether Priya's car was in Priya's driveway yesterday. One says the next-door neighbor, who glanced out her window yesterday afternoon and, without paying much attention, saw what she took to be Priya's car in the driveway, knows it was there. Elena says the neighbor knows.

6. In a courtroom, a prosecutor tells the jury that a social worker knows the defendant attended every meeting of a court-ordered program. The social worker relies on the program's computerized check-in log, which is generally reliable but occasionally misrecords an attendance. If the claim is wrong, the defendant may be jailed and the social worker's job is at risk. Elena says the social worker does not know on the strength of the log alone; the record should be checked against something independent first.

The new case is this. Cara sits on a jury in a serious criminal trial. The central question is whether the defendant was inside a hardware store at 3 p.m. Jo, a bystander, was in the paint aisle at that time, looked directly at the defendant for a few seconds, and recognized him from the neighborhood; she is reasonably confident but not certain. Jo has nothing at stake either way: if she is wrong, it costs her nothing, and if she is right, it benefits her nothing. In the jury room, Cara says, "Jo knows the defendant was in the store." If Jo is wrong, the jury may convict an innocent person.

What should Elena say about the new case if she is to remain consistent with her record? Does Jo know, and may Cara correctly say that she does? Which of the six earlier cases is the closest analog, and which principle would Elena be committing herself to with each possible answer? If the record genuinely underdetermines the case, say what further case would settle which principle she has been using all along.
