I am the secretary of a student-run tutoring society at my high school. Our constitution was drafted in 1998 and contains this clause: “Membership is open to all students who will assist in the instruction offered by the Society. Any member who fails to assist in that instruction for three consecutive weeks shall be deemed to have resigned.” Until recently, the clause was only ever used to remove members who stopped attending tutoring sessions without explanation.

We are now facing a case the drafters plainly did not anticipate. Last autumn a member joined who has never conducted a tutoring session. Instead, she rebuilt our previously unusable website, now runs the online booking system on which every session depends, produces all of the Society’s publicity, and wrote a funding application that has just secured a grant allowing us to hire two paid tutors. She attends every business meeting, votes on policy, and has asked the Society for a reference for her college applications. A faction of older members argues that she is a free rider: in their view the Society exists to provide instruction, and she is taking the benefits of membership—voting rights, standing, a reference—while other members carry the burden of teaching. She replies that she contributes substantially and that the 1998 drafters simply could not have foreseen that the Society’s administrative and fundraising work could be what makes the instruction possible.

I have to help the committee decide how the clause treats her membership, but my real question is interpretive. What does the clause require in her case under a plain reading of the words, under its purpose as the drafters would have understood it, under what the drafters would likely have said if asked today, and under what a reasonable member reading it today would take it to mean? If those approaches diverge, which should govern, and why? And beneath the disagreement, what would each reading imply about what makes free riding objectionable—failing to add value, failing to share in a particular common burden, taking advantage of a cooperative practice, or something else?
