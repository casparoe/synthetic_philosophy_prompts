For purposes of defamation law, I was taught a rough three-part test for distinguishing an actionable factual assertion from a protected expression of opinion: (a) verifiability, that is, whether the statement is the kind of thing that can be shown true or false rather than a matter of taste, evaluation, or speculation; (b) the reasonable recipient, that is, whether a reasonable reader or hearer, in the relevant context, would understand the challenged language as asserting an objective fact; and (c) surrounding context, including the medium, the genre, the norms of the relevant professional community, and any hedging or qualifying language. Treat this as a conceptual exercise within a general U.S. constitutional framework, without needing to resolve any particular jurisdiction's case law.

Now consider two cases.

Case 1. A credit rating agency downgrades a city's general-obligation bonds from AA to A-minus and publishes a report stating that the city has "deteriorating pension-funding discipline," is on an "unsustainable" fiscal path, and is likely to restructure its debt within five years. The city disputes the pension-liability assumptions underlying the judgment, and its audited figures appear to contradict them.

Case 2. A private forecasting service issues a seasonal hurricane outlook predicting that seven named storms will make landfall in the Gulf region, including two major hurricanes, and it explicitly bases the numbers on a proprietary ocean-temperature model. The model was later shown to be miscalibrated; in the event, only one tropical storm makes landfall. A resort owner who lost bookings sues, arguing that the forecast was a negligently made factual claim.

Work through each criterion for each case. The central difficulty is that a forecast is not shown to be false merely because the predicted event fails to occur; it is shown to be false, if at all, by what it asserts about the present data, the model, or the reasonableness of the inference. So ask, for each case, whether the actionable core, if there is one, is the rating or forecast itself, or an embedded factual claim about the world that could be false. Where the criteria underdetermine the answer, say which criterion needs sharpening, and whether the fact/opinion distinction is doing the real work or standing in for an unstated policy judgment about who should bear the cost of relying on predictions.
