In my environmental philosophy group we've been stuck for months on whether habitat destruction makes the world better or worse for wild animals in net, and I've started to suspect the real disagreement isn't where it looks. The concrete case is a patch of forest cleared for farmland. We all agree on the natural history: clearing it means fewer animals overall, a shift toward short-lived fast-breeding species, more crowding and disease among the survivors, and a big drop in predator numbers. But one side says the loss is tragic because those forest animals would have lived lives worth living on the whole, so the bulldozer destroys a huge amount of net positive welfare. The other side says the bulldozer actually prevents a huge amount of net negative welfare, because most of the lives it prevents were going to be short, hungry, and end badly. Neither side has moved in a year.

My working theory is that the real dispute isn't about any of the agreed facts but about whether lives that were never lived figure into the accounting at all: one side is summing over possible lives and calling the difference a loss, the other is summing over only actual lives and calling the difference a gain. But I can't tell if that's a substantive moral disagreement, a verbal one about "net welfare," or just a stand-in for a hidden empirical question about the average valence of a wild animal's life. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, and say what would count as progress — a study, a distinction, a new way of framing the question — or wether we should expect to stay stuck.
