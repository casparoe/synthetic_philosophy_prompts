I serve on the Digital Wellbeing Advisory Board of a large public university. The board was created to scrutinize the university's own participation in the attention economy, and our next case is the first under a standard we adopted last term, which bars the university from deploying "manipulative or deceptive" digital practices toward students but deliberately leaves that phrase undefined. The counselling service has asked us to approve a first-year wellbeing app, MindGarden, that delivers cognitive-behavioural exercises for anxiety and low mood. The vendor is candid that its "retention mechanics" drive the high completion rates: a daily streak whose interruption is framed as loss ("your garden will wither if you miss tonight's check-in"), variable rewards on completing exercises, and a "Bloom Circle" view showing each user how many peers in their cohort completed the module. The counselling service argues that drop-off is the main cause of poor outcomes, that the app only steers students toward something they already want to do, and that the design is transparent about how it works. Two board members contend that the university would be licensing an attention-harvesting machine wrapped in a wellbeing narrative, and that the loss-framing in particular pressures already anxious students through their own symptoms.

I have to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and it should give operational criteria the board can actually apply when distinguishing acceptable persuasive design from manipulation. It should also engage the strongest version of the losing position rather than caricaturing it. One distinction I keep circling is that we may be conflating three different things: influence on what a student chooses on a given occasion, influence on how they allocate their attention across a week, and influence on their standing capacity to govern either. If you think that distinction does real work here, use it; if it is the wrong frame, say so and replace it with one better suited to the decision.
