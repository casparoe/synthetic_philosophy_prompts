I am a retired natural scientist (my career was in chemistry) who has come rather late to moral philosophy, and I have been trying to bring to my reading the same care with definitions that I once demanded in the laboratory. I have been working towards explicit criteria for a theory of moral desert, and I have arrived at the following list. A desert claim, as I understand it, requires (1) a desert base: some fact about the person, typically an action undertaken or a quality cultivated, in virtue of which the treatment is said to be fitting; (2) proportionality between the base and the treatment, so that the response is neither excessive nor negligible relative to the base; (3) an appropriate link between the base and the person treated, so that the treatment falls on the very person who authored or embodied the base and not on a convenient substitute; (4) a non-instrumental justification, so that the treatment is appropriate because it is deserved rather than because it would deter, reform, compensate, or protect; and (5) an in-principle answer to the question why this treatment is the fitting response to this base, so that no unexplained residue remains at the centre of the account.

Now take the doctrine of karma, understood not as the colloquial expression about things coming round, but as the full metaphysical teaching: an impersonal cosmic process operates across repeated lives and apportions every being's fortunes and misfortunes automatically and exactly in accordance with that being's accumulated moral merit or demerit, with no judge, no executive, and no discretion in the system.

I would like you to work through my criteria one by one and tell me whether karma, so understood, qualifies as a theory of moral desert, and where the verdict is genuinely indeterminate rather than simply yes or no. I am especially troubled by two points. First, if the present sufferer does not remember the earlier deed, and the earlier bearer of the consequences differs from the present one in body, memory, and much of character, then in virtue of what is the present person the same agent as the earlier one, for the purposes of criterion (3)? Second, if the operation of karma is opaque to the sufferer, who cannot trace present misfortune to any particular past act, does that opacity matter to the intelligibility demanded by criterion (5), or is it enough that the connection would be transparent from an omniscient standpoint? Where karma fails a criterion, please say whether the failure shows that karma is not a theory of moral desert after all, or that my criteria have been shaped too narrowly by a legal and personalised picture of retribution, or some of each. I would also like to know what a fair-minded defender of karma and a fair-minded critic should each be required to grant. Please answer in British English.
