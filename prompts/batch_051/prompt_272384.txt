I am a PhD student in epistemology, and I have been assigned to run a single fifty-minute discussion section on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia for advanced undergraduates who have already studied elementary Bayesian confirmation and the distinction between propositional and doxastic justification. Do not explain the material to me; plan how I should run the section. I need the sequence of ideas, in an order where each step makes the next feel forced: where the distinction between first-order and higher-order evidence is introduced, how the question whether one can rationally believe p while regarding one's own total evidence as not supporting p becomes live, and where the enkratic requirement enters. Give me the single carrying example that should run through the whole session, and justify it against the stock cases — the sleep-deprived person checking arithmetic, the judge who is told she is biased, the red wall plus the drug — by saying what an adequate example must do and what it must not do if the example is not to settle the debate in advance. Then map the likely misconceptions. I expect at least these three: treating higher-order evidence as simply more evidence about the world; collapsing epistemic akrasia into ordinary weakness of will; and reading the enkratic requirement as a descriptive psychological claim about how beliefs feel rather than a normative claim about what combinations of attitudes reason permits. Say where in the sequence each misconception becomes available and how the plan should head it off. End with one question to ask the class in the final five minutes whose answer would not come out right unless the students have genuinely separated first-order from higher-order support, and tell me what I should deliberately leave open or refuse to settle within a single session.
