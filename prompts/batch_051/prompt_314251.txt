I'm a retired chemist — thirty-odd years in natural products, not in philosophy — and I've been reading lately about deepfakes and what gets called the epistemic backstop. One thing lab life teaches you is that science runs on a chain of custody: notebooks, instrument logs, the video of the behavior assay, field recordings. Deepfakes break that chain, because suddenly any audiovisual record can be manufactured after the fact. So let me propose a concrete mechanism and have you analyze it as a mechanism, not as a moral stance.

Suppose every institution that receives public research money has to run a provenance registry. Any audio or video used as evidence in a published study has to be deposited there, along with a cryptographic hash, within 72 hours of recording, and the registry is append-only and mirrored across institutions. A paper may only rely on audiovisual evidence whose hash is already in the registry. Older or third-party recordings can only be introduced if two independent witnesses certify them.

Now what actually happens? I want to know who's incentivized to do what — lab heads, postdocs, field ecologists, fraudsters, the registry operators. How does it get gamed: depositing already-doctored files early, corrupting the witness step, moving fraud to paper trails, claims that something was recorded but couldn't be deposited in time? What second-order effects show up in which science gets done — field sites with no connectivity, quick replication videos, labs in poorer countries? And are the failure modes fixable by tweaking the rule — delayed deposits, trusted witnesses, random audits — or do the fixes just reopen the hole they patched? I don't want a verdict on whether the idea is good or bad overall; I want an account of how it would actually run, and where it's weakest joints are.
