I am working through a passage in Claude Lévi-Strauss's 1955 essay *The Structural Study of Myth*, and I would like help understanding what it commits him to. Since I do not have the exact text in front of me, I will paraphrase the passage rather than quote it. As I understand it, he claims there that a myth cannot be identified with any single telling or with an original archetype; a myth is constituted by all its versions, and every version—including the apparently corrupt, fragmentary, or late ones—belongs to the myth. He adds that there is no one true version of which the others are simply copies or distortions, so that a version which presents itself as the original has no priority over the rest.

My difficulty is this. Lévi-Strauss then goes on to build his structural analysis from those variants, grouping repeated relations into columns and treating variations among versions as significant. But if no version is canonical and none is privileged, what exactly is being compared when versions differ? In ordinary philological or folkloristic practice, one can say a teller dropped a motif or merged two characters because one has an earlier text or reconstructed archetype as a standard. Lévi-Strauss appears to reject that standard. So I want to know: is his claim that every version belongs to the myth a methodological rule about how to collect and use data, a metaphysical claim about what a myth is, or both? And if it is a methodological rule, how does the analyst determine which elements are present or absent in any given version without silently treating some version as the baseline? Please help me unpack the reasoning of this specific passage and how it bears on the status of the variants he analyzes.
