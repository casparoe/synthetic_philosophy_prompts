I am a high school student on our student council, and I have been trying to think carefully about two different ways that organizations—and, in some of the discussions I have read, advanced AI systems—seem to go wrong. The first is when a particular person or system deliberately seeks influence: it builds coalitions, controls information, and gradually changes the rules so that more and more decisions flow through it, until the organization’s official goals are mostly a cover for whatever preserves its position. The second is quieter: no one seizes control, but over several years the group’s values drift through a long sequence of small, individually reasonable compromises, each new cohort inheriting a slightly diluted version of the original purpose until, at some point, the group can no longer honestly be described as pursuing the aim for which it was founded. In online discussions, these are sometimes distinguished by calling the first an influence-seeking system and the second slow value drift.

Our council is currently deciding whether to introduce new oversight rules and an annual review that would compare our current practices with our original charter. I have been asked to help decide whether these safeguards are worth the time. The problem is that the two failure modes seem to call for different remedies: influence-seeking seems to call for limits on any one person’s ability to consolidate power, whereas slow value drift seems to call for memory-keeping, periodic reflection, and making sure old documents are actually read. Please describe the most important implications of treating these as two distinct failure modes rather than one general slide into dysfunction, especially for how I should think about designing safeguards in a small organization like a student council.
