Suppose a university institute for games, ethics, and historical memory has just finished a year-long adversarial collaboration between two factions that have repeatedly argued past one another. The gravity camp holds that when a game depicts an actual armed conflict, especially one whose victims, survivors, or commemorating communities are still present, the work acquires obligations of seriousness and restraint. On this view, point-scoring for kills, cartoon physics, and upbeat respawn lines are not merely tasteless but constitute a failure to represent real suffering properly, regardless of the designers' intentions. The play camp holds that games are rule-governed fictions whose procedures themselves can become a form of commentary; no subject matter is categorically off limits to the medium; and mechanics the gravity camp reads as glorification can be deliberately used to implicate the player in the very appetite for violence that the game is criticizing. Each side rejects the other's one-sentence summary of its position.

Produce the joint document this collaboration would issue. Begin with a numbered agreed-claims section whose entries are substantive rather than vacuous: claims about how games select and frame historical events, how mechanics train attention and shape perception, how a work's subject differs from its stance, and what both camps owe to historical memory and to players. Then supply a disagreements table, each row giving a neutral formulation of one residual disagreement and, below it, a formulation of each camp's own view in wording that camp would sign. After the table, state the cruxes on which the disagreement actually turns. I suspect at least three: whether the recency or identity of a real conflict matters in itself, independently of the work's overall stance; whether reward-based mechanics can be decoupled from represented suffering or inevitably color it; and whether the player's agency turns an observer into an imaginative participant in a way that changes the applicable moral standard. For each crux, construct one invented game concept or scenario both camps would accept as bearing on it, record what each side predicts in advance, and state what result about the case would get each side to revise its view. Do not cite real titles, real studies, or external sources; invent the cases, and write the document so that neither faction would feel its position has been quietly reduced to a strawman.
