I've been going back and forth on griefbots, and I want to figure out what I actually think by working from my gut on cases. 

A widow chats every evening with a bot trained on her dead husband's texts and voicemails. She knows it isn't him and says it keeps her steady. I call that fine, though it makes me uneasy in a way I can't locate.

A company scrapes a dead teenager's public socials and sells access to a chatbot of her without telling the family. Not fine, and I don't think I need the family's say to know that.

A man clones his mother's voice to tell his kids the bedtime stories she used to tell them. Fine, even good.

A woman takes her dead sister's texts and scripts a bot to tell her that the sister forgave her for their last fight and loved her. Not fine: it told her what she wanted to hear, not what she had any evidence the sister would say.

A guy asks a bot trained on a dead friend's DMs whether to take a job and treats the answer as his friend's actual advice. Not fine; that's treating it as a vote from the dead.

An author's estate licenses a voice model of her to read a new speech at a memorial gala. Fine, as long as it's clearly labeled as a performance.

Is there one principle — or a small set — that captures all of these, or close? What does it say about the case I actually cant settle: a widower who, two years after his wife's death, asks the bot of her whether she'd be okay with him dating again? Tell me which of my verdicts the neatest principle can't keep, and be explicit about what's genuinely settled versus contested among people who work on this — don't make a contested view sound like the consensus.
