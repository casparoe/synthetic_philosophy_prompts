I'm in a graduate seminar on metaphysical grounding and fundamentality, and a classmate presented an argument that has been nagging at me all week. Our working picture was about as standard as it gets: some facts are basic and everything else is nothing over and above the basic facts; every non-basic fact has a ground; chains of ground terminate; and if a fact grounds another, then the first necessitates the second — a grounded fact could not fail to obtain once its grounds obtain. We were also treating facts about grounding as themselves part of the world: if one fact grounds another, then that A grounds B is something that obtains, over and above just A and B themselves. On this shared basis, my classmate ran the following argument:

(1) If A grounds B, then there is a fact, the fact that A grounds B; call it G. G is distinct from A and from B, because it concerns the connection between them.

(2) G, like any fact, is either fundamental or grounded.

(3) Suppose G is grounded in A alone. Then, by necessitation, any case in which A obtains is a case in which G obtains. So A grounds B in every case in which A obtains. That makes grounding necessary whenever its ground is present — which leaves no room for the view, taken seriously by several of us, that some grounding connections hold only given the actual laws of nature or other contingent features of the world.

(4) Suppose instead G is grounded, but not by A alone. Then it is grounded by A together with some further fact H, or by some further fact H on its own — where H is a fact about the essence of A, or a law governing what grounds what, or something of that sort. Then we can ask the same question about H. If H is fundamental, then the fundamental level contains a fact whose content is about grounding relations, which conflicts with the sparse picture of fundamentals as basic, non-relational facts. If H is grounded, then we can ask what grounds H, and what grounds the further fact that H is grounded, and so on. The maneuver has not stopped the regress; it has only moved it one level up.

(5) If G is itself fundamental, the same conflict arises immediately: the fundamental level now includes a fact about what grounds what.

(6) So the initial package is untenable. We must accept at least one of the following: grounding is necessary whenever its grounds obtain, the fundamental level includes relational facts about grounding, or every grounding claim generates a further fact that in turn demands a ground, with no point at which the demand ends. Each of those horns seems to me to abandon something we wanted, and my classmate says that is exactly his point.

I would very much like to know where this argument goes wrong, if it does, and what rejecting the offending step would cost. If I had to choose, I would rather keep the sparse fundamental level and the possibility of contingent grounding, and let the cost be paid elsewhere — though I cannot yet see what that elsewhere amounts to.
