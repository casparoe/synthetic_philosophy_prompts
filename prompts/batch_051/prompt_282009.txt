Lately I've been attracted to a deflationary view about life, and it rubs against an intuition I can't shake. The view is that 'life' isn't a natural kind—it's a cluster concept, and where the line falls is our imposition. Its main virtue is that it unifies and explains the history of the debate: every proposed definition (metabolism, homeostasis, reproduction, Darwinian evolution) either lets in things like fire and crystals or rules out mules and post-reproductive people, and the centuries of wrangling look like people adjusting the criteria to protect intuitions they already held. It also gives a tidy decision procedure: don't ask whether X is really alive, ask what would change if we called it alive. On that procedure, viruses, bacterial spores, and a synthetic cell that maintains itself against entropy but can't divide all become 'depends on the job the word is doing,' not cases with a hidden answer.

But my intuition is firm that there is a difference between a metabolizing cyanobacterium and a rock, and that the difference isn't just our convenience. The bacterium is importing materials, repairing itself, and dividing; the rock does none of that. Saying there is no fact of the matter, only line-drawing, feels like denying the most obvious fact I know. I'm not just protecting a classification habit; I think the word tracks something real.

So which should yield? Should I debunk the intuition—and what would a successful debunking have to show? Should I amend or abandon the deflationary view—and what would the amendment say? Or is there a reading on which both survive, e.g., a real difference at the core with no sharp boundary at the edges? I want the strongest case for each move and a verdict on what's most justified, not just a side.
