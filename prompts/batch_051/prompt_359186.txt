I'm on the program commitee for a museum exhibition about how ordinary people become participants in mass atrocity. We're planning a section on Adolf Eichmann's trial, and one of the proposed wall panels would present Arendt's epilogue argument from Eichmann in Jerusalem (1963). Without using her exact words, the panel would say something like: what unsettled her about Eichmann was not that he was demonic but that he was chillingly normal, and that this normality was, from the standpoint of legal and moral judgment, more terrifying than the atrocities themselves; the lesson she drew she called the banality of evil.

Half the committee wants the panel included because it pushes visitors beyond the comfortable monster explanation and makes the social machinery of genocide thinkable. The other half objects that the same passage sounds, in a museum context, like it softens Eichmann's culpability or validates his self-presentation as a mere officeholder; they worry visitors will read it as him basically just following orders. The director wants a written opinion from me before Thursday's meeting. I need a memo that recommends whether to include the panel, and if so exactly how to frame or contextualize it (for example with a sentence of interpretive text, a question to visitors, or something else), and if not what alternate treatment of Arendt's idea the exhibition could use. The memo should be something the committee can act on, with a clear bottom line and a time frame. It also needs to honestly address the strongest objection on the losing side rather than just restating the legal verdict. Make it American English.
