Compare the classical view of concepts, on which each concept is a fixed list of necessary and sufficient conditions, with the fluid-concepts view of the Copycat analogy-making program, on which concepts are transient assemblies of features constructed under pressure and capable of slippage when the situation disrupts a correspondence. Use the letter-string microdomain to discipline the comparison: suppose the source transformation is abc becoming abd, and the solver must produce the analogous transformation for kji. In abc, the changed letter is at once the rightmost letter and the alphabetically last letter, and both properties pick out c; in kji these properties pick out different letters, so any rule transferred from source to target must commit to one structure, or produce both answers as rival analogies. Which theory better explains the existence and reasonableness of competing answers in such cases, and which draws a better line between a productive conceptual slip and a mistaken overextension? Then say whether the fluid-concepts view's advantages in this artificial world are evidence against fixed-feature accounts of ordinary concepts, or only evidence that analogy-making is a special process operating on degraded local representations.
