I have spent a lot of time in online arguments about the ethics of eating animals, and I keep hitting the same wall. The standard definition of veganism includes a qualifier to the effect that one should avoid animal exploitation only so far as it is possible and practicable to do so. I first noticed how much weight this carries when I raised the case of a pastoralist family in a semi-arid region whose livestock provide most of what they eat, and the reply came back that veganism does not require what is impossible or impracticable, which seemed to settle the matter for everyone else in the thread. The same move appeared for a single mother relying on a food bank, and for someone with a medical condition that makes many plant staples difficult. I now think this little caveat is functioning as a one-line theory of moral obligation, but I cannot tell what the theory actually says. Please lay out the distinct claims the caveat might be making, as a menu. For each candidate reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, and say which reading the above uses actually require. For instance: does possible mean physically possible, financially feasible, compatible with staying alive and healthy, compatible with meeting one's other duties, or compatible with keeping some particular way of life going? Does practicable mean feasible in practice without extraordinary sacrifice, or merely that the cost must not be severely burdensome, or that one need not act when the surrounding society makes it costly and isolating? And does the qualifier define who counts as a vegan, describe the extent of an obligation, or function as an excuse after the fact? I want to know whether any reading robust enough to exempt the food-bank user and the pastoralist is also one that a reasonably demanding ethical position would accept.
