I got into an argument with a friend after we heard a story about someone threatening to expose an affair unless they were paid, and I still can't shake the puzzle. Say I know a true, damaging fact about you. It is legal for me to tell your spouse, your boss, or the public; it is also legal for me to ask you for money. But if I say "pay me five thousand dollars and I won't tell," that's blackmail — a crime. Somehow joining two lawful acts into a conditional threat makes the whole thing unlawful. And the pattern gets stranger: if I sue you and, as part of settling, you pay me to keep certain facts confidential, that's a perfectly normal, enforceable contract. A tabloid can even pay for a damaging story and then decline to run it. So it can't just be that paying for silence is wrong.

I'm not looking for the slogans like "it's a threat" or "it's exploitation." I want to know what picture of rights, consent, or legitimate bargaining the legal practice commits itself to underneath, even if judges never spell it out. Reconstruct the implicit theory the law seem to be running on, flag the places where the practice is actually compatible with several different theories rather than settling on one, and say what would have to change in how we regulate information-for-money deals if we instead ran on the theory that blackmail is wrong primarily because it suppresses true information that other people had a real interest in hearing.

Please make your answer exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
