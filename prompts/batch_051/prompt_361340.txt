Suppose we compare two figures. The first is a mid-eighteenth-century writer of no institutional position who publishes satires against the dogmas of the established church, the hereditary privileges of the court, and the superstitions of his age. We teach him as a hero of the Enlightenment; his mockery of authority is called intellectual courage. The second is a contemporary figure with no institutional credentials and a very large online audience who publishes video attacks on the consensus of climate scientists and public-health agencies, alleging that the establishment is corrupt, conformist, and self-interested. Mainstream judgment calls this second figure a spreader of misinformation and a danger to public reason. Structurally, the two acts look alike: an outsider uses rhetoric to destabilize an entrenched epistemic authority on the ground that the authority is serving itself rather than the truth. Yet the first is classified as progress and the second as epistemic vandalism.

I want to know whether any principle justifies this asymmetry, rather than merely explains it psychologically. The crude reply—that the first target was wrong and the second is right—is not available in advance; it is a verdict delivered from inside the second authority, and it would have seemed no more decisive to the first writer's contemporaries. Consider, instead, candidates such as these: (i) the institutions differ in kind, because the church claimed infallibility and punished dissent, whereas scientific bodies publish methods, state uncertainty, and change their minds when the evidence changes; (ii) the stakes differ, because undermining a settled public-health consensus can kill people now, while undermining the church's authority was the precondition of honest inquiry; (iii) the challengers differ in method, because the Enlightenment writer addressed critics, sought contrary evidence, and stood ready to be refuted, whereas the modern figure refuses correction; or (iv) the asymmetry is not justified at all—"Enlightenment" is simply the name we give to rebellion that succeeded, and "conspiracy thinking" the name we give to rebellion that failed.

Which of these, or what further principle, does the work best? Test it against near neighbors: a physician in 1847 who publicly attacks the medical establishment's refusal to accept that doctors themselves were spreading disease, and is ridiculed for decades before being vindicated; a contemporary outsider whose reasoning is scrupulous but whose target is a genuinely flawed expert consensus; and a contemporary outsider who recites a baseless rumor but happens, by luck, to be criticizing an authority that is in fact corrupt and self-serving. If your principle treats all three alike, you have not captured the asymmetry; if it requires hindsight, it is not a principle anyone could use at the time; and if it reduces to siding with whatever authority one already trusts, then the Enlightenment is not the neutral origin of critical reason but a tribal label. The question, then: what, if anything, makes the difference between the hero and the crank justifiable from within the moment of judgment—and what does your answer imply about whether the Enlightenment's critical spirit is a source of progress or only the name we give to the winning side of an epistemic fight?
