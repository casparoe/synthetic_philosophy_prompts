I recently joined the accessibility working group for my daughter's school district, and it has forced me to think about a set of trade-offs I keep making inconsistently. The committee ranks possible renovation plans for an old three-story building, and to keep comparisons clean we reduce each plan to two numbers. The first is a score from 0 to 100 for how independently my daughter can get around the building, where 0 means she can reach the library, the cafeteria, and every classroom on her own, and 100 means she cannot reach most rooms without a staff member carrying her chair up the short flight of stairs. The second number is the total number of minutes lost per day by all the other students combined, spread over roughly 2,000 students, from rerouted corridors and moved entrances; for scale, 150 minutes works out to about four and a half seconds per student, while 10,000 minutes is about five minutes per student. On both numbers, lower is better.

These are the pairwise judgments I actually find myself with.

1. Small steps. For any score n between 100 and 1, and whatever number of minutes m the current plan costs, I judge a plan with score n−1 and m+150 minutes better than a plan with score n and m minutes. A one-point improvement in my daughter's access is worth as much as 150 minutes of other students' combined time, and I would take that trade at every single rung from 100 down to 0.

2. Jumping to the end. But when I compare a plan with score 100 and no minutes lost to a plan with score 0 and 10,000 minutes lost, I choose the first. Presented with the whole change at once, I balk at 10,000 minutes, even though I would take it as a hundred small trades of 150 minutes each, adding up to 15,000.

3. Same score. Whenever two plans leave my daughter's score unchanged, I judge the one with fewer total minutes lost better.

I had always assumed that better-than is transitive, but these verdicts appear to contradict one another under that assumption. Is there a single principle — transitive or not — that captures all three, and if not, which verdict should I give up? I would also like to know what the resulting view says about a case I cannot settle myself: a plan with score 45 and no minutes lost versus a plan with score 0 and 7,500 minutes lost.
