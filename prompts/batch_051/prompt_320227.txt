Over years of reading about how societies respond to mass atrocity, I keep noticing a pattern for which I have no name. An amnesty offered in exchange for full disclosure is defended in one register as a way of securing truth: perpetrators will not give candid accounts if those accounts can be used to prosecute them, so immunity is the price of a historical record. It is defended in another register as a way of securing peace: the aim is not retribution but the creation of a political community in which former enemies can coexist. These two registers are not treated as alternatives. When a critic objects that the amnesty denies victims a legal remedy, defenders lean on the second register, as though the process were never meant to be punitive. When a critic objects that testimony purchased with immunity is unreliable, defenders lean on the first, as though the process were only ever meant to produce a record. A similar slide appears in the treatment of victim testimony in the same settings: the same account is treated as evidence when its historical contribution is challenged and as therapy or ritual when its evidentiary standing is challenged, with no stated rule governing which standard applies. I recognize that this sits near the familiar peace-versus-justice debate, but that is usually framed as an open trade-off to be balanced. The phenomenon I am noticing is less a trade-off than an institution held up by two distinct ends that are run together, each invoked to deflect the objections aimed at the other. Is there an established term for this exact configuration, and how does it differ from the peace-versus-justice tension, from ordinary equivocation, and from constructive ambiguity? If there is no accepted term, what should it be called, and what boundary cases would a definition need to classify? I am not looking for a bare label; I want the phenomenon itself delimited.
