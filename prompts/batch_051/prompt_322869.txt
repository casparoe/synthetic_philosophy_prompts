I teach a third-year undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of probability and scientific reasoning. The seminar has sixteen students and the session runs for ninety minutes. I would like a runnable classroom game, with clear rules and a chip-based payoff scheme that needs no real money, that makes participants feel the force of the claim that no finite binary sequence is random all by itself. The target idea is this: a string of 0s and 1s that looks patternless may nevertheless have been produced by a short deterministic rule, while a string with an obvious pattern may have come from a fair coin; the sequence alone does not carry its provenance, so the sentence 'this sequence is random' only acquires determinate content once we fix a generating process or an operative notion such as unpredictability against every computable betting strategy, or incompressibility under a fixed description language. The participants should run into this problem through the game before it is named, and they should not hear the term algorithmic randomness until the debrief.

Please organise your answer in the following sections, in this order, with roughly the lengths shown.

1. Learning aims (about 120 words). State what the students should come to see by the end, in terms of what they will be able to distinguish and what mistaken inference they will be able to resist, rather than as facts to be recalled.

2. Materials and preparation (about 150 words). List the physical materials, including chips or tally sheets, how the sequences will be displayed, how the true source of each sequence is kept from the students until the reveal, and anything the facilitator must prepare in advance.

3. Rules and payoff scheme (about 350 words). Give the complete rules of the game: how sequences are produced or revealed bit by bit, how gamblers stake chips on the next bit at even odds, how capital is tracked, and how many rounds there are. Include the exact binary strings you will use, of roughly length 24 to 30, together with the deterministic rules behind the rule-generated ones; the rules should be transparent and checkable on the spot. Ensure that at least one rule-generated string is built to pass the pattern-spotting the class is likely to try, while at least one genuinely random string contains a long, salient run of identical bits.

4. Running order (about 250 words). Provide a timed plan for the ninety minutes, with no opening lecture: an initial prediction or voting phase held before the students know the sources, the betting rounds themselves, and a reveal staged at the point where the central tension has become visible.

5. Debrief (about 400 words). Map specific moments in the game onto the conceptual points: the pattern-seeking and gambler's fallacies the betting should expose; the underdetermination of the generating process by any finite string; the point that a rule long enough merely to spell out the string achieves no compression; and the conclusion that randomness is better attributed to processes, or to infinite sequences under a fixed test, than to finite strings considered in isolation.

6. Predictable failures and remedies (about 180 words). Say what is most likely to go wrong the first time I run it, and how I should handle each failure without breaking the design.

7. Honest limitations (about 150 words). State which feature of the algorithmic conception of randomness the game cannot reproduce and must therefore be supplied directly by the facilitator in the debrief.
