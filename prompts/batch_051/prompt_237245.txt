Im a PhD student TA-ing a decision theory course and I set a short problem on the Allais paradox. One student's answer is strong on the mechanics but ive gone back and forth on whether a couple of his asides should cost marks, and since I have to return written comments I want those to be precise. The problem I set was: in the classic Allais problem a person chooses a certain $1,000,000 over a gamble offering an 89% chance of $1,000,000, a 10% chance of $5,000,000, and a 1% chance of nothing, and then chooses a 10% chance of $5,000,000 over an 11% chance of $1,000,000. State the independence axiom and show whether this pair of choices is consistent with expected utility theory; if not, say exactly which principle fails. The student wrote: the independence axiom says that if a person prefers lottery L to lottery M, then for any lottery N and any probability p they must prefer pL + (1-p)N to pM + (1-p)N, since the common component is irrelevant. In the first Allais pair the certain million can be broken into an 89% chance of a million plus an 11% chance of a million, while the gamble is an 89% chance of a million plus an 11% chance of a sublottery that gives five million with probability 10/11 and nothing with probability 1/11. The common 89% branch cancels, so the real comparison is between a certain million and a 10/11 shot at five million with a 1/11 chance of nothing. The second pair has the same structure with the common branch changed to an 89% chance of nothing: an 11% chance of a million versus an 11% chance of the same five-million sublottery. By independence the two choices should go the same way. The typical person takes the certain million in the first pair and the 10% chance of five million in the second, so this common ratio effect is a violation of independence, and no von Neumann-Morgenstern utility function can represent both choices. The standard repair is to replace objective probabilities with decision weights, as in prospect theory; that keeps the independence axiom intact because only the probabilities get transformed, which is why the Allais pattern is a descriptive quirk rather than a problem for expected utility as a normative theory.
