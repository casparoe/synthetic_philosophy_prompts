I'm a science and ideas journalist working on a long piece about self-deception, and before I impose a framework on my sources I want to audit my own intuitions against a set of cases. My verdicts, case by case:

- A husband who, after a friend drops a hint, stops opening the bank statements and tells himself that every marriage has rough patches while his wife is plainly having an affair: self-deceived.
- A smoker who has read only industry-funded studies and reports that the evidence about cancer is genuinely mixed: self-deceived.
- A patient who finds a lump, decides she will not think about it, and never books the appointment: not self-deceived, merely avoidant.
- A politician who loses dozens of election challenges, is told by his own aides that the fraud narrative is fabricated, and still sincerely insists he won: not self-deceived; the belief is false, but there is no duplicity toward himself.
- A professor who dismisses every rejection as the work of jealous rivals and genuinely believes he is underappreciated: self-deceived.
- A salesperson who talks himself into "I'm the best there is" before each pitch, half-believes it, and closes more sales for it: not self-deceived.

I have the sense that a single principle draws these lines, but I cannot state it. What principle, if any, captures all or nearly all of these verdicts, which of them would it force me to revise, and what does it commit me to about a memoirist who sincerely remembers confronting an abusive parent when every other account says he did not?
