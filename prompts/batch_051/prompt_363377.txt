I teach a small undergraduate seminar, and last month I attempted a surprise quiz and have not stopped thinking about it since. On the Friday before, I announced: there will be a short quiz on exactly one day next week, and you will not know which day it is until I hand it out. A student immediately produced the familiar backward induction — if no quiz had come by Thursday evening, we would know it must be Friday, so Friday could not be the day; but then once Thursday became the last eligible day it would be known, and so on back through the week — and concluded that my announcement could not be satisfied. On Wednesday I handed out the quiz. The class was genuinely caught off guard, including the student who gave the argument, and afterward several students told me I had done something impossible. I have been collecting variants of the situation since then and recording what I find myself wanting to say about each.

My verdicts so far are these. In the original five-day case, the Wednesday quiz was a genuine surprise, so the announcement was satisfiable and the student's argument must have a flaw, though I cannot state it. If I say on Sunday, "there will be a quiz tomorrow, and you will not know it until I hand it out," then no surprise is possible; the one-day announcement defeats itself. If I make the same five-day announcement while privately resolved to give the quiz on Friday, the quiz will not be a surprise, because by Thursday evening the class will know it must be Friday — so the truth of my announcement seems to depend on how I actually choose the day, not merely on the words. If I announce, "there will be a quiz one of these days, and you will not know it until it is handed out," with no fixed last day, the quiz can plainly be a surprise whenever it comes, so the puzzle requires a definite final day. If I instead announce, "there will be a quiz next week, and you will not be able to deduce the day from this announcement," the quiz can succeed, and I suspect this reading — surprise as non-deducibility rather than absence of justified belief — gets something right. Yet when I imagine that the students are flawless reasoners who know with certainty that I always tell the truth, I lose my nerve and think the original announcement could not surprise them; I dislike this verdict, because it makes surprise a defect of the audience rather than a property of my scheduling. And when I imagine announcing two quizzes on two different days next week, neither to be known the evening before, the arrangement feels more robustly satisfiable than the one-quiz case, though I cannot say why, since the structure looks the same.

I would like to know whether there is one principle about when an announced event can be a genuine surprise that captures all or most of these verdicts, or a small family of candidate principles. What would that principle say about the case I still cannot settle: a two-day announcement — the quiz will be on Monday or Tuesday, and they will not know which until it is handed out — where I have privately resolved to give it on Monday? And which of my verdicts would a coherent account force me to give up? I am less interested in the standard solution to the paradox than in learning which of my gut reactions can survive reflection.
