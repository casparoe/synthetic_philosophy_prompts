I keep noticing an asymmetry in how people judge frugality, and I can't tell if it's defensible or just a quirk. Take two people. One patches her jeans, eats leftovers, keeps the heating low, and lives on very little; everyone agrees she's virtuous, even admirable, and no one stops to ask whether her personal savings amount to anything in the grand scheme. The other lives pretty comfortably — flies, eats out, buys new clothes — but spends his weekends campaigning for building codes and subsidies that would get thousands of households to consume less. By any calculation he prevents far more waste than the first person ever could, and he'd say avoiding waste is exactly why he does it. Yet people hesitate to call him frugal, and plenty would say its none of his business to make other people frugal. We don't seem to have this hesitation about charity: someone who gives a lot is admired, and so is someone who runs a food bank and gets other people to give. So why is frugality different? Is there a principle that generates both the praise for being frugal and the unease about promoting frugality, or should one of those judgments go? Give me the strongest defense you can, and test it against the case where the campaigner is personally wasteful but genuinely effective at reducing waste overall.
