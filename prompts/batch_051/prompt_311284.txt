I keep going back and forth on anthropics, specifcally the self-sampling assumption versus the self-indication assumption, and I don't know which part of me to trust.

On one side, SIA seems to me to have the only genuinely principled rule: when you learn that you exist, you should update toward hypotheses on which your existence was more probable. That's just ordinary Bayesian reasoning, and it's tidy—weight hypotheses by the number of observers they predict who are in your epistemic situation. It also cleanly dissolves the Doomsday Argument, which I've always suspected was more of a clever trick than a real prediction.

On the other side, that same rule seems to license the presumptuous philosopher. If SIA is right, I should now be fairly confident the galaxy is teeming with aliens (or that future humans are vastly numerous) purely because such hypotheses contain far more observers like me. And I have a really firm intuition that I can't get astronomically large conclusions about aliens from the bare fact that I was born as one observer rather than another. That's exactly the kind of free information my gut says cannot exist.

I know the usual moves: bite the bullet, debunk the intuition as scope insensitivity or confusion about typicality, restrict SIA using some reference class, or fall back to SSA and swallow the Doomsday conclusion. But I want more than a menu. Walk me through what each option actually costs, what would justify dropping the intuition versus amending the theory, and whether there's any stable reading under which both could be right.
