I teach a philosophy of probability course, and this term it's full of stats and ML students. I want to give them the Principal Principle — roughly, that if you know the objective chance of an event is x, your credence should be x, provided you don't have inadmissible information like a glimpse at the future — in terms they already trust: parameters, data generating processes, aleatory versus epistemic uncertainty, posterior predictives, calibrated forecasters, scoring rules, leakage, model misspecification. I'm fluent enough in that vocabulary, so dont teach me basic Bayes; I want a systematic translation, not a loose analogy.

Cover at least these three pieces: the basic deference of rational credence to known objective chance; the admissibility clause and why it's needed (they'll want to know what kind of information counts as inadmissible); and the undermining futures problem for a Humean picture of chance, plus the New Principle proposed as a fix. For each, say where the translation is clean, where you had to fudge, and where the idea has no counterpart at all.

Then give me the residue report, because that's the part I actually want. My students will say the whole thing is just calibration — if you know the true probability, report the true probability, and the rest is philosophers keeping themselves in business. I suspect some of the Principal Principle really does collapse into calibration, and some of it doesn't, and I want you to sort that out. What exactly does admissibility become — just "don't train on the test set, no label leakage"? Is the New Principle anything more than a fix for model misspecification, where your model of the world contains a hypothesis that would undermine its own probabilities? And is there anything in the statistical vocabulary that plays the role of the philosopher's inadmissible-information clause, or is that clause just a patch over a hole in the original principle?

Keep the translation honest: when a piece of Lewis won't survive contact with the stats vocabulary, say so explicitly, and then tell me whether the loss is the statistics framework missing something real (the normativity of rationality, or chance as a feature of the world rather than a feature of a model) or whether the philosopher's version was vague in a way this exercise exposes.
