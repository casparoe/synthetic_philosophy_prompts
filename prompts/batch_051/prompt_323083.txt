I'm an American law student spending a term in Germany, and during a recent seminar on free expression a classmate dismissed my unease about hate-speech prosecutions with the proverb "sticks and stones may break my bones, but words will never hurt me." It wasn't just a throwaway: he was using it to argue that the European approach to regulating hate speech is aimed at an injury that doesn't really exist — or at least that speech isn't the kind of thing the criminal law should treat as harmful. I'd like to answer him carefully instead of simply asserting the opposite. As a philosopher of law, please work through the question systematically, beginning by brainstorming the different precise claims that proverb could be making. Then lay them out as a menu — from the trivially true to the substantive and controversial — and for each, say whether it is true, defensible, or false, and why. Finally, identify which of those readings, if any, would actually support the conclusion that hate-speech laws are unnecessary, and which readings would leave that conclusion unsupported. I want something I can take into the next seminar and use to sharpen the debate.
