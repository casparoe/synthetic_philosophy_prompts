I run a little discussion group that's been chewing on Schopenhauer's ideas about music, and I keep going back and forth on something. From what I understand, he thought the other arts only show you particular copies of things in the world, but music skips that step and gives you the will itself—the blind, restless striving underneath everything—directly, without the normal filters. So music doesn't represent an emotion like a painting might, it just is the striving made audible, which is supposedly why it grabs people at a level they can't argue with.

Here's the thing I want analyzed. In Paris in 1913, at the first performance of Stravinsky's The Rite of Spring, the audience supposedly started yelling, booing, and even fighting, drowning out the orchestra so much the dancers couldn't hear it. I don't need the history fact-checked; I'm interested in what this does to Schopenhauer. Would his theory predict this kind of meltdown, or is it actually a problem for him? On one hand, if music is a direct copy of the will's pushing and straining, maybe an audience getting physically swept up is exactly what the theory says should happen. On the other hand, I thought he also thinks art is supposed to let us step back and contemplate, so why would anyone end up in a shouting match in the theater? And then there's the dance and the fact that the music was brand new—does the theory have any room for those? After you work through it, tell me whether the event ends up as evidence for his picture of music or a case it can't digest.
