I edit a mid-size dictionary aimed at adult learners, and we're constantly relitigating when a new attested use of a word deserves its own numbered sense and when it's just acontextual shading of an existing one. Right now it gets settled by vibes and seniority, which is embarassing. I want a reusable decision procedure my junior editors could run on a candidate split in about fifteen minutes, before it comes to me for sign-off; we get maybe five candidates a week. Ground it in whatever philosophy of language and lexical semantics actually have to say about what makes two uses of a word the same meaning versus two meanings — ambiguity vs vagueness, polysemy vs contextual modulation, all that — but the deliverable is an ordered checklist with yes/no endpoints, not a theory essay. I know some philosophers would say words just have use-families and no settled meanings; I'd like the procedure to be honest about where that's true, i.e. where no test can settle it and an editor has to decide. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with 'don't split,' where it stops with 'split,' and which steps are judgment calls wearing a checklist costume. Then run it on three cases: run in run a company vs run a mile, healthy in healthy diet vs healthy patient, and crash in the car crashed vs the market crashed. I want at least one case where the procedure tells my editors something they'll hate, and a short note on what the procedure can't do.
