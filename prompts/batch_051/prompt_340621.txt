I am preparing the first meeting of a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of modality, and I want to hand out a diagnostic instrument: a "which position on possible worlds do you actually hold?" quiz. Please write an eight-question quiz with a scoring key. Each question should present a short concrete case or claim with two or three answer options, phrased so that a student can answer it without already knowing the labels. The point of the exercise is to sort readers by what they say about cases, so no question should simply ask whether someone is a modal realist or an actualist.

Build the quiz from the logical space rather than from a list of names. The diagnostic questions should test at least the following distinctions: (i) whether accepting an ordinary modal truth commits someone to entities that make it true; (ii) whether possible worlds, if accepted, are concrete or abstract; (iii) whether "I could have been a doctor" is best accounted for by trans-world identity or by counterpart theory; (iv) whether there are non-actual particulars such as possible talking donkeys, or only further ways for the actual inhabitants of the world to be; (v) whether possible-worlds talk is literally true or a useful fiction; (vi) whether a possible world is an additional thing over and above its constituents, or nothing more than a maximal arrangement of them; and (vii) whether there could have been fundamental properties that nothing in the actual world instantiates.

Use at least four of the following cases among the questions: the possible talking donkey; the possible blue swan; my having possibly been a doctor; a world containing nothing but 150 hydrogen atoms; and the possibility of alien fundamental properties—properties no actual particular instantiates.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least the following leaves, each described in a single sentence: Lewisian genuine modal realism (worlds are concrete objects on a par with our own); linguistic or propositional ersatzism (worlds are maximal consistent descriptions built from sentences or propositions); states-of-affairs actualism (worlds are abstract maximal states of affairs); Armstrong-style combinatorialism (possibilities are recombinations of the actual world's fundamental ingredients); and modal fictionalism (possible-worlds talk is a useful fiction and is not literally true). Where a combination of answers corresponds to no defended position, note it and diagnose briefly whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any two leaves the instrument cannot cleanly separate, and note whether the necessitism–contingentism dispute cuts across the tree rather than showing up as a leaf.

The audience is graduate students with a solid undergraduate preparation in philosophy and no prior graduate work in modal metaphysics.

Begin the response with the instrument itself, with no preface or commentary. Please write in British English.
