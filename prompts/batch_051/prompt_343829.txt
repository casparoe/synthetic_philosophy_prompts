I have been thinking about the value of individuality and self-expression — roughly, the Millian thought that people should be free to try out different ways of living rather than simply taking over the one they were born into — and I find that my verdicts on particular cases run ahead of any principle I can state. I want the principle, and I want to know which of my verdicts it forces me to change.

Here are my verdicts, stated as I actually feel them.

1. A young man in a conservative town writes experimental poetry under a pseudonym, drawing on sources his neighbors would despise. He publishes only online, for strangers; locally he lives an entirely conventional life. I think this is genuine self-expression, and the secrecy does not cancel its value — it is the price of having an inner life there at all.

2. A woman in her mid-twenties leaves a stable but dull job to live cheaply in the country and make furniture by hand, knowing she has no training and may fail. Three years later she gives up and returns to office work. I think the experiment had real value even though it failed: she learned something about that way of life, and about herself, that armchair reflection could not have taught her.

3. A teenager adopts a subculture wholesale — clothing, music, slang, politics, even food preferences — not because any of it appeals to her on reflection, but because she wants to be seen as a nonconformist. Her choices track the subculture's changing fashions within weeks. I think this is conformity wearing a costume, with little value either as self-expression or as an experiment; she is outsourcing her choices to a different crowd.

4. A man in a close traditional community follows the expected path — the family trade, the expected marriage, the expected worship — while privately rejecting much of it, purely because he cannot bear to disappoint his parents. I think his conformity is not valuable as self-expression, and something real about him goes un-lived, even if his choice might be justified on other grounds.

5. A man spends his twenties rejecting convention in dress, work, and romance, then in his thirties, after reflection, takes up a deliberately conventional life — a corporate job, a house in the suburbs, ordinary clothes — because he has come to see the point of these things on his own terms. I think this late conformity is as much an expression of individuality as the earlier rebellion was; chosen convention and chosen nonconvention are equals.

6. A woman reinvents herself every two years — new city, new profession, new friends, new self-presentation — each time declaring the previous self a phase. She never stays anywhere long enough to become good at anything or to be known by anyone. I think this is not valuable self-expression, and no way of life is ever actually tested, because none is lived seriously.

My tentative principle is that what matters is whether a way of life is chosen from genuine alternatives after reflection, and whether the person is the one doing the choosing; conformity is defective only when it is unreflective or coerced. But that principle seems to bless case 6, which I do not bless. Perhaps I need an added condition that a way of life receive a fair trial — lived long enough for its real costs and benefits to emerge — before it is adopted or abandoned. That would separate case 2 from case 6, but then I worry: if the value lies in testing ways of living, why are case 6's many tests not more valuable than case 2's single failed one? I cannot yet state the difference.

Please work out the best single principle that captures these verdicts, or the nearest defensible one. State it precisely, with the clause that handles each case; deliver your verdicts on two further cases of your own choosing; and tell me plainly which of my six verdicts the principle cannot save. I would rather see one or two of my verdicts overturned than be handed a complicated patchwork designed to save them all — so commit to one account and defend it.
