I've been trying to get clearer about what physicalists actually mean by 'physical', and I keep getting stuck on this argument a friend laid out for me. I half-suspect there's a trick in it but I cant tell where. Here's the argument:

(1) Physicalism is the thesis that everything that exists is physical.
(2) The word 'physical' has to mean either (a) what current physics describes, or (b) what an ideally completed physics would describe. There's no third option worth taking.
(3) If it means (a), physicalism is almost certainly false: current physics is incomplete and doesn't even posit things like conscious experience, so identifying reality with its ontology would leave out the mind.
(4) If it means (b), physicalism is empty: we have no way to rule out that a completed physics will simply include whatever we now call mental (by the end of inquiry it might posit fundamental mental properties), so the thesis collapses into 'everything is whatever the final science says there is' — which even a dualist could accept if the final science included the soul.
(5) Therefore physicalism is either false or trivial.
(6) So physicalism isn't a substantive, defensible thesis.

I've seen this called Hempel's dilemma, I think, but never quite spelled out this way. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails exactly? And if it is valid, which premise should a physicalist abandon, and what should they replace it with to keep physicalism both substantive and true?
