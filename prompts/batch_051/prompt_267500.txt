I run the digital collections unit of a regional heritage institution. Several times a year we receive software — floppy disks, hard drives, old code repositories — from dissolved companies, defunct studios, and the estates of deceased authors, and must decide whether to preserve, run, and grant access to it or treat it as locked. Write a reusable decision procedure for handling software whose owner is gone or unlocatable. Ground it in the distinction between an absent owner and an abandoned work: what must be true for software to count as relinquished rather than merely orphaned, whether prolonged non-use or non-enforcement ever constitutes abandonment, and how abandoning an intangible work differs from abandoning a physical object. The procedure should be usable by a non-lawyer curator in under an hour per item, list due-diligence steps in order, say where the decision must go to counsel, and flag points where a values judgment is dressed up as a factual finding. Then apply it to three cases: (1) a widely distributed shareware utility whose sole author died thirty years ago without a will; (2) a 1996 commercial game from a studio dissolved in 1998, where a successor company holds the trademark but cannot show it acquired the code; and (3) source code for a research tool whose author is alive but unreachable after ten years of documented good-faith inquiries. One worked case should return an answer a curator would not like, and the closing section should state what the procedure cannot settle because the underlying concept of ownership runs out.
