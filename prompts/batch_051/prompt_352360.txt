Question: Explain the doctrine of divine simplicity and evaluate the following objection: if God is wholly simple, then God's wisdom, power, and goodness are all numerically identical; but then the distinctions among the divine attributes are merely conceptual, and God cannot be said to be wise, powerful, and good in distinct, substantive ways.

Student answer: Divine simplicity is the thesis that God has no parts of any kind and no real distinction between God's essence and God's existence or among God's intrinsic attributes. According to Aquinas, God is wise and God is powerful do not pick out different components in God; both statements refer to the very same divine reality, although under different concepts. The objection in the question assumes that if the attributes are identical, the distinction among them must be an illusion. That is too quick. The attributes are not parts of God; each attribute is identical with the whole divine essence. Our concepts of wisdom and of power differ in sense, but they have the same referent. So the distinction is conceptual, not merely verbal, because the two concepts are not interchangeable synonyms. Aquinas further holds that a single simple cause can produce diverse effects, and that the perfections found in creatures pre-exist in God in a higher mode. It follows that God can genuinely be wise and powerful, even if God's wisdom and power are not two separate parts but one act of being considered under different descriptions. The objection therefore fails.
