I have been reading the rationalist-adjacent discussions of precommitment and personal identity for years, but a family situation has recently made the abstract question concrete. A relative of mine, who is in the early stages of Alzheimer's, has signed an advance directive refusing artificial nutrition and hydration should she later reach a state of severe dementia. In our family discussions, her husband has been defending the binding force of this directive with the familiar Ulysses analogy: just as Odysseus had himself lashed to the mast because he knew his future self would be unable to act on his considered judgment when the Sirens sang, my relative is tying her future self down now, and respecting the directive is simply respecting her autonomy as a self-governing agent. This initially felt compelling, but I have begun to suspect the analogy is doing much of the argumentative work. Odysseus, at the moment the Sirens actually sang, still wanted to resist them and endorsed the ropes even as he strained against them; the later self in severe dementia may not share the earlier self's values even by her own lights, and the whole difficulty is precisely that the later stage's relationship to the earlier self's projects, preferences, and identity is unclear. The Ulysses picture also seems to quietly import the idea of a single agent with stable all-things-considered values, and casts family members or clinicians as the loyal crew mechanically enforcing the right self's orders rather than exercising independent judgment.

I would like a rigorous audit of how far the Ulysses-contract analogy actually carries as an argument for honoring advance directives in dementia. Please give the mapping explicitly—what plays the role of the mast, the ropes, the Sirens, the crew, and the moment of deliberation on the ship—then identify where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the intended conclusion (that the directive should be enforced) from those that are merely incidental. Does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does the analogy presuppose the very account of personal identity and autonomy it is being used to establish? And if there is a better analogy for the authority of the earlier self, I would like to hear what it is and what it does and does not buy.
