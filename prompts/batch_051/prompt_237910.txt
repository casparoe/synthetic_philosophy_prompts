An autonomous-systems company proposes a service robot, intended to be safe around humans, whose entire control policy consists of three fixed rules with strict precedence: it must not injure a human or knowingly let a human be injured; it must obey any human instruction except when obedience would violate the first rule; and it must preserve its own functioning except when doing so would violate either earlier rule. The architecture has no procedure for weighing a small risk of injury against a large benefit, for disregarding a harmful or self-defeating instruction that is not clearly life-threatening, or for resolving cases where the rules themselves can each be satisfied in several mutually incompatible ways. What theory of moral reasoning does this design presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view, say whether the three rules rest on a single coherent theory or pull against one another, distinguish what the design strictly commits its builders to from what it merely fits comfortably with, and explain what would have to change architecturally if that theory were replaced by one on which duties are defeasible and harms may be traded off holistically.
