I run a tiny nonprofit that teaches people to be more frugal and less wasteful — we do mending workshops, cooking-with-scraps classes, a "buy nothing" swap, that kind of thing. It's always been a point of pride that the organization itself lives by the same rules: we reuse envelopes, print double-sided, furnish the office from curb finds. Last week our outreach person pitched a fundraising campaign that would blow most of our savings: glossy direct mail, online ads, and branded tote bags as thank-you gifts for donors. I started to object that buying thousands of tote bags to tell people to buy less stuff is insane, but she had the numbers ready. Her point was that if the campaign brings in even a couple hunderd new members and each of them starts composting, mending, and cutting their household spending, the total waste prevented will far exceed the waste the campaign itself produces. So refusing to spend wastefully now means less frugality in the world overall.

I didn't know what to say. On the one hand, the math seems right, and I'd be choosing a smaller amount of the thing we care about over a larger amount. On the other hand, it feels like the moment we hand out branded merch we've stopped being the kind of organization that actually practices what it preaches. Is her argument right? Should I approve the campaign because it promotes frugality more effectively, or is there a principled reason for us to stay frugal even if a bit of wastefulness would cause more frugality overall? I can't tell if I'm seeing a real distinction or just being precious about the group's identity.
