I've been going through Mill's Principles of Political Economy, Book II, Chapter II, where he argues that the essential principle of private property is about letting people keep what they have produced by their own exertions. He then says something that's been rattling around in my head: that the state would be justified in setting a limit on how much anyone may acquire by gift or inheritance, because inherited wealth is not the fruit of the recipient's own effort, and a great fortune falling into someone's lap is more like the favor of other people than a reward for anything they did. The way he separates earned from unearned acquisition feels really principled. It gives a clean rationale for estate taxes or even caps on inheritance: we are not attacking the parent's right to their own property, only the child's claim to receive it without having done anything to create it.

But here's the part I can't get past. I have a very firm intuition that if I work, save, and live modestly in order to leave something to my children, that is one of the central ways a parent expresses care and responsibility. It doesn't feel like mere favor in the lottery sense; it feels like the completion of a deliberate lifetime project that is already mine. If the state steps in and takes a huge chunk or sets a cap, it seems to be treating the family as a mere conduit or legal fiction, and treating my savings as if they ceased to be mine in any morally relevant way the moment I die. I can accept taxing very large dynastic fortunes, like the child of a billionaire, but I don't want to give up the idea that parents have a special, protected liberty to give to their own children without that being redescribed as a loophole.

I don't think I can just have both without mutilating one of them. Walk me through which should yield. Is Mill's principle strong enough that my parental intuition is just an attachment I should debunk? Or does his principle need to be amended to protect intra-family transfers up to some threshold? Is there a reading where an estate tax and an unlimited right of bequest can both be true, because the right concerns the donor's power to dispose, not the recipient's power to receive? But if so, does that dissolve into word-games once the tax rate gets high enough? I want the verdict negotiated, not just chosen.
