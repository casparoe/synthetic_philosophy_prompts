Act like a philosopher of language who actually enjoys trying to taxonomize ordinary talk, not just reciting the standard labels. I want you to think carefully and show your work, because I'm trying to figure out what I actually believe about speech acts, case by case, and I suspect my verdicts are slightly inconsistent. Before you settle on a principle, brainstorm at least three candidate principles and say which cases each one fails on.

Here's where my gut lands on a bunch of cases.

1. The chair of a meeting says 'the meeting is adjourned' while the group is still working through the last agenda item; everyone silently accepts it. I think the meeting is over. Half an hour later a random person at the back mutters 'the meeting is adjorned' to be funny; nothing happens. I think nothing happened, and it's not just because nobody took them seriously.

2. At a wedding, one partner says 'I do' but while saying it they're thinking 'I actually don't, and I'm leaving at the reception.' I still think the marriage happened, but it's defective somehow. If asked the next day whether they were married at the time, I say yes.

3. A friend says 'I promise I'll pay you back by Friday' with their fingers crossed behind their back, and I know about the crossed fingers. I find myself thinking they did promise, and that the crossed fingers makes it a promise they were lying with, not a non-promise.

4. An actor in a wedding scene says 'I do' with full conviction. Obviously not married. Same words, same sincerity, no marriage.

5. I say to a friend with noise-cancelling headphones on, 'I bet you ten dollars it'll rain tomorrow,' and they walk off without hearing. I think no bet occurred; I only attempted to bet. But if they heard and just ignored me, I think maybe the bet still occurred, or at least something did. I'm less sure here.

6. A judge, sitting on the bench in a regular court, sentences a defendant to probation, but privately believes the defendant is innocent and is only doing it because of a corrupt deal. I think the sentence happened and is fully valid, not merely defective. (Unlike case 2, where the private reservation made the act defective.)

7. A person says to their friend 'I forgive you' after the friend apologizes, but is actually still furious and nurses the grudge for years. I'm torn: it would be weird to say they forgave, but also weird to say the utterance did nothing at all. Maybe it created a public release but not the inner letting-go.

8. A CEO says 'you're fired' to an employee in a fury, with no HR procedure followed and no paperwork, then next morning says 'I didn't mean it, come back.' I think the employee was fired when it was said, and the 'I didn't mean it' retreat didn't un-fire them, though it may have re-hired them. But this clashes with case 5, where without uptake nothing happened, and with case 7, where without the right inner state it only half-happened.

So I've got a working principle, something like: an utterance performs an act just in case there's a standing conventional procedure for doing that thing, the speaker is an authorized participant, and the right circumstances are in place; private mental states don't matter except for making the act sincere or insincere; and pretend contexts don't count. But that principle seems to say case 5 (unheard bet) still created a bet, which my gut rejects, and it says nothing about why case 7 feels so different from case 6.

Please test the principle against each case, see where it breaks, and either repair it or give me the smallest set of principles that captures the pattern. Where my verdicts conflict — like case 5 vs case 6, or case 3 vs case 7 — tell me which verdict you'd overturn and why. And apply the repaired view to a case I keep going back and forth on: at a ship naming, the wrong person smashes the bottle and says 'I name this ship the Sea Slug', and the company's CEO later says 'yes, that's its name, we'll accept it.' Did the naming occur at the original ceremony, at the CEO's ratification, or not at all?
