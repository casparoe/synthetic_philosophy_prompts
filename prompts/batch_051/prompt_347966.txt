An argument with a friend last night left me holding a position on the wrong kind of reasons that I had previously only entertained, and I want to know how exposed it is.

My position is this. The considerations usually classified as wrong-kind reasons for an attitude are real reasons, but they are not reasons for the attitude at all. When an evil demon threatens to punish me unless I admire him, the threat does not give me a reason to admire him. It gives me a reason to bring it about that I admire him, or to want to admire him, or to evade the threat some other way. Admiration is answerable only to the qualities of its object, so no fact about the consequences of holding the attitude can count as a reason to hold it. On this view, the division between right-kind and wrong-kind considerations is not a division within the class of reasons for an attitude; it is a division between reasons for the attitude and reasons of another kind, chiefly practical reasons aimed at acquiring, avoiding, or extinguishing the attitude.

If this is right, the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem does not exactly get solved. It dissolves, or at least gets transformed. We no longer need a criterion that isolates the right kind from the wrong kind among reasons for an attitude; we need only the more general and independently motivated distinction between reasons to believe, desire, or admire something and reasons to act on or manage one's beliefs, desires, and admirations.

I would like you to tell me what would count against this view, and to be more precise than the usual rehearsed objections. Which cases make the redescription into reasons-for-action feel forced or inadequate? Are there examples where a consideration is about the object of the attitude and yet intuitively of the wrong kind, or about the attitude itself and yet intuitively of the right kind? Where does the view stand or fall with the fitting-attitude analysis of value, or with buck-passing accounts, which seem to need right-kind reasons to be reasons for the attitude if they are to explain value at all? What must I assume about the nature of attitudes — for instance, that each attitude-type has a constitutive standard of fittingness or correctness — and what happens if that assumption fails? If it matters for prioritizing, the evidence I would find most compelling is a case in which an honest speaker can find no action or auxiliary attitude to which an alleged wrong-kind reason attaches, and yet the consideration still seems irreducibly to recommend the attitude itself without bearing on whether the object merits it. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish the ones a committed defender of my position would concede as genuine costs from the ones they would reasonably dismiss as changing the subject, and tell me whether the view, charitably developed, is well insulated and, if so, whether that insulation is itself a reason to worry.
