Two of my colleagues got into it last month after a workshop on distributive justice, and it went on long enough that I started taking notes. Their both careful people, so neither was strawmanning the other, which is why I still can't decide who's right. The question was whether parties in the original position should choose principles of justice by maximin, the way Rawls says, or instead assign equal probability to each possible life and maximize expected utility, roughly the Harsanyi line. Here's the exchange as best I remember it.

(1) Dana (the Rawlsian): Behind the veil you have no basis for probabilities over who you'll turn out to be, in any objective or even subjective sense. That makes the choice one of strict uncertainty, not risk, and expected utility simply isn't available. Under strict uncertainty the defensible rule is maximin: pick the principles whose worst outcome is best. Rawls's conditions all hold here: no probabilistic information, the choice concerns the basic structure rather than ordinary gambles, and some alternative principles would leave the worst-off in unacceptable positions.

(2) Miles (the Harsanyi side): The veil doesn't just remove information; it imposes symmetry. You know you're one of the positions in society, and you know nothing that distinguishes them. A rational chooser in a symmetric situation assigns equal probabilities, not by guessing but because any non-uniform assignment would break the symmetry built into the setup. Given standard consistency axioms for preferences, that gives expected utility maximization, and with equal probabilities that's just average utility. So maximin isn't forced; it's one possible risk attitude among many, and an extreme one.

(3) Dana: The axioms you're invoking read probabilities and utilities off a preexisting preference ordering over lotteries. But behind the veil you don't have that ordering: you can't have preferences over being person A rather than person B while ignorant of everything that would ground such preferences. So the equal probabilities are just the principle of insufficient reason dressed up as symmetry. Maximin needs less machinery: comparisons of primary goods across representative positions, not cardinal utilities across persons.

(4) Miles: Maximin needs more machinery than you think. To say one arrangement leaves the worst-off better off, you're already comparing welfare across persons and across arrangements, so you haven't avoided interpersonal comparisons. And maximin ignores every outcome except the worst one. Take two arrangements: the first leaves the bottom at 10 and everyone else at 1000, the second leaves the bottom at 11 and everyone else at 12. Maximin prefers the second, which is absurd. And even if we grant strict uncertainty, why maximin rather than minimax regret? Minimax regret would have you minimize worst-case regret, and it doesn't fixate on the bottom cell the way maximin does. You need a separate argument for picking the most pessimistic member of the whole family of uncertainty rules.

(5) Dana: The 10-versus-1000 arrangement isn't one of the options the parties actually choose among. They choose between whole basic structures, and a society where everyone else is at 1000 while the bottom is at 10 is precisely a society with illegitimate inequalities in status, power, and security, not just a row in a utility table. So the example changes the subject. On regret: expected regret is an expectation, so it needs probabilities; and a non-probabilistic minimax regret measures regret in differences between what you got and what you could have had, which sneaks in exactly the cardinal, interpersonally comparable scale that was supposed to be under dispute. Still, I'll grant that maximin may not be uniquely forced; what I claim is only that it's the appropriate rule for a choice this weighty under ignorance this deep.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
