I am an undergraduate in an upper-level seminar on the deep history of inequality, with eighteen students and an eighty-minute session. I have been asked to lead a session on the claim that the turn to agriculture was, for most of the people who lived through it, a bad bargain in health, leisure, and equality. The course has already covered the hunter-gatherer affluence thesis and the archaeological and ethnographic evidence, so the students know the facts; I want the session to make them feel the structure of the decision rather than hear me summarize the evidence again. By the end, participants should see how a sequence of individually sensible intensification steps can jointly produce a regime that is worse on the very dimensions those steps were meant to improve, and why that makes the agricultural revolution look like a trap even if it is also a rational adaptation to hard constraints.

Please design a multi-round, small-group simulation, using simple counters or paper tokens rather than money or points, in which the class is divided into four or five bands that begin with equal endowments and then decide, across five generations, whether to remain mobile foragers, cultivate part-time, or commit to full agriculture. The rules should be arranged so that each round's cultivation choice looks locally sensible on its own terms: more calories per unit of land, better food security through the lean season, more surviving children. But the cumulative path should produce longer working hours, worse nutrition, rising population, lowered mobility, and stored surplus that can be raided or claimed by an emerging redistributor. I want the participants to hit the ratchet themselves: to notice, a round or two too late, that an earlier return to foraging looked possible but no longer is, because the population, cleared fields, and stores they have committed themselves to would make reversion catastrophic. The game should not have a single winning strategy; part of the point is that a band can improve its food security from round to round while its individual members become worse off. The facilitator should not lecture up front, and the phrase "Neolithic revolution" should not be used until the debrief.

Please include all of the following: the materials and exact rules, including a reversion rule that makes going back cost something real and a surplus rule that lets storage generate inequality within and between groups; a timeline for the eighty minutes covering a brief setup, the rounds, and the debrief; a debrief that maps what participants did in the game onto the hunter-gatherer affluence thesis, the skeletal and ethnographic evidence of declining health, the origins of durable inequality, and the two competing readings of the transition, agriculture as a trap entered step by step versus agriculture as an adaptive response to hard constraints; a paragraph on what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run, such as players gaming the counters, a group finding a collapse-and-restart loophole, or participants treating the simulation as a competition to be won, and how to correct for it; and an honest note on which features of the actual transition the simulation cannot reproduce, such as the scale of millennia, the absence of foreknowledge, and the irreversibility of species and landscape change, and so must be supplied by the facilitator during the debrief.
