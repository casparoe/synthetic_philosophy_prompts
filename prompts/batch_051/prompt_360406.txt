Okay so I fell down a rabbit hole about generics — like the sentences "ducks lay eggs" and "mosquitoes carry malaria." Both sound obviously true even though only female ducks of breeding age lay eggs and only a small handfull of mosquito species actually transmit malaria. Meanwhile "ducks are female" and "mosquitoes are harmless" sound wrong, even though they're statistically way closer to being true. So it's clearly not just about counting individuals.

I started reading around and apparently linguists and philosophers have been arguing about this for decades and nobody has budged. As far as I can tell the main camps are: (1) generics are true when the property is common or typical among the kind, (2) generics are about what's normal for the kind's life form or design, not what's statistically frequent, (3) generics get licensed by properties that are "striking" or that matter to us, like being dangerous, which is why the malaria one slips through, and (4) generics are about the kind having a disposition or causal tendency rather than about how many members actually have the property. And the strange part is everyone seems to agree on which sentences sound right; they just explain them totally differently, and the debate has been stuck for forever.

My working theory is that it isn't one disagreement — some people are asking what has to be true in the world for the sentence to be true, others are asking how our minds generalize from a few instances, and others are asking what the point of saying a sentence with so many exceptions even is. Like three different questions all being called "the semantics of generics." Is that diagnosis right, or is there a real disagreement hiding underneath — about the data, about what counts as an explanation, or about something else? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would actually count as progress: a new type of example, a distinction someone should've drawn, a change of question — or should we just accept they'll never converge? Also if my summary of the camps is off, just say so.
