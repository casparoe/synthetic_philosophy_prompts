I am a third-year undergraduate in an upper-level cognitive science seminar on concepts and categorization, and I need to write a two-page proposal for the course's independent research project. I have a rough idea that I think could be developed into a strong proposal, but I would like help with the framing.

The idea is this. The standard debate among prototype, exemplar, and theory-based accounts of concepts has been driven largely by natural-kind and everyday object categories, where graded typicality, similarity to stored instances, and explanatory causal structure tend to point in the same direction. Letterforms are a revealing case in which these factors pull apart. What makes every 'a' an 'a' is not, on the face of it, any set of shared low-level visual features: across typefaces, stroke weights, and handwriting styles, marks that share almost no measurable features are nevertheless unhesitatingly read as the same letter. At the same time, the category has graded typicality, and it has a distinctive normativity — small variations preserve the letter, while at some point a deformation turns an 'a' into a different letter or a non-letter.

I suspect each main account captures part of this but not all of it. A prototype view explains canonicity better than identity: the most typical 'a' is easy to describe, but many fully competent 'a's depart from it in nearly every respect. An exemplar view explains how novel tokens might be classified by resemblance to remembered instances, but it has trouble explaining the strong sense that a highly unusual token is still exactly an 'a' rather than merely similar to past 'a's. A theory-based account explains the role structure of bowls, stems, and crossbars, but letter identity survives styles in which those roles are deleted, merged, or heavily stylized.
