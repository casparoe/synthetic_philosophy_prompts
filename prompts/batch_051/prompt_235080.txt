I've been going in circles on a debate in philosophy of biology that seems to have stalled in a way I find a bit maddening: whether it's right to say DNA contains a genetic code, or whether 'code' is just a metaphor that got completely out of hand. The people who take the code talk literally point at the codon table. Triplets of nucleotides get mapped to amino acids through tRNAs and aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, and the mapping is chemically arbitrary in the sense that nothing about the chemistry of a codon forces it to mean a particular amino acid — the same codon can be assigned differently in a different organism with slightly different machinery. So, they say, the cell really is 'reading' an arbitrary convention, the way a transcriptionist reads shorthand. The sceptics reply that there's no sender, no reciever with conventions, and no semantic content; it's just an elaborate causal chain, and calling it a code quietly imports intentionality that isn't anywhere in the system. Adding to the muddle, the very molecules that do the 'translation' — the synthetases — are themselves products of the genes they translate, so it's not obvious where the interpreter is. Everyone in this argument knows the molecular biology perfectly well, and they still don't move.

My working hypothesis is that they're not really arguing about biology at all, but about what a thing has to do before it deserves the word 'code': one side is using a thin, structural notion of code as an arbitrary systematic mapping, while the other is using a thick, semantic notion that requires meaning, representation, or at least some sort of sender-receiver relation. But maybe that's too glib, and there's a real empirical question underneath about whether representational vocabularies do causal-explanatory work in genetics. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict: what kind of disagreement is this, and what — if anything — would count as progress — a distinction, a theorem, an origin-of-life result, or just abandoning the word 'code'?
