You are an expert in the history of economic thought and the philosophy of science. I want a carefully argued answer, not a textbook summary. Start by briefly reconstructing Malthus's core argument in your own terms, but keep that section concise; the real work is evaluating whether the argument survives two specific later developments.

Malthus argued roughly as follows. Any human population, when unchecked, tends to grow at a rate that compounds, while the means of subsistence can at best be expanded much more slowly, because land is fixed and cultivation is subject to diminishing returns. Since population always tends to press against the food supply, some check must bring the two into line. The check may operate by raising mortality through famine, epidemic, or war, or by lowering fertility through postponed marriage and self-restraint. The long-run prediction was that the living standards of the mass of humanity remain near subsistence: any improvement in real wages or public relief is only temporary, because it triggers earlier marriage and more births until wages are bid back down.

Two developments since Malthus are usually taken to have broken this argument.

First, the demographic transition. As societies industrialize, mortality declines, and eventually fertility declines as well, not merely through misery or self-denial but through deliberate family limitation as incomes, education, urbanization, and the changed economic value of children alter reproductive choices. Population growth slows or reverses while living standards rise.

Second, the food supply stopped behaving like a fixed, land-limited quantity. Industrial nitrogen fixation, the Green Revolution, mechanization, and other agricultural technologies made output per hectare grow rapidly, so that food production rose faster than population over a sustained period.

Now consider the claim that Malthus was right but early: the underlying idea that population and resources tend toward equilibrium, and that this adjustment can occur either through misery or through changed reproduction, is correct; but the original theory fatally overspecified how quickly the check would operate and what form it would take, and underspecified the role of technology and endogenous fertility decline.

Your task is to give a calibrated verdict on Malthus's argument. Does it survive these developments, does it need repair, or is it dead? Specifically:
