I'm a backend engineer on a payments platform, and a debate has come up on my team that turns on how we should talk about risk; I don't have any philosophy background, so I'd appreciate help sorting out the underlying reasoning. One of our services has a known defect: when it handles more than about four hundred concurrent checkouts, a race condition in the session cache corrupts shared state, and the corruption would, left alone, crash the process almost immediately. The service has never once gone down in production. It is protected by a supervisor thread inside the process that detects the corruption as soon as it begins and immediately rebuilds the cache from the database, fast enough that the crash never happens. The race still occurs under heavy load, and the corrupted state still briefly exists, but it is repaired before the process can fail. Doug, a senior engineer, wants us to move this service out of the high-fragility bucket in our risk register. His argument is that there is no realistic event that would take the system down: the service as actually deployed includes the supervisor, and the supervisor reliably converts every would-be crash into a brief repair. I keep objecting, but I struggle to say exactly what is wrong. The defect is still sitting in the code; if the supervisor were disabled, or if the rebuild ever lagged behind, the process would crash the first time it cleared the threshold. Doug replies that this is like asking how a load-bearing wall would stand with the beams removed.

A second case keeps coming to mind, and it divides people differently. Imagine another service with a similar race condition, but instead of repairing the corruption after it occurs, a watchdog monitors the concurrent-session count and, just before the dangerous level is reached, flips a configuration flag that serializes access to the session cache. This changes the code path so that the race can no longer occur at all, and the process continues running under the higher load. That service has also never crashed, and Doug's rule would also consign it to the low-fragility bucket, since the only realistic path to failure is one the system shuts off in advance. What I want to know is this: are these two services fragile? Is Doug's test — look for a realistic scenario in which the thing would break — the correct test, or does it confuse the system's never actually breaking with a property of the system? And are the two safeguards importantly different, or are they just two ways of reaching the same conclusion?
