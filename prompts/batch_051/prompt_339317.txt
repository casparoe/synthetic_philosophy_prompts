I'm a novelist, in the middle of rewriting a manuscript in which a marine biologist and a philosopher of biology keep arguing about whether a Portuguese man o' war is one animal or a bunch of animals. The trouble is I can only write the obvious version of the argument: it's a colony of clonal zooids, but it sure behaves like a single body — wierd, right? I know the real debate is more subtle than that, and I dont want either character to be a strawman.

Could you walk me through the actual live positions on biological individuality? Not the history of the debate — the conceptual layout. Use the man o' war as the anchor case, and bring in an ant colony (the usual superorganism candidate), an aspen clone, and a human with their gut microbiome (the holobiont case) wherever they sharpen things. I want to know the main criteria people actually use to call something "one organism": physiological integration, genetic homogeneity, being a unit of selection, immune self/nonself discrimination, any others that do real work. And then how those cases come out differently depending on which criterion you pick. Here's what I care about most: where do the criteria themselves conflict or start to look question-begging? When two reasonable criteria put the same case on opposite sides, is there any principled way to say one of them is right, or does the whole dispute partly dissolve into a verbal one? I'd like each character's position stated as strongly as it can be stated, and I want to know what actually hangs on the answer — whether the word "organism" tracks a single real kind or just a loose cluster of features that happen to correlate.
