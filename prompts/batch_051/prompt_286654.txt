I have been trying to work out for myself how a person should act when they are not completely sure which moral view is correct. The question is not which moral view is true, but what to do now, given that I assign some confidence to more than one view and they point in different directions. Before I adopt any answer, I want the full list of requirements that an adequate rule for acting under moral uncertainty has to meet. I am not asking for the rule itself; I am asking for the specification that any proposed rule gets judged against.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and say whether it is non-negotiable or only the preference of one camp. Then flag any subset of the requirements that probably cannot all be fully satisfied at once; that is the part I most want to see. Finally, say which single requirement does the most work in eliminating the rules people usually propose.

In particular, the list should speak to each of the following:

- The comparison problem. Two moral views may not share a common unit for measuring how much better or worse an option is, so a rule that says to maximize expected moral value may be doing arithmetic on scales that were never made comparable. What must a rule do about this instead of pretending the scales line up?
- Permissibility and vetoes. If one view says an act is merely allowed and another says it is badly wrong, when, if ever, should the permissive view win? When should a low-probability view that treats an act as forbidden function as a veto, and when should a near-certain view that the act is fine let me ignore a far-fetched view that it is catastrophic?
- Views with no numbers. Some defensible moral views only rank options as better or worse without saying by how much. The rule must not force such a view into an invented numerical scale merely to make the arithmetic easy.
- Acts versus plans. The rule should say whether it applies to single acts or to whole policies, since a rule that chooses tolerable acts one at a time could produce a life that every view I actually hold considers worse than a plan chosen all at once.
- Uncertainty about the framework itself. If I am uncertain about how to handle moral uncertainty, that looks like the same problem one level up. The rule should say whether it applies to itself or stops the regress somewhere, and where.
- Factual versus moral uncertainty. Some of my uncertainty is about what the world is like and some is about what matters. The rule should state whether these are combined in one calculation or kept apart, and why.
- Wagers and doomed bets. A tiny chance of a very large moral catastrophe, or of a view that demands extreme sacrifice now, can dominate a calculation if the numbers are allowed to run free. Under what conditions should a rule ignore a small probability rather than let it dictate the act?
- Options that keep options open. Whether an adequate rule must sometimes prefer an act that is worse on every view I currently hold because it leaves future choices available if my moral views change.

Then, briefly, score against the list the rule that follows the most likely view, the rule that maximizes expected moral value where the views can be made comparable, and the rule that only forbids acts that every non-negligible view condemns. Keep the scoring short; the requirements themselves are the point.
