I have been defending a position about the relation between first-order and higher-order evidence, and I want to know what it would take for me to be shown wrong. The position is that epistemic akrasia is never rationally permissible. By epistemic akrasia I mean the state of believing a proposition while also judging, of that very belief, that one's total evidence does not support it — the doxastic analogue of judging that one course of action is best while doing another. On the view I find compelling, a rational agent cannot stably occupy this combination: if I acquire higher-order evidence that my first-order assessment is unreliable, I must either move my first-order attitude in response or fail to take the higher-order evidence seriously. It is not a view about what people in fact do; it is a claim about what rationality permits.

What I am asking for is a map of the position's exposure. What specific evidence, arguments, cases, or formal results would count against it, and how decisively? Please sort the candidate defeaters from those that would be fatal to those that would merely be awkward, and for each one say both what it would establish if it succeeded and whether a defender of the anti-akrasia view would themselves concede that it counts. I have in mind the usual suspects: cases in which someone has a clear first-order insight they cannot shake even while their higher-order judgment says they are unreliable in exactly that domain; disagreement with people one regards as epistemic peers; the worry that the view is self-undermining, since it tells one to follow one's first-order judgment at the very moment one has higher-order grounds to distrust it; and accuracy-first or scoring-rule arguments suggesting that a single coherent credence cannot answer simultaneously to first-order and higher-order evidence. Include anything important that I have missed. For each candidate, make clear whether — if it worked — it would show that akrasia is sometimes permissible, that the notion of rational permissibility is doing no work in framing the question, or only that the anti-akrasia position collapses into a nearby rival such as uniqueness or permissivism. Finally, say whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability by its defenders' qualifications, and whether you think that would be a defect for a normative claim of this kind. Tell me how confident you are in your overall assessment, and single out the point you are least sure of.
