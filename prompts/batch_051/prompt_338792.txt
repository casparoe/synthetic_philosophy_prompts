I work as a model risk reviewer at a mid-sized insurance carrier. Each month I evaluate several predictive models that underwriting, claims, or pricing teams want to move into production, and I write a recommendation for a model governance committee that will decide whether the proposed model replaces the incumbent; the committee will not read derivations, so my reasoning has to be defensible in plain language. The decisions affect pricing approval, fraud referral, and claims triage, so the stakes are material, and regulators occasionally review our model choices. I have a statistics background but no training in algorithmic information theory, and I typically have about four hours per model.

My colleagues and regulators keep invoking Occam's razor in a loose way, treating it as self-evident that the simpler model should beat the more complex one whenever performance is roughly equal. I need something more defensible than that slogan, and something I can actually run.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure, grounded in what Solomonoff induction, Kolmogorov complexity, and minimum description length genuinely support or do not support about Occam's razor, for deciding when a simpler predictive model should be preferred over a more complex one — and when it should not be. The procedure must be usable by someone who cannot compute Kolmogorov complexity. It should take the form of an ordered set of questions or tests: what to check first, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure returns a definite recommendation in favor of simplicity or complexity, and where it must stop and hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is an encoding choice or a value tradeoff rather than a factual question.

Make the conceptual translation explicit as you go. Simplicity is not an intrinsic property of a model; it is relative to a description language, and two models from different families may not be comparable in raw complexity terms. Show me how to approximate something like total description length — the length of the model plus the length of the data given the model — using quantities I can actually compute or estimate, such as negative log-likelihood, effective parameter count, compressed size of fitted parameters and residuals, and out-of-sample performance. Flag the traps: counting raw parameters, complexity hidden in hand-engineered features, uncharged hyperparameter and architecture search, and models that compress training noise rather than structure.

Then run the procedure, step by step, on three worked cases:
1. A 30-feature logistic regression slated for replacement by a gradient-boosted tree ensemble with nearly the same AUC but better top-decile lift.
2. A linear pricing model slated for replacement by a modest nonlinear model that reduces residual error substantially on both training and validation data but is harder to explain to regulators.
3. A hand-coded rules engine versus a neural network trained on raw inputs, where the two models do not share a representation space.

At least one of these worked cases should end in a recommendation the asker will not like. Close with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do: where it is estimating an uncomputable quantity, and why the choice of coding scheme is not a mere technical detail but carries real theoretical weight.
