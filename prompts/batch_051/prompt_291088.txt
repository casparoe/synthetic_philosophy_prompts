I need a case built to order for a section I'm drafting on the morality of punishing without fault, and I want it to go against the usual grain. The standard story is that strict liability is the one you'd dread as a defendant — it punishes you even when you did nothing wrong — while a negligence or mens rea rule is the one that respects you as a free agent by asking what you knew, what you foresaw, and what you did about it. I want a realistic scenario where a defendant who actually took reasonable care would still, ex ante, rather live under strict liability than under a negligence rule.

Constraints: ordinary setting (a small construction subcontractor, a landlord, something like that), no exotic technology, no perfect courts, and the scenario itself should be one paragraph. The defendant shouldn't be irrational, self-loathing, or weirdly eager to pay. And to keep it honest, the expected payout under strict liability shouldn't be lower than under negligence — it can even be a bit higher — so the preference has to come from somewhere other than strict liability just being cheaper in expectation. My hunch is the real work is done by hindsight bias in the negligence inquiry plus the fact that strict liability doesn't force a court to publicly decide whether the defendant is to blame, but I want you to check that rather than just take my word for it.

After the scenario, walk through: (a) what a negligence rule woudl do in the case and what strict liability would do, (b) the ex ante calculation from the defendant's point of view, (c) which feature or features of the case actually generate the preference — isolate the one doing the work, (d) what happens if you remove that feature (say, make the court accurate about whether care was reasonable, or strip the stigma out of a negligence finding), and (e) how a defender of fault-based liability would attack the case and whether you think the attack lands. The point of the case for me is to argue that punishment without fault can sometimes be less disrespectful of agency than fault-finding after the fact, so stipulating the preference won't cut it — I need the mechanism.
