Bit of an odd one, but I've ended up running a weekly metaphysics lunch at work (long story, involving a failed book club and a stubborn colleague), and last session we got onto Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles. I made the mistake of saying Black's two-spheres universe was obviously a knock-down counterexample, and now I can't stop going over it.

For next week I've sketched an essay that I'd like comments on before I get too far. The idea is that Black's example and quantum indistinguishability are really the same problem in different dress: how to count things when no property does the counting. In Black's universe you can say there are two iron spheres two miles apart, but the description can't say which is which; every qualitative fact true of one is true of the other. The usual relational dodge doesn't help, because in an otherwise empty and completely symmetric space the only candidate relational properties are symmetric ones like 'is two miles from a sphere', and both spheres have them. My move is then to say that 'there are two' in both cases is best read as a property of the whole system — the universe having sphere-count 2, or the quantum state having occupation number 2 — rather than a relation between two already-individuated items. So Black hasn't refuted the principle about individuals; he's shown that cardinality can be a holistic feature, and the principle isn't actually in play.

I'm particulary unsure whether the quantum analogy earns its keep or is just decoration, and whether the holistic-count move is a principled distinction or a face-saving dodge. Could you comment on those two points, and also flag any place where I've mangled either Black's setup or the force of the original principle? And be honest about whether the conclusion is even interesting — I've been at this to long and may have lost all perspective.
