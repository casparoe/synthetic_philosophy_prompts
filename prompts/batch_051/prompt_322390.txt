Last week in further maths club I tried to prove that the axiom of choice is not really an axiom at all. My teacher looked at the argument and said only that the logic was 'too fast', but would not tell me why. I have written the argument out as numbered steps.

(1) Take any collection of non-empty sets, finite or infinite.
(2) By the definition of 'non-empty', each set in the collection has at least one member.
(3) Therefore, for each set in the collection, there is at least one member that could, in principle, be selected.
(4) Since step (3) holds for every set, there must be a single rule, list, or function that selects one member from every set in the collection at once.
(5) So, for any collection of non-empty sets, there is a choice function: a function that assigns to each set exactly one of its own members.
(6) The axiom of choice says precisely that every collection of non-empty sets has a choice function.
(7) Therefore the axiom of choice follows from the meaning of 'non-empty', and treating it as a separate axiom looks redundant.

I cannot see what is wrong, yet the conclusion feels too strong, because if it were this easy nobody would have found the principle controversial. Is the argument valid?
