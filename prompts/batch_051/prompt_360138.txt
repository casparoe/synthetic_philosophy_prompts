I help run a small rationality training group — a sort of dojo — that meets weekly for two hours. The group grew out of a rationalist reading circle, and our sessions have drifted toward discussion and away from practice. The idea I keep returning to is that rationality can be trained the way martial arts are trained: with katas, meaning small, repeatable drills that isolate a single cognitive move — a pre-mortem, a prediction registered with a prior, a forced update on disconfirming evidence, a check for the affect heuristic — and that are practiced until the move compiles into something more automatic. The hard part, as in martial arts, is transfer: people get fluent at the kata inside the dojo and never deploy the move in the wild, or they deploy it woodenly at the wrong moment. I have a working distinction I want any procedure you give me to respect. It seems to me a kata can train at least three different things: executing a move when prompted to execute it, recognizing in real time that a live situation calls for the move, and calibrating the internal feeling that should act as the trigger. A drill can improve one of these while leaving the other two untouched, and I suspect most of our current drills only train execution.

What I want from you is a reusable decision procedure — a checklist or flowchart in words — for taking any candidate drill from intake through a decision to run it, adapt it, or drop it, with the transfer problem treated as the central bottleneck rather than an afterthought. The procedure should be usable by me and the two other co-instructors, none of whom are professional philosophers or cognitive scientists, and it should fit our constraints: drills must run in twenty minutes or less, participants are bright non-specialists who will not do homework, and I am the one who ultimately signs off on the curriculum but I want the procedure to be executable by the other instructors without my judgment being silently smuggled in. Please think carefully before answering, and start by brainstorming what a good kata would have to include — for example, the cue in the wild, the move itself, the feedback signal that tells the practitioner whether the move was done well, and a plausible path from the dojo version to the messy real-life version. Then give me the procedure itself, with the steps in the order they should be run, the hard cases it has to handle, and the points where judgment cannot be eliminated marked explicitly as such. Apply the procedure to two or three candidate drills as worked examples, and make at least one of them a case where the procedure tells me to drop or gut a drill I am attached to. Be honest about what the procedure cannot do: it should not pretend to measure transfer it has not actually checked for.
