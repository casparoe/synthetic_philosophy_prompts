I have spent the past several months caring for my father, who has Parkinson's disease, and the way his symptoms fluctuate with his medication has made an abstract philosophical problem feel painfully concrete. I would like you to help me reason through it.

Here are the criteria I was taught for something's being a genuine cause of an event:

(i) Difference-making: if the candidate cause had been absent or relevantly different, the event would not have occurred or would have differed.
(ii) Non-redundancy: the candidate does not merely accompany or track the real work of some other cause.
(iii) Manipulability: one could in principle alter the event by intervening on the candidate while holding other relevant factors fixed.
(iv) Specificity: the candidate explains the particular character of the event, not merely the fact that some event or other occurred.

Now consider the case. During my father's off periods, after his levodopa has worn off, he forms a clear intention to rise from his chair but cannot initiate the movement; he says his body will not cooperate. An hour after taking his medication, with what seems to be the same intention, he rises smoothly. The neurologist explains it this way: the cells that supply his basal ganglia with dopamine are dying, and without sufficient dopamine the motor program his intention has selected cannot be released. If one holds the intention fixed, the difference between staying seated and standing tracks the dopamine level rather than the intention. Yet the intention is not idle: he never stands unless he intends to stand, and the dopamine does not decide that he should stand rather than sit. This strikes me as a lived version of the causal exclusion problem: when a mental state and its neural realizer are both candidates for being the cause, the neural state can look like the complete cause and leave nothing for the mental state to do.

On the four criteria above, does my father's intention qualify as a genuine cause of his standing up on the occasions when he does rise? Take each criterion in turn, and say where the criteria underdetermine the verdict—for instance, whether the answer depends on which contrast class one chooses. Then say whether the case shows that the criteria themselves need revision: perhaps they confuse a mental state with the physical machinery that implements it, or they conflate an action's being selected with its being enabled. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would most plausibly push you to the opposite verdict, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
