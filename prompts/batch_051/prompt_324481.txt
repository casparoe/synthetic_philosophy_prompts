Consider a counterfactual in constitutional design. In 2040 a newly founded city of two hundred thousand adults adopts a founding charter requiring that every binding public decision be made through a single social-choice procedure satisfying three clauses. First, universal domain: the procedure must accept any combination of complete, transitive preference rankings the citizens happen to have over the options on the table, with no pre-filtering of admissible preferences. Second, weak Pareto: if every citizen strictly prefers one option to another, the procedure may not rank the latter above the former. Third, a personal-decisiveness clause: for each citizen, there is at least one pair of options that differ only in some designated private matter of that citizen's life—whether they publish a manuscript, accept a marriage proposal, or practice a form of worship—and the citizen's strict preference between those two options is to be conclusive for the social ranking, regardless of what anyone else prefers. The drafters know in the abstract that the three clauses can conflict: with the right pattern of preferences, each clause forces a ranking that one of the other clauses forbids. They judge such cases to be logical curiosities unlikely to surface in ordinary governance, and they add no priority rule establishing which clause wins in a conflict.

Predict, with reasoning, how this city governs itself over the next thirty years. I want the chain of mechanisms, not a restatement of the impossibility result: which clause gives way first when a conflict arises, whether the retreat is open or disguised, how the legal and political system rationalizes the violation, whether the resulting arrangement is stable or cycles between interpretations, and what the city's actual decision procedure looks like in practice after a generation. Mark clearly which parts of your prediction you regard as robust under almost any plausible model of political behavior, which parts are guesses about this particular polity, and state at least one condition that would have to hold for the original three-clause charter to survive in recognizably binding form.
