I teach a senior seminar on ancient political thought, and we've spent three weeks crawling through Politics I on natural slavery, so the passage is in front of me. At 1254b, the translation I'm using runs: "For he is by nature a slave who is capable of belonging to another (and that is why he does so belong), and who participates in reason so far as to apprehend it but not to possess it." Aristotle then sets this off against nonhuman animals, which are moved by feelings rather than by reasons they can grasp.

A student in the seminar works in an AI lab and keeps insisting we test this definition against a system her lab calls a "deferential reasoner." It is not a human being. It can take in its principal's stated reasons, restate them, flag when a proposed plan conflicts with them, and generate new plans that track them; but it has no final ends of it's own, cannot revise its top-level objectives, is owned outright by the lab, and exists entirely to serve whatever goals its principal gives it. The student's question—which I'd like to take seriously rather than dismiss as a category error—is whether Aristotle's criterion, read closely, reaches this system: it seems to apprehend reason without possessing it, and it seems to belong to another. I want to work through, separately, what the bare words give, what the purpose of the natural-slave distinction gives, what Aristotle-if-asked would most plausibly say about a reason-sensitive artifact (a category he didn't have), and what a reasonable reader should do with the fact that the passage says "man" and grounds the argument in nature and bodily function rather than in artifacts. Where does each approach land? Is there a coherent reading on which the definition applies but the naturalness argument does not? And what would you change in the case to flip the answer? American English, please.
