i've been half-reading about this old problem called bradley's regress and i want someone to check a theory i've been fiddling with. the problem as i understand it: a proposition like "the tomato is red" is meant to be tomato plus redness plus some instantiation relation gluing them together. but then the glue needs another relation to glue it to the tomato and the redness, and that new relation needs another one, and so on, forever. every time you add the glue it becomes just another item in the list that then needs gluing. so either you get a vicious regress or the unity of the proposition stays a mystery.

my theory is that the regress is lazy. in functional programming an infinite list like `ones = 1 : ones` is perfectly well-defined even though it has no base case, because the recursive call sits under a constructor and you only evaluate as much of it as you demand. the proposition is the same kind of coinductive structure: the instantiation relation is a node whose fields are the tomato, redness, and a thunk which, when forced, returns the next instantiation relation. there's no base case, but there dosent need to be one, because the unity isnt the final item of an expansion -- it's the fact that the generator is productive at every step. asking what stops the regress is like asking for the last digit of pi: a category mistake, not a gap.

i've been calling it coinductive unity, or lazy instantiation. i realise i might just be restating the problem in haskell cosplay. is there a real difference between this and the standard move that says the regress is harmless? does it actually explain how a bunch of parts become a single proposition, or does it just rename the fact that they do? where does the analogy break? be brutal.
