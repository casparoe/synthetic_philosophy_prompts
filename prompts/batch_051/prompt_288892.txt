I keep going in circles on the whole "is the world becoming less violent" debate and I want someone to diagnose the debate itself rather than give me another take. The pro-decline side says: look at the long-run numbers, war deaths and homicide rates per capita are way down compared to any premodern period you can reconstruct, and the trend even shows up within the last century depending how you count. The anti-decline side says: per capita is a trick, absolute death tolls grew because population grew; "violence" keeps being redefined to leave out famines, colonial state violence, structural stuff, drone warfare with no reliable body count, and so on; and the further back you go the more the data is just guesses, so the historical graph is half artifact. Both sides seem to accept roughly the same numbers and still nobody moves. My hypothesis is that it's really three arguments stacked in a trenchcoat: (1) a verbal dispute about whether "violence" means direct interpersonal harm or also includes things like the state and economic arrangements, (2) a value question about whether per-capita or absolute is the normatively relevant framing, and (3) a factual disagreement about ancient baselines that will be permanently underdetermined. Is that diagnosis right? If not, what's the actual structure? I want to know which parts are substantive, which are just differnt questions being called one question, and whether there's any fact of the matter about the headline claim at all. I'd rather get the structure than a verdict.
