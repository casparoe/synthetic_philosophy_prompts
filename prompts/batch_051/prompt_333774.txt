I have recently been arguing, in a curriculum review committee, about whether to replace a discussion-based seminar with a polished online course that produces the same assessment results with less wasted time, and the dispute has forced me to think through what the activity of inquiry actually adds. Consider a variation on Nozick's experience machine. A completed-knowledge machine can, in one night and without effort or risk, give you all and only the true, justified beliefs in a domain you currently care about. You would wake up knowing the domain completely; you would have no memory of the process, and because nothing in that domain would remain unknown to you, you would never again feel curiosity, surprise, or the pull of a new problem there. Work through what each of the following would say about whether to enter: a hedonist, a Nozick-style opponent of the original experience machine, and a defender of the view that knowledge is worth having for its own sake. Does the original experience-machine argument transfer to this case, or does it only show that we would refuse a machine of illusions, while remaining neutral on whether the value of knowledge lies in the resulting cognitive state rather than in the activity of inquiry? If you would refuse, say whether that commits you to treating curiosity as a mere instrument for acquiring knowledge, as an independent good, or as an intellectual virtue partly constitutive of a good epistemic life.
