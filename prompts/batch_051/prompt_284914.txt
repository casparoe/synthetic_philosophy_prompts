Two people in my philosophy reading group got pretty heated about the grandfather paradox the other night, and I’m still not sure who to side with. They’d both read David Lewis’s paper The Paradoxes of Time Travel (1976), or at least the section on the grandfather case, but they came away with completely opposite lessons. Here’s roughly the exchange as I remember it:

A: Lewis’s real point is that Tim can kill Grandfather, because he has the gun, the training, the opportunity, the motive—nothing about Tim’s local circumstances stops him. The reason he doesn’t is a separate issue: the fact that Grandfather survives afterward is itself a fact, and that fact is inconsistent with Tim killing him. So there’s no contradiction unless you smuggle in the same fact twice under two different descriptions of “can.”

B: That’s just equivocation dressed up as a solution. If the actual history includes Grandfather’s survival, then Tim cannot kill him, full stop. Saying he “can” in some narrow, ability-only sense just means he would kill him if the past were different. But the past isn’t different, and that’s exactly the problem. The paradox hasn’t been dissolved; it’s been renamed.

A: But ordinary language works that way too. You can speak French, but you cannot speak French while asleep. Both uses are legitimate, and the grandfather paradox only looks like a contradiction because we shove the ability sense and the compossibility-with-all-facts sense together.

B: That analogy is off. In the asleep case, a temporary state blocks the exercise. In the grandfather case, the act being performed is not just blocked—it would erase the conditions of the agent’s own existence. That’s a deeper kind of impossibility. Calling it “not compossible” doesn’t tell us anything new.

A: You’re treating “would erase his own existence” as if it’s a causal force reaching back and tying Tim’s hands. But at the moment he’s standing there with the gun, nothing local stops him from firing. The fact that he will in fact fail is a global constraint, like the fact that I won’t finish my whole to-do list today—not a fact about any particular item on the list being impossible right now.

B: If he will fail with certainty, then “can” is just doing modal-logic work and nothing practical. A live ability requires a live possibility. If the setup guarantees he fails, then it was never a live possibility, and the whole scenario is hiding the contradiction inside the word “can.”

A: The setup doesn’t guarantee it independently of his own failure—the guarantee is just the fact of what happened, which includes his failure. That’s no different from saying a deterministic prediction known in advance makes the agent unable to falsify it. You’re importing a general argument about freedom and foreknowledge, not a specific paradox about time travel.

B: I don’t think it’s the same. Determinism is causal, but here the impossibility is logical: the history contains both Tim’s birth and Grandfather’s survival, so the killing is ruled out by the very specification of the case. That’s special to time travel, and compossibility language just papers over it.

I couldn’t referee this because every time one of them sounded right, the other came back with something that seemed right too. Could you adjudicate move by move? For each step, say which claims you think are solid, which overreach, and how you’d resolve or reframe the underlying disagreement.
