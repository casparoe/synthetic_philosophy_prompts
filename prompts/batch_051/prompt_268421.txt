I have been turning over a parallel between two things that do not usually meet, and I want a committed verdict on whether it is genuine rather than a survey of possible readings.

The first is the weak version of linguistic relativity (the defensible end of what is often called the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis): not the claim that language sets a ceiling on thought, but the claim that the distinctions a grammar makes cheap, obligatory, or easy to mark shape what speakers habitually encode, recall, and find natural to reason about. The second is a phenomenon in formal and computational notation: the choice of representation in mathematics or programming makes some objects and transformations primitive and others derived, and this in turn shapes which generalizations look natural, which proofs or programs are short, and which mistakes are easy to make. One encodes a differential equation in coordinate-free language or in a particular basis; one chooses a type system under which a whole class of erroneous states cannot be expressed at all; one switches from lists to sets to make order irrelevant. These choices look to me like the same kind of cognitive scaffolding as grammatical categories.

The obvious disanalogy is that a notation is deliberately designed for a task and can be revised when it misleads, whereas a natural language is acquired early, shared by a community, and not in the control of any individual speaker. But I suspect that disanalogy may be downstream: both could be cases in which a representational system supplies affordances and blind spots, with the difference lying only in how the system is acquired and who can change it.

I want the parallel checked rather than praised. In particular, I am treating distinctions made cheap or obligatory by a grammar as corresponding to constructs made primitive or convenient by a notation, and habitual patterns of attention and reasoning in speakers as corresponding to which theorems are easy to state and which definitions look natural to practitioners. Please take a definite position on whether that mapping is right, say what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer between them, and give at least one specific prediction the parallel would make that fails on one side. Then commit: is this a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy with a real but limited core, or a confusion—and why?
