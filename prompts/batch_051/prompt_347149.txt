I’m a PhD student in legal philosophy, writing a dissertion chapter on how judges draw the line between actionable statements of fact and protected expressions of opinion in defamation law. I’ve been coding the opinions of one state appellate judge—I’ll call her Judge Mears—because she’s unusally candid about the test she’s actually applying, not just the formula she cites. Here are five of her rulings, with the facts simplified. (1) A restaurant critic’s one-star review calling the risotto “a soggy, inedible failure” was protected opinion. (2) An investment analyst’s note cutting a stock to Underperform with a $12 price target, which added “our checks with four suppliers indicate orders have collapsed,” was actionable when it turned out the suppliers had never been contacted. (3) A columnist’s tweet calling a congressman “a liar and a crook” was protected hyperbole. (4) A review-site post saying “in my opinion, this contractor is unlicensed” was actionable, because the license was a matter of public record and the “in my opinion” did not matter. (5) A rating agency’s downgrade of a city’s bonds, based on published financials that it disclosed accurately but weighted differently than the city would have liked, was protected.

The case I can’t predict: a company publishes an algorithmically generated “quality score” for nursing homes, giving one facility 23/100 and saying it “scores below the state average on every measure we track.” The fine print says the score is “an estimate generated from public data and is not a statement about any specific incident.” The public data file the company pulled was out of date; with current numbers, that facility is above the state average on three of the tracked measures, and the nursing home sues over the “below average” line and the score. If you’re willing to play judge, I want a consistency analysis rather than a summary of the law: what are the candidate rules that fit her first five rulings, which of those five is the closest analog here, give me a best guess, and spell out what Judge Mears would be committing herself to about ratings and algorithms if she went each way. Take your time and work through the candidates rather than jumping to a verdict. And if my framing is off—say the fact/opinion label is doing less work in her cases than something like “was there a checkable false premise, and would a reasonable reader treat it as baked into the score?”—tell me that plainly. I’d rather have my coding scheme corrected than get a tidy answer.
