In my discussion group people keep defending a claim about concepts by reaching for the family-resemblance idea: members of a concept are like members of a family, held together by a criss-crossing network of overlapping similarities rather than by one essential feature, so asking for the defining trait of, say, a game is a mistake. As someone who spent a career on heredity and variation, I keep noticing that real families are held together by something the analogy quietly drops in: shared descent, a common genome, a branching history. I want to know how far the analogy actually carries. Map it out for me — what corresponds to what (the parents, the offspring, the shared traits, the family name, the unrelated neighbor, and so on), where the mapping breaks, which of those breaks actually matter for the conclusion my group wants (that concepts needn't have essences and definitions are usually hopeless), and whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the family picture was carrying the argument the whole time. Exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
