I've spent the last week going down a costly-signaling rabbit hole after a forum argument about whether expensive weddings still count as honest signals when everyone knows they're performative. Like anyone who's hung around the rationalist internet, I'm primed to see signaling everywhere, but I keep tripping on the relationship between the old theory and how trust actually works now. The classic line — Zahavi's handicap principle, and the Grafen-style formalizations — says a signal is reliable only if it costs the sender enough that a low-quality sender can't afford it: think peacock tails, conspicuous consumption, expensive displays of commitment. Honesty is guaranteed by the sender's cost; otherwise it's cheap talk and recievers should ignore it.

But a lot of modern honest signaling seems to run on something else entirely. A background check, a degree verification, an escrow account, a cryptographic proof, a public rating system with real consequences — these make a claim credible because an outside party verifies it, or because lying gets punished down the line, not because sending the signal itself is a handicap. The sender pays almost nothing at the margin. Does the handicap principle survive this? Is the cost really just displaced onto the verifier or into a reputation the sender would lose, or does the cheap-verified-signal case show that sender-side cost was never the core mechanism — just one way among several to align incentives? I want to know which premise actually breaks and whether there's a repaired version that's weaker but still worth holding onto. Looking for the argument, not a literature review.
