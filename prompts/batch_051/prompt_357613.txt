Take Mencius's child-at-the-well case. In the familiar scenario, someone who suddenly sees a small child about to fall into a well feels alarm and concern before he can calculate any advantage—whether winning the parents' gratitude, earning neighbors' praise, or avoiding a reputation for callousness. Mencius takes this uncalculated response as evidence that human beings have an innate sprout of compassion, which proper education and self-cultivation can develop. Xunzi denies that the untutored heart contains any such moral sprout: what good appears in human conduct is built through deliberate effort, ritual, and instruction, operating on a nature otherwise inclined toward desire and contention.

Now vary one feature of the case. The observer is not an ordinary passerby but someone whose years of professional training have systematically taught her to override the impulse to respond to another person's distress—an interrogator trained to treat every display of distress as a possible manipulative performance. When this observer sees the child about to fall into the well, she registers the danger accurately but feels no spontaneous alarm and no inclination to help.

What would Mencius and Xunzi each predict about such an observer, and how would each explain her response after the fact? Does the variation leave their disagreement intact, or does it now favor one side? If they still diverge, identify the further observation that would discriminate between them; if they converge, specify the smallest additional change to the case that would separate them again.
