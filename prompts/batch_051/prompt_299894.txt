I've been stuck on the paradox of fiction in a weirdly practical way. My officemate, who works in one of the big emotion labs, insists that when a person cries at the end of a film the sadness is the same kind of state as sadness about a real loss — maybe weaker or shorter, but the same type of emotion. I find that hard to believe. It seems to me the thing we feel toward a fictional character is more like what some philosophers call a quasi-emotion: it borrows some of the surface features of emotion (tears, a lump in the throat, a racing heart when the killer appears) but it lacks the deeper functional profile of a real emotion, like actually priming us to act, or persisting in ways that reorganize our goals once the credits roll. But honestly I don't know how I'd tell the difference except by stipulating a definition.

Since you're good at operationalizing concepts without pretending the concept disappears: design the single best empirical test you can of the claim that the sadness or fear people report feeling for fictional characters is the same emotion-type as sadness or fear about real people. Be concrete about the manipulation, the measure, and what pattern of results would favour each side. Then say what a positive result would and wouldn't settle — in particular, whether a test showing the bodily profile is identical could ever settle whether calling it "the same emotion" is right, or whether that last step stays conceptual no matter what the data show. And if you think the claim is untestable as stated, say what weaker, testable version of it you would substitute. I have a feeling their is a cleaner experiment here than we've been able to see.
