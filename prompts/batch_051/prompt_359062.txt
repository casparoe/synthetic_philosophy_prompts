I have been trying to isolate the scope of superrationality: the practice, made famous by Hofstadter's writing on the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, of reasoning in symmetric, no-communication games as if my own choice and the other player's choice must be the same, because the other player is in the identical situation and is an equally good reasoner. I have a set of verdicts on particular cases, and I would like to know whether there is a single principle behind them, what that principle predicts for cases I have not listed, and which of my verdicts it forces me to give up.

My verdicts are as follows. (1) In a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with symmetric payoffs, no communication, and an instruction sheet telling each of us that the other is a careful reasoner in the identical situation, I cooperate: if I were to cooperate, a mirroring reasoner would cooperate, and if I were to defect, a mirroring reasoner would defect, and I prefer both-cooperate to both-defect. (2) Same game, but the other player is a random person from the general public with no special tendency to reason as I do; I defect. (3) Same symmetric game, except that the other player has already chosen and the choice sits in a sealed envelope; the other, before choosing, reasoned from the same symmetric description and knew I would choose afterward. I still cooperate. (4) A one-shot dilemma with asymmetric payoffs: both matrices have defection dominant, but I would prefer the jointly cooperative outcome while the other would prefer the jointly defective outcome. I defect, because each of us would settle on a different 'same choice,' so the mirroring assumption cannot get started. (5) A symmetric Stag Hunt; I hunt the stag without much hesitation, since there is no conflict between what I prefer conditional on the other player's choice and what I prefer for the two of us jointly. (6) A symmetric minority game: N identical careful reasoners each pick left or right, the minority side wins, and if everyone picks the same side nobody wins. Here I think superrational reasoning goes silent, since there is no single choice everyone could make and thereby win, and I randomize 50/50. (7) The same one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma as (1), except that the other is a close friend whom I know reasons about abstract puzzles almost exactly as I do, though we have never discussed this one. I lean cooperate, but with much less confidence than in (1).

Please give me a committed account rather than a menu of options: formulate the principle or small set of principles that best captures these verdicts, stated precisely; show how it delivers each of (1) through (7) or which of them it overturns; and say what it predicts for some cases I have not listed — for example, a symmetric three-player game with two mutually exclusive ways for everyone to act identically, both superior to any divided outcome, and a one-shot symmetric Prisoner's Dilemma in which I estimate the other is about 80% likely to mirror my choice. I would rather have a simple principle that forces me to revise one or two of my verdicts than a baroque epicycle that saves all of them, provided you tell me honestly which verdicts have to go and why the intuition behind them was misleading.
