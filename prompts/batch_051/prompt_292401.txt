I'm a software engineer with no real philosophy background, but I keep getting into this argument about whether the laws of nature are something over and above the actual sequence of events, or just summaries we impose on the sequence. I can only think about it in programming terms, so here's the setup I'd like you to use. Write a dialogue between two engineers, M and H, who have the complete state history of a tiny simulated universe up to the present tick (say a 100-by-100 cellular automaton run for a few billion ticks), but they don't have the source code and can't open the box. They're also not allowed to settle the argument by appealing to whoever built the simulator; the only evidence is the sequence itself. M holds the Humean view: the laws of this little universe just are the shortest program (or best compression) you can fit to the log, and two programs that both fit the data so far are just two summaries of the same facts, with no hidden fact about which one is "really" driving the box. H holds the anti-Humean view: the executable is real, it constrains and explains the sequence, and two programs that match the past but diverge on the next tick describe genuinely different possible worlds, even though nothing in the data can tell them apart. Make them actually argue, no strawmen: each has to restate the other's position in a way the other accepts. They should work through at least: whether the law explains the data or the data explains the law; what a counterfactual like "if tick 42,000 had been different, the machine would have gone here" could even mean; and wether a distinction you could never detect is still a real distinction. Don't let them use the labels "Humean" or "anti-Humean" in the dialogue; keep it in engineer talk. End with each conceding at least one point, not just agreeing to disagree.
