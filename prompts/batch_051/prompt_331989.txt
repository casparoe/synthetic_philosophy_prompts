I'm trying to get clear on the different ways a deterministic system can resist explanation even in principle—cases where you know the exact rule but there's no way to learn what it will do except by running it or by citing the whole output. The list I have so far: (1) chaos, where errors in the initial conditions grow exponentially so any imprecise measurement eventually becomes a different trajectory; (2) undecidability, where there's no general algorithm for telling whether an arbitrary program halts, let alone what it computes; (3) Wolfram's computational irreducibility, where a simple rule (Rule 30 being the poster child) has behavior with no shortcut—the only way to know step n is to simulate all n steps; (4) Kolmogorov incompressibility, where an output has no description shorter than itself, so the only complete 'explanation' of it is just the thing. I've got the nagging feeling there's a fifth mechanism of a genuinely different kind—one where the no-shortcut verdict doesn't come from initial-condition sensitivity, diagonalization, brute simulation, or incompressibility. Give me one thing that belongs on this list but isn't just a special case, restatement, or combination of items (1)–(4). Then tell me honestly whether it earns its place, or whether on reflection it collapses into one of the four.
