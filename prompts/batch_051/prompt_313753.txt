At the pub last week, two of my friends got into a long argument about whether argument itself could be a sport, and whether treating it as one would improve it or wreck it. I kept out of it mostly, but I've been turning it over since. Could you go through what each of them said and adjudicate it move by move — who's right about which specific point, where either of them overreaches, and what neither of them thought of?

Dan: Competitive debating already basically is a sport. There are two sides, a judge, time limits, a winner. The obvious move is just to admit that and build proper leagues, rankings, maybe even spectators and coaches. If we did that, argument would get better the way tennis got better once it went professional.

Priya: But a sport is scored against its own rules. An argument is supposed to be scored against the world, if it's scored at all. If you turn it into a sport, you're changing the aim from "is this true or well-supported?" to "does this win?" Thats a different activity.

Dan: That's like saying chess can't be a sport because it doesn't tell you about the world. Chess is all about winning inside its rules, and we still think it makes people sharper.

Priya: Chess is sealed off from the world, so it can be evaluated entirely by its own rules. Arguing is always arguing about something — immigration, the budget, whether a vaccine works. Whether a move in an argument is any good depends on subject-matter facts, not on a rulebook. As soon as judges become the scoreboard, the subject matter stops being the scoreboard.

Dan: But judging already happens. Student debating competitions have judges, and people who do them seem to get better at thinking on their feet.

Priya: Do they get better at thinking, or at the sport? You can win a competitive debate while being wrong about the motion. The format rewards speed, confidence, and sounding clever; it doesn't reward being right.

Dan: So what? Every sport suffers from judging mistakes. Boxing has bad scorecards. Doesn't mean we shouldn't have boxing.

Priya: A bad boxing scorecard is a mistake about boxing. A bad debate decision is a mistake about the actual topic. And once the game is optimised for convincing three specific people in the room, it drifts toward rhetoric. You'd get more polished public lying, not better public reasoning.

Dan: Only if you set it up badly. You could pick judges for subject-matter expertise, score people for conceding points, penalise spraying thirty weak objections because they take too long to rebut. The reason public argument is in such a bad state now is that it's never been treated as a craft with spectators, replays, coaching. Professionalising it would put careful reasoning in front of an audience.

Priya: Spectators are exactly the problem. A sport needs fairly even contests to be watchable, so you'd manufacture balanced sides even when one side is clearly right. You'd turn every empirical question into a round-earth side and a flat-earth side for the sake of a good match, and the flat-earth side would just get better at beating experts on the day.

Dan: But that already happens on television! The news books two people to shout at each other precisely because there's no structured alternative. A properly run sport would at least give us transparent scoring, records, and some accountability for bad arguments over time.

Priya: Accountability to whom? To the same judges you've trained the players to please. Sometimes the best outcome is that no one wins, because the evidence is mixed — but a sport can't leave the final as a draw every week. It has to crown a champion.

Dan: Draws exist in football, and you still get champions eventually. Anyway, the question isn't whether we'd get it right on day one. It's whether treating argument as a sport would improve it overall. I think yes — and the fact you keep appealing to actually being right just shows the rules would need to be built around that.

Priya: Or maybe it shows the analogy is doing more work than it can bear. Reasoning's final test isn't a scoreline. Bolt a scoreline onto it and you give people an incentive to optimise the scoreline, full stop.

So, please go through this exchange and say who's right at each turn — not just who wins overall. I want to know which moves are sound, which ones are dodgy, and whether there's a way of putting together the best version of each position. In particular, is there a version of "sport" where Dan's point about professionalisation goes through without Priya's worry about the score replacing the truth, or is one of them just confusing two different activities?
