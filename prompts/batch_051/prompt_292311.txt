I am mapping the logical space of accounts of shared intention — roughly, the state two or more people are in when they intend to do something together, rather than merely each intending the same outcome in parallel. My running catalogue looks like this.

1. Searle's we-intention view: shared intention consists in each party's having an irreducible we-intention, an individual intention whose content is that one is doing one's part as part of our doing X, and which cannot be analyzed into an ordinary I-intention plus beliefs about the others.

2. Bratman's interlocking-intention view: shared intention is a web of individual intentions that the group perform the joint action, together with meshing subplans and common knowledge of these intentions; there is no collective attitude over and above suitably interrelated individual attitudes.

3. Gilbert's joint commitment view: shared intention is a joint commitment of two or more persons to intend, as a body, to do X; it creates directed rights and obligations among the parties, and cannot be rescinded unilaterally or reduced to a conjunction of personal intentions.

4. Tuomela and Miller's we-mode view: intending together requires a shared we-mode intention held from the group's perspective as a member, backed by the group's ethos and collective commitment, and not analyzable into private pro-attitudes plus mutual belief.

5. Kutz's participatory-intention view: agents act together when each has a participatory intention — an intention to make one's contribution to a joint project, where the content of the intention includes a conception of the collective act and of one's own role in it; sameness of conception and common knowledge are not required.

6. Team reasoning: the unit of decision is the team rather than the individual; each participant asks what we should do relative to a team objective and performs their component of the team-optimal plan, rather than maximizing their own payoff given expectations of what the others will do.

I want a seventh entry whose underlying structure differs from all six, not a rewording, special case, or combination of items already on the list. It would help if it avoided the three recurring moves these six share: reducing shared intention to interrelated individual attitudes, postulating an irreducible we-attitude in the individual, and imputing a group-level commitment or subject of agency. If, in your view, no such seventh account exists, construct the strongest candidate you can and then say, with the same care, where it threatens to collapse into one of the existing six. For whichever entry you give, explain what it captures naturally and what it renders obscure, and compare it to the closest members of the list.

Then step back and say what would change your mind about your own verdict: which argument or consideration would lead you to classify the proposed account as a variant of an existing one, or to abandon it as no account of shared intention at all. Finally, say where in your reasoning you are least confident.
