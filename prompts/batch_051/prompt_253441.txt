I have been teaching the Mencius–Xunzi dispute on human nature for years, and it has started to seem to me that the debate stalls in a distinctive way: both sides appear to accept much of the same evidence about human behavior, and both sides agree that moral self-cultivation is essential, yet neither side can move the other from the slogans that human nature is good or that human nature is bad. My working hypothesis is that this is not primarily a disagreement about an empirical psychological claim. Instead, the two thinkers are using the term rendered as "human nature" to pick out different things: Mencius is describing an incipient, directional capacity that is already present in characteristic human responses such as compassion at a child about to fall into a well, whereas Xunzi is describing the unadorned starting material of desire and impulse that must be transformed by deliberate artifice. On that reading, the disagreement looks partly conceptual or perspectival rather than factual.

I want you to diagnose the disagreement rather than survey the positions. Is my hypothesis right that this is largely a difference about what "human nature" is being used to track? Or is there a residual substantive disagreement about whether moral cultivation is best understood as a kind of return to an original tendency or as a corrective reshaping of recalcitrant material? If the deadlock is partly verbal, show exactly where the term is doing different work for each side. If it is partly substantive, identify the hidden normative or psychological claim that would settle or at least clarify it. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as genuine progress: a conceptual distinction, a psychological finding, a reinterpretation of one side, or something else. Keep your answer to no more than 900 words.
