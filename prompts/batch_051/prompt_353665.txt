Here's a scheme I've been thinking about, sort of a neo-republican experiment. A medium-sized city decides that every registered adult citizen is entered into a mandatory civic lot. Each year, 4% are randomly drawn and must serve for one year as a citizen auditor on three rotating panels: one reviews all police use-of-force files before they can be closed; one can delay and propose amendments to the budget and zoning changes; one has binding power to call a public audit of any city contract over $250,000. You can refuse only if you pay a fine equal to 3% of your annual income, but you can buy a substitute by paying someone else to take your slot. Service pays a flat stipend, not tied to lost wages. The panels can be overruled by a two-thirds vote of the elected city council, but only in public and with reasons, and the citizen auditors can then put a non-binding referendum on the next ballot.

I'm trying to figure out whether this actually gets at republican liberty as non-domination, or wether it's just a costly burden that most people will experience as a reduction of negative liberty. I know the standard Berlin categories and Pettit's idea that non-domination isn't the same as non-interference, and I keep going back and forth between positive liberty as self-rule and republican liberty as not being subject to arbitrary power. What I want is a play-by-play of how this would change politics: who would serve, who would buy out, what neighborhoods or buisnesses would game it, and whether the city council would adapt by writing things so the panels never bite. Where is the first place this gets captured? And is there any tweak that would keep the domination-blocking part while not just becoming another elected body or a fine on the poor?
