I'm a PhD student straddling philosophy and economics, and one of my committee members keeps insisting that interpersonal comparisons of utility are a matter of fact. When a policy hurts Alice a little and helps Bob a lot, they say there's a real answer to whether Bob's gain outweighs Alice's loss, not just an answer we stipulate by picking a social welfare function. I want to say that's either a disguised normative choice or untestable. But maybe it isn't. Design the strongest empirical test of the claim that there's a real, non-stipulated fact of the matter about whether one person's gain outweighs another person's loss. I had a half-baked idea: have each person report the smallest amount of money they'd actually notice recieving (a just-noticeable difference in money), ask them to judge how a fixed painful stimulus compares to multiples of their own "noticeable" money unit, and then use some common calibrator to bridge across people. Fix that idea or replace it with something better. Then say exactly what result would count in favor of the claim, what a null result would show, and what even a clean positive result would leave undecided — especially the gap between "the units line up" and "Alice's gain really outweighs Bob's loss."
