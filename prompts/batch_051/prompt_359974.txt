I am a retired physical scientist — I spent my working life in a laboratory, where disagreement was an occasion for better measurement rather than rhetoric — and I have recently come to philosophy through the epistemology of markets. I now convene a workshop of fourteen people (graduate students, postdocs, and a few philosophers) for a single ninety-minute session. I want to run something hands-on, not a lecture.

The idea I would like the participants to feel before anyone names it is the no-trade theorem: when rational expected-utility maximizers share a common prior and this is common knowledge, pure differences in private information should not generate mutually agreed speculative trade. If you are willing to sell me a claim at a price, your very willingness is a signal about what you know; I should update on it, and the price should shift until the trade no longer looks good to one of us. A quiet market can be evidence of successful information aggregation rather than of failure.

Please design a game that brings this into the room. It should be runnable in ninety minutes with paper, cards, and a board, and it should use points rather than real money. The following constraints seem right to me, but improve them if you see a better version: there should be a simple hidden state selected by a public chance rule whose prior everyone knows; each participant privately observes a signal about the state; and in the first phase everyone holds identical endowments and identical payoff tables, so that any trade has to be information-driven. I would also like a second phase that introduces a legitimate non-informational reason to trade — differing risk tolerances or differing endowments — so the debrief can contrast the phase in which the market dries up with the phase in which trade is rational after all.

Please include:

1. Complete rules and a materials list, simple enough to set up in ten minutes.
2. A facilitator script or instruction sheet worded so that the theorem is not front-loaded; the participants should run into the impasse themselves and hear it named only later.
3. A timeline for the ninety minutes: setup, the two phases, and the debrief.
4. A debrief that maps specific moments in play onto the formal pieces: the common prior, common knowledge of rationality, the offer or bid as a signal that reveals private information, the self-defeating character of information-based trade, and the contrast with trade that is legitimate because of risk or endowment differences.
5. A section on what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run — participants trading for amusement, failing to treat a counterparty's willingness as evidence, the group accidentally building in a failure of common knowledge or a non-common prior, and so on — and how to handle each by changing a rule, an instruction, or a payoff rather than by explaining it away.
6. An honest note on what the game cannot reproduce — for instance, the full hierarchy of common knowledge or the idealized one-shot Bayesian actor of the formal result — and what the facilitator should say to supply that missing piece.

By the end, I want the participants to see that their own eagerness to speculate on private information is itself evidence that tends to undermine the speculation, and that a quiet market is not necessarily a broken market. Please write your answer in American English.
