Over the past four decades the study of nationalism has been split between broadly modernist accounts—for which nations are late-eighteenth- and nineteenth-century products of state centralization, industrial transformation, print capitalism, and invented traditions, associated with Hobsbawm, Anderson, and Gellner—and ethnosymbolist accounts, associated with Anthony D. Smith and his students, which hold that modern nations are normally assembled out of premodern ethnic cores: named populations with shared myths of descent, symbols, memories, and a durable sense of solidarity. What strikes me is how little the two sides have converged. They are not, for the most part, fighting over the same body of evidence with different weightings; they seem to inhabit different evidentiary worlds. Modernists read medieval and early modern chronicles, legal codes, and panegyrics as the culture of a thin clerical and aristocratic stratum, and treat the absence of mass national consciousness before the modern era as decisive. Ethnosymbolists read the same materials as the visible trace of wider identities, and point to the recurrence of ethnonyms, origin myths, and boundary markers as evidence that collective identities persisted and could later be mobilized. Each side can cite its own favored cases and explain away the other's; the argument recurs at conferences and in the journals without anyone moving.

I am a PhD student in historical sociology, and I am less interested in taking a side than in understanding the structure of the impasse. My working hypothesis is that the two camps are using nation to pick out different things—a modern political community claiming sovereignty and mass participation, versus a named cultural community bound by a shared past and sharp internal–external boundaries—so that the question How old are nations? silently splits into several questions: How old is nationalism as an ideology? How old are states organized along national lines? How old are the ethnic identities out of which modern nations were built? But I doubt that separating the terms dissolves everything. Even after such separation, there appears to remain a substantive empirical disagreement about how thick premodern collective identities were, how far down the social scale they reached, and whether the continuity between an ethnic core and a modern nation is real or retrospectively projected by historians. I suspect there may also be a methodological disagreement about what can be inferred from overwhelmingly elite-produced sources, and perhaps a difference in starting intuitions about when a population counts as a nation at all. Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is—verbal, empirical, methodological, or one of starting intuitions—and say what would count as progress relative to that diagnosis, as opposed to one more exchange of favorite cases?
