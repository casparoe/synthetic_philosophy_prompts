I want you to act as a professional philosopher of religion who's thought a lot about divine foreknowledge, immutability, and the point of prayer. Think carefully, and don't just reach for a canned answer: start by brainstorming a few candidate principles and test them against my cases before you commit.

I've got a working rule about petitionary prayer that seems right in the abstract, but my actual confidence in particular cases doesn't match it. Here's the rule: since God is omniscient and immutable, there's no point asking God for something that is already settled, because God already knows the outcome and won't revise the plan. Petitionary prayer only makes sense for open futures.

But my verdicts are all over the place:

1. Before a job interview I pray, "let me get this job if it's good for me." Totally fine; this is just what petitionary prayer is.
2. After the interview but before anyone meets to decide, I pray the same thing. Also fine.
3. After the committee has decided but before I've gotten the email, I catch myself praying "please let me have gotten it." This feels a little off, but I can't call it wrong, and I wouldnt stop someone doing it.
4. A friend was on a plane that went down. I don't know if she survived. I pray "please let her be alive." Here I'm clearly asking about something already settled (she is or isn't alive), but this feels like exactly what a person should pray. Fine.
5. Later I learn she died. Now, "please let her have survived" does not feel like prayer at all; it feels like wanting a different world. But "please have mercy on her" feels right even though her life is over. So: retroactively changing an event is off; asking God's mercy on someone already dead is fine.
6. "Please don't let there be a nuclear war in the next decade." Obviously fine.
7. Driving to a busy neighborhood, "just let there be a parking spot on Elm Street." I'd call this dumb and superstitious, but I can't give a principled difference between it and case 2.
8. When I pray the line asking for God's kingdom to come, I'm asking for something God has already promised will happen anyway. That feels not just fine but central, and it doesn't feel like I'm trying to change God's plan.

So my working rule handles 1, 2, and 6, but seems to condemn 3 (sort of), 4, the mercy half of 5, and 8, which I'm not willing to give up. And it gives no traction on why 2 and 7 feel different.

What I want from you: (a) a better principle—or a small set of them—that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, ideally by saying what petitionary prayer is actually doing other than informing or changing God; (b) an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle can't save and whether those verdicts should go; and (c) the principle stated precisely enough that it issues verdicts on new cases. Please test it on a few I haven't listed: praying for a friend's exam result after they've taken it but before grades come out; praying that a tornado heading toward a town tonight changes direction; and praying "if it isn't too late" for somebody who may already be dead. Don't just list the standard defenses of petitionary prayer; I want to know which ones actually survive my cases.
