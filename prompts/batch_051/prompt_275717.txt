I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I recently read a bit about metaphysical grounding — roughly, the idea that some facts or entities obtain or exist in virtue of others, and that this "in virtue of" relation is supposed to run downward and eventually bottom out in fundamental facts or entities. I keep being struck by how much the whole apparatus resembles a build or dependency graph. If fact A grounds fact B, then B depends on A in something like the way an executable depends on a library: remove the library and the executable no longer builds, yet the library does not cause the executable in any temporal, physical sense. Fundamentality looks like the set of nodes that depend on nothing (no outgoing edges in the usual package-manager picture; equivalently, no incoming edges if you draw grounding arrows from ground to grounded). And the metaphysicians' typical requirement that grounding be well-founded — no infinite descending chains, no cycles — sounds exactly like the constraint that a dependency graph be a DAG so that a topological ordering exists and a build can run to completion.

I cannot decide whether this is a deep structural identity or just me pattern-matching on the word "depends." In software, circular dependencies are routinely tolerated as a code smell rather than an impossibility, and some ecosystems handle mutually recursive modules through lazy linking or import-time resolution. Does the fact that we can live with cycles in software show that the ban on circular grounding is just a modeling choice, or does the software case differ from grounding in exactly the respects that matter — for instance, because a build has a real causal and temporal construction history, whereas grounding is supposed to be synchronic and explanatory rather than productive? Conversely, if the analogy is substantive, does anything transfer from software back to metaphysics? I am thinking of the distinction between declared and actual dependencies (what a manifest says versus what the linker really pulls in), or between direct and transitive dependencies in a package manager, which looks like it might sharpen the philosophical distinction between immediate and mediate grounding — or might just muddy it.

Please assess the analogy point by point: state the intended mapping precisely, say what would have to be true of both sides for any conclusion to transfer from one domain to the other, and identify at least one thing the parallel predicts that is false. Write your answer in American English.
