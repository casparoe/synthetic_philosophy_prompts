So here's one I keep going back and forth on. Commercial aviation has a funny division of cognitive labor. For emergencies, there are the memory items — the stuff you're supposed to know cold, tested by rote in the simulator, to be done without touching a checklist. But for a huge amount of normal flying — before-taxi, before-takeoff, approach, landing — the rule is basically the opposite: do not trust your memory; read each item off the printed or electronic checklist and do it, even the obvious stuff. A pilot who skips the checklist and nothing goes wrong can still get written up or have their certificate pulled. So the institution deliberately decides which cognitive work stays in the head and which gets pushed out into objects in the cockpit, and then holds the pilot individually responsible for the whole head-plus-checklists system working. What does that practice presuppose about where cognition — or at least cognitive responsibility — stops? Does it quietly rule out the extended mind thesis, the idea that a reliably used external resource can become part of the cognitive process, or is it actually a pretty clean case of it? Reconstruct the implicit theory, and tell me what training, certification, or enforcement would have to change if we took extended cognition seriously from the start.

Give me your best reconstruction, but be explicit about how much of it the practice really forces on us and how much is merely compatible; if several theories can license the same practice, say so, and tell me which parts are doing the philosophical work. And if the whole question is badly posed — if a practice cant presuppose a theory of mind in that way, or I'm smearing together cognitive extension and legal blameworthiness — tell me that flat out instead of serving me a tidy story.
