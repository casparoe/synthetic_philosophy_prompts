I am a sixth-form student, and over the past year I have adopted a fixed rule about political speech: whenever a politician or government official states a factual claim, I treat that claim as false until it has been corroborated by documentation from a source independent of the government making the claim — a court transcript, a leaked primary document, or a statistical body that is not answerable to ministers. I apply the rule to every official and every party, including claims that seem trivial or easily checked, because I have come to think that political speech is not a neutral description of the world but a continuation of political action, and that credulity is the greater risk.

I recently read Hannah Arendt's essay "Lying in Politics: Reflections on the Pentagon Papers" (1971), and this passage made me unsure whether my rule is defensible:

"A characteristic of human action is that it always begins something new, but this does not mean that it is ever permitted to start ab ovo, to create ex nihilo. In order to make room for one’s own action, something that was there before must be removed or destroyed, and things as they were before are changed. Such change would be impossible if we could not mentally remove ourselves from where we are physically located and imagine that things might as well be different from what they actually are. In other words, the ability to lie, the deliberate denial of factual truth, and the capacity to change facts, the ability to act, are interconnected; they owe their existence to the same source, imagination."

On one reading, Arendt vindicates my rule: she says lying is not an unfortunate accident that better politicians could avoid, but is rooted in the same imaginative capacity that makes political action possible at all, so systematic distrust is the rational stance. On another reading, the passage undermines my rule: if the source of deception is a capacity that also makes action possible, then it is present wherever there are acting human beings, including the institutions and documents to which my rule makes me defer. My rule would then not remove the problem; it would merely relocate my trust to sources that are themselves products of the same political action.

Which of these readings is closer to Arendt's view, and is my rule defensible in light of it? If the rule is too crude, what would a revised version look like that keeps an appropriate caution about political lying without collapsing into blanket cynicism?
