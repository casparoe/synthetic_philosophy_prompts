Dana chose a guaranteed $1 million over an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing. Then she chose a 10% chance of $5 million (with a 90% chance of nothing) over an 11% chance of $1 million (with an 89% chance of nothing). Marco called her inconsistent: in both cases, after you cancel the common 89% branch, you're left choosing between an 11% chance of $1 million on one side and a 10% chance of $5 million plus a 1% chance of nothing on the other, so by the independence axiom of expected utility theory she should make the same choice both times. Dana said the two situations aren't the same: in the first one option is a sure thing, and the certainty of a sure thing counts for something, whereas in the second everything is a longshot, so you may as well swing for the bigger prize. She said the independence axiom is the thing under dispute, not a rule she has to obey. Marco said certainty is doing psychological work that a theory of rationality should refuse to reward, and if she abandons independence she's open to money pumps or intransitive preferences. Please adjudicate move by move: who is right about which point, is Dana actually violating independence, and does the certainty effect make her choice rational or just predictably human?
