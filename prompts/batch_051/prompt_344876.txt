I keep going back and forth on this, and I'd like a philosopher who works on collective responsibility and the philosophy of history to help me think it through properly—not just to tell me which side is standard. Take your time, maybe start by steelmanning both sides, then give me a real verdict.

The thing is, I find the methodological-individualist line about responsibility pretty compelling: responsibility attaches to agents who can deliberate, intend, and answer for what they did, and a nation isn't an agent like that. There's no single mind of Germany that sat down in July 1914 and chose war. So a sentence like Germany was responsible for the First World War ought to be false, or at best shorthand for a very long list of individual people and what they each did.

And yet I can't shake the feeling that the shorthand is doing real work and that the individual-level story leaves something out. When I read about the Fischer controversy and the old war-guilt debate, saying that Germany bears a distinctive responsibility for 1914 doesn't strike me as a category mistake or loose talk. It strikes me as the kind of claim historical argument can get right or wrong, and as something that would be lost if we were only allowed to talk about the chancellor, the generals, and the Kaiser one person at a time.

So the clash is this: should the theory that only individual agents can be responsible give way, or should the intuition that collective-responsibility claims about 1914 are tracking something real give way? Give me the strongest possible version of each, explain what it would cost to abandon each one, and say wether there's a reading on which they're compatible—for instance, groups bearing a different kind of responsibility than persons, or historical blame being something an explanation can assign without positing any group mind. Don't just referee it; tell me what you would actually give up and why.
