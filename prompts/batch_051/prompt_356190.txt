I'm on the committee that runs my math department's annual geometry prize. The rules have said forever that solutions have to be straightedge-and-compass constructions, no exceptions. This year a student submitted a solution to the angle trisection problem — which is famously impossible under those rules — by defining a curve from two simultaneous uniform motions: a line slides down across a square at constant speed while a ray from the top-left corner rotates at constant speed, and the curve is just the set of intersection points. He then shows that if you're allowed to use that curve, trisecting any angle is almost trivial. The curve itself is not straightedge-and-compass constructible; it's a mechanically generated transcendental curve (not algebraic, so there's no hope of generating the curve itself with the classical tools). Half the commitee says that settles it: disqualify the entry, because the whole point of the prize is to work inside the classical constraints. The other half says the curve is a perfectly well-defined mathematical object, and since straightedge and compass are themselves idealized mechanical tools, there's no principled reason to draw the line exactly there. I need to circulate a short decision memo by Friday. Draft it: admit, reject, or admit with conditions, and give the reasoning in a way the student and the rest of the faculty could actually respond to — including giving the strongest argument on the losing side its due. Oh, and write it in American English.
