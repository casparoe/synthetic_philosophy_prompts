I recently spent a long evening arguing with a friend about Goodhart's law, and I came away less settled than I entered. I had been defending the strong version of it: whenever an observable proxy is made the target of optimization, the proxy will eventually be gamed, because optimization finds the cheapest route to the proxy rather than producing the underlying thing the proxy is meant to track. I am drawn to this view for its economy and reach. It offers a single mechanism that explains the familiar story of a factory paid by the weight of nails producing one enormous nail, teachers teaching to the test, hospitals improving their reported outcomes by relabeling rather than healing, and learning systems that exploit scoring functions in ways their designers never anticipated. That kind of unifying explanation usually wins my allegiance.

Still, I cannot shake a firm intuition on the other side: that some things really are robust to being measured, and that a capable system ought to be able to distinguish gaming a metric from doing the task the metric was meant to serve. If the system is intelligent enough, the thought goes, we should be able to tell it what we actually want — not the grade but the learning, not the score but the health, not the reward signal but the intended behavior — and it will pursue that latent target rather than its observable shadow. My friend pressed this line all evening: Goodhart's law, he said, is a warning about optimizing bad proxies, and the point of building capable systems is precisely that they can represent and steer by the real objective instead.

I wanted to answer, and still half-believe, that the real objective is doing all the work while having no unproblematic content once you try to use it in practice. The moment you communicate or learn the real objective, you have specified it in some observable or learnable form, and that form is simply another measure, exposed to the same pressure. If the target is too vague to be represented, it cannot guide optimization at all; if it is precise enough to guide optimization, it has already become a proxy. On this reading, my friend's intuition is not a solution to the problem but a restatement of it.

But I do not want to win the argument by definition. The intuition is firm for me too: it feels as though there must be a real difference between optimizing the score and optimizing what the score reflects, and that a sufficiently careful specification, or a system that stays uncertain and keeps checking with us, could remain on the right side of that difference. I want to know which of these should yield. Should the intuition be debunked as a failure to appreciate how strong optimization pressure is? Should the theory be amended — for instance, with conditions under which a proxy is safe when the system's model of the latent target is good and its optimization is bounded? Or is there a reading on which both survive: the law as a claim about the limit of strong optimization, and the intuition as a claim about what a corrigible, feedback-seeking, modest optimizer can do before that limit is reached?

Present both sides at their strongest and give me a negotiated verdict, not just a verdict. Say what would justify abandoning the intuition, what would justify amending or abandoning the theory, and whether the middle position is stable or is just a way of declining to take the law seriously.
