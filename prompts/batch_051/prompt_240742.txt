I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I would like an honest assessment of a view I have been developing. I call it the legacy-code view of grammar. The core idea is that a language's morphology behaves like an old codebase. Regular patterns such as walk/walked or cat/cats are general functions, while irregular forms such as go/went or child/children are hardcoded overrides that an older version of the system left behind and that were never refactored away. Suppletion is the limiting case of a hardcoded override, where the general function is not reused at all. Sound change is a schema migration: it rewrites the data uniformly but breaks existing call sites, and analogy then patches those call sites piecemeal. The substantive claim is that the distinction between a rule and its exceptions is not a fact about the language but an engineering decision about which parts of the codebase to treat as the maintained abstraction and which to treat as legacy hacks. Is this coherent, or is it mostly a metaphor? Has something like it already been worked out under another name? And where does it break: give me the two or three strongest objections and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
