At my department's ethics discussion group, two members, Maya and Dev, got stuck arguing about a student campaign. The campaign demands that a clothing retailer either substantially raise wages at a supplier factory in a low-income country or cut ties with it. The factory pays above the workers' local alternatives, but shifts are long and wages are far below what the retailer could afford.

Maya argued that the campaign is likely to harm the people it claims to help. The workers took these jobs because they were their best available option; the alternatives are worse. If the brand exits, the workers fall back onto those worse options. Since the exchange makes both sides better off relative to their own baseline, calling it exploitation does not by itself show that anyone should be pressured to alter or abandon it.

Dev replied that doing better than a desperate baseline does not settle whether the arrangement is objectionable. If someone drowning agrees to hand over half their future income in exchange for rescue, the deal is mutually beneficial relative to drowning, but it is still wrongly exploitative. The question is whether workers receive a fair share of the value their labor creates, not merely whether they beat their previous options. Severe need weakens the kind of consent that would make the arrangement morally unproblematic.

Maya came back that in the rescue case there is a prior duty to help that the rescuer is exploiting, whereas the retailer did not create the workers' poverty and is offering an option that improves on it. And if the campaign succeeds in forcing a large wage increase, the factory may close or relocate, restoring the worse baseline. A campaign has to show that it can make the workers better off, not just express disapproval.

Dev answered that a firm can benefit from a desperate baseline it did not create: it can pay as little as it does only because the workers' alternatives are terrible. Profiting from background injustice can make one complicit in exploitation even without having caused that injustice. Moreover, coordinated campaigns have sometimes raised standards without causing exit; the threat of exit is a prediction, not a necessity.

Maya then said that workers themselves often oppose such campaigns because they value the jobs, and dismissing that as false consciousness is paternalistic. Moreover, a fair share of the value created presupposes some independent standard for fair wages, whereas actual wages are set by supply and demand.

Dev responded that people can rationally prefer an exploitative arrangement to their worse alternatives; that is why exploitation is often consented to rather than chosen wholeheartedly. And appealing to supply and demand does not answer the fairness question, because the bargaining power that shapes the market outcome is precisely what is being challenged.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate the disagreement point by point.
