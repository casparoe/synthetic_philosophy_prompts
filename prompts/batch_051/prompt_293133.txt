I've been arguing with someone about what "unpredictable" should mean in a deterministic system, and I want a case built to order to make the distinction sharp. Poincaré in Science and Method has the thought that a very small cause which escapes our notice determines a considerable effect we cannot fail to see, and then we say the effect is due to chance. Use that as the target.

Construct a single deterministic dynamical system, no quantum noise and no stochastic forcing, that satisfies all these at once:

1. It has a strange attractor with a positive largest Lyapunov exponent for variable x. An initial uncertainty of 10^-10 in x grows to order-one uncertainty in under a minute, so x is practically unpredictable.
2. Another variable y in the same system is regular and remains predictible from the same initial measurement precision for at least 1000 seconds.
3. The system is deterministic throughout, so the right verdict is that x is epistemically unpredictable but not ontic-random in the Kolmogorov sense.

Then do the check: give the equations and parameter values (a standard three-variable ODE like Lorenz is fine if you justify the y part), state the prediction horizons for x and y, and explain exactly why the positive Lyapunov exponent forces the x verdict while determinism blocks the ontic-randomness verdict. This is for a blog post arguing that Poincaré's "chance" is an epistemic modeling notion, not an eruption of real randomness.

Finally, separate what is settled (positive Lyapunov exponent implies sensitive dependence, etc.) from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and say which feature of the case does the real work, what happens if you turn the dissipation term down until everything is regular, and how someone who thinks all unpredictability ultimately traces to quantum-level randomness should attack the case.
