I teach a survey of classical Chinese philosophy, and although I can handle the Western side of the syllabus cold, the Zhuangzi is only recently something I've read end to end instead of in excerpts. A collegue of mine who's writing a book on Tang dynasty poetics gave me the following summary of Zhuangzi on death, which I jotted down pretty much verbatim: "Zhuangzi sees death as part of a larger process of transformation. Life and death are like the four seasons, so there's no more reason to cling to being alive now than there is to wish summer would never end. That's why when his wife died he drummed on a tub and sang instead of wailing: once you see death as just another turning, grief starts to look like a failure to understand how things really are. The text even suggests the dead are better off than the living — at rest, free of burdens and quarrels — so a reasonable person should meet death with a kind of welcoming equanimity rather than fear. In this way Zhuangzi is close to Epicurus, who also thought death is nothing to fear because it is simply nonexistence." I had been planning to adapt this into a handout, but the last couple of sentences especially make me wonder whether my friend has quietly imported a Western consolation-of-philosophy framework into a text that is doing something stranger. Could you go through the paragraph and tell me how fair it is? I'd like to know which parts track what the Zhuangzi actually says, which parts overstate or gloss things anachronistically, and whether the closing comparison to Epicurus helps or hurts a student trying to get Zhuangzi right.
