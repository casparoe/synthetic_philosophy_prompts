I’m in a seminar on Marx, and the instructor keeps insisting that exploitation and alienation should be treated as two separate theoretical strands: exploitation belongs to the mature political economy, where it names the appropriation of surplus labor from the worker, while alienation belongs to the early humanist Marx, where it names the worker’s estrangement from species-being, from the activity of labor, and from the product. But when I read Capital Volume One, Chapter 25, I came across this passage:

“within the capitalist system all methods for raising the social productiveness of labour are brought about at the cost of the individual labourer; all means for the development of production transform themselves into means of domination over, and exploitation of, the producers; they mutilate the labourer into a fragment of a man, degrade him to the level of an appendage of a machine, destroy every remnant of charm in his work and turn it into a hated toil.”

Here Marx seems to move fluidly between a concept of exploitation and a vocabulary of being mutilated, degraded, and reduced to an appendage—precisely the language I would have called alienation. And this is in Capital, not the 1844 Manuscripts. I’m not sure how to map the two concepts onto what the passage is doing. Is this passage still fundamentally about exploitation, with the talk of mutilation and degradation being rhetorical overflow? Or is alienation doing independent explanatory work here, and if so, what does it explain that exploitation cannot? Or is the distinction itself a misleading artifact of how Marx’s texts are periodized? Compare the two concepts as rigorously as you can, then use the passage to say which concept bears the weight, whether either is reducible to the other, and give me a criterion for telling, in any passage of Marx, whether a claim is a claim about exploitation, about alienation, or about both.
