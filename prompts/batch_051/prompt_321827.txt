I'm the compliance lead at a company that ships lab glassware, ceramic insulators, and glass-fronted electronics. Every week my team has to decide, for maybe twenty SKUs at a time, whether a carton has to carry a FRAGILE warning and what the warning should actually say, especially whether it describes the product or the package. The legal standard we work under is loose: basically don't mislead anyone about how the thing will hold up under ordinary shipping and handling. We aren't experts, we can spend about an hour per SKU, and I'm the one who signs off before the packaging run goes out. If we over-label, carriers ignore it and damage claims get kicked back; if we under-label, we eat smashed product and sometimes a regulator asks questions.

I've heard philosophers who work on dispositions have a toolkit that's basically built for this: a disposition like fragility, plus the cases where a thing still has the disposition but something blocks the manifestation (a mask), and where the thing would lose the disposition the moment the trigger happens (a fink). I only half-remember this stuff, so the procedure has to carry the theroy for me. I want a reusable decision procedure my team can run on any SKU: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with "don't label" or "label," and where it stops being a procedure and becomes a judgment call I have to make. Then run it on three actual SKUs: (1) a borosilicate reagent bottle shipped inside its own fitted foam overpack—naked, the bottle shatters if dropped a meter onto concrete, but the overpack survives a two-meter drop with the bottle in it; (2) a glass screen with a factory-applied polymer film, so that under impact the glass cracks but stays in one sheet instead of shattering into pieces; (3) a composite casing that would crack under the kind of shock we're talking about, but it has a shear-thickening layer that hardens in the first milliseconds of an impact, so when it's actually struck it doesn't flex the way it normally does. I want the procedure to say wich of these it can't fully settle, and I'd like it to give me at least one answer I'm not going to like.
