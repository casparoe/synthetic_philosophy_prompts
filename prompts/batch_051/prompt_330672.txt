I have adopted a rule for evaluating any proposal to abolish or alter an existing practice, policy, or institution. Before I may support the change, I must be able to state in concrete terms what problem the existing arrangement was originally meant to solve. If I cannot state such a purpose, I treat that as evidence that I do not yet understand the arrangement rather than as evidence that it is pointless, and I default to leaving it intact. I apply the rule to legacy procedures at work, to old electoral rules, and to personal habits that irritate me. The principle feels like a fair reading of Burkean conservatism and of the image of Chesterton's fence: do not tear down a fence until you understand why it was erected. But I worry that my rule is status quo bias dressed as epistemic humility. Is it defensible as a policy for managing my own ignorance? Where does it systematically misfire—for institutions that arose by accident or path dependence, for purposes that are now obsolete, or for purposes that were bad from the start? Is there a restated version that keeps the benefit while avoiding the bias, perhaps by distinguishing a merely reconstructed purpose from a purpose that can be shown to be still served, or by assigning the burden of proof differently? And how could I tell from the inside whether I am protecting accumulated knowledge rather than merely protecting myself from the costs of change?
