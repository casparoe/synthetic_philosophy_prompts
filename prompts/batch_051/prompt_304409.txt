I'm a novelist, and Ive been trying to write a character whose inner life is a void—not sad, not bored, but empty in the specific way the book is supposed to make legible—and I dont actually have a clear enough picture of what the felt void is supposed to be doing in a person. Before I read any more theory, or any more self-help that borrows the word, I want the spec: what would an account of existential emptiness—the sense of an inner void, hollowness, the feeling that inside there's nothing—have to get right to count as adequate for someone trying to render it from the inside?

Give me the desiderata list, with the case or consideration behind each. It has to distinguish the feeling of emptiness from the judgment that one's life is meaningless, because a character can make that judgment and feel rage, and another can feel hollow while insisting their life is full of meaning. It has to distinguish absence of feeling from a felt presence of absence: the flatness of depression isn't the same as the uncanny sensation of missing something you can't name. It has to say why the one word shows up in places that shouldn't obviously be the same thing—the depressive's anhedonia, the mourner who says they're hollow now, the mystic who says the empty soul is the one God can fill, the meditator who reports that seeing no self is a relief, the burned-out worker who says she has nothing left to give. Is that one underlying phenomenon with different colorings, or a family of distinct phenomena sharing a metaphor? An adequate account has to take a stand on that, not just list synonyms.

Also give me the distinctions it can't collapse: feeling empty versus being empty (if that's even a real state), emptiness as symptom versus emptiness as achievement or opening, the void that terrifies versus the void that feels spacious and calm, and the difference between someone who wants the feeling gone and someone who cultivates it. Mark which of these requirements are non-negotiable and which are just a camp's preference—I suspect the clinical and the contemplative traditions are quietly imposing different demands, and I want that made explicit.

If some pair of requirements can't be jointly satisfied—say, an account that treats the void as pure privation, an absence with no positive character of its own, versus an account that treats it as a distinct felt quality with its own phenomenology—flag it and say why it matters, because that tension is exactly what I'd be writing into the character. That's the part I most want.

If you glance at the usual lenses at the end—existentialist, Buddhist, clinical—keep it to a sentence or two each; the criteria are the thing.
