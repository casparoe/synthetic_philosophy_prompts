Hey, I'm putting together a 90-minute session for an undergrad seminar (14 students) on whether argumentation could be a sport and whether that would improve it. I want a runnable classroom game where the students feel one specific mechanism instead of talking about it in the abstract, and I want the evidence for it to come out of the game itself, not from my commentary during play.

Here's the angle I want isolated. When people say make debate competitive, they usually mean combat: you score points by refuting the other side, concessions cost points, someone wins. But there's another model, racing: you score your own progress against a benchmark, and other people getting better doesn't take points off you. I want the activity to give everyone direct experience of both modes, plus a cooperative baseline, so the class can compare what each does to their actual arguing behavior. The takeaway I'm going for is a felt sense that the scoring rule isn't neutral — same people, same topic, same evidence, different argumentative moves depending on whether the reward structure is zero-sum or benchmarked — and that the slogan "competitive debate is good for reasoning" is too coarse to be for or against.

So the game should be built so the group can watch whether a points system changes what they do: do they stop conceeding when the evidence flips, defend their starting position, hunt for cheap objections to score, sandbag the benchmark? Have the design generate some countable evidence of this — concessions, mind-changes, which move type earned points — that the debrief can point back to.

Constraints: 14 people, 90 minutes including debrief, no expert judges, no internet. Scoring must be simple enough that participants can apply it in the moment (peer-scored is fine). Use small real stakes, like picking next week's topic or getting out of the reading response. It should survive one or two tryhards and one or two free-riders. And please don't just hand me a standard classroom debate format; the point is to isolate the mechanism, not to teach argumentation skills.

For the debrief, map specific moments in the game to the underlying machinery: score-tracking vs truth-tracking, the metric-becomes-target problem, combat vs racing models of competition, and the idea that reasoning is partly for persuading others so disagreement is our best de-biasing tool — plus where that idea fits. Also say what predictably goes wrong when this is run and how the facilitator should handle it, and be honest about which part of the real-world thing the game can't reproduce (long-run reputational incentives? identity as a debater? you tell me).
