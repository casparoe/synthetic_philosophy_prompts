Back when Appel and Haken proved the four-colour theorem by computer, a standard objection was that this wasn't really a proof: no single human can survey the case-check, so it ends up looking more like an experiment that reports a result than a proof a mathematician can hold in their head and verify. Since then the four-colour proof has been fully formalized in Coq, and the Kepler conjecture's proof got the same treatment in the Flyspeck project. The formal proofs are, if anything, longer and even less surveyable than the originals. But now what a human has to check is small: the kernel of the proof assistant, and the statement of the theorem as formalized. Does the old unsurveyability objection survive this development, or was it always really about were you place your trust rather than about the size of the proof? If it survives, give me the strongest version of it; if it dies, tell me what the remaining worry should be called and whether it's still a philosophical problem about mathematical knowledge or just engineering.
