So I recently got roped into being treasurer of my building's tenant board after the last one took kickbacks from a contractor, and now everyone is suddenly very worried about incorruptability. My dad gave me his working criteria for it: an incorruptible person (1) can't be bought, with money or favors or the promise of a soft future, (2) can't be pressured, by threats or blackmail or being frozen out, (3) won't use the position to enrich themselves even in ways no one would ever detect, and (4) does all this as a matter of the role itself, not as a performance.

The person I keep turning over is my partner's uncle, who spent thirty years as the bookkeeper for an old-style political machine. The machine itself was rotten: fake contracts, permits for friends, ghost employees. But the uncle was famous inside it for being completely straight. He kept immaculate books, never skimmed, refused envelopes from contractors, and once quit for a year rather than sign off on an expense report he knew was fabricated. And yet he was the one who made the machine survivable. He'd lie to government auditors out of loyalty, and considered whistleblowing a worse betrayal than theft.

So: was he incorruptible? Go through the four criteria one by one, and say where he passes, where he fails, and where the criteria just don't decide. Then tell me whether this case shows the criteria are missing something or need changing—whether being incorruptible in his sense has anything to do with being good, or whether we've got one word doing two jobs.
