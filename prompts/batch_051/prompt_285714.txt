Not sure whether this is a philosophy question or a systems one, but I'll ask anyway. I'm a backend engineer who's been reading Nozick on the side, and the utility monster thought experiment keeps reminding me of a starvation bug in CPU scheduling. Here's the version I have in my head. Take a preemptive priority scheduler with one CPU. A strict policy always runs the highest-priority runnable process. If one process has unboundedly high priority and never blocks, it holds the CPU forever and every other process starves, even though the scheduler is following its policy exactly. The usual fix is not to get rid of priorities but to add some fairness mechanism: aging, where waiting processes slowly accumulate priority, or a guaranteed minimum share per process or per user, so that everyone makes progress even if that costs some throughput. Now swap the vocabulary. Moral patients become processes, utility becomes CPU time, the utility monster becomes the unbounded-priority process, total-utility maximization becomes the scheduling policy, and prioritarian weighting or a side-constraint floor under everyone's share becomes the fairness patch.
