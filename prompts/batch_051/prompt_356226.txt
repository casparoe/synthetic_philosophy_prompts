I have been trying to sort out something that has been bothering me since two conversations I had in the same month. An acquaintance in the United States completed an egg donation cycle and was paid a few thousand dollars; around the same time, a relative living in Europe told me that in her country egg donation could not be paid, and that carrying a child for someone else was not legally available to her at all. The more I looked into it, the less it seemed like a mere difference in regulation and the more it seemed like two different pictures of what a person is doing when she provides eggs or carries a pregnancy. In the American arrangement, at least in the states where it is allowed, egg provision and gestational surrogacy are treated roughly like skilled bodily services: they are advertised, contracted for, negotiated, and paid. In much of Europe, the same acts are framed as gifts or as self-sacrifice that money would corrupt; payment is banned or limited to expenses, and the contracts are often unenforceable or the practice is illegal. My own rough label is that the American system treats reproduction as a service the body performs, while the European system treats it as a gift the person gives, but I suspect both labels are too crude, which is exactly why I want the underlying views dug out. So my question is not which system is better. It is: what theory of the body, of reproductive work, and of the line between a person and a thing does each system presuppose? Does the European framework actually presuppose that eggs and pregnancy are the kind of thing that may never be exchanged for money, or only that payment would corrupt the motives of the people involved? Does the American framework presuppose that reproductive labor is relevantly just like other bodily labor, or can it treat reproduction as special in some way and still allow contracts? And where is each practice philosophically underdetermined — that is, where could several rival theories have produced the very same law?

3. Where each system is underdetermined (about 350 words): identify the places where the same law or clinic practice could be licensed by several different theories, and the places where a system seems inconsistent with its own stated rationale.
4. What would have to change (about 250 words): choose one of the two systems and say concretely what would have to change in law or practice if it adopted the other system's core presupposition wholesale.
5. Bottom line (about 200 words): your honest verdict on whether the two systems really rest on two different philosophies or mostly on two different legal histories, plus the single consideration that most pushes you to that verdict.
