After years of teaching the incompleteness theorems, I've become more and more convinced by the Lucas–Penrose line: the human mind can't be captured by any consistent formal system, because for any such system, once I accept that it's consistent, I can see the truth of its Gödel sentence—but the system can't derive that sentence. I know this argument has been attacked from every angle, but I've never been able to sort the attacks into ones that genuinely threaten the view and ones that are just noise. So I'd like you to map out what would count against the anti-mechanist position, and put the defeaters in order from the ones that should actually change my mind to the ones I can comfortably absorb. I'm especially interested in what a committed defender would themselves concede as a real weakness, not just what critics say. For example, how much weight does the argument put on my ability to know my own consistency, or on the assumption that I'm reasoning about a fixed, well-specified formal system? Is the real pressure the indexing problem—that my seeing a system's Gödel sentence as true depends on how that system is presented to me? What about the fact that any particular piece of human mathematical output can in principle be produced by some finite machine, so the argument has to be about a kind of ability rather than any actual performance? I'll admit that last one unsettles me most. Seperately, if I go with Penrose's stronger version, that the missing ingredient is some non-computable physical process, what would count against that specifically? I'd also like an honest answer to whether the view has been qualfied into unfalsifiability by appeals to an 'idealized human reasoner'—and if it has, whether that's a fatal problem for a position like this, or just the price of doing philosophy of mind. Give me the map of where this view risks being wrong.
