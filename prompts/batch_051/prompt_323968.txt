I serve on the Gender Equity Steering Committee at a university whose remit is to remove barriers that distort students' educational choices rather than to achieve any predetermined distribution of men and women across departments. Our largest initiative, Pathways into Quantitative Fields, is up for renewal this term. It was launched five years ago around an explicit theory of change: that the persistent underrepresentation of women in fields such as physics, engineering, and computer science is substantially driven by stereotypes and cultural messaging, and that a package of interventions—implicit-bias training for instructors, workshops on stereotype threat for incoming students, role-model talks featuring counter-stereotypical scientists, and reworked introductory curricula—would therefore narrow the gap. A member of the committee has now circulated a dissenting memorandum arguing that this theory of change is empirically in trouble. She points to what is often called the gender-equality paradox: by several large cross-national measures, sex differences in educational and occupational preferences, including the tendency to pursue STEM degrees, are larger rather than smaller in countries that score highest on indices of gender equality and economic development, with the Nordic countries serving as the usual illustration. Her conclusion is that if more egalitarian social conditions coexist with larger preference differences, then a stereotype-first explanation is probably mistaken, and renewing a programme built on it would be irresponsible. Others on the committee reply that the paradox itself is contested, both in its statistical construction and in what it licenses; that aggregate cross-country patterns say little about whether any particular student's choice was made under the weight of demeaning stereotype; and that a finding about average preferences, whatever its source, does not settle what the university should do, given that the committee's charge is to ensure that choices are unconstrained rather than that they come out equal.

The committee's division tracks a deeper disagreement about whether the paradox should be read as evidence of biologically rooted differences expressing themselves once constraints are removed, as an artefact of how gender equality and STEM preference are measured, or as a sign that greater economic security lets people pursue intrinsic interests with less countervailing pressure. The memo need not resolve that disagreement, but it should make clear which reading its recommendation relies on and why.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before next month's vote. It should recommend one of three courses of action: renew the programme essentially as designed, discontinue it, or renew it with substantive conditions (for example, a revised theory of change, altered intervention content, or different outcome measures). The document must give a clear, actionable recommendation with reasons the committee can actually vote on; it should address the gender-equality paradox head-on rather than treating it as a distraction; and it should honestly engage the strongest considerations on the losing side, including whatever is correct or worth preserving in the dissenting memorandum. The committee is closely divided and will be reading for whether the memo fairly represents both positions.
