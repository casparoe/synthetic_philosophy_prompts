I have been puzzling over whether the principle of procreative beneficence survives the shift from Mendelian to polygenic embryo selection, and I want a rigorous analysis of the argument rather than a literature survey. Do not give me a bibliography, references, or citations.

By procreative beneficence I mean roughly this: if a couple is already undergoing IVF and has several viable embryos to choose among, they are morally required, other things equal, to choose the embryo expected to have the best life — the best chance of the best life — and not merely a life free of disease. The principle was originally defended against a background where the paradigm cases of embryo selection were monogenic conditions: a single variant of large effect, comparatively high penetrance, and a reasonably predictable difference in outcome between the embryos. Set aside for now the broader question whether selection for non-disease traits is permissible at all; assume a liberal eugenics framing in which parental choice is presumptively legitimate, and ask only whether this particular argumentative engine still runs.

Now suppose the practical situation changes. The available testing shifts to polygenic embryo screening: instead of a few Mendelian markers, the couple receives genome-wide polygenic scores that aggregate thousands of small-effect variants into a probabilistic index for traits such as educational attainment, height, schizophrenia risk, or healthspan. These scores are population-referenced, capture only part of the heritability, are substantially pleiotropic, and rest on assumptions about gene–environment interaction that are not well resolved. The couple is no longer choosing between a near-certain disease and its likely absence; they are choosing among several embryos whose predicted values differ by modest amounts on an index with wide uncertainty around each embryo's estimate, and the ranking itself is a noisy estimate of a latent ranking.

The question: does the standard procreative beneficence argument survive this development, does it need repair, or is it dead in this setting? Specifically, identify which premise or premises of the argument the shift bears on, and say what the argument has to be true in order to retain its force, and whether it is in fact true under polygenic screening as I have described it. If the argument can be repaired, state the repaired principle precisely, and say how much of its original force it retains and what it would actually recommend in practice. If it cannot be repaired, say what, if anything, should take its place — for example, whether we are left mainly with disease avoidance rather than enhancement, or with a much weaker principle that only activates when the expected difference between the top-scoring embryo and the others is large relative to the prediction error.

In your answer, briefly note the main repair strategies you considered and rejected before settling on the strongest one, and be explicit about where your own reconstruction is softest.
