Two friends of mine argued about a mutual acquaintance, Lena, who works in the public-relations office of a company whose overseas factories are known to use forced labor. Mark said Lena is not complicit in the abuses: she does not authorize them, her press releases do not cause them, and if she resigned the company would replace her within a week, so nothing she does changes what happens on the factory floor. Ana replied that this applies the wrong test. Complicity, she said, is not only about what your actions cause; it is about what you join and lend your name to. Lena helps the company present itself as respectable, and that service helps the enterprise continue even if no single message can be traced to any particular abuse. Mark answered that by that standard nearly everyone in a modern economy is complicit in wrongs committed by companies they work for or buy from, and the concept stops distinguishing anything. Ana said that conclusion is real: much of ordinary economic life does implicate us, and the honest response is either to accept that or to change the structures, not to redefine complicity away. I could not settle it. Go through their exchange and adjudicate it: identify who is right about which specific points, where each argument overreaches, and what, if anything, both of them miss about what it is to participate in wrongdoing without causing it.
