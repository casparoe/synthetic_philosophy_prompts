My town's historical society just voted to correct a plaque that tells a founding story the county archives flatly contradict, and its turned into this huge fight with my grandmother leading the opposition. I've been trying to work out what I actually think by listing cases where a community's memory and the written record disagree, and what my gut says should give way. These are the verdicts I keep coming back to.

1. A local mining-town story says the place was founded in 1902 by workers who broke a strike; the county records show no mine there until 1907 and say it started as a railroad camp. I think the public marker should be corrected.
2. A church's founding story says the founders stayed up all night praying and were kept safe from a mob; two founders' diaries mention no mob that night and get the year wrong. I think the story should still be told in Sunday school unchanged.
3. A nearby city's memorial says 90 men were killed in a massacre; the state archives say 57. I find myself trusting the memorial over the archive, because the archive was part of the government that did the killing.
4. But when a group's memory says a certain family was part of the founding generation and descendants use that to claim leadership positions and land, and the record clearly shows they arrived a generation later, I think the record should win even though it will hurt them.

Am I just being inconsistent, or is there a principle that makes these four hang together? If there is one, what does it say about the easy and hard parts, which of my verdicts does it force me to revise, and what would the principle say about a case where I trust the recording institution but the living memory still insists?
