Question: 'Explain Bertrand's paradox and why it is usually thought to create a problem for the principle of indifference. Does the paradox show the principle is false, or only that it is incomplete?'

Student answer: "The principle of indifference says that if there are n mutually exclusive and exhaustive possibilities, and no reason to favour one over another, each receives probability 1/n. In a continuous setting this naturally means taking a uniform distribution over whichever parameter describes the possibilities. Bertrand's example shows that the rule cannot be right. Take a circle and ask for the probability that a randomly chosen chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Method one: pick two random endpoints uniformly on the circumference. Fix one endpoint; the chord is longer than the side just in case the other endpoint falls within the 120° arc centred on the diametrically opposite point, which is one third of the circle, so the probability is 1/3. Method two: choose a random radius and then a random point on it as the chord's midpoint; the chord is longer than the side exactly when the midpoint is closer to the centre than r/2, which is half the available length, so the probability is 1/2. Method three: pick the midpoint uniformly in the whole disc. The chord is longer than the side exactly when the midpoint lies inside the concentric disc of radius r/2, whose area is one quarter of the original disc, so the probability is 1/4. Each method applies the principle of indifference, yet they give different probabilities for one and the same event. A sound rule cannot assign three different values to the same event, so the principle must be false. The deeper lesson is that there is no objective probability of a random chord; the answer merely records an arbitrary choice about how to generate the chord. Therefore the principle of indifference is false, not merely incomplete."
