I sit on the first-year curriculum committee at a college that requires all incoming students to complete a reflective-journaling sequence. The program director proposes a new setbacks and self-knowledge assignment: students must select a significant past failure or regret, narrate it in immersive first-person detail, identify the underlying character flaw it reveals, and reread and annotate their entry weekly for the rest of the term. A modified version on the table would have students describe the event in the third person, focus on what they would do differently, and write only once. One faction argues the original is exactly the examined life the program promises; another argues it risks training vulnerable students in depressive rumination, and that self-examination is not always beneficial. Draft the short decision memo I will circulate before our vote: recommend approve, approve with modifications, or reject; give your reasons; engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side; and propose one or two criteria the committee could use to distinguish beneficial from harmful self-examination assignments in future proposals. The memo must end with an actionable resolution.
