I have recently retired from a career in the physical sciences and taken up philosophy, beginning with some older debates about the nature of logic itself. Last week I read Lewis Carroll's dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" for the first time, and the line I cannot get out of my head is the Tortoise's reply when Achilles talks as if logic could force him to accept a conclusion: 'Whatever Logic is good enough to tell me is worth writing down.' He then asks Achilles to enter one more conditional into his notebook, and the same move can evidently be repeated without end. In my working life I applied modus ponens and other elementary inference rules hundreds of times a day without ever reflecting on what licenses them, so I am not sure how seriously to take this line. It seems to me it could be making several quite different claims: that a rule of inference is just one more premise among others; that any normative demand of logic can be represented as a proposition without loss; that no rule tells us how to apply itself, so every application requires a further rule; that accepting premises and accepting a rule is never enough in itself to bring about acceptance of a conclusion; or, much more tamely, that the whole impasse arises only because Achilles keeps arguing instead of simply drawing the inference.

Please separate these readings and any others you find, and say which are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, and which are actually false. But I am not looking for a survey of possibilities. I want you to commit yourself: on the reading you judge strongest, does the Tortoise expose a genuine problem in the justification of deduction, or has he merely refused to use modus ponens while staging that refusal as a difficulty for logic? Give your reasons, and say plainly what the cost of your answer is, both for the view that deduction needs a non-circular justification and for the view that it needs none.
