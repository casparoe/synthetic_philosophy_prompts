Next week I am presenting to a history department seminar of roughly twenty people, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a restatement of the paper. The thesis I will defend is that counterfactual reasoning in history can support judgments about differential causal contribution — how much a factor mattered for an outcome — but cannot by itself support judgments about historical inevitability. My argument is that contribution claims need only a difference-making test against a background held fixed: we ask whether the outcome would have been absent without the factor, holding as much else constant as the scenario allows. Inevitability claims need something stronger: an ordering of alternative histories by closeness to the actual course of events. I argue that the surviving evidence at best fixes that ordering locally, not globally, so inevitability language in historiography is usually driven by an unearned metric of similarity. The paper distinguishes minimal short-run divergences from long-range branching scenarios and from structural claims of the form that some large-scale outcome or other was bound to occur; it argues that only the first kind carries causal-weight information, while the latter two quietly presuppose a global closeness ordering; and it closes by showing that when historians slide from a without-this-not-that result to a claim that the outcome was overdetermined, the implicit choice of background is doing the real work rather than the evidence. My running example is the familiar controversy over the outbreak of the First World War, used only to separate these two kinds of counterfactual claim.

The audience: most are social and cultural historians who regard counterfactual history as unfalsifiable storytelling and think the discipline's task is to interpret surviving traces rather than imagine alternatives; two or three are economic historians comfortable with explicit counterfactual benchmarks of the sort common in cliometric work, who will concede the first half of the thesis and attack the second; one is a philosopher of history sympathetic to the project but impatient with hand-waving about closeness; and one senior early-modernist who suspects that all contingency-and-necessity language smuggles in present-day commitments. The talk is twenty-five minutes, followed by forty-five minutes of questions, and I cannot introduce new formal machinery in my replies.

Prepare me for the objections in the order this particular room is likely to raise them. For each objection: who raises it, phrased in their vocabulary, how likely and how damaging it is, the reply I should give in the time available, what to concede, and what to hold. Mark the places where my reply is weakest, and then, separately, tell me the objection I should be hoping nobody asks and why it would land hardest with this audience.
