For a short paper in my political philosophy elective, I have been comparing Edmund Burke's caution about abstract innovation with G. K. Chesterton's fence example, in which a would-be reformer encounters a fence across a road and, instead of simply taking it down, first asks why it was built. I have mostly been reading the fence as a rule about reform: one may not remove or alter an existing institution until one has reconstructed the reasons for its existence, because otherwise one might destroy something whose purpose one failed to notice. If that is the rule, however, it seems to prove far too much. A reformer can never be sure that every reason has been found, and for any institution at all someone can always suggest another hidden function that has not been ruled out. The principle then appears to make meaningful reform impossible, or to demand a kind of omniscience that no person actually has. I also took Burke to be saying roughly the same thing: that existing institutions should be preserved because they carry accumulated wisdom, and that change should come slowly and in small increments.

But my teacher suggested that Burke and Chesterton are not making the same argument, and I cannot see clearly where they come apart. I would like to know what the fence principle is actually claiming. Is it an empirical generalization that old institutions usually serve real purposes, a procedural claim about who bears the burden of proof in arguments over change, or a substantive conservative principle that change should be avoided? And what exactly does the reformer owe to an institution whose original purpose is obscure: must he reconstruct every actual reason for it before touching it, or does due care require only that he make a serious attempt to understand it and address the reasons he does find?
