ok so my friend and i recently spent an entire evening arguing about our family's shared woodlot—twelve households co-own it, mostly for firewood and a little maple sugaring, and the usual "everyone just take what they need and pitch in when you can" thing has completly broken down. the same three families do almost all the thinning and fix the sap lines, while a couple others take way more firewood than seems fair and nobody feels like they can say anything.

my friend's idea, which we went back and forth on, is this. no hard quotas on wood or sap, ever. instead every household logs what they take (cords, gallons of sap, etc.) and what they give (maintenance hours, materials bought, and so on) on a shared spreadsheet pinned to the sugarhouse door, visible to everyone. each spring you compute a single "draw score" per household: total resource value taken minus total contribution value, using fixed rates the group sets in advance (say X kg of sap = 1 cord = 4 hours of labor). then the quarter of households with the lowest scores—the biggest net givers—become next year's rule committee, and they set the rules for the following season unilaterally: weights, rates, any new caps, whatever, no vote. the quarter with the highest scores get no say at all for a year. his argument is that the people who actually hold back from overuse will make sure the commons survives, and it avoids turning everything into prices and fines. i find it kind of ingenious but also suspect it goes sideways.

so what would this actually do? walk me through how people would game the spreadsheet, what kind of politics would form around getting onto the rule committee or avoiding the disenfranchised quarter, whether the low-score families would genuinely be good stewards or just self-interested in a different way, and whether the whole thing would protect the woodlot better than the current norm-based mess. and what fixes could shore up the worst failure modes without turning it into a plain market?
