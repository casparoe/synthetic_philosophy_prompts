I keep coming back to the argument that my individual choices don't matter: one person's flight or cheap eggs is a drop in the ocean, so why bother. I've been trying to imagine a country that actually removed that feature of the situation. So here's the scenario. Starting next January, a rich democratic country brings in a system where every purchase that causes a measurable harm to someone else — the carbon from a flight, the welfare cost of battery eggs, the local air pollution from a petrol car trip — automatically adds the full estimated social cost of that harm to your monthly bill, itemised, and the shop or airline have to show you the amount on the till screen before you pay. Nothing else changes. I want a reasoned prediction of how consumption of these things shifts over the next ten or twenty years, how it plays out differently for rich and poor households, and what the politics of the system look like after a while. Walk me through the mechanisms and incentives; tell me which effects are robust and which are guesses, and what would have to hold for the policy to backfire badly. Also be honest about where the 'my choices don't matter' argument was doing real work and where it was just an excuse. No headings, bullet points, bold, or tables — continuous prose like an email, and British English.
