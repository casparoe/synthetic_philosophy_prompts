So I stayed up way too late arguing with a friend about whether there can be reasons without anyone who has them, and I keep going back and forth. On his side (he's the naturalist) theres this idea he calls free-floating rationales: the shape of a gull's wing has a rationale — more lift with less drag — even though no gull ever figured that out, and natural selection is not a mind that thought it either. The rationale is just there, and the selection process tracks it. I find that genuinely attractive, because it explains all the design-like talk in biology without smuggling in a designer, and it seems to give normativity a foothold in the world that isn't spooky: there is a fact about this arrangement of bones being better than that one, and that fact is a reason that obtained before anybody noticed it.

But I also have this firm intuition that a reason has to be someone's reason — something that can figure as a ground for acting or believing. If nothing can take it up, then calling it a reason is just a metaphor for a cause, and equivocating between the two is a category mistake, like a verdict with no judge or a score with no game. The friend says that intuition is just leftover anthropomorphism and I should get over it. I can't tell if he's right, or if the intuition is tracking something the theory leaves out.

Walk me through the strongest version of each side and then negotiate. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that gets dropped — is that a debunking story, or a distinction between reasons and rationales, or something else? What would it take for the theory to be narrowed or amended so the intuition survives? And is there a reading where both come out true: free-floating rationales are real, but they only become reasons once some mind-ish condition is met? I dont want a false tie; I want to know which concession costs less and why.
