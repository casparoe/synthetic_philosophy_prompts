I am a computer science major taking a philosophy course on shared agency and joint action, and I have noticed that I keep reading the course material through the lens of distributed systems. I am fluent in that vocabulary: consensus protocols and quorums, replicated state machines and logs, atomic commit and two-phase commit, message ordering, failure detection, safety and liveness, and the standard results about how common knowledge behaves when communication is asynchronous or lossy. Please give me a systematic translation of the main philosophical accounts we have studied, done one by one: the planning-theoretic account of shared intention, the plural-subject account, and collective-acceptance or we-mode accounts.

For each account, please say which claims translate cleanly — for example, whether meshing subplans is naturally read as something like a jointly consistent commit across processes, or whether the common-knowledge conditions can be read as a distributed common-knowledge condition; which claims can be forced into the vocabulary only with distortion and where exactly the distortion lies; and which claims have no distributed-systems expression at all. I want the translation to be honest: do not import distributed-systems assumptions and present them as if they were already in the philosophical account, and do not quietly replace normative or first-person-plural notions with causal or message-passing notions unless the account is really committed to that.

Finally, report on the residue. For the elements that fail to translate — I am thinking especially of joint commitment, obligations to one's co-participants, entitlement to rebuke, and the sense in which a group acts or believes as a body — tell me whether they point to something the distributed-systems vocabulary is structurally unable to say but that a full account of joint action must include, or to something the philosophical account leaves under-specified enough that no clean formal translation is possible yet. I would also like to know which of the three accounts survives the translation most intact, and whether that is because it is the most formally precise or because it has already, perhaps without saying so, absorbed distributed-systems-like ideas.
