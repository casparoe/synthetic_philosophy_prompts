I'm a science journalist working on a piece about sign languages and what they do to the definition of language. I went back to Sapir's Language, and early on he makes this move: walking shows up in a child no matter what the social group, speech is learned from a particular social group; one is biological, the other cultural. But then when he mentions sign languages he seems to treat them as secondary substitutes for speech, which feels like a dodge. I have a pile of verdicts and no principle that fits them. A home sign system invented by one deaf child with hearing parents is a real language, just not a shared one. Nicaraguan Sign Language, created by children in a school, is a full language. A village sign language used by deaf and hearing people alike is full. But a mime acted out at a party is not language, it's depiction. Charades are not language. Emoji strings are not a language. A written script for a spoken language is not a separate language, and a fixed repertoire of traffic signals is not a language. The home sign case and the mime case both look improvised and gestural, yet I want them on opposite sides, so the improvised/systematic line isn't doing the work. What principle captures my verdicts, and which does it force me to give up? I want it in continuous prose, no headings, bullets, bold, or tables, like an email.
