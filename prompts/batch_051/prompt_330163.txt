Two analysts at the same firm, Elena and Rafi, spent an afternoon arguing about the probability that a candidate drug will clear Phase III. They work from the same institutional prior and both consider themselves careful Bayesians, but they have seen different evidence: Elena has spent weeks with the published immunology literature, and Rafi has read a proprietary toxicity report that Elena has not seen. At one point they wrote their posterior probabilities on a whiteboard: Elena wrote 30 percent, Rafi wrote 80 percent. Then they began arguing about whether the disagreement itself could be rational.

Elena argued it could not. Each announced number, she said, is itself evidence. Her 30 percent and Rafi's 80 percent are summaries of what each has seen, and hearing the other's summary should make each revise toward the other. If they each trust that the other is a rational Bayesian updating on a shared prior, then continuing to hold different posteriors after the announcement is not a difference of evidence but a sign that at least one of them is not updating as they claim.

Rafi replied that this moved too quickly. A posterior is a compressed summary, not the evidence that produced it. His 80 percent might rest on a toxicity signal Elena has not seen, while her 30 percent might rest on a pattern in the published trials he has not read. Exchanging numbers is not the same as exchanging findings, he said, and different people conditioning on different information should be expected to arrive at different posterior probabilities.

Elena answered that the agreement result she had in mind contains no step in which the underlying signals are exchanged. The point of the theorem is precisely that two rational Bayesians with a common prior cannot make their posterior probabilities common knowledge and still disagree. Once each knows, and knows the other knows, that the numbers are 30 and 80, those two numbers cannot both be the posteriors each would stand behind after taking the announcements into account. Either one of them must revise or one of the assumptions has failed.

Rafi said the assumptions are exactly what he doubts. Real analysts do not literally share one prior down to every event, and they do not carve up the space of possible trial outcomes the same way. The theorem, he said, is a consistency condition for an idealization, not a discovery about what two actual researchers should conclude in a noisy lunchtime argument.

Elena responded that if the theorem is allowed to lose all force whenever someone invokes an imperfectly shared prior or misaligned partitions, then it can never discipline any real disagreement. The moment two people disagree, she said, one of them could always insist the idealization does not apply; but then the result is being treated as unfalsifiable, and it would be strange to think that the rational response to hearing a very different posterior from a competent colleague is to change nothing.

They asked me to referee and I could not settle it. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: which claims are correct, which are overstated, and what exactly would have to be true for each side's use of the theorem to apply to this case?
