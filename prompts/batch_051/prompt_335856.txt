I finally wrote out an argument my brother-in-law has been making at family dinners, and I'm annoyed to admit it's got me cornered. He grew up Catholic, I'm basically Baptist, and this is his thing:

(1) The Protestant rule is sola scriptura: the Bible alone is the final authority for faith and practice, not any church or tradition.
(2) But no text can settle what it means by itself; a text has to be read, and the big passages we actually fight over can be read in more than one plausible way.
(3) So whenever somebody says the Bible says X, what's really doing the work is some reader's interpretation of the text, not the text itself.
(4) If that reader is just the individual believer, then private judgment is the real final authority. If that reader is a church or council, then the church is the real final authority. Either way, the text alone is not the authority.
(5) So sola scriptura can't actually be lived out: it collapses into private judgment or a de facto magisterium, both of which it was supposed to rule out.
(6) And the same move applies to any document that's supposed to be authoritative—a constitution, a contract, a statute. The judge or the lawyer or the agency head becomes the real authority, since those documents also have conflicting plausible readings.
(7) Which would mean the Constitution isn't really the law; the Supreme Court's readings are the law. He doesn't want to say that either, because then the text is doing no work at all.

I think something slips between (2) and (4), but I've tried to specifiy it three times and can't. Which step should I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd be willing to give up (6), but I don't think I can reject (6) without already having found the problem in (2)–(4).
