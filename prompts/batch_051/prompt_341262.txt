I am a high school student helping to run my school's philosophy club, and this month we are preparing a debate on how far a short-term visitor ought to follow the everyday conventions of the country they are visiting. Our club is split. One side insists that conventions are only conventions: because tipping, queuing, and similar practices are not genuine moral rules, a visitor who violates them does nothing seriously wrong. The other side insists that when you visit a place you take on some obligation to go along with how things are done there, at least up to a point. I have ended up with the job of writing the opening position paper, and I want the middle section of my argument to be a tier list rather than a general claim, because I suspect the right answer depends on features of each specific practice.

We agreed to focus on five everyday cases rather than laws or formal ceremonies: tipping in restaurants where tipping is customary and the staff rely on it for income; joining the back of a queue instead of walking to the front; jaywalking across an empty street in a place where pedestrians are expected to wait; haggling over prices in a market where bargaining is the standard practice; and removing one's shoes before entering someone's home where that is the local norm. For each case, suppose the visitor knows the local practice and the violation would be noticed, but no one's rights are being violated in an obvious physical or legal way.

Please rank these five conventions into tiers from most to least morally binding on a visitor, and assign each one a score out of ten for how obligatory it is for a visitor to follow it, where ten means it would be seriously wrong to violate it and zero means it is completely optional. For every item, give a sentence or two explaining which features of the practice drove the ranking, for example the degree of harm or reliance involved, whether the rule merely solves a coordination problem or carries additional moral content, how much the local expectation depends on the person playing the role of a visitor, and whether there is a reasonable fallback if the visitor declines to take part. I would also like a final sentence saying which single feature, in your view, should carry the most weight when deciding whether a convention binds a visitor, so that I can use that principle to explain the tier list during the debate.
