I'm in an epistemology seminar that's been circling around Fitch's paradox of knowability, and I'm getting increasingly confused about what exactly Fitch actually showed. I've been working through Fitch's 1963 paper 'A Logical Analysis of Some Value Concepts,' specifically the part called Theorem 5. My paraphrase of it is: if there is some true proposition that nobody in fact knows, then there is also some true proposition that is impossible for anyone to know. The contrapositive would be: if every truth is knowable, then every truth is actually known. That much I think I have right. Anyway, my friend—who is annoyingly confident—summarized the whole thing to me over coffee like this: 'Fitch proved that verificationism is self-refuting. The principle that all truths are knowable collapses into the absurd claim that all truths are already known. Here's why. Take any unknown truth p. Then the proposition that p and nobody knows p is also a truth. If every truth is knowable, that conjunction must be knowable. But to know it you'd have to know both conjuncts: you'd know p, and you'd know that nobody knows p. Since knowledge is factive, knowing that nobody knows p means nobody knows p is true; but the knower themself would know p, so somebody does know p after all. Contradiction. So the conjunction is unknowable. Therefore if all truths are knowable, there can't be any unknown truths, so omniscience follows. That refutes verificationism and basically any view that ties truth to verification.' I suspect he's moving too fast somewhere, but I dont know enough formal epistemology to say where. Which parts of his summary are accurate? Which are subtly wrong or overstatements? And what would a defender of the knowability principle say in response to the specific proof he runs through?
