I'm a high school student in a philosophy and society class, and I'm writing a case study on whether competitive environments build or corrode character. I'd like your help thinking through one real case rather than the usual abstractions.

In the 1990s, the energy company Enron cultivated an aggressive internal culture: employees were ranked against one another in a forced-ranking system that regularly dismissed the lowest performers, pay and bonuses heavily rewarded short-term deal-making and reported earnings, and the firm expanded into newly deregulated energy markets. Enron was widely celebrated as proof that competition brings out excellence. In late 2001, it collapsed in one of the most notorious accounting-fraud scandals in American corporate history.

I want to analyze Enron's collapse through two specific mechanisms, not the vague slogan. On the building view, competition improves character insofar as it provides honest feedback, rewards disciplined effort, and teaches people to cope with loss and improve within clear, stable rules. On the corroding view, competition degrades character especially when the stakes are high and outcomes are zero-sum: it crowds out intrinsic motivation, treats rules as parameters to be managed rather than constraints to respect, and normalizes treating competitors and colleagues as instruments.

Please do the mapping carefully. Who were the relevant actors competing, both inside the firm and in the markets it entered, with what stakes, and under what rules? Which features of the collapse does each view genuinely explain, and where does each view misdescribe the episode or fall silent? Finally, I want your honest judgment about what the case does to the two views: is Enron best read as strong evidence for the corroding view, or is it better read as a case of administered ranking and distorted incentives that never satisfied the building view's preconditions in the first place—and what would that second reading show about the limits of both views?
