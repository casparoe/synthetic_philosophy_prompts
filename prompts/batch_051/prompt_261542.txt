In On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life, the second of his Untimely Meditations (1874), Friedrich Nietzsche argues in the opening section that there are two opposing forces needed by any healthy individual, people, or culture. One is the capacity to forget, to set a horizon around oneself, to close oneself off from the press of the past. The other is the capacity to remember, to feel historically, but only within limits and at the right moments. He insists that the unhistorical and the historical are both necessary for health; but the section is especially striking because it treats forgetting as the background condition of ordinary human life: the past recedes unless intentionally kept alive through monuments, archives, or historical study.

Now consider a development that seems to reverse that background. In a society with pervasive digital recording, cheap storage, and recommendation systems that resurface the past automatically, forgetting is no longer the default. The past is preserved by default; it is the capacity to forget that now requires effort—deletion, digital erasure, platform design, or legal mechanisms. I am not asking about digital privacy policy, but about what happens to Nietzsche's argument. Does his claim that the unhistorical is a necessary condition for life, health, and action survive when the unhistorical is no longer given by human psychology and social custom but must be actively produced or institutionally guaranteed? Or does the argument now support a different conclusion—that the relevant task is to build forms of artificial forgetting? I want a definite verdict: state which premise of the argument the development bears on, and state whether the argument is dead, needs repair, or goes through nearly unchanged. In your answer, do not merely restate the distinction between monumental, antiquarian, and critical history; instead, explain whether those three modes presuppose the background of forgetting and what becomes of them when memory is abundant and forgetting is scarce.
