I'm a distributed-systems engineer — I think comfortably in terms of consensus, leader election, quorums, fencing, network partitions, split-brain failures, linearizability, state-machine replication, and the CAP theorem — and I know the philosophical literature on split-brain patients only in outline. I want a systematic translation, not a survey.

Take this background as given. In the classic split-brain experiments, after the corpus callosum is cut, information presented to one hemisphere may not be available to the hemisphere controlling speech: a patient can deny seeing an image while the hand controlled by the other hemisphere selects the matching object; the two hands sometimes work at cross-purposes; and the speaking hemisphere often supplies a fluent but false explanation of actions initiated by the other side. Outside the laboratory the patient mostly behaves as one person.

The philosophical debate concerns whether consciousness remains unified in such patients. For this exercise, reduce the debate to three positions. (1) The one-subject view: the patient remains a single subject of experience, and only capacities and access are divided, not consciousness itself. (2) The two-streams view: each hemisphere hosts its own stream of consciousness, so there are two experiencing subjects. (3) The indeterminate-count view: the ordinary concept of a mind, or subject, gives no determinate answer to how many subjects there are.

Translate each position into the vocabulary of a distributed system, as literally and as honestly as possible. Use the obvious base mapping — the patient as the system, the hemispheres as nodes, the severed corpus callosum as the cut channel or broken replication link, behavior as writes, and speech and other observable outputs as externally visible commits. But do not stop at relabeling. Push the mapping: ask what could play the role of a quorum, of a fence, of a stale read, of a conflicting write that gets resolved against the body, of a commit log that records which node originated an action. Be careful not to smuggle in the engineering assumption that a partition is a fault to be eliminated, or that there ought to be a single authoritative write path; if those assumptions enter, flag them as imports rather than as renderings of the philosophical claim.

Within each translation section, use these three subheadings: Translates cleanly, Only under distortion, and No expression. Under No expression, say what has no home at all in the distributed-systems vocabulary.

Then write the Residue report: assess whether the failures of translation are a limit of the distributed-systems vocabulary — for example, its silence about phenomenology and about what it is like to have two streams — an imprecision in the philosophical positions, or a warning about the whole demand to count subjects the way we count authoritative nodes. Say which parts of the split-brain debate the distributed-systems vocabulary genuinely clarifies, and which parts it should never be asked to settle.

Respond in exactly the following titled sections, in this order, aiming for about 1,200 words overall:

1. Setup and ground rules, roughly 150 words: commit to the specific distributed-systems concepts you will use and the rules of honest translation you will follow.
2. The one-subject view, translated, roughly 250 words.
3. The two-streams view, translated, roughly 250 words.
4. The indeterminate-count view, translated, roughly 250 words.
5. Residue report, roughly 300 words.

Each of sections 2 through 4 should use the subheadings Translates cleanly, Only under distortion, and No expression.
