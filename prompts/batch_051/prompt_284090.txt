Hey, I teach an upper-level seminar on formal epistemology and probability — about 14 students, 80 minutes, and they already know basic Bayesian conditioning and what a partition is. I want an activity where they feel why Jeffrey conditionalization exists instead of me lecturing it: the idea that some evidence is uncertain — you don't learn a proposition for certain, you just shift your odds on an evidence partition, like seeing a bit of cloth by dim candlelight and becoming 0.7 that it's green without ever being certain it's green. I want them to run into that by having to actually bet.

I have a half-baked design, can you make it runnable? Deck of 100 cards: 60 red, 40 orange. Each card has a symbol on the back, star or moon, and the base rates are public: P(star|red)=0.75, P(star|orange)=0.25. Students never see the actual color, only the card through some colored filter/gel or bad lighting that makes red and orange genuinely confusable. After looking they give (a) their posterior that the card is red, as a real number strictly between 0 and 1, and (b) their posterior that the back shows a star. We score (b) with log scoring and fake money, then later reveal only the symbol, never the color, so the uncertainty about the color never gets resolved. Round one lets them do it however they like — I expect most will wing it and their star-probabilities will be all over the place in a way that ignores the base rates. Round two imposes the rigidity rule: their P(star|red) and P(star|orange) stay fixed at 0.75 and 0.25 no matter how red the card looks, since the filter tells them nothing about the deck's composition, and their star-probability has to be the mixture q×0.75 + (1−q)×0.25, where q is their red-posterior. Maybe a third round where they look twice through two differet filters and update twice, without ever conditioning on a settled report, just to feel that the input probabilities can keep moving.

Please turn this into a proper 80-minute activity: pair structure or roles, exact materials (what kind of filter or bulb actually works?), a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened to Jeffrey conditionalization — and I'll name the view only afterwards. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and end with an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce. My worry: it just takes the q, the new probability of red after looking, as given, and doesn't do anything to justify that direct shift — the activity might smuggle in the very move Jeffrey needs to defend. Flag that and say how to handle it in the debrief.
