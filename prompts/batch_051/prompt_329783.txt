I cover science and ideas for a living, and the hardest calls are the ones where two researchers I respect look at roughly the same data and end up on opposite sides. A few years back I read a book by a veteran science editor (the kind that promises to teach reporters how to handle expertise) and he summarized the epistemology of disagreement roughly like this: when two people are equally informed and equally sharp, and they've both looked at the same evidence, neither is justified in keeping their original confidence. Rationality pushes them toward each other, and in the ideal case where they're exactly evenly matched, that means meeting in the middle. From there he drew a practical lesson: a reporter should treat peer disagreement as evidence that the evidence is balanced, and shouldn't call one side more likely unless there's a clear asymmetry in credentials or track record. I've mostly followed that rule since. Then a philosopher I interviewed for a story—one who thinks you can sometimes stick to your guns even when a peer disagrees—told me the editor's account is a common misreading of conciliationism and that the rule I took from it doesn't actually follow. I cant tell if he's right or just invested in making his corner sound subtle. Can you audit the editor's summary for me: what would a conciliationist actually accept, whats an oversimplification, and does my reporting rule survive once the summary gets corrected?
