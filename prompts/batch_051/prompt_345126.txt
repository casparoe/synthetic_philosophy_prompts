I'm a student in a course on the foundations of probability, and we have been working through Bertrand's chord problem and its relationship to the principle of indifference. I want you to produce the document an adversarial collaboration would produce between two positions that keep missing each other in seminar.

Position A, the qualified defender of indifference, reads the paradox as a warning about ill-posed questions rather than a refutation of the principle. On this view, once you specify the symmetry structure the prior must respect — the relevant invariance conditions, such as rotation invariance in the chord case — the apparent ambiguity disappears, because what looked like one problem was really several different problems wearing the same informal description. The three classical values for the chord probability are, on this view, answers to different questions.

Position B, the conventionalist, draws the opposite lesson. On this view, the paradox shows that there is no unique, principled notion of non-informative: every symmetry or partition choice is a choice with content, and the idea that a transformation-group argument delivers the uniquely rational prior just relocates the arbitrariness rather than removing it. The honest position is to give up on a single objective prior and work instead with explicit subjective priors, imprecise probabilities, or classes of permissible distributions.
