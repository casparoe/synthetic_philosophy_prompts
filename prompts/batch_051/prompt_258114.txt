I am preparing a worked example for a jurisprudence seminar on statutory interpretation, and I want a case that forces textualism and purposivism to come apart rather than converge on the same result. The example uses a model provision I have drafted in the register of the old Victorian offences of extortion by letter, rather than any real Act:

“Whosoever shall send, or cause to be sent or delivered, any letter or other writing demanding money or any other valuable thing of any person, without reasonable or probable cause, and with intent to extort the same, shall be guilty of an offence.”

The case I intend to put to the students is this. D wishes to extort £500 from V and records a voice message on a popular messaging application in which she says: “Pay me £500 by Friday or I will send those photographs to your employer.” The application transcribes every voice message into text automatically; V, who is deaf, never plays the audio and reads only the transcript, which appears in the app as a block of text on his phone. D has typed nothing to V, and the transcript is produced by speech-to-text software on V’s device, with small inaccuracies, and is never seen or approved by D. The original audio self-deletes two minutes after it is first played, and in fact it is never played by V at all.

I would like to know whether, on the words of the provision, D has sent or caused to be sent or delivered “any letter or other writing”, and how a textualist and a purposivist each would deal with the transcript, the audio, and the self-deletion. I am less interested in the outcome under any particular jurisdiction’s canons than in what the two methods make of each element of the case, where they would find the answer indeterminate or the evidence relevant, and whether their usual justifications survive contact with a statute whose wording has been overtaken by the technology it now has to govern. Please also address the counterfactual the seminar will press: if the provision’s original drafters were asked whether their section reached spoken words converted into writing by machinery at the recipient’s end, what should each method say about whether, and why, that answer matters?
