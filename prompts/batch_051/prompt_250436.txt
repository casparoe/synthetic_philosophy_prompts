I write about science and ideas for a living, and for a while now I've been reading back through the old philosophy journals for a piece on debates that quietly stop instead of ending with a winner. The one I can't get out of my head is the fight over the analytic-synthetic distiction. As I understand it: Carnap and the logical empiricists built a huge amount on the idea that some truths hold just in virtue of meaning, the analytic ones, while others have to answer to the world, the synthetic ones. Quine argued in Two Dogmas of Empiricism that nobody has ever given a non-circular account of analyticity, that the distinction cant be drawn without already assuming something like synonymy, and that the whole notion rests on equally unexplicated ideas like meaning. His rival picture was that our beliefs face experience as a whole web, with no sentence immune to revision in principle. Grice and Strawson then shot back that he was demanding a standard of definition nobody could meet, and that competent speakers have no trouble sorting the all-bachelors-are-unmarried kind of sentence from the all-bachelors-are-unhappy kind. Putnam and others eventually piled on from different directions, and then the literature just... moved on. Not because anyone had been refuted. People started building successor notions — conceptual roles, two-dimensional semantics, natural kind terms, externalism about meaning — some of which look suspiciously like the old distinction sneaking back through a side door. But almost nobody declares victory or defeat.

My working theory is that what stalled was never one debate. I suspect there were at least three fights sharing a single heading: one about epistemology, whether some claims get justified in a special meaning-based way; one about semantics, what meaning would have to be for some truths to be fixed by it; and one about psychology, how individual minds actually handle sentences that feel definitional. Quine's arguments were strongest in the first fight and weakest in the third, but the parties kept using the same words across all three.

So what I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict. What kind of disagreement is the stalled version of this? A substantive one that just got hard, a verbal one over the word meaning, three different questions under one name, or an ill-posed question philosophy was right to abandon? Be concrete about what each side actually said without turning it into a survey. And then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a formal result, an experiment, a new distinction, or an argument that none should be expected. I keep going back and forth on whether this topic even deserves the essay, and I'd rather know what the disagreement actually is before I write it.
