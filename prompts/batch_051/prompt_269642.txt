I've come around to thinking the common prior assumption isn't just a technical convenience in game theory, but is pointing at a real normative truth about rational disagreement. Drop the formalism and the claim is roughly this: if two agents start from the same prior over some domain, update on the same information, and all of that is common knowledge between them, then any remaining difference in their posterior credences shows that at least one of them reasoned badly. Rational agents can't end up disagreeing after seeing the same evidence from the same starting point. I know the theorem already, so don't lecture me on its conditions — I want to know what would count against the philosophical claim.

In particular: what would have to be true for this to be false, or true-but-toothless? If rational permissivism were right — if the same body of evidence could rationally support more than one conclusion — that looks like a direct counterexample, but is it a serious possibility or a fringe position, and what is the strongest form of it? Does the whole setup secretly assume that "the same prior" is even well-defined for finite, physically embedded minds that don't share literally all background beliefs? Would imprecise credences, bounded rationality, or computational limits leave the formal result standing while killing it's normative bite? And which of these supposed defeaters would a committed defender actually concede counts against them, and which would they dismiss as changing the subject? I want them ranked from "this kills the view" down to "this is just annoying," plus a honest verdict on whether the view has been insulated by idealization — and if so whether that insulation is a bug or a feature.

Format:
- The view, stated as strongly as you can (about 80 words)
- Candidate defeaters: what would have to be true for it to be false or vacuous (about 250 words)
- The ranking, fatal to awkward, one or two sentences each (about 200 words)
- What a defender should concede vs. what they can dismiss (about 120 words)
- Bottom line: is it insulated, and does that matter (about 100 words)
