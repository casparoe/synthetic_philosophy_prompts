I am a safety engineer at a company that fields autonomous mobile robots in hospitals. Our robots carry medications, laboratory specimens, linens, and meals. The navigation software lets us specify routing and interaction policies: for example, whether a robot may use a shortcut through a ward where its motor noise and presence are known to disturb sleeping patients, or whether it may enter a crowded corridor and cause people to step aside abruptly. My job is to review proposed policies rather than individual trips, and I do this about once a week. The stakes are real; a badly chosen policy could delay a critical medication or cause a patient to fall. I was trained as an engineer, not a philosopher, and I need a procedure I can run in under an hour, with a one-page form documenting each decision so my supervisor or the hospital safety committee can audit it.

I want you to turn the doctrine of double effect into a decision procedure I can use in these policy reviews. The procedure should be a sequence of questions or tests I work through in order, with clear pass and fail conditions for each. As I understand the doctrine, an action with both good and bad effects can be permissible only if the action is not of a kind that is wrong in itself, the bad effect is not intended either as an end or as a means to the good effect, the good effect is what the agent intends, and the good effect is weighty enough to justify permitting the bad effect. Please correct or refine that understanding where doing so matters for making it usable.

Be explicit about how the procedure should handle the fact that the action under review is really a policy chosen by a design team. Say whose intention counts, and how I should determine intention when team members disagree or when a bad effect was foreseen and accepted but not endorsed. Mark every step at which the procedure cannot eliminate judgment, and state what I should record on the form at that point.

Then run the procedure on three proposed policies:

1. A policy permitting a robot to take a shortcut through a restorative-care ward to avoid a delay of roughly four minutes on a stat medication delivery, where the route is predicted to wake or disturb patients in a unit where sleep is medically important.
2. A policy instructing the robot, when its path is blocked by a disoriented patient who does not respond to a polite request to move, to emit a short, loud alarm specifically because the alarm startles the patient into stepping aside, where the only alternative route would delay a delivery long enough to risk a serious outcome.
3. A policy under which the robot logs a delivery as completed at the door when the robot instead left the item at a nurses' station around the corner, where this practice prevents staff from paging the ordering physician over a minor timing discrepancy.

I am deliberately not telling you which answer I would prefer. If the procedure condemns a policy I might like, say so plainly, and say what in the procedure forces that result. Finally, state clearly what the procedure cannot do: which trade-offs it does not settle, and where I should escalate to a human ethics board rather than run the checklist.
