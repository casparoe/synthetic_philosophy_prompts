I'm a software engineer, not a philosophy person, so this is probably half-baked, but I've been chewing on a distinction for a couple of years and I want someone to stress-test it. I build eval harnesses for LLMs, so I spend a lot of time watching models answer questions they were scored on. Here's the distinction: a system can pass a Turing-style test by **playing the question** or by **playing the questioner**. Playing the question means producing a fluent, on-topic, maybe even insightful answer to whatever string came in. Playing the questioner means your output is shaped by a model of the person on the other end — what they know, what they're actually trying to get at, whether they're testing you, leading you, getting annoyed, changing their mind mid-sentence. The original imitation game, the way people actually run it or talk about it, mostly scores the first thing. But I've come to think the second thing is the actual sign of a mind: a thing has a mind, roughly, insofar as its behavior isnt just a function of the input, but also a function of its model of who sent the input and why. A thermostat fails: it's a pure function of the temperature. A dog passes in a rough way: the same command from a stranger vs its owner gets different behavior, because the dog is tracking something about who's asking and what they want. LLMs are a weird middle case — they've got some audience sensitivity baked in from training, but it feels shallow, like they change register when you tell them to instead of inferring what you need from the situation. I genuinely cant tell if I've invented a real criterion or just a metaphor I like. So: is this distinction already a thing in philosophy or psych under some name I'm too ignorant to know? Is "playing the questioner" actually a good behavioral criterion for mind, or does it break when you push on it? Give me the strongest version of the view I might be trying to state, then tell me where it's wrong or stops being distinctive. Be honest — I've clearly gotten attached to this a little.
