Me and a friend got into a long argument about whether taxation is just state-run theft. He kept saying that the government takes a chunk of what you earn, and if you refuse, men with guns eventually show up, so what's the real difference from a mugger except the uniform? I said there has to be a difference, because we can vote on taxes and the rules apply to everyone. But afterward I couldn't stop thinking about how the law itself treats the two cases, and it's strange. If I refuse to pay my taxes, the state can garnish my wages, put a lien on my house, and eventaully jail me, and the legal system does not treat that as a taking of my property. But if my neighbor tricked me into signing over the same amount from my bank account, that's theft — the state would prosecute him and make him repay me. So the practice seems to presuppose that these are completely different kinds of acts, not just a difference in who holds the gun. What I want to know is: what theory of property, or of consent, does this legal practice actually presuppose? Does it force you into thinking property rights are created by the state in the first place, or is it equally compatible with a natural-rights story where property exists before the state and the government is just setting the terms of membership? Is the practice coherent, or does it quietly depend on several different theories at once depending on which part you look at? And if a legal system really took the taxation-is-theft view seriously, what would have to change — would tax enforcement have to stop, or would the definition of theft have to be rewritten, or something else? I'm less interested in whose slogan wins and more in reconstructing the implicit theory the law is running on, including the places where the practice is genuinely underdetermined between rival theories.
