I have been developing a distinction I call sortal occlusion, and I want a candid appraisal of it before I take it further. The distinction is this: a bare sortal such as horse supplies the criteria for counting, identifying, and classifying the things it applies to. When a modifier is prefixed, as in white horse, the modifier does not merely narrow the range of horses; it occludes the base sortal and institutes a new sortal with its own criteria of identity and application. My central claim is that this gives the right reading of the old School of Names argument that a white horse is not a horse. One of that argument's moves is that asking for a horse is satisfied by a black or yellow horse, whereas asking for a white horse is not; on my reading, that shows only that the two terms are not substitutable in requesting, counting, and identifying contexts. On my reading, the not in the conclusion a white horse is not a horse expresses a denial of sortal substitutability rather than a denial that the animal before you is equine. This lets me say that the School of Names were right about the terms while ordinary talk is right about the animal. What I want to know is whether sortal occlusion is a real distinction or just compositionality dressed up in a new name; whether something like it already exists in the logical or linguistic literature under another name; and where it breaks, for instance, does it force me to say that a brown horse is not a horse as well, and if so, should I accept that or treat it as a reductio of my reading? Reply as exactly five numbered assessments, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
