I'm putting together a film-and-philosophy unit for an intro class and I keep going back and forth on Groundhog Day. The obvious readings are the virtue-ethics one (Phil gradually becomes a decent person) and the existentialist one, but I want to pair it with the old worry about whether a finite mind eventually exhausts the possibilities of experience. The thought, roughly, is that a person only has so many desires, pleasures, and projects to go around, so an indefinitely extended life — or, in the film's version, an indefinitely repeated day — would eventually flatten into pure repetition and tedium, and the only question is how long it takes before you've had everything there is to have.

The movie seems to me to run both ways on this. Early on Phil treats the loop as a license to burn through pleasures — seduction, money, petty crime, binge-eating, suicide attempts — and all of it goes gray on him, which looks a lot like exhaustion. But then the film jumpes forward and shows him learning piano, ice sculpture, French poetry, and the fine-grained needs of everyone in town, and it presents that as genuine growth rather than just a fancier way of killing time. Part of the tangle is that whether experience gets used up depends on how you count experiences: if you count by raw felt quality, a finite creature plausibly has a finite palette and the worry bites; if you count partly by relational content — being the one who noticed what this particular person needed on this particular loop — then the supply might not run out even for a finite mind.

Read Groundhog Day through that exhaustibility worry. Where does the film genuinely dramatize the idea that a finite mind can run out of meaningful experience, where does it resist or complicate that idea, and does the movie end up taking a position on whether the real problem is the size of the world and the person's cognitive limits, or something about the person's stance toward other people? I'd like a reading that is faithful in both directions — one that neither reduces the movie to an illustration nor stretches the philosophy to fit — and that ends by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate about immortality and tedium that a plain argument doesn't. And please write the response in American English.
