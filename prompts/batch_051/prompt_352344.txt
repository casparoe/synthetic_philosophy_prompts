I have read rationalist-adjacent blogs and forums for years, and one strand of debate there keeps compressing a whole axiology into a single line: only the absence of craving and suffering matters. The people who repeat it use it mostly as a conversation-stopper. When someone argues that knowledge, deep relationships, or the satisfaction of a lifelong ambition is good in itself, they answer that none of these things matter except as instruments for reducing craving or suffering, and that a world of minds with no wants and no hurts would be strictly better than our world even if nobody in it understood anything or did anything. I suspect the sentence is doing several different jobs at once. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims it could be used to assert, separating at least the motivational reading from the moral reading from the claim about what value consists in, saying clearly whose craving and suffering are in view and over what time span, whether craving matters because it is itself a form of suffering or in addition to it, and whether the relevant absence is a felt state of peace, a mere lack of negative experience, unconsciousness, or death. For each candidate reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive and probably false, or merely terminological, and say which reading, if any, would actually license the move of dismissing knowledge, relationships, and preference-satisfaction as having no value in themselves. I would rather see the readings set out consecutively as a menu with verdicts than have one treated as the natural interpretation, since I suspect the slide between readings is doing the rhetorical work. And I would appreciate it if you said how confident you are in what you claim and singled out the point you are least sure of. Please write in continuous prose, as you would in a thoughtful email, and avoid headings, bullet points, bold text, and tables.
