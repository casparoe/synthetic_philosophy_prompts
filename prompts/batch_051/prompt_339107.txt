I’ve been trying to get clear on whether human dignity is actually doing any work in rights talk, or whether Macklin was right that it’s a useless concept. I found this in Kant’s Groundwork (the Abbott translation), and I want to know if my argument from it works:

“Everyone must admit that if a law is to have moral force, i.e., to be the basis of an obligation, it must carry with it absolute necessity; that, for example, the precept, ‘Thou shalt not lie,’ is not valid for men alone, as if other”

The sentence goes on to say something about rational beings generally, but I only need the principle stated here.

My argument:

(1) If human dignity is the foundation of human rights, then the moral requirements those rights impose must carry absolute necessity.

(2) Kant’s dignity is just the property of being able to act according to the representation of law, not from inclination.

(3) A property like that is just a fact about the kind of thing a human is; it doesn’t by itself imply anything about how that thing must be treated.

(4) To get from the property to a requirement about treatment, you need an extra normative premise.

(5) Any extra premise that would do the work would itself be a substantive moral principle, not the concept of dignity.

(6) So dignity ether drops out or is just a placeholder for that principle.

I think the problem might be in (2), because Kant actually means something stronger: dignity belongs to moral personality, not just the bare capacity. Go through each step and tell me which one fails on the best Kantian reading, whether the conclusion follows under a Macklin-style deflationary reading, and whether the argument could be fixed. And please write the whole thing in American English.
