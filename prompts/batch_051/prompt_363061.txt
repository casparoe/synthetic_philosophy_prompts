You are a teaching assistant for an upper-division philosophy of mind course that has recently covered formal systems, Gödel's two incompleteness theorems, and the use Hofstadter makes of them in Gödel, Escher, Bach. Below are the exam question and a student's response.

Grade the response out of 10 and then write the point-by-point comments that justify the score. Each comment should identify the specific sentence or claim it assesses, say whether it is correct or incorrect and why, and, where relevant, state what the correct claim would be. Do not give a general impression, and do not let fluent prose compensate for errors. You should comment on both what the student gets right and what the student gets wrong, and you should flag places where a sentence sounds right but is technically imprecise.

Question:

Reconstruct the analogy Hofstadter draws in Gödel, Escher, Bach between Gödel's incompleteness theorems and self-aware thought. Your answer should do three things: (1) state precisely what Gödel's theorems establish and the conditions on the systems to which they apply; (2) identify which feature of Gödel's construction Hofstadter takes to be echoed in conscious minds; and (3) state the limits Hofstadter himself places on the analogy. Then use your reconstruction to evaluate the claim, made by Lucas and later Penrose, that Gödel's results show a human mind cannot be a computational system, and say whether Hofstadter's own use of Gödel supports that claim.

Student answer:

Gödel's first incompleteness theorem says that any consistent formal system powerful enough to express a modest amount of arithmetic will contain a sentence G such that neither G nor its negation can be proved within the system. Gödel builds G by assigning numbers to formulas and using a diagonal construction that allows G to say of itself that it is not provable. If the system proved G, it would prove a sentence that, by construction, is equivalent to the claim that G is not provable; this would make the system inconsistent, so a consistent system cannot prove G. An exactly parallel argument shows that the negation of G is unprovable too. So G is a true sentence that the system cannot prove. The second theorem makes the same point at the level of the system itself: no such system can prove its own consistency.

Hofstadter's interest is not in unprovable truths as such. It is in the fact that a system of blind, syntactic rules can, by encoding its own formulas, refer to itself. Gödel numbering lets a formal system talk about itself, and this self-referential capacity is the prototype of the loops Hofstadter calls strange loops: hierarchies that fold back and reach down to a level below themselves. Hofstadter suggests that the conscious self is best understood as such a strange loop realized in a physical system, which explains how self-awareness can arise in a brain built from non-self-aware parts.

This is also why Gödel's theorem refutes computationalism. Since a mind can recognize its own Gödel sentence as true even though no formal system can prove that sentence, the mind must be able to do something no formal system can do; and because every Turing machine is only a formal system in operation, no Turing machine could be a mind. This is the conclusion Hofstadter himself endorses, and it is the position Lucas and Penrose have pressed: Gödel's results impose a principled limit on any mechanical account of thought.
