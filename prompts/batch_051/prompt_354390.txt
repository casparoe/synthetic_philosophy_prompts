I'm a control systems engineer with no formal philosophy background. I work daily with state-space models, actuators, disturbances, observers, controllability, observability, and feedback. Recently I've been reading around in the modern Stoic revival, and I keep running into its central doctrine, the dichotomy of control. The popular version says: distinguish what you can control from what you can't, and direct attention only to the former. The stricter Stoic version, as I understand it, is narrower still: only our judgments, impulses, desires, and aversions are up to us (ta eph' hēmin, in the Greek), while the body, property, reputation, and office are not up to us. Some modern writers soften this into a trichotomy that also admits a category of things over which we have merely partial control.

I would like a systematic translation, not an analogy or a motivational gloss. Restate the strict doctrine, the popularized slogan, and the trichotomy version, as faithfully as possible, in the vocabulary of control engineering: controlled inputs versus exogenous disturbances; full controllability versus mere influenceability; observability and state estimation; feedforward, feedback, and model-predictive control; deterministic versus stochastic disturbances; and the difference between changing an actuator and changing the reference signal. In particular, show where the popular version treats something as under our control when, by your field's standards, it is at best partially controllable or only statistically picked up in a feedback loop.

Then give a residue report. Which claims survive the translation cleanly, which survive only by distortion, and which cannot be expressed in control-theoretic vocabulary at all? I especially want to know whether up to us is just causal controllability, or whether an irreducibly normative or agential remainder is left over: responsibility, authorship, what one may rationally stake one's happiness on. If there is a residue, tell me whether it marks a limitation of the control-theoretic vocabulary, or whether it shows that the popular Stoic division is less precise, or more quietly normative, than it presents itself as being.
