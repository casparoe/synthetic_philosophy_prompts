Below is a draft of a short essay I plan to use as the opening of a longer paper. I would be grateful for comments on (1) whether the central argument is valid, (2) whether the deflationary conclusion is interesting or merely terminological, and (3) whether there is a strong objection I have missed. Please respond in American English.

I have been thinking about what it means to say that a writing system is phonemic. The familiar contrast between an alphabet like Spanish, which is treated as relatively transparent, and one like English, which is treated as full of historical spellings, suggests that scripts can be graded by how faithfully they encode the sounds of their language. I want to resist the picture behind that suggestion. A script does not record a pre-given inventory of linguistic units; it records a selection of units as significant, and in doing so it embodies an analysis of the language—a theory, in a loose sense, of which differences matter.

A phoneme is not an observable unit in the speech stream. It is an abstraction justified by minimal pairs, complementary distribution, and alternations. So saying that a letter stands for a phoneme is comparing two abstractions: the units of the script and the units of a particular phonological analysis. Whether the vowel sound in "cute" is one phoneme or a sequence of /j/ plus /u/ is a choice of analysis; the distributional facts can be described either way. If so, how phonemic the spelling "cute" is depends on which phonemicization one adopts, and there is no language-independent fact that settles the question.

The conclusion should not be overstated. Plenty remains objective: a script may mark a contrast consistently or sporadically; a consonantal script, or abjad, may mark the consonant skeleton while leaving vowels largely to the reader; a spelling such as "sign"/"signature" may preserve a morphophonemic identity at the cost of surface transparency. What is given up is only the idea of a single, theory-free measure of how phonemic an alphabet is. The better question is what analysis of the language the script embodies—what it treats as the same, what it treats as different, and what it leaves to the reader's knowledge of the language. That is an interpretive question rather than a measurement.

My main uncertainty concerns the move from the theory-ladenness of phonemic analysis to the relativity of phonemic transparency. A critic might object that, once we fix the phonological grammar of a particular language variety and a particular literate community, how closely the script tracks that grammar is a perfectly objective matter. Should I accept that and retreat to the weaker claim that the appearance of a theory-free question comes only from tacitly choosing one grammar, or is there a way to keep the stronger claim? I would welcome comments on this, and on any step that seems too quick.
