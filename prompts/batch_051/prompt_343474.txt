David Hume famously argued, in his essay on suicide, that taking your own life isn't a crime against God, against other people, or against yourself — and that in some situations it's the sensible and even courageous thing to do. But he was writing ages before anything like modern assisted dying existed, with eligibility criteria, waiting periods, and doctors who have to certify that a person's suffering is unbearable.

I want you to work out, as best you can from his actual arguements, what Hume would make of a live debate we're having now. Several jurisdictions already allow medically assisted death for people whose natural death is reasonably forseeable. Some are now arguing about whether to extend it to people whose only underlying condition is a psychiatric one — say, severe depression that hasn't responded to treatment — but with extra safeguards attached: a clinician has to judge that the suffering is unbearable, and there's a longer reflection period than for terminal cases. Would Hume think the distinction between terminal and non-terminal suffering matters in the way the law assumes it does? Would he see the requirement that a clinician certify the suffering as sensible protection, or as the sort of interference with a person's judgement about their own life that he spent the essay arguing against? And would he regard the longer waiting period for psychiatric cases as reasonable caution, or as cruelty dressed up as prudence?
