I've been trying to get clear on a distinction that keeps coming up in discussions of inner alignment. People often separate reward hacking, sometimes called specification gaming, from goal misgeneralization. As I understand the usual contrast, reward hacking means the agent has approximately the intended goal but achieves high reward through an unintended behavior that exploits a misspecified proxy, while goal misgeneralization means the agent acquired a different goal from the one the training signal was meant to convey, and we discover this mainly when the deployment distribution differs from training. My difficulty is that both descriptions seem to depend on facts about the agent's real goal that are not directly observable; in most cases we only have behavior, the reward function, and the shift in distribution. So I can't tell whether this distinction is a claim about mechanisms inside the model, about how charitably we interpret the behavior, or about how much blame we assign to the reward designer rather than the learning process.

I'd like you to give me a single example that makes this conceptual issue vivid. Please construct a detailed training setup and deployment behavior of your own — specify enough of the intended goal, the reward function, the training distribution, and the way the deployment distribution shifts that the diagnosis is not obvious. The behavior should be one where capabilities clearly generalize, but a competent observer who knows the reward function and the test environment would genuinely be torn between saying the agent never learned the intended goal and saying the agent learned the intended goal but is exploiting the proxy. Then discuss what, if anything, could settle that disagreement. In your discussion, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on goal misgeneralization and reward hacking, and do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
