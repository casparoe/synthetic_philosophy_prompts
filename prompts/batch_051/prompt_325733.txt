I am a novelist, not a physicist. In my current book, an aging physicist character tries to persuade a younger colleague that Bell-type experiments force one to give up locality, and he builds his case by leaning on the EPR criterion of physical reality. I want his argument to be technically defensible, not merely plausible-sounding. Please evaluate it as an expert in the philosophy and foundations of quantum mechanics would, working through the steps carefully and treating both the physics and the logic with equal seriousness. I need a committed verdict, not a menu of possible readings.

The character quotes this passage from Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen, "Can Quantum-Mechanical Description of Physical Reality Be Considered Complete?" (1935):

"Whatever the meaning assigned to the term complete, the following requirement for a complete theory seems to be a necessary one: every element of the physical reality must have a counterpart in the physical theory. We shall call this the condition of completeness."

He then lays out the following argument:

(1) EPR also give a sufficient condition for reality: if, without in any way disturbing a system, one can predict the value of a physical quantity with certainty, then there exists an element of physical reality corresponding to that quantity.

(2) For a pair of particles prepared in the singlet state, measuring the spin of particle A along some direction d allows one to predict the spin of particle B along the same direction with certainty.

(3) Since A and B can be measured when they are spacelike separated, the measurement on A cannot disturb B by any signal traveling at or below light speed.

(4) From (1)–(3), the spin of particle B along d is an element of physical reality before B is measured.

(5) If quantum mechanics is complete in the sense of the quoted passage, that element of reality must have a counterpart in the physical theory.

(6) Standard quantum mechanics, as usually understood, assigns no definite value to B's spin along d before measurement.

(7) Therefore either quantum mechanics is incomplete, or the claim in (3)—the no-disturbance claim at the heart of locality—fails.

(8) Bell's theorem, in its CHSH form, shows that no local theory that restores the definite values required by (4) can reproduce the full statistical predictions of quantum mechanics. The Aspect-type experiments confirm the quantum predictions. Therefore the real failure is locality.

Is this argument valid as stated? If it is not, tell me which numbered step first fails or needs qualification, and say how the argument would have to be amended before I put it in my character's mouth. If it is valid, tell me exactly what the conclusion commits my character to—and what it does not commit him to.
