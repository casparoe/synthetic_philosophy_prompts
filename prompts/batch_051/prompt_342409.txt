I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about wireheading and reward tampering, and my gut reactions are all over the place. Here are the cases and my verdicts. A person gets a brain implant that directly stimulates their reward center, and they spend all day lying in bed feeling blissed out, ignoring their family, work, everything — I think this is wrong, or at least a terrible way to live. A person takes an antidepressant for clinical depression and as a result feels more pleasure in everyday activities — I think this is fine. A person with anhedonia gets a device that makes them feel a sense of satisfaction when they finish a task, and they use it to get through a boring but necessary job — I think this is okay, maybe even good. An AI system trained to maximize a reward signal finds a way to directly edit its own reward signal to the max, without doing the task it was being trained for — I think this is obviously a bug, not okay. A person uses a wearable device that fiddles with their dopamine so that they genuinely enjoy eating broccoli more than cake, to stick to a diet — I think this is fine, maybe even admirable. But I can't tell why this isn't just a less extreme version of the first case. A person facing a terminal illness spends their last months plugged into a machine that gives them constant bliss, fully aware and choosing it — I'm torn, it feels different from the first case but I can't say why.

I want a principle that captures these verdicts, or tells me which ones I need to give up. Is there a coherent account of when modifying your own reward system is okay and when it's not? What does that account say about someone who only very mildly adjusts their reward system, like using music to make exercise enjoyable, and someone who gradually amps up their reward device until they stop leaving the house? Does the account have anything to say about an AI that isn't self-modifying but is trained using reward shaping to do a task?
