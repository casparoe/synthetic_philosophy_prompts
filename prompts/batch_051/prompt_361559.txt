I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas beat, and I keep needing a working definition of "understanding" because the piece I'm writing keeps sliding into the old fight about wether language models understand anything or are just statistical parrots. (Please don't tell me that fight is settled; my inbox says otherwise.) My gut is that a physicist who can explain why the sky is blue, notice when a follow-up question clashes with what she said earlier, and then repair the explanation clearly understands something. A thermostat doesn't. A four-function calculator doesn't, even though it's a lot more reliable than I am. What I can't do is say what the difference consists in.

So here are the cases I want the definition to run against. Clear yes: the physicist. Clear no: the thermostat, the calculator. The hard ones: (1) a current large language model with no tools, explaining why the sky is blue, correcting itself when I point out an inconsistency, and predicting how two different experts would disagree; (2) a student who has memorized an entire physics textbook and can answer every standard question, but fails on a question that requires recombining the material in a way the book never displays; (3) the same language model hooked up to a retrieval system so it can quote sources; (4) a chess engine that plays superhuman chess but can't produce a single sentence about a position; (5) a lookup table with an entry for every possible prompt under two hundred words, each paired with a perfect response, but with no other structure to it; (6) a person who uses "because" correctly in almost every sentence but can't explain what the word does. I lean yes on (1) and (3), no on (5), and I genuinely don't know about the rest.

Here's what I'd like back. Propose a working definition of "understands" or "understands X" that gets my clear cases right and gives a definite verdict on each numbered case. Run it through all of them. Tell me where it surprises me or forces me to revise a lean. And if the definition turns out to be doing something suspect—like resting on how the system was built rather than what it actually does in the world—say so.
