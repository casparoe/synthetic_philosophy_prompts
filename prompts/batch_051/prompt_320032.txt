I run referee education for a semi-pro soccer association, and I'm trying to write the video-review protocol our three-person crews use when they go to the monitor. Here's the thing I keep noticing: when a referee expects a call before the replay loads, because of the crowd, the angle, the player's reputation, or what the assistant flagged, what they then "see" on the screen mostly matches what they already believed. I've read enough to know this has a name, cognitive penetration of perception, and that people genuinely disagree about whether the expectation changes the perceptual experience itself or only the decision you make downstream. I don't care about the academic fight; I need a procedure my refs can actually run.

So: you're an expert on the philosophy of perception and the cognitive science of top-down effects. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming the different mechanisms by which a prior expectation could contaminate a video review. Then write a decision procedure, grounded in what that field actually supports, that a crew chief with no philosophy training can run in under two minutes at the monitor, several times a match, with the crew chief having to sign off on the final call. It should list the questions to ask in order; say what counts as a sufficient answer to each; say where the procedure stops with "stick with the on-field call"; say where it hands off to a human because the science is contested rather than settled; and flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. In particular, make the procedure seperate what you can settle from the screen—geometry, timing, contact points—from what you can't, like whether the referee's perceptual experience itself was penetrated or only their judgment after the fact.

Then run the procedure on two cases and show the reasoning at each step: (a) an offside where the assistant's flag went up a beat late, after the attacker scored and started celebrating, and the freeze-frame is genuinely tight; (b) a handball where the defender's arm is out, and the first thing the referee sees is a slow-motion loop clipped to make the contact look deliberate. Make the procedure come out against the original on-field call in at least one of them, and end with an honest note about what the procedure cannot do—the places where an experienced referee's judgment just has to take over.
