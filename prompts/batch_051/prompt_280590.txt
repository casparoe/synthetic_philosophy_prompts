You are a professional philosopher working in normative ethics and practical reason. Treat this as a supervision problem: I am a thoughtful non-specialist with a genuine theoretical tension, not someone asking you to recite the standard taxonomy. Think carefully, argue precisely, and do not confuse me with a colleague.

I find myself caught between a theory I am attracted to and an intuition I cannot shake, and I want a negotiated verdict rather than a declaration of which side wins.

The theory is roughly this: moral reasons are overriding reasons. If an act would be wrong, no amount of personal enjoyment, aesthetic payoff, or convenience justifies doing it; if an act is morally required, that is what I have most reason to do, full stop. On this picture, the best kind of person is the moral saint: someone who is as morally good as a human being can be, whose deepest commitments are moral commitments, and who shapes her projects, relationships, and tastes around what morality demands. The theory has systematic virtues I find genuinely compelling. It is simple. It gives a single, unified answer to what I have most reason to do. It explains why moral considerations often seem not merely strong but silencing. And it leaves no fudge factor: if you take morality seriously, the ideal life is the life of maximal moral virtue.

But I also have a firm intuition that this ideal is somehow impoverished. A life in which every nonmoral good is admitted only because it serves moral improvement seems to me not merely less rich, but in an important way failed or alienated. The person who reads literature only to enlarge her sympathetic imagination, who keeps friendships because they make her more effective at helping others, who plays the violin because it helps her raise money for charity, and who never lets anything absorb her for its own sake does not strike me as the most admirable kind of person. I suspect I would not want to be her, and I suspect I would not especially want her as a friend. I want to love particular people and be absorbed by activities in ways that are not constantly held before the bar of whether they make the world morally better. This is not a prejudice I already half-disown; on reflection, the intuition feels quite firm.

I need to know what it would take for each side to yield. In particular:

1. Under what conditions should I conclude that my intuition is a debunkable bias, for example a bit of self-indulgence, ordinary partiality, or a failure of imagination about how rich the saint's inner life could be? What would a successful debunking explanation actually have to show before I should discard the intuition?

2. Under what conditions should I amend or abandon the theory instead? For instance, should I deny that moral reasons are always overriding; should I hold that there are nonmoral final values that are not merely instrumental to moral ones; or should I deny that the ideal human life is the life of maximal moral virtue, holding instead that an ideal life includes morality among other independent goods?

3. Is there a reading on which the theory and the intuition are compatible? The obvious candidate is that the moral saint can love nonmoral goods for their own sake, not merely as instruments, so long as this sustains a richer and therefore more morally effective character. But would that genuinely preserve what the intuition cares about, or would it merely let the saint treat nonmoral goods as useful while telling a comforting story about loving them for their own sake?

Please begin by brainstorming at least three distinct candidate resolutions, and say explicitly before you argue which one you will defend and which you think is hardest to defend. Then give your all-things-considered verdict. I care less about which move you ultimately prefer than about what would justify each possible move, and about whether the compatibility reading is real or just verbal.
