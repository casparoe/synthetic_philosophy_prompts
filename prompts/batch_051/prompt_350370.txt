I am a high school senior and the student representative on my school district's Substance Use Policy Review Committee. Our current policy is effectively prohibition on campus: a student caught in possession of any controlled substance is given a five-day suspension and referred to the school resource officer, and a second offense can mean expulsion. The committee is now considering a health-first alternative for first-time personal possession only. Under it, a student would face no suspension and no police referral, but would be required to attend a counseling session and a short educational program, while the school's existing penalties for selling or distributing a drug would remain unchanged. The disagreement on the committee is sharp. Some members frame the proposal as the right lesson from Portugal's well-known decision to stop criminally prosecuting personal drug possession and from the U.S. states that have legalized recreational cannabis: punish genuinely harmful behavior, not the mere fact of use, and keep students in school so they can be helped. Others argue that the proposal is de facto decriminalization on campus, that it blurs the line between prohibition and permission, and that students will read it as the school's quiet approval of drug use.

The committee is deadlocked, and I have been asked to draft the decision memo that will frame our vote. I need the memo to recommend one of three courses of action — keep the existing policy, adopt the health-first replacement for first-time possession, or adopt a hybrid, for example replacing suspension with counseling only for a first cannabis-related offense while keeping harsher sanctions for other drugs — and to defend that recommendation against the strongest objection on the losing side rather than against a weakened version of it. I have been reading about the Portuguese reform and the U.S. cannabis legalization experiments, and it seems to me that our committee uses the words prohibition, decriminalization, and legalization more loosely than the policy debate does. So please be precise about how those three concepts apply when the sanctions are school discipline rather than criminal penalties, and say explicitly where the analogy between school policy and adult drug policy breaks down. The memo should be written so that a superintendent and a skeptical teacher could actually act on it.

Because I will have to answer questions about my own confidence in the recommendation, please close with a brief note saying what would be most likely to change your mind: which empirical claim about adolescent development, deterrence, or the effects of Portugal-style reforms, or which value judgment about what a school is primarily for, would, if it turned out differently, lead you to a different recommendation — and where in the reasoning you think the memo is most fragile.
