Here's one that's been bugging me. Mill gives what he calls a very simple principle in the first chapter of On Liberty: the only justification for interfering with someone's liberty, in a civilized community, is preventing harm to others. Then, almost immediately, he says the principle doesn't apply to children or to what he calls backward states of society, where he thinks despotism is legitimate provided it's aimed at improvement. He actually uses barbarians as the example of people you're allowed to rule like children for their own good.

Then in Considerations on Representative Government, in the section where he discusses dependencies, and in the memoranda and petitions he drafted defending the East India Company (where he worked for most of his adult life), he applies that same caveat to India. Representative government isn't for a people who haven't reached a certain level of civilization; British rule over India is justified as a paternal despotism whose whole point is to develop the country until self-government becomes possible. He's completely unapologetic about it.

The tension: the harm principle is supposed to be a universal principle grounded in utility, but as soon as he needs it, he declares the majority of humanity outside its scope. Either the principle isn't actually the simple universal thing he says it is, or the carve-out is doing a enormous amount of unargued work. And it gets worse: in The Subjection of Women he argues that women's subordination rests on mere custom and the law of force, and that custom is no justification at all. So why is the supposed backwardness of Indians a justification for ruling them by force, when the supposed nature of women isn't? I'm paraphrasing throughout, not quoting.

Can these be reconciled? I want the candidate reconciliations laid out seriously — for example, that the exception actually follows from his utilitarianism because temporary despotism is the policy that maximizes the future liberty of the colonized, or that he means something narrower by despotism in the India case, or that the two books are addressing different questions so there's no real contradiction — and then a verdict on which reconciliation is honest and which is special pleading. Where does the best reconciliation break down?
