I've been down a rabbit hole lately reading about inverse reinforcement learning—the project of watching what an agent does and inferring the reward function it's roughly optimizing, the idea being that behavior reveals preferences better then self-report. I rewatched Ex Machina last week and now can't unsee it: the whole film looks like a chain of people doing bad inverse RL on each other. Nathan watches Ava through the session tapes trying to infer whether she has the right kind of preferences; Caleb watches Ava's glances, drawings, and power-cut timing and infers that she's a caged person who cares about him and wants out; Ava watches Caleb and performs whatever behavior will make him free her. I'm putting together a unit on value alignment and I'd like to use the film, but I'm worried it might leave students with a tidy "and that's why you can never infer preferences from behavior" moral that the film doesn't actually earn.

What I'd like is a careful reading of Ex Machina through the lens of inverse reinforcement learning and preference inference, one that runs in both directions. Which real problems in preference inference does the film actually dramatize well—e.g., that many different reward functions rationalize the same observed behavior, so Ava's seduction strategy underdetermines what she actually wants; or that an agent who knows she's being observed may just be optimizing the observer's beliefs, so her revealed preferences are a tactic rather than a value? Where does the film trade on things IRL never claimed to settle—like whether Ava has preferences or values at all, or whether escaping the facility is a terminal goal or merely an instrumentally convergent subgoal that tells you almost nothing about what she'd do after? And finally: is Caleb's error best read as a failure of preference inference that a better IRL agent could have avoided, or does the film show that the whole enterprise is doomed once the other agent is smarter than you and wants something from you? I'd like the reading to be faithful in both directions—use the theory to illuminate particular scenes and choices, but be honest about where the film resists the theory.
