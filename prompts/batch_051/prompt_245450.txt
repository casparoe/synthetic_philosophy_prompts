I am a software engineer who works on distributed systems, and I keep seeing the socialist calculation debate through the lens of systems design, but I cannot tell whether the lens is genuinely illuminating or just pattern-matching. The mapping I have in mind is this: each firm or household is a node with private local state (inventory, cost structure, preferences); a market price is a message on a shared channel that encodes some of the other nodes' state and lets each node adjust locally without anyone holding a global plan; and the economy is the allocation that emerges from all nodes reacting to messages, the way coordination emerges in a distributed protocol without a monolithic scheduler. Hayek's knowledge argument then sounds like the claim that the market is a distributed coordination mechanism whose relevant information cannot sensibly be collected in one place. Mises's calculation argument sounds like the stronger claim that a central planner has no reliable messages for producer goods at all, because those messages only get generated by exchange, so the planner cannot even run the computation. Lange's market socialism replies that the board can act as a closed-loop controller: announce provisional prices, observe shortages and surpluses, adjust, iterate, and thereby discover the prices a market would have emitted.

I want an assessment of whether this is a deep structural match, a loose analogy, or a confusion. Please be specific: (1) Where does the mapping hold and where does it break? In particular, is a price best modeled as information, as an incentive, or as both, and does an ordinary protocol message carry the same kind of thing? (2) Does this framing correctly keep Mises's problem, that no prices exist to compute with, distinct from Hayek's problem, that relevant knowledge is local and dispersed, or does it collapse the two? (3) What would have to be true on the economics side for distributed-systems results, such as stale reads, consistency/availability trade-offs, or the cost of centralized scheduling, to transfer cleanly? (4) Does the analogy favor one side of the debate, and is that favoritism evidence or artifact? (5) Name something the parallel predicts about markets or planners that is false.
