I just got back from a three-hour argument with a friend about whether there are objectively rational priors, and I'm left genuinly split. She's an objective Bayesian of the maxent stripe: given whatever constraints your background knowledge actually supplies (a known mean, known variance, a known range), you pick the distribution that maximizes entropy subject to those constraints, because it's the unique distribution that adds no information beyond the constraints and leaves everything else as undecided as possible. I'll admit the view has a lot going for it. It gives a tidy algorithm where subjective Bayesians just shrug; it turns the principle of indifference into something with a principle behind it; and it makes "uninformative prior" a well-defined optimization problem rather than a vibe.

But I can't shake the opposite conviction: ignorance doesn't have a probability distribution. The arbitrariness seems to me to be hidden in the choice of the underlying measure or parameterization. Suppose I know only that some quantity lies between zero and one. The maxent prior is uniform over that interval. But if I parameterize the same quantity by its odds or its log-odds, a uniform prior on the new scale corresponds to a sharply non-uniform prior on the original one, and on the log-odds scale it isn't even normalizable. My friend says I'm confusing a prior with a density: the constraint set transforms along with the variables, and the maxent distribution picks out the same probabilities over the underlying propositions, just expressed in different coordinates. Fair enough for constraints with actual content. But when the constraints are empty or near-empty, that move seems to me not to work: you still have to pick a measure before entropy is even defined, and calling one measure "uniform ignorance" is just doing the very thing the objective Bayesian said she'd eliminated.

So the clash: a theory with real systematic virtues (uniqueness, a decision procedure, a way to turn partial information into probabilities) against my stubborn sense that no rule can turn a lack of information into a precise probability assignment. I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there's a reading on which both survive — maybe maxent priors are rational only relative to a chosen parameterization, or maybe my intuition is a folk-idea about information that entropy already cashes out. Give me the strongest version of both.
