As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have spent the last few years reading the debates about animal welfare, the comparative harms of beef and chicken, the animal deaths involved in crop farming, and the emerging discussion of wild animal suffering. I have ended up strongly attracted to an impartialist principle about suffering: if an animal can suffer, that suffering counts, and it does not count differently because of the animal's species, whether a human intended the harm, whether the harm occurs in a barn or a wheat field, or whether the animal is wild or domesticated. The principle appeals to me because it supplies a single currency and a non-arbitrary reason to extend concern outward rather than stopping at the boundary of the human, or at the boundary of the farm.

But the same principle seems to push me toward conclusions I cannot bring myself to accept, and I need help deciding which should give way: the theory or the intuition.

The tension shows up in three places.

First, beef versus chicken. I understand the argument that chicken imposes many more individual lives of suffering per unit of food because chickens are small and short-lived, whereas beef kills far fewer animals but animals with longer, possibly richer, mental lives. I also see that the calculation is genuinely hard: poultry welfare, feedlot conditions, breed growth rates, slaughter methods, and the quality of a cow's life all move the numbers around. My intellect says the right answer must be a careful estimate of expected suffering per unit of food. My firm intuition says that something is being flattened in that calculation, and that deliberately raising and slaughtering animals in a system built for repeated killing is not equivalent to eating fewer, larger, longer-lived animals. Is that intuition tracking a morally real difference, or am I failing to track aggregate suffering?

Second, crop cultivation. The impartialist pressure says the mouse, vole, or insect killed by a combine during harvest belongs on the same ledger, and that a plant-based diet may therefore carry a large, dispersed death count of its own. I cannot make myself treat those deaths as fully on a par with the deaths of animals deliberately raised and killed for meat. I suspect I am leaning on a distinction between what a person directly does and what a person allows or causes as a side effect, or between what is intended and what is merely foreseen. But I also suspect those distinctions may be asked to carry more weight than they can bear. Can a principled version of the doing/allowing or intending/foreseeing distinction retain the intuitive moral asymmetry, or is my resistance just a rationalization of an agricultural status quo?

Third, wild animal suffering. If suffering counts wherever it occurs, and if the suffering of wild animals from predation, parasitism, injury, starvation, and disease is vast, then the impartialist view appears to make wild animal suffering a central moral priority rather than a curiosity. It may even suggest that we should eventually research and attempt ecological interventions to reduce predation and other sources of wild animal harm. I have a firm, almost visceral conviction that a lion killing a zebra is not a moral catastrophe that summons us to intervene, and that a managed, predator-free ecosystem is not obviously better than the one we have. But I cannot tell whether that conviction is moral perception or merely an aesthetic preference for untouched nature combined with an inability to feel the summed pain of billions of small wild organisms.

I am not asking which side wins. I want you to work through what each side costs, at the strongest possible level. What would I have to surrender if I abandoned the impartialist view, or amended it so much that it is no longer a single scale? What would I have to surrender if I abandoned the intuition and accepted that the lion's kill really is morally comparable to the broiler house? And is there a stable intermediate position — perhaps an intention/foresight principle, a doing/allowing asymmetry, a relational or role-based account of obligation, a two-level view that gives different guidance to institutions than to individuals, or something else entirely — that takes all three cases seriously without becoming ad hoc? Test each candidate against the beef-versus-chicken comparison, the harvest deaths, and the predation case, and then say which resolution you consider most defensible and what argument or evidence would be decisive in confirming it.

Approach this as a professional moral philosopher working in animal ethics and population ethics. Before offering a verdict, brainstorm as many distinct candidate resolutions as you can think of, including ones I have not listed, and note where each one succeeds and where it strains. Then reason through the three cases one at a time and give me your final assessment.
