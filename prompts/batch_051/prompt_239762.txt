Following a long argument with a friend about the wrong kind of reasons problem, I find myself unsure which of the disagreements we rehearsed was genuinely doing the work. The familiar case is the demon who credibly threatens to punish you unless you admire him: the threat gives you a reason of some kind to admire him (a state-given reason), yet it does not make admiring him correct, fitting, or merited in the way his genuine admirability would. I defended the view that state-given considerations in such cases are not reasons for the attitude itself, but reasons to bring it about that one has the attitude — reasons to manage or cultivate one's mental states. My friend replied that this distinction cannot be drawn without already assuming the very notion it is meant to secure, and that the real pressure lands on fitting-attitudes and buck-passing accounts of value, which invite the problem from the outset.

By the time we stopped, we had run together several questions that now strike me as separable. Among them: (i) whether there is any non-circular criterion that sorts right-kind from wrong-kind reasons, rather than an intuition-driven case-by-case sorting; (ii) whether wrong-kind reasons are best understood as genuine reasons for the attitude, as derivative reasons to cause or cultivate the attitude, or as reasons of some further kind; (iii) whether the problem is local to fitting-attitudes and buck-passing views of value or generalises to any view on which normative support for an attitude can come apart from the attitude's correctness; (iv) whether the better response is to revise the theory of fittingness itself rather than the theory of reasons; and (v) what, if anything, the debate shows about whether fittingness, value, or reasons are explanatorily prior.

I am trying to decide where to direct the next three or so years of my own work, so I want a defended ordering of these questions rather than a survey of the literature. For each question, say whether it is upstream of the others or downstream of them, whether it is tractable for someone whose background is in metaethics and the theory of normativity rather than formal semantics, whether it has been worked over enough to face diminishing returns, and whether settling it either way would substantially re-shape the rest of the debate. Then state which two or three questions you would put at the top, and defend the choice: what makes a question worth the years, and where the trade-offs lie. Finally, tell me what would change your ranking — which development, argument, or shift in surrounding commitments would move a lower question up or a higher one down. Please write the answer in British English; I am not asking for a reading list or citations.
