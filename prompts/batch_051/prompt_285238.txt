In my game theory club last term we worked through the centipede game: two players take turns choosing to stop or pass over a fixed number of moves, each pass grows the pot and hands the move to the other player, and a player who stops takes the larger share of the current pot. The standard backward induction argument says the first player should stop on move one, because the second player would stop at the last move, and so on backwards. I have been trying to pin down what feels wrong about this, and I have built a small distinction around it.

I call backward induction 'node rationality': at any node, a player should choose the move that gives them the higher payoff, assuming that every later decision will also be made by node rationality. I contrast it with 'trajectory rationality': a sequence of moves is rational when the sequence considered as a whole gives a player a better payoff than the sequence that node rationality selects, and the player can see this in advance. My tentative theory is that the centipede paradox arises from treating node rationality as if it were the whole of rationality. A player at the first node can see that the path on which both players pass for several moves leaves them both with more than stopping immediately, and I think that by itself makes passing a rational move, even though the backwards, node-by-node logic rules it out.

What I am looking for is an honest assessment of the distinction rather than a restatement of the standard solution. Is 'trajectory rationality' coherent, or does it secretly assume that a player can choose or bind the other player's moves? Does it collapse into an idea that already exists under another name, and where is that idea usually criticized? And where does my distinction break — does it, for example, treat the value of a whole path as if it were the value of a single action available now? Above all, I want to know whether the word 'rational' is doing two different jobs in those two definitions, or whether this distinction genuinely exposes a gap in backward induction.
