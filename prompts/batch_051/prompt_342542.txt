Act as a research methodologist working at the boundary between philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and think carefully before you answer. Work from this passage of George Orwell's "Politics and the English Language":

"But an effect can become a cause, reinforcing the original cause and producing the same effect in an intensified form, and so on indefinitely. A man may take to drink because he feels himself to be a failure, and then fail all the more completely because he drinks. It is rather the same thing that is happening to the English language. It becomes ugly and inaccurate because our thoughts are foolish, but the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts. The point is that the process is reversible."

There are two causal directions in there. One is uncontroversial enough: muddled thought leads to bad writing. The other is the interesting one, and it is usually read as finger-wagging about style rather than taken literally. I want you to take it literally as a causal hypothesis: slovenly verbal habits make foolish thought easier. Start by brainstorming the constructs you would have to operationalise, especially what could count as "slovenly language" and "foolish thought" once you move beyond Orwell's own examples and into political speech. Then give me the best realistic test you can design for the claim that language habits corrupt thought rather than merely signal it, saying what result would count for Orwell's claim, what would count against it, and what even a strong positive result would leave unsettled (for instance, that both might be downstream of a third variable). Finish by saying how far a positive result would transfer to his wider political framing of that claim, and what part of that framing no experiment of the sort you design could touch. Use British English, and keep it tight — dont pad it out with a summary.
