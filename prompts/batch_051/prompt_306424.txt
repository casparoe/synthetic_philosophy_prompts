Two friends of mine got into it after a logic seminar about the Carroll dialogue where Achilles keeps turning the rule of inference into a new premise and the tortoise accepts it but never reaches the conclusion. The one who brought it up claimed the tortoise shows deduction can't be justified: any justification of modus ponens either writes the rule down as an extra premise, which just postpones the problem, or it uses the rule itself, which is circular. The other said that's a category error — rules of inference aren't premises, and accepting 'if p then q' is already a commitment to inferring q from p; asking for a further license misunderstands what acceptance is. The first shot back that 'acceptance already commits you' just re-asserts the rule in fancier words — it describes what we do, but gives no help to someone who genuinly withholds the transition. The second replied that the story is comedy, not an argument: Carroll rigs it by having the tortoise accept every premise offered and still refuse to draw the conclusion, so its real target is the confusion between implication and inference, not the foundations of logic. Adjudicate move by move — for each of the four moves, say whether it's clearly mistaken, somewhat off, defensible, or clearly right, and justify briefly. Then say who is right about what, and what important thing both of them are missing.
