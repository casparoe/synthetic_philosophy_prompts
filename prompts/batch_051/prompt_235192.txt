Okay, so I'm giving a workshop talk next month defending a dissolution of the paradox of blackmail, and I want a prep sheet that sounds like the actual audience will be in the room.

My thesis: the paradox is a fake paradox caused by individuating acts too coarsely. People say that revealing embarrassing information is legal on its own, and asking for money is legal on its own, so threatening to reveal unless paid should be legal too. I think that's a scope confusion about what a permission gets you. The blackmailer has a Hohfeldian liberty to disclose — she's under no duty not to tell — but that liberty doesn't license turning not-disclosing into the condition of a demand. A liberty covers your own doing or not doing; it isn't a lever against someone else. So the threat is a different act than the disclosure, and its wrongness doesn't wash out just because disclosure would have been fine. My positive story is that the offer manufactures the only reason the victim has to pay: until the threat exists, silence doesn't have a price, so the blackmailer is selling something the proposal itself creates.

The audience is a criminal-law theory workshop. Most of them have read the law-and-economics literature and talk about blackmail as a strange transaction where someone sells your own secret back to you; a couple are analytic moral philosophers who obsess over act individuation and will probably think my account just relables the paradox instead of dissolving it. Nobody is a libertarian about blackmail, everyone thinks it should be criminal, and I expect their real worry is that my dissolution could be run on any crime to make it disappear. The senior person in the room is a deontologist who thinks coercive threats and dishonest proposals already explain all of it, so she'll see my machinery as redundant.

Give me the eight or so objections I should expect, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way these specific people would put them, and say who in the room is most likely to ask each one. For each objection, tell me what to concede, what to hold, and give me a two-minute reply, plus a note on where that reply is soft. Also tell me what I should grant up front before they ask so I don't burn question period defending things I don't need. And I want the question I should be hoping nobody asks, with what I say if it comes anyway.
