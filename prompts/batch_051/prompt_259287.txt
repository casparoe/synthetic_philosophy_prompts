Here is an asymmetry I keep encountering in how people reason about democracy, and I want to know whether it is defensible or just a double standard.

When people argue that democracy carries a streak of recklessness, the examples they reach for are almost always ancient Athenian ones: the Mytilenean debate, in which an assembly voted in anger to kill every adult male in the city and reversed itself only the next day; the Sicilian Expedition, a war the assembly voted for that ended with the city's fleet and army destroyed; and the trial of Socrates, in which a democratic jury sentenced the city's most famous philosopher to death. These three failures are treated as evidence about democracy as such — about the impulsiveness, overconfidence, and conformism to which popular rule is prone.

Yet when a modern representative democracy starts and then botches a distant war, convicts an innocent person, or punishes a dissenter, those failures are not normally treated as evidence about democracy as a form of government. They are treated as failures of a particular administration, an intelligence agency, a court, or a media environment — mistakes to be repaired from inside the system. So the same style of inference, from a system's conspicuous failures to its characteristic vices, is applied to the ancient cases and withheld in the modern ones.

Is that asymmetry justified? If you think it is, state the principle that draws the line — for example, that ancient Athens lacked the mediating institutions of modern constitutional democracy, so its failures reveal the raw demos while modern failures reveal the mediating parts — and test that principle against a nearby case (an unmediated modern referendum, a failed aid program, three wrongful convictions in one legal system). If you think it is not justified, say which direction the correction should run: we should stop using Mytilene, Sicily, and Socrates as an indictment of democracy, or we should start treating our own conspicuous failures as one.
