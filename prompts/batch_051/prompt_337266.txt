Take the familiar Wilt Chamberlain example from debates about distributive justice. In the original case, viewers freely pay a small fee to watch Chamberlain play, and he ends up with far more than the equal shares from which everyone began. The standard entitlement-theory conclusion is that the resulting distribution is just, since it arose through consensual transfers from a just starting point, and that taxing away his earnings to restore the earlier pattern is objectionable in roughly the way that forced labor is.

Now vary the case. Remove the paying viewers and the performance. Chamberlain, who has done nothing for the money, receives the entire fortune as an inheritance from a distant relative. The relative acquired the wealth through a lifetime of ordinary consensual exchanges, and wrote the will freely; every step from the original just starting point up to the moment of death is unproblematic by the lights of the theory. The only change is the terminal transfer: a bequest to a recipient who supplies no labor, service, or exchange, from a giver who is no longer present when the property moves.

Run the standard analysis on this modified case, and answer the following as precisely as you can.

1. Does the entitlement conception of justice treat the inherited distribution as just for exactly the same reasons as the original? Identify the premise that does the work, for example justice in transfer, and say whether the recipient's passivity must be argued away or whether the theory simply does not care about it.

2. Does the forced-labor analogy carry over from a tax on performance earnings to a tax on an inheritance? If it does not, give the precise disanalogy, and say which feature of the original example gave the analogy its force. Consider also whether a defender could attach the analogy instead to the freedom to receive gifts or to bequeath, and whether that weakened claim would still support the original anti-tax conclusion.

3. Identify the weakest step in the three-part structure of just acquisition, just transfer, and the closure principle according to which whatever arises from a just situation by just steps is itself just, once the terminal step is a bequest. In particular, address whether a transfer executed after the giver's death carries the same weight as a simultaneous transfer between living agents, and whether the argument needs to ascribe interests or rights to the dead in order to go through.

4. State which competing positions are strengthened by the inheritance variant and why: for example, views that make justification depend on deservingness, on equality of opportunity across generations, or on the claim that the dead should not exercise continuing control over resources.

Conclude by saying whether the original anti-tax conclusion survives the variation, and, if it does survive, specify the additional commitments about bequests, dynastic accumulation, and the status of the dead that its defender must now make explicit.
