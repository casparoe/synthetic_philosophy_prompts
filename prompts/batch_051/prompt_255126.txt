I keep trying to reconstruct an argument from a half-remembered undergrad philosophy of math seminar, and I'd love a careful verdict on it. It's supposed to show that Cantor's actual infinities are impossible and that only potential infinities exist — that talking about "the set of all natural numbers" as a completed thing is already the mistake. My reconstruction goes like this:

(1) If an actual infinite existed, then the Hilbert's Hotel description would be coherent (never mind bricks and beds): a completed collection with one room for every natural number, all occupied, where the manager can move the occupant of room n to room 2n and thereby free up all the odd-numbered rooms.

(2) After that move, the even-numbered rooms are a proper part of the collection of all rooms, but there's a one-to-one correspondence between the even rooms and all the rooms (n maps to 2n), so by Cantor's criterion the part is the same size as the whole.

(3) But a proper part of a completed whole cannot be the same size as the whole; the whole is always greater than the part. That's about as basic as it gets.

(4) So the situation in (1) and (2) is impossible, and since the impossibility comes from the completed infinite itself rather than from anything about hotels, no actual infinite can exist.

My suspicion is that (3) is the culprit, or maybe "same size" is sliding between two meanings: in (2) it means "can be put into one-to-one correspondence," while in (3) it means something like "has no more and no fewer members," and those come apart exactly when the whole is infinite. But I also can't tell whether a finitist could patch (3) into something that isn't just "finite wholes and infinite wholes behave differently, and I refuse to count the second as wholes." So: is the argument valid as written? If not, exactly which step fails and on what reading? And is there a non-question-begging version of it, or does the finitist just have to assert the part-whole principle as a bare axiom?
