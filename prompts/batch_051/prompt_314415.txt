I’m a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I’ve been trying to reconcile two things I read by Nick Bostrom. In his 2003 simulation argument paper, he writes: “It is then possible to argue that, if this were the case, we would be rational to think that we are likely among the simulated minds rather than among the original biological ones. Therefore, if we don’t think that we are currently living in a computer simulation, we are not entitled to believe that we will have descendants who will run lots of such simulations of their forebears.” In his later paper “Astronomical Waste,” the argument is that the expected value of the far future is so many orders of magnitude larger than anything near-term that even tiny reductions in existential risk have more expected value than all other causes combined. These two claims seem to pull against each other: the first says I shouldn’t confidently expect a future in which our biological descendants run vast numbers of ancestor-simulations unless I already think I’m in one, while the second seems to treat the vast far future of our actual descendants as the thing that dominates our decisions. Are they actually inconsistent? If they are, which one should I treat as Bostrom’s considered view, and why? If they can be reconciled, what distinction does the reconciling work, and how do I tell that from special pleading?
