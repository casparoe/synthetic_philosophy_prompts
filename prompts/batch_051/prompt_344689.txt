In a conversation about translation, a friend who translates poetry answered a question about why she had replaced an image with something quite different by shrugging and saying, "traduttore, traditore" — translator, traitor. When I pressed, she insisted she wasn't defending the choice, just describing what translation is. I've since seen the phrase used as if it settled things, but I can't pin down what claim it is actually making. Could you separate the distinct precise theses that one sentence might be expressing — for example, that something is inevitably lost, that some things are untranslatable, that translation necessarily distorts the original's meaning, that the translator inevitably betrays the author or the reader or the source language, and that any of this carries moral weight — and lay them out as a list saying which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false? Then tell me which reading my friend's use of it actually needs, and whether that reading justifies her treating the question as already answered.
