I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I keep running into a sentence that seems to do way more work than people admit. A few weeks ago my city council approved a settlement in a police misconduct case: an officer broke a man's jaw during an arrest, the city paid out, and as part of the deal both sides signed a nondisclosure agreement, so the victim can't say what happened and the city won't release the bodycam footage. At the meeting someone got up and said "the public has a right to know what happened here." Everyone nodded, but I realized I don't actually know what claim is being made.

Can you take "the public has a right to know" and pull apart the precise theses it could be expressing? I can think of a few: that citizens collectively have a claim on the government's records; that the settlement is public money and so the terms should be public; that there's an interest in oversight weighty enough to override the parties' interest in confidentiality; that sealing the facts is undemocratic even if no specific harm results. But those don't all stand or fall together, and some of them are boringly true while others seem really demanding. Also, where does the burden sit: is the city forbidden from agreeing to secrecy, or just required to disclose after the fact? And does the right belong to "the public" as a collective, or to individual citizens who want to know?

What I want is a menu of distinct readings, with verdicts about which are platitudes, which are substantive but defensible, and which are false or overbroad, and then tell me which reading the speaker at that meeting needed for his argumet to go through against the NDA in this case—and whether that reading survives.
