I am the curator of a small history of science museum, and in three weeks I shall give the opening address for our new exhibition, From the Astrolabe to the Air Pump: Instruments of Discovery, 1400–1700. The rooms are arranged under the familiar labels ‘Late Middle Ages’, ‘Renaissance’, and ‘Scientific Revolution’, and the audience will mix our trustees, several of whom are professional historians of science, with a much larger number of visitors who have come for an afternoon of beautiful instruments rather than a lecture on method.

I have become convinced that these labels are largely retrospective inventions: the tidy unity of each period, and the clean break between one period and the next, are imposed on a longer, more uneven, and more collaborative process than the labels suggest. Our own objects make the difficulty concrete. The astrolabe in the first room embodies exactly the habits of precise observation and mathematical prediction that the ‘Scientific Revolution’ was once said to introduce, and the air pump in the last room did not settle the experimental method at a stroke. Yet our wall texts, school-visit leaflets, and shop all rely on the shorthand these labels provide. I do not want to denounce the exhibition from its own lectern, but I also do not want a historian in the front row to find the address credulous.

Please write me an eight-minute opening address in British English. It should be organised around a single, definite thesis about what a period label can honestly do: I am inclined to think the museum may keep using these labels as a filing system for objects, but that it goes wrong when it lets them do explanatory work, as if ‘the Scientific Revolution’ were a cause of the instruments rather than a retrospective name for a bundle of changes. Commit to that thesis or to a clearly stated refinement of it, and let it control the structure and the examples rather than laying out the debate and leaving the matter undecided. After the speech, add a brief note telling me where the address leans on a contested reading of the historiography or simplifies for the sake of the occasion.
