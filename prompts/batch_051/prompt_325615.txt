I'm a novelist — which means I know just enough about Gödel to be a nuisance. The book I'm working on has a retired mathematician as its protagonist, and he's convinced himself that the incompleteness theorems prove something the rest of the field is too polite to say out loud: that no computer program, however sophisticated, could do what a mathematician does, because a person can see the truth of a sentence that no consistent formal system could prove from inside itself. There's a long argument scene early on, at a wedding reception, between him and a machine-learning researcher half his age, and I keep writing both of them as though they're giving position papers rather than actually disagreeing. I'm fairly sure their disagreement isn't one thing; it's four or five small disagreements stacked under a trench coat.

So here's the thing I'd like from you. The proposition I want your credence on is this: there is a sound, non-question-begging argument from the incompleteness theorems to the conclusion that no Turing-equivalent computation — a real-world computer program, say — can reproduce human mathematical reasoning in full. I want your honest number, but more than that I want the reasoning forced into the open. Decompose the credence into the terms it actually depends on. Some candidate terms I keep circling: whether the human mind is even the right kind of object for the theorems to bite on (consistent, finitely specifiable); whether one can identify the formal system the mind is supposed to be without changing the subject; whether a machine can always be built to prove any given system's Gödel sentence anyway; whether the human act of seeing a sentence is true adds anything beyond an assertion a machine could make; and whether the argument, even if it worked, would tell us about intelligence or only about mathematical self-description. Add terms or kill these as you see fit. For context: my protagonist would put his credence at about 0.85, the machinie-learning guy at about 0.1. I lean toward the old man, but only because I've spent more time in his head. Where does the real disagreement live, and which sub-question is your own number most exposed to?

For the shape of the answer: exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no wrap-up. Items one through four should be four terms in your decomposition (your choice), each with a rough probability and a one-line justification. Item five should give the combination into your overall number, say which term that number is most sensitive to, and name one piece of evidence that would move it most. No citations needed; this is for a scene, not a bibliography.
