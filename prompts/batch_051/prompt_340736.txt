I'm on my university's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, and we're redoing how we weigh animal harms against scientific benefit. The current rubric mostly just counts animals, which everyone agrees is bad but nobody could settle on what replaces it. One of the neuroscientists on the committee proposed a weighting key by brain size—rough classes, not exact neuron counts, so a macaque would count as maybe a dozen or two mice—on the grounds that a bigger brain underwrites a richer mental life, future-directed desires, social memory, and so the same injury can be a worse harm. The immediate fight is a protocol that could be run either with around forty mice or with three macaques. Under the brain-size key the macaque arm comes out worse; without it the mouse arm comes out worse just by raw numbers. The committee split down the middle and I got tapped to draft something everyone can vote on.

My own suspicion is that brain size is irrelevant except as a fallible proxy for other capacities, and that baking it into a formula is uncomfortably close to weighing harms by body mass; the pain machinery is conserved enough across mammals that a cut is a cut. But I also don't think sentience is an on/off switch, and I take seriously that the badness of a harm can differ with the complexity of the subject's psychology. I need a short policy memo for the full committee saying whether and how brain size (or related neural measures, like neuron count) may enter our harm-benefit analysis, with criteria reviewers can actually apply to a concrete protocol, a bottom-line rec to adopt, reject, or modify the key, and an honest treatment of the strongest point on the loosing side. I don't want a punt disguised as nuance.
