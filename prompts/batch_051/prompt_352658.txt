Having come to philosophy late, after a career in cosmology, I have been working through Leibniz's theodicy with the kind of attention I used to give to models of the early universe. His claim that this is the best of all possible worlds rests on a definite picture: God surveys complete alternative worlds and actualizes the one that maximizes perfection, where perfection is understood as the greatest variety of phenomena governed by the simplest and most fruitful laws. Now suppose that modern cosmology is right in one of two ways. Either the universe is spatially infinite, or inflation produces a multiverse in which every physically possible history is realized in some region. I am trying to determine whether Leibniz's argument survives that development. It seems to me to attack the premise that there is a single world selected from a range of alternatives, and perhaps also the premise that the comparison among candidate worlds yields a maximum at all, since infinite or exhaustive totalities need not have one. Please work through this carefully: identify which premise the multiverse or infinite universe bears on, say whether the theodicy can be repaired while remaining recognizably Leibnizian, and if it cannot, explain what should be said in its place.
