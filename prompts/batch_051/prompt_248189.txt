A colleague in a philosophy of mind seminar sent me the following argument, which she called the scale argument about insects. She thinks it proves too much, but she cannot identify the mistake. Here is the argument as she laid it out.

(1) For any given insect, there is some nonzero probability p that the insect is sentient, where sentience means the capacity for felt pain and pleasure. Reasonable people dispute the value of p, but even a very small value, such as one in a thousand, is enough for the argument.

(2) Ordinary human activity affects insects in vast numbers: pesticide use, agriculture, driving, mowing, and keeping lights on in the evening each kill or harm thousands to millions of insects per person per year. Let N be the number of insects an action affects.

(3) If an action harms N insects and each has probability p of being capable of suffering, the expected number of suffering-capable beings harmed by the action is N times p. With N in the millions and p around one in a thousand, a single ordinary action can carry an expected cost of hundreds or thousands of lives capable of feeling pain.

(4) Uncertainty about whether a being is sentient does not justify treating it as certainly insentient. To set p to zero without evidence would be exactly the kind of dismissal that expected-value reasoning forbids; the small probability is a discount factor, not a reason to ignore the possibility.

(5) Therefore, an ordinary convenience such as an unnecessary car trip or mowing a lawn has an expected insect-suffering cost large enough to dominate the human benefit, provided the suffering of an insect is given any more than negligible moral weight.

(6) But then, if we are consistent, ordinary life becomes morally dominated by insect welfare: we should avoid needless movement, reconsider any activity that kills insects as a byproduct, and treat apparently trivial choices as decisions of enormous moral significance. This conclusion strikes her as something a sound argument could not establish, so it suggests the argument proves too much.

She concludes that at least one premise or step must be false or invalid, yet each seems plausible on its own. Which premise or inferential step is most plausibly rejected, and why? What does rejecting it cost, and why might it have looked correct? In particular, say whether the mistake is best located in the expected-value framework, in the interpretation of p, in the move from expected numbers to moral dominance, or in the inference from significant insect-welfare impact to the specific practical conclusions drawn in step (6). Then give your own diagnosis of the argument.
