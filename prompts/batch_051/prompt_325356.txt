Hey, I'm a PhD student in decision theory and a friend in the history of philosophy cornered me with a Buridan's-ass argument that seems to prove way too much, and I can't find the step I want to reject. Here's his argument, numbered the way he wrote it:

1. If a choice isn't backed by a reason that favors the chosen option over its rival, then the choice is arbitrary.
2. A reason that favors A over B has to be grounded in some respect in which A is better than B.
3. In a genuine Buridan case — donkey equidistant between two bales of hay, equal in every relevant way — there's no respect in which the left bale is better than the right, or the reverse.
4. So there's no reason that favors either bale over the other.
5. So choosing either bale is arbitrary.
6. Arbitrary choice is no more an exercise of rational agency than a twitch is.
7. But not choosing is itself worse than either bale, since the donkey starves. So the donkey faces three options — left, right, starve — and none of them is rational.
8. And since everyday life is full of exact ties (two identical cans on the shelf, two identical gas stations), rationality is silent across a huge chunk of ordinary choice.

So it looks like either we have to give up the idea that rational choice needs reasons, or we have to deny that genuine ties can occur, and I don't love either. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep premise 2, if that matters. Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion — the five moves I most need to understand my way out.
