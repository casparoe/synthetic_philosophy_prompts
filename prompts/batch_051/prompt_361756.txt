For the past several weeks I have been involved in deploying a language model at my company to draft customer-support replies and product descriptions. Last week it produced a draft email to a client stating, in the same smooth and confident register it uses for everything, that our platform integrates with a third-party tool it has never integrated with. When I flagged the message, the engineer who maintains the model shrugged and called it a hallucination, then suggested we add a disclaimer rather than change anything fundamental. That evening I described the incident to a friend, and we argued for hours. He began by insisting that the system lies to our customers: it asserts falsehoods as fact. I objected that lying seems to require an intention to deceive, and a language model does not intend anything. He then shifted ground and said this makes the case stranger, not simpler. The output is not a plain error either, he argued, because error implies aiming at the truth and missing, whereas the system is not aiming at the truth at all. What it does instead is produce language shaped to sound appropriate and credible while being indifferent to whether it is true — and he claimed this is a distinct kind of failure, one that our habit of saying "it hallucinated" conveniently papers over. I find "error" too forgiving and "lying" too mentalistic, but I am not convinced my friend's third category is fully coherent, and I cannot tell how much the distinction should matter.

I am trying to decide how to think about this before our next review of the tool. Which description — lying, error, or whatever my friend was gesturing at — is most defensible for a system of this kind? And does the classification have practical consequences? In particular, should distinguishing these categories change how drafts are reviewed before they reach customers, what disclaimers we attach, how the model is trained or prompted, and where responsibility falls when misleading text goes out?
