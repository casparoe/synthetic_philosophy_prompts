I'm trying to pin down what I actually think about whether beliefs, desires, and expectations can change how things literally look, as opposed to just changing how you judge, report, or where you look. Going by my gut on cases:

- After a long day hike with a heavy pack, the last climb looked steeper to me than the same slope had looked in the morning. I think that's a real change in the look itself, not just me being cautious.
- A radiologist friend says a tumor on a chest xray "pops out" in a way it never did when she was a trainee. The image is the same; her seeing changed. I think that's attention and filtering, not a change in how the image looks.
- Show people a gray banana on a callibrated screen, tell them it's a banana, and they'll nudge their color match toward yellow. I think that's expectation leaking into the report; the raw look is still gray.
- An arachnophobe looking at a live spider says it's larger than it actually is. I think fear is messing with the sizing judgment, not the actual look of the spider.
- The Ebbinghaus illusion: a circle ringed by small circles looks bigger than the same circle ringed by big ones, and it keeps looking that way after you've measured them and know they're equal. That's genuinely visual, and it shows knowledge sometimes can't touch the look at all.
- The dress photo: same pixels, and people see white/gold or blue/black depending on how their visual system is reading the lighting. That also feels genuinely visual to me — but unlike the banana case, it's the visual system using something like an assumption, so I'm not sure why I drew that line there.
- A golfer who's been putting well says the hole looked bigger to him than it did while he was putting badly. I go back and forth — could be the look, could be the after-the-fact story.

My tentative principle is: it's a change in perception proper only when it's stubborn, i.e., knowing better doesn't make it go away. That fits Ebbinghaus, and maybe the dress if you hold the lighting interpretation fixed. But it makes me nervous about the hill case (I knew I was tired, so my report could be biased), and it might actually make the radiologist case count as perceptual, since she can't unsee the tumor once she's learned the pattern — and I wanted to call that attention. The golf case just sits there making me feel like my principle is post hoc.

Is there a principle that gets all of these verdicts, or close — one that says which effects are inside the seeing and which are downstream of seeing? What would it predict for a case I didn't list, like someone who is told a blob in a noisy photo is a camouflaged animal and afterwards can't unsee the animal? And which of my verdicts should I give up?
