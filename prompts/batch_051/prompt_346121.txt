I'm reading Cicero's On Old Age (De Senectute), the dialogue where Cato the Elder does all the talking, and I keep noticing that he basically hands down verdicts on a fixed list of complaints about getting old. As I read it, the four complaints and his rulings are:

1. That old age pulls you out of active affairs. He says it only pulls you out of the kind of affairs that need physical strength, and leaves the more important ones — judgment, counsel, authority — alone. So mostly acquitted.
2. That it wrecks the body. He says yes, the body weakens, but the body was never what mattered, and you should use whatever strength is left; the mind stays sharp if you keep working it.
3. That it strips away the pleasures. He says the pleasures it removes are the low ones, and the higher pleasures of conversation, study, and friendship remain or even grow. So the charge fails.
4. That it brings death near. He says death is not an evil at any age, and at old age it is natural — like ripe fruit falling of itself rather than being torn off green. So the fear, not the proximity, is the real problem.
