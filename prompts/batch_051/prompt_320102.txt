I keep going back and forth on whether a scripted customer-service "sorry" is a real apology, and I want someone to adjudicate. The working criteria I'm using for apology are these four. (1) Acknowledgment: the speaker has to accept that something wrong was done—by them or by whoever they're speaking for—not just that the hearer is upset. (2) Sincerity: the speaker has to actually feel regret. (3) Uptake: the hearer has to be able to recognize the utterance as an apology and accept it, at least in principle. (4) Normative change: the utterance has to count as a commitment going forward, so that continued resentment becomes somewhat less appropriate after it.

Now the case. A customer-service rep at a big ISP reads the line the company wrote for him: "I'm really sorry for the inconvenience this has caused you." The rep did nothing personally wrong and on this call feels basically nothing—it's his last week, he's checked out. The company has authorized the line and put it in the official script, but internally it avoids admitting fault for legal reasons, so if anyone is apologizing here it has to be the company speaking through the rep. The problem is the company doesn't feel regret; it's not the sort of thing that can feel anything. The customer knows the line is scripted but still feels slightly recognized, says thanks, and hangs up.

Was an apology performed, and by whom? Check the case against each of my four criteria. Where a criterion gives a mixed answer, say whether that's a flaw in the criterion or a sign that apologizing is more like a move in a game (with the company as the authorized player) than an expression of a private feeling. And give your verdict on whether this is an insincere-but-real apology or not an apology at all despite the words, and whether the criteria need a fifth condition about who counts as the speaker.
