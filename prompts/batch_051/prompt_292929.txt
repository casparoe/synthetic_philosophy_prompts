I've been going around with my brother-in-law about what it means to call someone incorruptible. He thinks our city councilor is incorruptible because, in twenty years, the guy has never once taken a bribe and obviously never will. I think that's way too cheap, because the same councilor is visibly worked by flattery, by threats to his family, by wanting to be liked, and by a smoldering need to have a building named after him. We couldn't agree whether "incorruptible" is specifically about money, about temptation in general, about sticking to your own values, or about sticking to the right values.

So: give me a working defintion of "incorruptible" that gets these seven cases right. (1) A judge who turns down gifts and recuses herself from a friend's lawsuit — clearly yes. (2) A transport minister who steers contracts to his cousins and takes a cut — clearly no. (3) A port official in a graft-heavy city who refuses envelope after envelope of cash despite being underpaid, but who has never once been offered the slow, respectable corruptions: a board seat later, a school named after his father, a consultancy for his son. (4) A police sergeant who would never take a dollar but will lie under oath to protect her partner, because loyalty to her squad is the thing she ranks highest. (5) A revolutionary organizer who cannot be bought, blackmailed, or seduced away from his cause — but the cause itself is monstrous. (6) A reporter who, over two decades, comes to genuinely like the industry PR people, adopts their framing over drinks, and gradually softens his coverage, with no single moment of anything you could call a deal. (7) A hermit with almost no desires, who faces no real temptations because almost nothing tempts him — is he incorruptible or just untested? I'm confident 1 is yes and 2 is no; 7 is the one that bugs me most.

Format the answer in four sections: Working definition (100–150 words: state the definition plainly, then unpack each term). Running the cases (300–350 words: push all seven through the definition, record each verdict, and flag the awkward ones). Pressure points (100 words: which cases the definition got wrong or left undecided). Revision or defense (150 words: either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts, and then say whether being incorruptible in your final sense is always good).
