I'm not a lawyer or anything, just a city council staffer who got stuck on the committee that reviews our municipal code. We keep running into drafting language like "for purposes of this section, X shall be deemed to be Y," and nobody can explain to me why that's okay sometimes but feels like a cheap trick other times. A colleague handed me Henry Sumner Maine's Ancient Law, and in his chapter on legal fictions he gives this definition—I'm paraphrasing because I don't have the book in front of me—that a fiction is kind of an assumption that lets a legal rule keep its outward wording while its real operation gets changed, so the change is hidden or made to look like it isn't a change. That hit me as exactly what we do on Tuesdays.

We meet monthly, have maybe ten minutes per provision, and the city attorney wants something a junior analyst can actually use. Could you write a checklist or decision procedure, like six to eight questions in order, that we can run a proposed legal fiction through before it goes to council? It should separate harmless drafting shortcuts, where the pretense is just for convenience, from ones that genuinely alter what the law says is true about the world without adding that alteration up front. And please mark which steps are value calls rather than neutral tests. Then run it on two or three examples: (1) a provision saying a notice mailed to a vacant property counts as received after three days; (2) a provision saying a detached garage is deemed part of the main dwelling for occupancy purposes; and (3) maybe one where the procedure tells us to kill a fiction council actually likes, so we can see it working when the answer is unpleasant.
