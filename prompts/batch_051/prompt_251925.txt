I want to know whether a well-known argument in the ethics of memory survives a specific change in its background conditions. The argument, associated with Avishai Margalit, runs roughly as follows: a community has a duty to remember past wrongs done to it or to its members, because shared memory partly constitutes the community and because letting the victims be forgotten wrongs them a second time; on this picture, remembering is an achievement requiring deliberate effort, institutions, and transmission across generations, since forgetting is the default. David Rieff has pressed the opposing case, holding that too much collective memory can feed grievance and conflict and that some forgetting is salutary. Now change the background condition: suppose we live in a society of cheap, automatic, and effectively unlimited digital memory, where the default is no longer forgetting but the indefinite retention of nearly everything, and where what is scarce is not the capacity to remember but the capacity to let something lapse. Does the Margalit-style argument for a duty to remember survive this change? Which premise is actually under pressure—the premise that memory requires effort, the premise that shared memory constitutes community, or the premise that forgetting wrongs the victims—and does the argument now change its practical import, perhaps becoming a duty to curate, to attend selectively, or even to forget? I am asking what happens to the argument itself, not what particular policies you would endorse.
