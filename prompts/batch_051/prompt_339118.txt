I've been turning over Good's theorem on the value of information and I can't get it to line up with something I feel in my gut. The theorem, as I understand it, says an expected-utility maximizer should never strictly prefer not to receive costless information. The argument seems airtight: with free information and perfect recall you can always choose to ignore the new evidence, so you're at worst as well off as before, and in some cases the information lets you pick a strictly better act. I can even prove a toy version to myself and get zero as the lower bound, never a loss.

But I also can't shake the conviction that there are things I'd pay not to know: a genetic marker for an untreatable illness, what my closest friends actually think of my work, the precise words someone used about me after I left the room. These aren't cases where the information expands or contracts my options in any useful way. It just lives in me afterwards and makes things worse. And I want to say refusing it isn't a failure of rationality — that someone who eagerly wanted it might be the one with the strange utility function.

I don't want someone to just pick a side. I want a negotiated answer: what would it take for the intuition to be debunked and dropped, what would it take for the theorem's assumptions to be the thing that gives way, and is there a reading on which both survive — say by counting the pain of knowing as a genuine cost that belongs in the utility function from the start, or by treating the act of refusing information as itself chosen for its downstream effects. Where does the rational-ignorance move have to do its work, and does the theorem actually contradict the intuition or only a straw version of it? Lead with a two-sentence bottom line, then give the longer answer, and keep the whole thing under 600 words.
