I have been thinking about Lewis Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise, and I have arrived at a position I call the License Account of inference. I would like an honest assessment of it.

The License Account holds that a rule of inference such as modus ponens is neither a premise nor a proposition; it is a license — a standing permission to move from propositions one accepts to a further proposition. A license can be expressed in conditional form, for instance by saying that anyone who accepts P and if P then Q may accept Q, but the expression is not the license itself. The expression is a proposition about the license; the license is the authorization to make the move. Licenses, unlike propositions, are not true or false. They are granted, exercised, declined, or revoked.

On this account, the regress Carroll dramatizes arises only because the Tortoise keeps demanding that the license be supplied to him as an additional premise. Each time Achilles complies, the license is converted into a conditional proposition that the Tortoise can accept while still refusing to draw the conclusion; he then needs a further license to use that new proposition, and so on. The regress is therefore an artifact of misrepresenting a license as a piece of content, not evidence of a gap at the bottom of deduction. A deductive transition is justified not by adding a further truth to the premises but by a reasoner's acceptance of the license that authorizes the move. Once the premises and the license are accepted, nothing remains to be justified; there is only the drawing of the conclusion.

A plain example: in a board game, a player who sees the position on the board and accepts the rule for how a piece moves does not need an additional premise stating that the rule should be applied to this position. The rule is what makes the move count as legal rather than being an inert sentence alongside the board. I think the Tortoise is best understood as someone who wants to play logic while refusing to let the rules apply from within.

I realize this may sound close to familiar distinctions — between rules and propositions, or between knowing-that and knowing-how — and I want to know whether the License Account is merely one of those in different wording or whether it earns its keep. Please give me the three strongest objections and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. I am particularly worried about the following. If drawing a conclusion is the exercise of a license, and exercise of a license is voluntary, then a reasoner who accepts the premises and the license but refuses to draw the conclusion seems to have made no mistake at all. Yet ordinary thought says that such a person is logically irrational. Does the License Account have the resources to explain why, or does it turn logical rationality into an act of choice?
