Here's a habit I've developed over years of teaching aesthetics and reviewing papers. When I need to judge whether something is genuinely creative — a paper, a poem, a composition, a philosophical move — I apply a little test: could I reasonably describe it as an earlier piece with a few identifiable things changed? If yes, I'm inclined to call it uncreative. Just a knob-twiddle. This habit comes from something like Hofstadter's knob-twiddling picture, where creativity is mostly variation on a theme — you take a pattern, you turn some knobs, and the hard part is knowing which knob-turns are fertile and which are noise. But lately I've realized my test may be rigged. After the fact, almost anything can be re-described as some predecessor with the right knobs turned, since I get to choose the predecessor and the parameters once I've seen the new work. So my verdicts might be tracking how good I am at redescription, or how many old works I happen to remember, rather than anything about the object. Worse, if the genuinely creative act is exactly to notice that one particular knob on a familiar theme opens a whole new space, my heuristic gives that act the lowest score, because I can state the knob. A colleague tells me my rule is just neophobia dressed up as theory. But I dont want to swing to the opposite view and count every random mutation as creative. Is the heuristic defensible? Is there a non-question-begging version that separates fertile variation from mere knob-twiddling without falling back on the claim that you can't define it but you recognize it when it happens? And should the standard differ by context — grading a student, reviewing a tenure file, or judging an argument I'm trying to think through myself?
