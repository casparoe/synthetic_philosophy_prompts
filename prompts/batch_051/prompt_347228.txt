In a recent argument about climate and resource policy, one participant kept returning to an analogy: Easter Island is the Earth in miniature. On the story he was relying on, the island was an isolated, closed system; its people went on cutting forests and raising statues while competing among themselves, until the society collapsed. Since the Earth is also an isolated, closed system, the analogy was doing real work: it turned the island from a historical case into a warning that our civilization will meet the same end unless we impose strict limits on consumption and growth.

I have since learned that the ecocide story is contested. Some archaeologists now argue that rats introduced by the settlers did much of the deforestation, that the islanders adapted with stone-mulched gardens rather than starved before European contact, and that the real demographic catastrophe came later, with European disease and slave raids. I am not asking for a final verdict on that dispute; I need to know how much of the analogy's force depends on the standard version being right.

Please audit the analogy as an argument, not as a historical question. Give the correspondence table: what maps to what — the island's closed ecosystem, the palms, the chiefs who commissioned the statues, the ordinary islanders, the surrounding ocean with no nearby land — and say what plays each role for a planetary, industrialized civilization. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are merely true: scale, internal heterogeneity, substitution and innovation, trade, institutions, and the fact that the people making the analogy already know the island's ending and can treat it as a warning while the islanders had no comparable example to study. It also matters what "collapse" is being used to mean in the source case — population crash, loss of political complexity, abandonment of a way of life, or something else — and whether the analogy depends on running more than one of these together. In particular, note where the analogy quietly imports features of the source — smallness, simplicity, tight feedback, a single culture — that the target lacks. Finally, tell me how much of the original argument survives if the revisionist account is roughly right: if Easter Island was less a case of internal overshoot than of adaptation followed by an external shock, does the analogy still carry the conclusion about strict limits, or was it really just illustrating a separate theory about resource overshoot? Where does that leave the policy conclusion?
