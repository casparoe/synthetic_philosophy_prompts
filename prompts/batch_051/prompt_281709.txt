I'm a novelist by trade, but I teach an evening intro to philosophy of mathematics course — partly because it payed the rent, partly because I'm drafting a novel set in a math department and wanted to hear how people actually talk about the foundations. That was the idea, anyway. Right now I'm mid-way through grading finals, and one answer keeps making me go back and forth. Philosophy of math isn't my corner of the field, so I want a careful grader's ruling before I put a letter grade on it. The question I set was:

"Explain what logicism, formalism, and intuitionism disagree about concerning the nature of mathematics, and identify one serious problem for each school."

The student wrote:

All three schools are trying to answer the question of what makes a mathematical statement true, and they split three ways. Logicism says mathematical truth is really logical truth: Frege and Russell tried to show that arithmetic can be defined in purely logical vocabulary and derived from logical axioms. The classic problem for logicism is Russell's paradox — the set of all sets that aren't members of themselves — which blew up Frege's original system and pushed Russell into fixes like the ramified theory of types, plus the worry that his axioms of infinity and reducibility don't look purely logical. Formalism says mathematics is just the manipulation of meaningless symbols according to formal rules, so a sentence is true when the rules allow you to derive it and false otherwise; Hilbert's program was to formalize all of mathematics and prove the resulting system consistent using only finitary reasoning. Intuitionism, from Brouwer, says mathematical objects are mental constructions, so a statement is true only if we actually have a construction or a proof that exhibits the object. That's why intuitionists reject proof by contradiction in general — deriving a contradiction from the negation of P doesn't build the thing P talks about — and why they can't even accept the irrationality of the square root of 2, since that proof only works by contradiction. The three schools disagree, in short, about whether mathematics is logic, syntax, or mental construction. For problems: logicism faces Russell's paradox and the non-logical feel of its extra axioms; formalism faces Gödel, since his second incompleteness theorem seems to block the finitary consistency proof Hilbert wanted; and intuitionism faces the problem that it invalidates large parts of classical mathematics and hasn't really been able to reconstruct the parts that matter.

Could you grade this the way a careful philosophy instructor would? Give me a letter grade or a score out of 10, and then the point-by-point comments that justify it: what the student got right, exactly where they're wrong or conflating two different views, and whether each mistake is a minor slip or something that should move the grade by a full step. I'd rather be fair than fast.
