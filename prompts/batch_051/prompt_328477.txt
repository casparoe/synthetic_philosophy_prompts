I have been going back and forth with a colleague who works at a quantitative fund about whether the Kelly criterion is merely a useful heuristic with attractive long-run properties, or whether it is in some stronger sense rationally mandatory for anyone who repeatedly faces the same favourable bet. He has defended the following argument, which feels too quick to me, and I would like a definite verdict on it rather than a menu of possible responses.

The setup is this. A gambler begins with wealth W0 and faces an indefinitely long sequence of independent even-money bets on a biased coin that lands heads with probability p > 1/2. In each round she chooses a fraction f of her current wealth to stake. If she wins, her wealth is multiplied by (1 + f); if she loses, it is multiplied by (1 − f). The argument then runs as follows.

(1) Expected final wealth after n rounds is maximised by staking everything in every round, f = 1, because each individual bet carries positive expectation and the optimal stake scales with current wealth. (2) But staking everything leads to ruin as soon as a single loss occurs, and with independent rounds the probability of at least one loss in n rounds is 1 − p^n, which tends to 1; meanwhile the expectation is (2p)^n·W0, which grows without bound. So expected-wealth maximisation recommends a policy that almost surely ends in ruin, which is absurd as a standard of practical rationality. (3) What actually tracks the gambler's realised trajectory is the long-run growth rate of wealth. For a fixed fraction f, (1/n)·log Wn is the average of independent per-round terms, so it converges almost surely to p·log(1 + f) + (1 − p)·log(1 − f). This expected log growth, the log of the geometric mean growth factor per round, is maximised at f* = 2p − 1, the Kelly fraction. (4) A fixed-fraction strategy with a higher growth rate in this sense will, with probability tending to one, overtake any fixed-fraction strategy with a lower growth rate, and within the class of fixed-fraction strategies f* is the unique maximiser. (5) A rational gambler in this repeated setting should value what happens with probability tending to one, not an expectation inflated by a set of improbable jackpot trajectories; therefore the uniquely rational policy is to stake f* in each round, and any other fixed fraction is not merely worse but rationally impermissible.

I suspect the trouble is somewhere around steps (2) and (5), but I cannot say exactly where, and I half-suspect my colleague has smuggled a substantive theory of rationality into what looks like a mathematical argument. Please give me a committed judgement: is the argument valid? If it is not, identify the first step that fails, give the strongest reading of it on which it is true, and say whether the argument still reaches the conclusion on that reading or collapses into something an ordinary expected-utility theorist could accept. If you think it is valid, defend the contested step and say exactly what a critic must bite. Please write your answer in British English.
