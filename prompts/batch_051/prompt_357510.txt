I've been rereading Fearon's rationalist explanations for war—the puzzle being that war is costly ex post, so there should always be some bargain both sides prefer to fighting, and the three standard explanations being private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and indivisible issues. In my philosophy of technology seminar I keep trying to sell students on the idea that the current AI development race is best explained by the second of these, and I'd like someone to check the argument before I committ to it in print. My claim, bluntly: the AI race is a commitment problem, not an information problem. The major labs and states aren't privately misrepresenting their intentions. Most of them say, and I think mostly mean, that they would prefer a world where everyone slows down and does serious safety work, because a race is expensive and dangerous. But each actor knows the others have an overwhelming incentive to defect later, especially as AGI gets closer, because the first mover may capture a huge share of everything: markets, compute, political advantage, maybe the future. So any agreement to slow down cannot be made credible, even when everyone honestly prefers it to racing. This would explain the awkward pattern where all the public talk is safety and all the hiring and compute budgets are race.

I know the obvious objection: I'm taking a theory of war and stretching it over ordinary economic competition, or dressing up a prisoner's dilemma in lab coats. So which is it? Where exactly does the analogy break—what features of war does Fearon's mechanism actually need that an AI race lacks—and do the differences matter to the explanation, or just to the label? And is there anything about AI that makes the commitment problem harder than a conventional arms race, or is this just nuclear deterrence logic with GPUs?
