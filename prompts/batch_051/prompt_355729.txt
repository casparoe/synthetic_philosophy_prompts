For class on thursday I want you to work through this. A medical licensing board's code of ethics includes the following clause: "No physician shall perform any procedure whose principal effect is to alter a patient's personality, memory, or the emotional capacities by which she forms and pursues her values, except when necessary to treat a diagnosable disorder." Now the case. A woman in her forties, healthy by any ordinary standard, lost her son last year and asks a neurosurgeon for an implant that would permanently suppress her capacity for grief, envy, and boredom. She says grief is not her—its something that happened to her, and she has a right to decide which emotions belong in her own mind. The clause was written with old-style lobotomies and covert drugging in mind, not with a competent person who wants to edit herself. But read literally, does it reach her? Does it reach her if we read it by its purpose, or by asking what its drafters would have said about elective grief-suppression? And put the rule aside for a second: if she goes through with it and later cannot even miss the grief, has she lost something she is no longer capable of counting as a loss? I want the argumentative structure, not a guess about what a real board would do.
