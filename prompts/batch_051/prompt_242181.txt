I keep coming back to C. Lloyd Morgan's *An Introduction to Comparative Psychology* because it's one of those books that's mostly remembered for a single methodological rule, while the actual arguments around it are more interesting. The famous passage, from chapter III, is:

"In no case may we interpret an action as the outcome of the exercise of a higher psychical faculty, if it can be interpreted as the outcome of the exercise of one which stands lower in the psychological scale."

On its own it reads like a boring parsimony principle, and the way people now use it — "don't attribute rich cognition to animals when a simpler mechanism suffices" — makes it sound like he was just trying to keep animals stupid. But when I went through the surrounding pages, Morgan is not at all saying animals lack consciousness or that we should default to treating them as little machines. He's worried about something more specific: the tendency of evolutionary psychologists, Herbert Spencer and Romanes especially, to read human-style faculties like reasoning, abstraction, and aesthetic sense into animal behavior on the basis of single anecdotes. He's in favor of animal consciousness, but against what he thinks of as bad inference.

So here's my question. I want to use Morgan's canon as a case study in what a methodological principle actually does in science. Specifically: is Morgan's canon a *default rule*, the way "innocent until proven guilty" works in law — i.e., you start with the lower explanation and require a lot of evidence to move up the scale — or is it better understood as a *tiebreaker*, only invoked when the evidence genuinely underdetermines two readings? The reason I ask is that the first reading makes it sound like a bias toward deflationary explanations, and I don't think that's what Morgan intended. But the second reading makes the canon almost empty, because if the evidence really underdetermines two explanations, what's the scientific basis for choosing either one? There's also a third possibility, which is that the "scale" of faculties from lower to higher is doing hidden work, and the rule is really a bet about evolutionary continuity — namely, that you shouldn't attribute to an organism a capacity whose emergence you can't explain in its lineage. Anyway, what I want is an analysis of what kind of principle this actually is, what work it can legitimately do in comparative cognition, and where it breaks down or gets misapplied. Does it survive contact with modern work on, say, corvid problem-solving or octopus behavior, and if it does survive, what did it actually prohibit?
