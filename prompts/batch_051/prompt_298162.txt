I'm a PhD student in legal philosophy, and I've been circling around something from my rule-of-law seminar. We were reading about desuetude — the idea that a statute can stop being law if it has gone unenforced for so long that people no longer orient their conduct to it — and someone floated a concrete mechanism. A state enacts a Dormant Offenses Act. Any criminal statute that has produced no convictions (not just no charges, but no actual convictions) in 25 consecutive years is automatically repealed. The only exception is that the attorney general can issue a written finding, renewed every five years, saying there is a current and specific public interest in keeping the statute alive; anyone can petition the AG to let a law lapse, and a refusal to do so is reviewable in court. If a law lapses, bringing it back requires new legislation through the normal process, and the revived statute applies only prospectively.

I don't want to litigate whether its a good idea in the abstract. I want to know how this machinery would actually behave. Who would petition, and how would advocacy groups use the process as leverage? What would prosecutors and police do differently inside the 25-year window — especially around year 20 — and could the 'no convictions' trigger be gamed by engineering a token conviction just to reset the clock, or by quietly declining to charge so a law you dislike lapses? What does the mechanism do to the expressive role of a statute that stays on the books even though nobody is ever convicted under it? And am I wrong to think the revival-by-reenactment rule creates its own weird incentive, since legislators who want a dormant law gone can just wait, while legislators who want it revived have to spend public time defending a law nobody has used for decades? I'm interested in whether these failure modes are fixable by tweaks, or whether the whole design self-undermines.
