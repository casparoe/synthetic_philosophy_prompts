I've been following an epistemic policy for a few years now and I can't tell if its a defensible shortcut or just a bias. It goes back to that Bernard Williams example: a man's wife is drowning, and instead of just jumping in he pauses to check whether saving her would be permitted by some impartial rule; Williams thinks the pause is itself a failure—that it's one thought too many. I've turned this into a rule I use all over the place. When I'm evaluating a moral theory, it earns a large penalty if it would require someone acting straightforwardly on love, friendship, or a long-term commitment to first route the action through a general justification. When I'm evaluating actual people, I treat visible let-me-check-this-is-justified behavior before a plainly human act—helping a friend, siding with a colleague who's being mistreated—as a red flag about their moral judgment, even though I can think of careful people who would pause purely out of scrupulosity. And I use it on myself: last month I almost hesitated before telling a grieving friend I'd fly out for the funeral, because some part of me was running a duty-calculus about work, and I treated that hesitation as evidence I'd been badly trained rather than as me being conscientious. Is this policy of treating the pause as evidence actually justified? I know Williams was making a critique of impartialist theories, but I'm using it as a cheap heuristic for judging both people and theories, and I worry I've built a machine that rationalizes partiality while ignoring cases where the pause is exactly the right amount of thought. What would the defensible version of the rule look like, and under what conditions does it break?
