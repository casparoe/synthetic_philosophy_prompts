I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, but I share a lab with people doing pain psychophysics, and a chapter from a popular-science book on pain has been making the rounds here. It's about pain asymbolia, and the author's gloss has been bugging me because it sounds right in places and too quick in others. The book says, roughly, this: in pain asymbolia, usually after damage in or around the insula, patients can still detect a noxious stimulus, tell you where it is, and rate its intensity, but they don't mind it — they don't grimace, don't withdraw, sometimes even smile or laugh at a pinprick. The author concludes that the pain sensation itself is affectively neutral, a bare signal of tissue damage, while the felt badness — the part that makes pain awful — is supplied by a separate emotional or evaluative response. And from there the author draws the further conclusion that pain is not intrinsically bad at all: it's bad only because, and only to the extent that, we mind it or judge it to be bad.

I'm sympathetic to the basic dissociation, since morphine reports often sound the same way — people say the pain is still there but it doesn't bother them. But I'm suspicious of the slide from "these two components can come apart" to "the sensory part is neutral and the badness is all in a response on top." A lesion case seems to show at most that two things that usually travel together can be prised apart, not that one is the real pain and the other an add-on. I also half-remember evaluativist and imperativist accounts of pain — the idea that pain is a perception of damage as bad, or a bodily command to stop doing whatever you're doing — that would treat the asymbolic patient as having a defective or incomplete pain, not an intact neutral perception plus a missing attitude. So could you give me a careful accuracy audit of the book's summary? I want to know which bits are fair descriptions of the clinical phenomenon, which bits quietly assume a philosophical view, and whether the conclusion that pain is only bad because we mind it actually follows from the accurate parts. I'd rather not go around rehearsing a distinction that's only half-earned.
