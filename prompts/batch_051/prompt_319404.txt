I am trying to determine which account of concepts my own categorization judgments commit me to, and I would like you to extract the principle behind them rather than survey the alternatives. Below are my verdicts on seven cases, reported as honestly as I can.

A penguin is a bird and a robin is a bird, but a robin is a better, more typical bird than a penguin. So whatever determines membership in the category bird does not also determine how good an example something is.

A raccoon that is dyed, surgically reshaped, and given a false scent so that it is superficially indistinguishable from a skunk until genetically tested is still a raccoon; a skunk altered in the reverse direction is still a skunk. For these animals, ancestry and hidden structure outweigh surface appearance.

Chess, solitaire, tag, and a child's invented chasing game are all games, yet I cannot state a definition that covers them. They appear to hang together by overlapping similarities rather than by a common element.

A dandelion growing in my lawn is a weed; a dandelion of the same species growing in a restored prairie is not. This is, for me, a difference in category membership and not merely a difference in how much the plant bothers me. Whether something is a weed depends on context and a human purpose.

Now the pair that worries me. If a clear, drinkable liquid behaved exactly like water but turned out to have a different underlying chemical structure, I would not call it water. Yet I call both jadeite and nephrite jade, and I continue to do so after learning that they are two distinct minerals. The first verdict makes hidden structure authoritative; the second denies it.

Three, seven, and 447 are all odd numbers, but three and seven strike me as better examples of the category than 447. Even where a definition cleanly settles membership, my concept carries a typicality gradient over and above the definition.

An experienced mushroom forager will refuse to classify a toxic look-alike with the edible species it resembles, while a novice will call them both the same mushroom. I regard the forager's classification as better, not merely more cautious: the novice is using a shallower set of features.

Can prototype, exemplar, or theory-based (theory-theory) accounts of concepts, or some combination of them, deliver all seven verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and what is the tightest coherent principle that preserves the rest? State the principle explicitly, identify any verdict it cannot accommodate, and apply it to a case I have not settled: a life-sized animatronic dog that behaves indistinguishably from a real dog in ordinary interaction—should it count as a dog in the sense in which children use the word, or be excluded, and on what ground?
