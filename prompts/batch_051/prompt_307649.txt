I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and I help run a monthly reading group for people with the same background: about 12 people, 90 minutes, mixed levels (a few programmers, a couple philosophy grad students, the rest just long-time readers). I want a hands-on game that gets them to actually feel the problem of logical uncertainty. Not a lecture: I want them to run into it themselves before I name it.

The thing I want them to come away with, which they should experience before I say it, is that uncertainty about facts already determined by math or by a computation you haven't done is a different kind of problem from uncertainty about hidden empirical facts, and that trying to be a perfect Bayesian over logic only makes sense if you're logically omniscient, which none of us are.

Something in the space of a play-money prediction market on statements about an opaque but deterministic process would be ideal — e.g., statements about the output of a small program or a cellular automaton none of them have run, where some logical implications between the statements are cheap to check and some are expensive. I want the game to push them into a bind where their prices cant be both honest about not having computed the answer and classically coherent, and where they get dutch-booked exactly on the implications that were cheap enough for someone else to spot. Bonus if the group reconstructs the explicit tension rather than being told it.

Give me concrete rules, materials I can print or improvise, a payoff scheme that uses play money or points rather than real money, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the ideas: classical Dutch book arguments against incoherence, a logically omniscient adversary versus a resource-bounded one, and the eventual-consistency/limits idea behind logical induction. I'd like one specific note in the debrief about what the activity can't reproduce — I suspect the asymptotic, convergence-over-time part of logical induction is exactly the thing a one-shot game cant show — and a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, especially with the people who already half-know the idea and think assigning probabilities to strings of math just works. Keep the terms logical uncertainty and logical induction for the debrief, not the setup. And feel free to tell me if this needs to be run as two rounds with different computational costs, because my current guess is the lesson lands best when some implications are trivially easy to check and some are obviously too expensive to compute in the session.
