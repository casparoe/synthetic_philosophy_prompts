I write about science and ideas, and the happiness beat has gotten weird. On one side there are the psychologists with set-points and hedonic adaptation; on the other there are philosophers arguing about whether happiness is a feeling, a life-satisfaction judgment, or something richer, and each of them has one lab result or one personal case they keep waving around. Then I read Émilie du Châtelet's short essay on happiness — the 18th-century French physicist and philosopher — and she makes the standard menu look thin. Her rough line: you have to free yourself of prejudice, you have to let yourself have strong passions even though they bring real suffering, and you should hang on to a few illusions about yourself and your future rather than curing yourself of them all. And when the stormier passions let you down, study is the fallback, a quieter and more durable pleasure.

Its a strange mix — part epistemic hygiene, part sanctioned self-deception — and I'm not sure the available theories of happiness can hold all of it. So before I write the piece, give me the full spec: what would a theory of happiness have to get right to be adequate? Not your preferred theory, but the desiderata. For each requirement, tell me the case or the empirical result that forces it and why a theory can't just dodge it.

At minimum I want the list to rule on the following. Whether feeling happy in the moment, being satisfied with your life, and living well are three things the theory owes us or just one — and which of them, if any, is the real thing the word tracks. Cases where a person is wrong that they're happy: the contented manipulated person, the self-deceived spouse, the person on the treadmill who reports high life-satisfaction but treats her own days as something to get through. The adaptation findings that good and bad fortune both wear off, which sit awkwardly with any theory that tells us to chase big external changes. Du Châtelet's claim that passions are necessary even though they hurt — is that a requirement a theory must respect, or should a theory diagnose her as confusing intensity with happiness? And her claim that a few illusions are necessary — does that force us to say happiness can be truth-insensitive, or can a theory explain that point away?

Then score the usual contenders against your list in a sentence or two each, and tell me which single requirement knocks out the most of them and which requirements are actually negotiable. And tell me how confident you are in the list, and which requirement you're least sure about.
