I've been chewing on whether the old anti-naked-statistical-evidence argument survives what's happened to forensic evidence, and I'd like a careful take.

The familiar line runs like this. You're run off the road by a bus, but all you can show is that it was a blue bus and that the Blue Bus Company owns 80% of the blue buses on that route; the other 20% are scattered among tiny operators. That's an .8 chance, so it seems to clear the preponderance standard, yet nearly everyone — courts included — thinks it isn't enough to win. Or the rodeo gatecrasher: a little over half of the 1000 people in the stands never bought a ticket, so a randomly picked attendee is more likely than not to be a gatecrasher, but nobody thinks that alone is enough to make a particular person pay. The usual moral drawn is that a merely statistical probability about a class isn't proof: proof has to attach to this event and this person, not to how often people like them crash or sneak in or offend.

But here's the development that seems to scramble that distinction. Modern DNA evidence — especially for messy mixture samples with several contributors — often isn't a clean match anymore. A lab runs the mixture through probabilistic software that compares it to the suspect's profile and outputs a likelihood ratio: the mixture is X times more likely if the defendent contributed than if two unknown people did. That number is built partly on population allele frequencies and a heap of modeling assumptions, and the software is sometimes proprietory, so the finder of fact gets something that feels case-specific and anchored to the exact sample in front of them, while still being a statistical artifact generated from a reference population. It's not the 80% blue bus base rate, but it's not an eyewitness either.

So here's what I want to know. Does the anti-naked-statistical-evidence view survive this kind of evidence? Which premise of the old argument is actually under pressure, and is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still worth holding — maybe not a blanket rule that statistical evidence never suffices, but a requirement about auditability, or about whether the inference can be explained in terms a finder of fact can genuinely assess? Or should we just give up on the idea that there's a meaningful line between individualized and statistical evidence?
