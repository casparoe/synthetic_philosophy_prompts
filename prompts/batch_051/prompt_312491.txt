For a piece I am writing on how scientific language treats unobserved entities, I have been working through Berkeley's so-called master argument against unperceived objects. Reconstructed in my own terms, it runs as follows.

(1) Suppose I try to conceive that some object — a particle, or a tree — exists entirely unthought-of.
(2) For the attempt to succeed, there must be some object of my thought that I represent as unthought-of.
(3) But whatever is an object of my thought is, by that very fact, being thought of by me.
(4) So the object in question turns out to be both unthought-of and thought-of at once, and the attempt refutes itself.
(5) Therefore no proposition asserting the existence of an unthought-of object can be coherently conceived.
(6) If such a proposition cannot be coherently conceived, it cannot be meaningfully asserted or denied; so sentences like "the tree is there when no one is looking" or "the particle has a definite position when no measurement is made" are not false but meaningless.

The conclusion appears to prove too much: it dissolves rather than settles the realism debate about unobservables, and it would make ordinary talk of things persisting unobserved strictly unintelligible. Yet each step seemed compulsory on the way down. I would like, if possible, to hold on to the thought that those sentences are meaningful. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? If the fault lies in a premise or in a transition between steps, give the reading under which that premise seemed true, the reading under which it fails, and say what a defender of Berkeley's conclusion would have to surrender or add to repair the argument.
