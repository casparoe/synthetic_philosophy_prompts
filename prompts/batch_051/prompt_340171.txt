I'm a PhD student in cognitive science working on LLM interpretability, and I've been going back and forth with a labmate about whether chain-of-thought faithfulness is a testable property at all. His claim is the stronger one: that when a model answers a reasoning question, and its chain of thought riffs through certain considerations before concluding B, those written considerations are often an actual cause of the final answer — not just a fluent post-hoc story generated downstream of the same hidden state. I'm not convinced, but I also can't say what evidence would change my mind, which bothers me.

The obvious test — check whether the answer matches the chain of thought — is hopeless, since a confabulated gloss would also match. Probing the hidden states tells you what information is around, but information being present isn't the same as those words doing causal work. Intervening on the model (ablations, activation steering) moves the answer and the rationale together, so it doesn't seperate "the rationale caused the answer" from "both are downstream effects of one computation."

My impulse is to build a dissociation setup: fine-tune a model on pairs where the same rationale appears with different final answers and the same final answer appears with different rationales, so the two become statistically independent during training; then, at test time, hold the rationale fixed while steering the hidden computation toward a different answer, or hold the answer fixed while swapping rationales, and see what gives. I genuinely can't tell whether this would settle the question or just measure something about a weird fine-tuned regime.

So, concretely: design the strongest test you can for the literal causal claim that a chain-of-thought transcript produces the final answer. Say what you'd vary, what you'd measure, and which outcome counts for or against faithfulness. Then be honest about what a positive result would and wouldn't show — whether we'd be testing token-level causation or only some operational stand-in, and whether the result would generalize past the exact model and setup. If you think no test can avoid begging the question, say why, and give me the least-bad fallback.
