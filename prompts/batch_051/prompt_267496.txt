I'm a philosophy professor who somehow ended up on my university's Finance and Estates Comittee. We meet on Thursday to decide on a proposal to rip out the remaining gas boilers and heat-pump the main science block, with a full fabric upgrade as part of the same job. Capital cost is £14m. The estates team's base case has it saving around £550k a year in gas and electricity over a 30-year appraisal, with a carbon-reduction figure on top that everybody outside estates thinks is optimistic. At our standard 5% real discount rate the net present value comes out at roughly −£1.2m, so it fails our normal investment test; at 1.5% it is comfortably positive. Half the committee reads the first number and says we cannot afford it. The finance director says we are not a climate charity, and every pound we sink into heat pumps is a pound not earning 5% in the endowment. The other half insists the 1.5% number is the honest one, because the people reaping the savings are future students and staff, and they shouldn't count for less just because they arrive later. I've been trying to make the point that the 5% rate quietly packs together two distinct things: a guess that later generations will be wealthier, and the far more questionable claim that later utility simply matters less. The room glazes over.

Could you draft the short decision memo I'll circulate? It should lead with a clear recommendation (approve, reject, or approve with conditions), and it must give the strongest version of the case on the losing side rather than a strawman. Include a section explaining what the 5% discount rate is actually doing, in language an accountant will accept.

Sections, in this order, with rough lengths:
Recommendation (100 words)
The proposal and the numbers (150 words)
The case for approval (250 words)
The case against (250 words)
What the 5% discount rate is actually doing (200 words)
Conditions and next steps (100 words)
