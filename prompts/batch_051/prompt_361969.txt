I'm a retired physicist (not a computer scientist, though I've done my share of Fortran) and I've been running a small philosophy-and-science discussion group for the last year. About ten adults, ninety minutes once a week, mostly retired doctors and engineers plus one current philosophy undergraduate. I'd like a single hands-on session for them, not a lecture, built around an idea I only half understand and would like them to feel the pull of.

The idea is something like program equilibrium—that's the name I keep bumping into, anyway. Here's my rough sense of it. In an ordinary one-shot prisoner's dilemna, two self-interested players should both defect, even though both would do better if they cooperated. But now suppose the players don't choose an action on the spot; instead each must submit a rule, ahead of time, that can condition on what the other rule does—and suppose the rules are readable by everyone before any action is executed. Then you can get mutual cooperation as a stable outcome in what is in some sense still a one-shot game: a rule of the form cooperate exactly if your rule would cooperate against my rule can meet a copy of itself and both cooperate, and a rule of the form cooperate only with rules equivalent to me, otherwise defect seems to hold cooperation together in its own way. The thing I want the group to feel is the difference between choosing an act and choosing a conditional strategy—that making your choice depend on the other person's choice, and letting them see that dependence, can change what rationality permits.

Could you design a runnable ninety-minute activity? Maybe start with a few rounds of plain one-shot prisoner's dilemma, anonymously paired, with small real payoffs (there's a box of decent biscuits involved) so they watch cooperation fall apart. Then move to a tournament where each person submits a written conditional rule—plain English is fine, no code required, though I can help the coders write theirs precisely—and the rules are posted or read aloud before matching. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the idea, with no lecture up front: the game should produce the experience first, and the vocabulary only afterward.

Act as a philosopher who works on game theory and designs hands-on teaching games. Take your time, and maybe sketch two or three possible mechanics before settling on one. Be specific enough that I could run it next week: exact rules, payoffs, matchings, how to handle vague rules, what will predictably go wrong, and how to debrief. Also be frank about which part of the real idea a classroom game cannot reproduce—for example, real programs can read each other's actual source code, not just a promise written on an index card—and what I'll have to supply myself. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the design choices, and single out the one thing you're least sure will actually produce the experience I'm after.
