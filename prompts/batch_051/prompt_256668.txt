I am a software engineer rather than a philosopher, but I have been working through Book XI of Augustine's Confessions in translation, and I have begun to think the time discussion there bears directly on how I reason about ordering and duration in distributed systems. The passage that has stayed with me is the one in which Augustine examines what happens when he recites a psalm. As I understand him, he says that before he begins, the whole psalm is stretched out in expectation; as he sings, each syllable passes out of existence as soon as it is sounded, so that only the present syllable is actually present; and afterwards the whole is held together in memory. He then claims that what he measures when he measures the psalm's length is not some external thing that stands still, but an impression or stretching of his own attention. His conclusion, if I have followed him, is that time, or at least the measuring of time, is a kind of distension of the mind itself.

I have tried to reconcile that with the way I already think about time when designing systems, and I have written down six commitments. I cannot see where the stress is, and I would like an audit rather than a lecture. Please go through the set and tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other, which only seem to, how severe each genuine tension is, what the cheapest repair would be in each case, and which single revision would do the most to make the whole set cohere.

Here are my commitments:

1. Only the present state of a system, or of the world, actually exists; past and future states are not real.
2. Local temporal ordering between events is objective: if process A flushes a buffer before process B reads from it, A's write is earlier than B's read whether or not any mind observes it.
3. Augustine's analysis in Confessions XI is substantially right about measurement: duration cannot be measured except by a mind that holds earlier and later parts together in attention, memory, and expectation, so the measurement of time is mind-dependent.
4. There is no global present or global now across a distributed system; what counts as simultaneous, or even as ordered in a particular way, depends on the observer's clock or frame of reference, so simultaneity is relative.
5. Past and future states do genuine explanatory work in accounting for present failures: for example, a present crash is caused by an earlier timeout and will be fixed by a future rollback.
6. A correct account of time should also cover systems with no conscious experience, such as a database that enforces transaction ordering, so consciousness cannot be essential to time itself, even if it is essential to our experience of measuring time.
