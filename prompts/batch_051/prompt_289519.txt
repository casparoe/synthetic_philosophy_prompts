I have been following the distinction between process-based and outcome-based supervision since I first encountered the process reward model results, and I keep running into the same unresolved tension in my own thinking.

On one side there is a view I find close to compelling. For any task whose objective is externally checkable, the only non-arbitrary training signal is that objective itself. Any additional signal about how the answer should be reached imports a prior about the right kind of reasoning. If our prior were correct in sufficient detail, we would not be training a model to discover a procedure; we would write the procedure down. Process supervision therefore penalizes valid but unfamiliar derivations, and it creates a second, fuzzier target that can be gamed in the familiar Goodhart sense: optimizing the proxy can drive it away from the thing it is meant to track. The theory has real systematic virtues. It is simple, it uses only the ground truth, and it never replaces the task's own standard of success with an evaluator's taste.

But I also have an intuition I cannot talk myself out of. Suppose a model trained with only final-answer feedback performs well on validation, but on inspection its generated reasoning is a post hoc rationalization for answers it arrived at by other means, or it reliably uses an identifiable shortcut that holds in the test distribution but fails under a mild shift. I find myself unwilling to say the model has learned what I wanted it to learn, even if the outcome metrics are fine. The process seems to be our only handle on how the model will behave when the distribution shifts or when the task stops being externally verifiable. If we give up on supervising the process, we are staking everything on the hope that performing well by our current tests just is the kind of reasoning we would eventually endorse.

I can see moves on both sides. The intuition may be an anthropomorphic artifact: we do not ask a chess engine's search to resemble a human's explanation, so why should an LLM's chain of thought be something we would endorse? The theory may also be demanding more than finite data can bear, since outcome labels can be so sparse that a process signal is simply regularization that makes the search tractable rather than a claim about true reasoning. But neither move dissolves the worry, because the question is exactly whether process supervision tracks something the outcome leaves out.

Give me the strongest version of each position and then a negotiated verdict: should the intuition yield to the theory, the theory yield to the intuition, or is there a reading on which they are compatible? What would justify each move, and what evidence should change my mind? Do not settle for a generic it-depends; commit to the shape of the boundary, even if parts of it are fixed by facts I would have to measure.
