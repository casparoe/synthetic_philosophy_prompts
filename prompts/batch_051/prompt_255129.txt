Something I keep going back and forth on. Popper's old anti-historicism argument says there can't be real laws of history, roughly for three reasons: history is a one-off process, so you can't test universal regularities by rerunning it; trends aren't laws because they hold only under background conditions; and the growth of knowledge makes long-run prediction impossible, because predicting what you'll know tomorrow is already a way of knowing it today. Plus the prediction itself can feed back into the world and change the very thing it predicted.

Now suppose somebody actually pulled it off, sort of. Big digitized datasets of historical states, wars, famines, institutions, and machine-learning models trained across lots of societies that get genuinely good at predicting, out-of-sample, state collapses or revolutions a decade ahead—better than the historians. I want to know whether Popper's argument survives that, and in what form. Which premise is the development actually bearing on? The one-off-process point seems untouched: the model is still fitting patterns inside a single historical record, and predicting collapses isnt the same as having a law of history. But the knowledge-growth point might bite harder: once the model and its risk scores are public, governments and insurgents start responding to them, and the regularities the model learned could erode. Is that just the reflexivity point showing up again, or does something deeper break?

Could a Popperian just shrug and say: fine, there can be quantitative forecasting of history, but not laws in the sense that matters—no unconditional claims about where history must go? Or does a model that robustly predicts across hundreds of societies force us to admit that the uniqueness worry was overstated? I'm less interested in the definition of "law" than in which part of the argument still does real work once you grant that the model works.
