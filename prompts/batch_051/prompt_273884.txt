I keep coming back to an inconsistency in how people treat emotions, and I want to know if there's a principled line here or just a habit. On the one hand, we say you can't help how you feel: emotions happen to you, sometimes against your will, and it would be absurd to blame someone for feeling afraid or startled. On the other hand, we do blame people for plenty of emotions. If someone feels a flash of envy at a friend's good news, we might say "that's not very generous." If someone nurses resentment for years, we treat it as a character flaw, not just an unfortunate weather pattern in their head. So far you might say: the difference is that the brief, automatic stuff is outside our control, while the long-lasting, articulated stuff involves the person's own thinking. But that doesn't hold up. A sudden rush of disgust at, say, seeing two men kiss feels just as automatic as a startle, and most people would still think worse of someone for having it. And a long-lasting sadness after a death is something we'd never blame. So it's not just duration or how much reasoning went into it. What I'm after is a principled account of why we treat some emotions as things the person is answerable for and others as things that merely happen to them. Is the real distinction that some emotions are judgments the person endorses, some are perceptions imposed on them, and some are bare feelings? Or is the practice of emotionally blaming people just a leftover from a bad theory of the passions that we should give up? I'm not looking for a survey of positions; I want someone to actually carve the phenomenon at its joints and defend the boundary, including the awkward cases. If your view is that no emotions are blameworthy, say so and explain what we should do with the resentment case.
