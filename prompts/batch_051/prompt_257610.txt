I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been trying to think through an asymmetry in the way people around me judge medical interventions. Most of them have no hesitation about a 75-year-old receiving a coronary bypass, a course of chemotherapy, or a hip replacement: these are straightforward medicine, and they are generally approved because they give the patient more healthy life. Many of those same people, however, become uneasy, and some turn moralistic, at the idea of a therapy that slows or reverses the ageing process itself, so that a 75-year-old regains the physiology of a 45-year-old and can expect several additional decades of health. To keep the comparison clean, suppose the two interventions are matched in the healthy years they add and in their risk, cost, and uncertainty; the only remaining difference is the target — a specific pathology in one case, the ageing process itself in the other. I want to know whether anything justifies that asymmetry, or whether it is simply comfort with the present human condition, an attitude the longevity debate sometimes calls deathism.

Please proceed in three steps. First, build the strongest possible case for the asymmetry: that disease treatment restores a person to a species-typical condition while ageing reversal exceeds it; that mortality gives our choices their weight and shape; that wide use of such a therapy would entrench inequality, generational hierarchy, or social stagnation. Second, test each of those justifications against nearby cases — a 35-year-old treated for a fatal inherited condition, a healthy adult using a cognitive enhancer without any illness, a public-health programme that adds decades of life by eliminating childhood diarrhoea — and say which justifications survive, which fail, and why. Third, give a verdict: should the asymmetry be kept, reversed, or abandoned, and what would change your mind? I am after the structure of the argument rather than a survey, and please answer in British English.
