I'm the pricing manager at a mid-sized agricultural insurer, the kind that writes policies for frost damage on fruit and drought on row crops. Our actuarial team has models that spit out a single probability for an event, like '12% chance of a frost event in this region in March,' and in principle the underwriters are supposed to price off that number. In practice they override it constantly. A trader hears the Pacific is running cold and wants to nudge the frost number up; another drops the drought number for a big account because the client has never claimed and is making noise about leaving; someone else has a hunch about a dry spring. This happens dozens of times a week, each override moves premium by a few thousand, and its starting to show up as real money and real arguments in the pricing committee.

I've been told there's a principle in formal epistemology—the Principal Principle, I think—that is actually on point hear: if you know the objective chance of an event is 12%, your rational degree of belief in that event should be 12%, except when you have a particular kind of information that isn't already summarized by that chance. I want that turned into a decision procedure my underwriters can actually follow. Give me a checklist of questions to ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with 'defer to the model,' where it stops with 'override allowed' and what documentation is required, and where it stops with 'this is a judgment call, kick it up to the pricing committee.' The users are underwriters with no philosophy background, they have about 15 minutes per decision, and large overrides already need sign-off, so the procedure just has to handle the epistemic question, not the commercial one.

Then run the procedure on two proposed overrides so I can see it working. First: an underwriter wants to raise a frost probability above the model's 12% because current sea-surface temperatures are unusually cold and the firm's crop model does not include sea-surface temperature as an input. Second: an underwriter wants to lower a drought probability from the model's 9% to 4% for a longstanding client because the client has never filed a drought claim and is threatening to move their book elsewhere. Make sure that in at least one of the cases the procedure gives an answer I won't like, and don't soften it; also say plainly what the procedure cannot settle. As you work through it, mark clearly which steps are forced by the epistemology and which are just company policy you're choosing, because I'll need to defend that distinction to the committee.
