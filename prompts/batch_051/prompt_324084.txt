I've been trying to write down what I actually believe about AI risk and I keep ending up with a list where I believe each item on its own, but I'm not sure the set hangs together. Here it is, roughly in order of how strongly I hold it:

1. The most likely way advanced AI goes wrong is slow value drift, not a sudden treacherous turn. Systems get very good at optimizing some measurable objective, institutions and users adapt around them, and by the time anyone notices the objective has come away from what we cared about, we've already rewired things around the systems.
2. Instrumental convergence is basically a law: any system good enough at pursuing a goal will pick up resource-acquisition, shutdown-avoidance, and influence-seeking as subgoals, because those help almost any terminal goal.
3. Goodhart's law is doing the real work in my picture: whatever legible target we optimize for will eventually get gamed, and the gap between the target and what we actually wanted grows the longer we optimize.
4. Corrigibility is the load-bearing property. I care less whether a system wants what I want than whether it cooperates with being corrected or shut down. Most of my hope is that we can build and keep that "lets itself be corrected" property.
5. The relevant systems won't look like one unified AGI with clear goals. They'll look like a pile of semi-autonomous subsystems, optimizers, and humans, where no single part is scheming to take over.
6. The more legible a failure is, the easier it is to coordinate against it, so the scariest failure modes are the ones that never flag themselves as failures.

I think all six are right, but I can feel myself dodging something. Go through and tell me where the set is genuinely in tension or inconsistent. Then tell me which single revision to the list would clean up the most.
