I have been thinking about Zera Yacob, the seventeenth-century Ethiopian thinker who, as I understand him, held that each person already possesses the instrument needed for moral knowledge — their own reason — and that inherited custom and religious authority are more likely to distort inquiry than to aid it. Suppose a community decided to take this literally and built an institution around it. Every member, at age fifteen, before being formally taught the community’s moral or religious tradition, would spend a year in a plain room or small cabin with food and basic comforts provided but no books, no teachers, and no one to discuss ethics with. Their only assignment would be to use their own reasoning to work out how to live and how to treat other people, writing down the precepts they reach and the chain of reasoning behind each one. At the end of the year the whole community would gather to hear each person’s conclusions, and only afterward would the traditional moral curriculum begin. The written records would be kept and compared across generations.

Walk through what this institution would actually do to the people raised under it, to the community’s received morality, and to the idea that reason unaided by tradition converges on ethical truth. Would the year of solitary inquiry mostly test rationality, temperament, or something else? Where would it be gamed or turn into a performance, and could the community detect that? What happens when the young reasoners return with conclusions that conflict with the tradition, and which conflicts would the community find hardest to absorb? In the end, would the institution be more likely to vindicate Zera Yacob’s confidence in untaught reason, or to undermine it by showing how much of what we call moral reasoning is supplied by the surrounding culture? I am not asking whether the scheme is a good idea in the abstract; I want to understand how it would actually behave.
