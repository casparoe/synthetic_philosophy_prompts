I've been working through a bargaining-theory handout and it summarizes the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution like this. In a two-player bargaining problem, start from the disagreement point and the utopia point — the best payoff each player could get in any feasible deal. Draw the line between them; the KS solution is the furthest point along that line that's still feasible. The effect is that each player's gain ends up the same fraction of their best possible gain, so KS maximizes the smaller of those two fractions: its the relative egalitarian solution. Axiomatically, you get KS from Nash's axioms by swapping independence of irrelevant alternatives for individual monotonicity — if the feasible set expands without improving player 1's best possible payoff, player 1 shouldn't come out worse. The handout then says Nash's failure of monotonicity is just IIA doing its work, since IIA stops the solution from tracking how players' best possible payoffs change; so individual monotonicity isn't really an extra fairness principle, just the removal of a pathology. It also relays the standard knock that KS ignores the shape of the feasible set except for the utopia point, so its fairness amounts to little more than a scaling choice. This all sounds neat, but I've also seen Nash billed as the fairness benchmark, and now I'm not sure the handout is being fair. Could you go through it and tell me what's right, what's subtly off, and whether someone defending KS would accept that last criticism as stated?
