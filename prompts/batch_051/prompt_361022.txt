I have been working through the mathematics of stake sizing for repeated favorable bets, and a line of reasoning keeps delivering a conclusion I am certain must be wrong, but I cannot locate exactly where it goes bad. Suppose an agent is offered an indefinitely long sequence of independent even-money bets on a coin that lands heads with probability p, where 1/2 < p < 1. The agent commits to betting a fixed fraction f of her current bankroll on every round: a win adds the amount staked to the bankroll and a loss subtracts it. We will evaluate strategies by their expected bankroll after n rounds.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) Each individual round has positive expected value because p > 1/2, and for a single round the expected value is larger the larger the stake.
(2) Rational choice under risk consists in maximizing expected value, so among fixed-fraction strategies the rational one is the one that maximizes expected bankroll.
(3) For any f between 0 and 1, the expected per-round multiplier is p(1+f) + (1−p)(1−f) = 1 + f(2p−1), which is strictly increasing in f.
(4) Since the rounds are independent, the expected bankroll after n rounds is the initial bankroll times this expected per-round multiplier raised to the n-th power; so expected bankroll is also increasing in f.
(5) Therefore expected bankroll is maximized at f = 1: stake the entire bankroll every round.
(6) At f = 1, however, eventual ruin has probability 1: the first losing round leaves nothing, and with p < 1 a losing round occurs with probability 1 in the long run. Indeed, at f = 1 the expected bankroll after n rounds is W0(2p)^n, which grows without bound, while the probability of having anything left after n rounds is p^n, which tends to zero.

So a criterion that sounds as if it merely prefers better expected outcomes to worse ones ends up recommending a strategy that almost surely destroys the bankroll, whereas the usual Kelly rule says to bet only the fraction f = 2p−1. I can see that steps (1), (3), and (4) are just arithmetic, and (6) is a probability fact; the suspicious step is (2). But I cannot tell whether (2) is simply false as a general principle of rational choice, or whether it is true for a single isolated round and the mistake lies in extending it to many multiplicative rounds, or whether the whole argument is a valid reductio showing that expected-bankroll maximization is the wrong norm for this setting. Which is it? If the fault lies in (2), give the strongest reading of (2) on which the arithmetic in (3)–(5) is correct, and say exactly what a defender of that reading would have to give up.
