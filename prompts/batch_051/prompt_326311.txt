I have a running set of verdicts about whistleblowing, loyalty, and betrayal that I have never been able to fit under one principle. I want you to find the principle behind them, or tell me which of my verdicts have to go.

Here are the cases.

1. An engineer at a chemical plant discovers that the company has been quietly releasing solvent into a nearby river at levels above the legal limit. She files an internal safety report and hears nothing for six months, then sends the documents to the state environmental agency. I think she did the right thing—indeed, I think she was obliged to do it.

2. A quality-control manager learns that a car manufacturer knows about a brake defect that is fatal in rare conditions and has decided to pay expected lawsuits rather than issue a recall. After the internal compliance hotline produces no action, he leaks the internal risk analysis to a newspaper. Fine.

3. A junior aide on a political campaign secretly sells the candidate's private medical records to a tabloid for fifty thousand dollars. The records show a chronic condition the candidate had kept private but that in no way impairs fitness for office. I think this is exactly what betrayal looks like: not fine.

4. A soldier in a conscript army leaks to a journalist the coordinates of an ammunition depot, believing the war to be unjust. The depot is in an area where civilians are also present. I think this is not fine.

5. A diocese finance officer discovers that a bishop has been paying victims of abuse to stay quiet and has reassigned the perpetrator to another parish. He goes to the police. Fine—even though his colleagues call him disloyal.

6. A personal assistant tells a politician's spouse that the politician is having an affair. I think this is not fine, even though what she said was true.

7. A nurse quietly reports a colleague who has been diverting patients' pain medication to her own use. Fine.

8. A compliance officer reports her supervisor's long-running embezzlement of client funds. She partly dislikes the supervisor and partly enjoys seeing her caught. Still fine, though the pleasure makes me uneasy.

9. A data analyst learns that her company's privacy policy states it never sells customer location data, when in fact it does. To prove this she leaks a dataset of customer names and purchase histories to a reporter. I want to say this is fine, because the concealment is serious—but I also feel she has exposed innocent customers in a way that may not be excusable. I genuinely do not know.

I am struck that in cases 2 and 3 both actors leak to the press, and in cases 5 and 6 both actors breach a loyalty they owed an employer, and yet I treat them very differently. A tentative principle I have been toying with is this: a breach of loyalty or confidentiality is justified only when it is aimed at exposing or stopping a serious wrong, when there is no equally effective and less disloyal route available, and when the harm it can be expected to prevent is greater than the harm it causes.

Please do three things. First, state the principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that best captures the verdicts above, and show how it handles each case. Second, say precisely where my tentative principle must be revised, and which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate or would overturn. Third, tell me what your principle says about three cases I have not settled: a teacher reporting a colleague who has been drinking in the classroom; an employee leaking a legally authorized but ethically dubious surveillance program; and a criminal defense lawyer breaching attorney-client privilege to reveal that her client committed a past murder for which an innocent person is currently imprisoned.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, without preamble and without restating the question.
