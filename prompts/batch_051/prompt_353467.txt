I keep noticing a move in debates about whether wisdom can be taught, and I’m wondering if it has a name. One side will say: sure, wisdom can be taught — that’s what case studies, mentoring, and studying history are for. The other side will say: no, you can’t teach someone to judge when a rule doesn’t apply; that only comes from experience. Then a few minutes later the first side retreats to “well, I didn’t mean teach it like algebra, I meant cultivate it through practice” — and then goes right back to saying wisdom is teachable as if the strong claim had been defended. Meanwhile the skeptic does the reverse: they concede you can “train” or “form” wise character, but then act as if that concession changes nothing and they’ve still shown wisdom can’t be taught. It doesn’t feel like a plain equivocation between two senses of “teach,” because both sides seem aware their are two senses; they just silently swap them when it’s convenient. Is there a name for this specific argumentative pattern? The closest thing I know is the old problem about whether virtue can be taught, but that’s the substantive question, not the pattern itself. I want to know whether the pattern has been identified, what it’s called if so, and how it differs from plain equivocation, motte-and-bailey, and moving the goalposts. If there’s no established name, what would you call it, and where are its edges? Keep the answer under 300 words.
