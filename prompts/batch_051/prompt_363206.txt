In a seminar on comparative authoritarianism, a classmate passed along an argument about performance legitimacy that none of us can figure out how to press. The argument runs:

(1) A regime is legitimate just in case those subject to it have sufficient reasons to accept its authority as binding on them. (2) Citizens have such reasons when, and because, the regime reliably delivers the goods they care about most. (3) What Chinese and Singaporean citizens care about most, over sustained periods, is material prosperity and public order, not primarily competitive elections or adversarial civil liberties. (4) The two regimes have in fact delivered growth and order more consistently than most comparators. (5) Therefore their citizens have sufficient reasons to accept their regimes as binding, and the regimes are legitimate by the standard stated in (1) and (2). (6) By parity of reasoning, any regime that securely delivers whatever goods predominate in its population—whatever those goods are, and however they are distributed—is legitimate. (7) But this would count as legitimate, say, a highly effective government that secures prosperity and order for a comfortable majority while stripping an unpopular minority of basic rights, which we are unwilling to accept. (8) So either a premise must be rejected, or one of the inferences equivocates or shifts.

I would most like to keep the empirical track-record claims in (3) and (4), but beyond that I am not sure what has to give. Which step should we reject, and what does rejecting it commit us to?

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. The first item should identify the exact premise or inference you would reject; the remaining items should explain why it looked plausible, what it costs to reject it, how the best standard position would repair the argument, and what remains true after the repair.
