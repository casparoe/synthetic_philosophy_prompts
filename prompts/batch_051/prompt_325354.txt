Specifically I want to know: (a) is the math part actually right, (b) is the coordinate-system analogy misleading, (c) is the final claim too strong, too weak, or just trivial, and (d) is this even worth writing or am I rediscovering something every freshman math major knows.

Working title: Which one do I call i?

Here are the notes.

I wrote a polynomial root finder as a side project and wanted the output in a canonical order. Real roots: easy, sort ascending. Complex roots: no natural total order, so I sorted lexicographically by (real, imag) and called it a day. But for x² + 1 = 0, that ordering decides which root is i and which is -i. If I'd picked the other tie-break, the labels flip. And as far as I can tell, no boolean test built only out of field operations and real constants can return True for i and False for -i. The operation a + bi ↦ a - bi fixes every real number and preserves addition and multiplication, so it maps any such test's true-set to itself; if i passed, -i would pass too. (If you let the test hand back an actual complex number, it can tell them apart trivially—just evaluate f(z) = z and you get different answers—but then the two answers are conjugates of each other, so the symmetry isn't really broken.)

The contrast I keep coming back to is x² - 2 = 0. There, in the real numbers, one root is negative and one is positive, and "positive" is a real property, so the ordering singles one out and sorting ascending is genuinely canonical. But if you only worked inside Q(√2) as an abstract field, I think even those two roots are symmetric—the conjugation map √2 ↦ -√2 fixes Q and preserves the field operations—so the asymmetry only shows up once you import the ordering of the reals. In other words, whether two roots can be told apart depends on how much background structure you're treating as given. For i and -i, the symmetry survives even when you work in all of C, because C has no ordering compatible with the field operations anyway: if i > 0 then i² = -1 would be positive; if i < 0, its square is also positive; either way contradiction. C over R is as big as it gets for this, and conjugation still swaps the roots while fixing R pointwise.

Now the part I actually want to defend. I think "the roots" should be thought of as an unordered pair, and any ordered list I emit in code is a convention I chose on top of that. Choosing which root is i feels like choosing a coordinate system: arbitrary, but not cost-free, because later choices have to be consistent with it. Flipping i and -i is the same move as flipping the orientation of the plane. If I relabel i as -i and also flip what I mean by counterclockwise, everything I compute looks identical; if I relabel the root but refuse to flip the orientation, all my rotations come out mirrored. So the choice of i and the choice of orientation are coupled, and the real content of a calculation is what happens under this coupling. The claim I want to make: the only fact about which root is i is a covariance fact—that is, a fact about which other choices have to travel with it. Any statement that uses i but doesn't say how it transforms when the label is swapped is not really a statement about the mathematical objects, just about my convention.

Where I expect pushback: this smells like the structuralist stuff I half-remember people saying about numbers—that there are multiple equally good set-theoretic constructions, and none of them is "the" number. And I know mathematicians already live with exactly this kind of thing around orientations, handedness, maybe gauge freedom in physics. But I don't have the vocabulary, and I don't know whether my "covariance is all there is" formulation is right or just a vibey restatement.

The specific things I'd like comments on: (1) Is the claim about i and -i, and the contrast with x² - 2, correct? (2) Is the coordinate-system analogy wrong in an important way—in particular, the thing that maps between the two labelings here (conjugation) is not an arbitrary change of basis; it's an automorphism that fixes all of R. Does that make my claim stronger or weaker than a coordinate-choice claim? (3) Take the two primitive cube roots of unity. I think there's no canonical one, and that choosing the one with positive imaginary part is itself just the i-convention in disguise. Am I right? (4) Where would a philosopher who actually does this stuff push back hardest, and is there anything in here that isn't just structurelism with extra steps?
