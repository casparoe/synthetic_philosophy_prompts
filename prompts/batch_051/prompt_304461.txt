I am preparing a talk for a constitutional-theory workshop, and what I want from you is not a defense of my paper but a prep sheet for the question period.

The thesis I will defend is a qualified one: constitutional precommitment — entrenched rights, supermajority amendment rules, an independent central bank, a constitutional budget rule — is best understood not as a surrender of democratic self-government but as one of its instruments, in roughly the way Ulysses' order that he be bound to the mast was an exercise of self-control rather than an injury to it. I grant the obvious disanalogies: a people is not a single agent with one will, the people bound are not the people who did the binding, and entrenchment can be a way for today's majority to lock in its preferences. My claim is that these disanalogies can be answered if the binding was adopted through a genuinely popular process and remains revisable through a demanding but real amendment procedure.

My compressed argument: (1) a democratic people can coherently prefer at one time that its later, more agitated self not be able to undo a commitment in a single moment of panic or short-term temptation; (2) precommitment does not remove an issue from democratic control but changes the democratic procedure governing it, making change slower and more demanding rather than impossible; (3) many institutions embedded in actual democracies — constitutional review, central bank independence, fiscal rules — are precommitments of this form, and rejecting them wholesale is a far more radical move than most of the audience wants to make; and (4) the Ulysses comparison does not require believing the framers were wiser than everyone now, only that they could know something about the circumstances under which future decision-makers would predictably decide badly.

The audience: mostly democratic theorists and constitutional lawyers with strong commitments to popular sovereignty. They worry about what they call the dead hand of the past; they think a constitution must in principle be capable of being revised by the people who live under it; and they suspect precommitment talk is often a polite name for entrenching current preferences against future majorities. Several work on constitutional amendment and emergency powers, so they know the technical issues.

Give me the questions I will actually get, phrased as close as possible to this audience's own vocabulary, and ordered by how likely and how damaging they are. For each, say whether the objection has real force or is mostly rhetorical, who in the room is likely to press it, and give a short reply — one that could be delivered in under two minutes — with a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold firm on, and identify the one reply that has to land for the paper to survive the questioning. Include the objection I should most hope nobody asks, and be blunt about it.

Throughout, separate what is settled among people who work on this topic from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus.
