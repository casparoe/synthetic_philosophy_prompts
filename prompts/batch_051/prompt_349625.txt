ok so ive been going back and forth on arguments about demandingness, charitable giving, whether you can buy a nice laptop while people are starving, that whole genre, and i keep hitting the same wall: everyone leans on the concept of supererogation but nobody actually specifies what a theory of supererogation is supposed to do. its always "the stuff beyond duty, like saints and heroes," and then they move on. so before i read any more of this, i want the spec.

what does a theory of supererogation actually have to get right? give me the requirements list: the cases it has to handle (or explicitly explain away), the distinctions it cant collapse, the structural features it has to be consistent with, and the consideration behind each one. flag which are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's taste.

things im especially worried about:

- the baseline problem. wether an act counts as supererogatory depends entirely on where the duty baseline sits, and that baseline is doing silent work in every argument. the theory should have to say what sets the baseline -- impartial expected good? social roles? reasonable expectations? the agent's own projects? -- and own the fact that different baselines move the boundary between duty and beyond-duty.

- the anti-collapse requirement. it has to explain why the supererogatory doesnt collapse upward into the obligatory (if its so good, why isnt it required?) and doesnt collapse downward into the merely permissible (if its not required, what makes it better than just allowed?). holding off both pressures at once is the whole trick.

- the pattern problem. one missed chance to do something heroic isnt wrong, but a whole life with zero beyond-duty acts feels like it might be criticizable. is the category only defined act-by-act, or does the theory owe us something about the shape of a whole life? if act-by-act, why are patterns allowed to matter for character judgment but not for deontic status?

- the baseline creep problem. does doing supererogatory stuff this month raise the bar for what counts as supererogatory for you next month? if yes, the category turns into a treadmill where the baseline chases you upward. if no, then "above and beyond" has no memory and that seems odd too. the theory needs an explicit rule about whether the baseline depends on agent history.

- risk and recklessness. a self-sacrificial rescue with high odds of death is the stock example, but what about a "rescue" that has a real chance of making the victim worse off, or of harming third parties? can a supererogatory act be reckless, and does expected-value reasoning apply inside the category, or is the category about the sacrifice the agent accepts and not about the outcomes at all? i want to see the criterion.

- the prerogative smuggler. i see people use "supererogatory" to cover both "heroic sacrifice" and "permissibly favoring my own projects over the impartial good." those are different jobs. the theory should keep them apart, or argue explictly that theyre the same. if it cant, its just relabeling an agent-relative prerogative as supererogation.

- the praise/failure gap. failing to do something supererogatory is supposed to be not-wrong, but i want the theory to say what if anything is left: is there a moral remainder, is the person open to a kind of criticism that isnt blame, or does "you didnt have to" fully settle it?

then, if any subset of those requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, say so -- i suspect a theory of supererogation is being asked to be simultaneously a theory of demandingness, a theory of reasons, and a theory of praise, and those might not fit in one package. and once the list is up, tell me which single requirement knocks out the most popular ways of carving the territory. keep the scorecard short; the list is the part i actually want.
