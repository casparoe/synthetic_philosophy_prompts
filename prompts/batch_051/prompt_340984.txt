I have been reading Friedrich Nietzsche's *On the Genealogy of Morals*, and I am stuck on a short passage from the Preface, section 3. I spent my career in the natural sciences, so I am used to treating an account of how something came to exist as distinct from the question of whether the thing is valid or well-grounded; but I keep being told that philosophers often run these together, and I want help seeing whether Nietzsche is doing so here. The passage is this:

"Fortunately I soon learned to separate theological from moral prejudices, and I gave up looking for a supernatural origin of evil. A certain amount of historical and philological education, to say nothing of an innate faculty of psychological discrimination par excellence succeeded in transforming almost immediately my original problem into the following one:—Under what conditions did Man invent for himself those judgments of values, 'Good' and 'Evil'?"

He is describing how his youthful question about the origin of evil gave way, once he had some historical and philological training, to a question about the conditions under which human beings invented the value-judgments "good" and "evil." My difficulty is this: what exactly is the relation between tracing the conditions under which a moral concept was invented and evaluating that concept? Is he claiming that uncovering its origins somehow undermines the concept's authority, or merely that it explains something about the concept's character? And does his later use of etymological and historical material depend on the assumption that a word's or concept's original meaning is its true meaning—the very fallacy I have seen attributed to certain older etymological arguments—or is he doing something more subtle? I would like help understanding whether the passage commits him to a genetic fallacy, and if not, what philosophical work he thinks genealogy is actually doing.
