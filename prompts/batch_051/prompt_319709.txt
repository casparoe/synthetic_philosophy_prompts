Construct a thought experiment to specification, drawing on the School of Names and the white-horse dispute traditionally attributed to Gongsun Long.

The argumentative structure to be tested is this: a demand that can be satisfied by any horse has different satisfaction conditions from a demand that can be satisfied only by a white horse; from this difference the Dialogue draws the conclusion that a white horse is not a horse. Do not quote or cite the text; restate the argument in your own words wherever you need it.

Then construct a single ordinary scenario, no more than one paragraph, in which this exact style of inference — from a difference in satisfaction conditions to the conclusion that a white horse is not a horse — is doing genuine practical work. The scenario must have these features:

- It is set in one mundane institutional context of your choosing: a stable, a dispatch office, a warehouse, a registry, a clinic.
- A practical outcome turns on the difference in satisfaction conditions between the two demands: who has fulfilled an order, whether a procedure applies, which item gets released, and so on.
- The object in question is, by ordinary predicative use, plainly a horse. The scenario must not make anyone confused about the meaning of is, and it must not stipulate a special technical sense of horse.

The purpose of the case is to isolate what the white-horse inference can and cannot establish: the difference between two terms' satisfaction conditions on one side, and the denial that a specific sort falls under a general kind on the other. A satisfactory case must not simply re-run the Dialogue's yellow-or-black-horse illustration with new animals; it must put the inference to work inside a structure of rules and requests that does not already presuppose the School of Names' conclusion. The scenario must generate the two verdicts from the demands and rules you describe, not stipulate them.

After presenting the scenario, show that it meets the specification by working through each step:

1. State the rule or request, the object, and the verdict each demand yields about the object under the satisfaction test.
2. State the practical verdict that the School of Names-style reasoning supports in that setting, and why that verdict is correct there.
3. State the ordinary predicative verdict for that same object, and identify the single feature of the case that allows both verdicts to be correct at once.
4. Say what would happen if that feature were removed: would the scenario collapse into a bare observation that two terms differ in extension, into a genuine contradiction, or into an equivocation on is? Justify the classification.
5. Finish with the strongest reply a capable defender of Gongsun Long could give while keeping the School of Names' core distinction between naming a shape-kind and naming a color-qualified sort. Then say whether that reply repairs the inference from satisfaction conditions to non-identity, or merely relocates it.
