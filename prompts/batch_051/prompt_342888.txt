I'm a microeconomic theorist, comfortable with the formal machinery of preference orderings, utility representations, constraints and feasible sets, willingness to pay, and the distinction between complete, incomplete, and lexicographic preferences. I have only a layperson's grasp of moral philosophy. I've been trying to understand the debate about whether human dignity is the foundation of rights or a useless concept. On one side there is the Kantian view: human beings as such have a dignity, an inner worth that is fundamentally unlike a price, and this dignity, rather than welfare or consent or autonomy alone, is what grounds the prohibition on using persons as mere means; the sphere of dignity is marked by the absence of any price or equivalent, so what has dignity cannot be exchanged for, or replaced by, something of equal value the way priced goods can. On the other side there is the deflationary critique I have seen attributed to Ruth Macklin: that dignity does no independent work, that when the term is used in arguments about rights it expresses nothing beyond respect for persons or their autonomy, and that it is redundant at best and question-begging or covertly theological at worst.

Please restate both positions in the vocabulary of decision theory, as faithfully as you can — the dignity-first view as some combination of constraints on admissible social choice, lexicographic priorities, or goods placed outside the utility calculus, and the deflationary view as the claim that this formalization adds nothing beyond a standard model of rights as constraints protecting each agent's authority over her own life. Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which you had to distort to fit, and which cannot be expressed in the decision-theoretic vocabulary at all. In particular, I want to know whether the Kantian claim that dignity has no equivalent is best modeled as a lexicographic preference, as an incompleteness in the social ordering, or as something the preference-and-constraint framework simply cannot say. And on the other side, I want to know whether formalizing Macklin's critique in this way builds in her conclusion from the start — that is, whether the model can even represent a non-autonomy-based notion of dignity except as a redundant label. My honest prior is that this debate is mostly a dispute about where to draw the boundary of the feasible set and which tradeoffs are inadmissible, and that the deflationary side is right. Tell me whether the translation exercise vindicates that, or whether something real fails to survive it.

If the task as I've posed it can't be done faithfully — if, for example, the decision-theoretic vocabulary forces you to represent dignity as a preference even when it is not one — say so plainly and tell me where the translation breaks down rather than smoothing over the break.
