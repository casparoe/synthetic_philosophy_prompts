A.J. Ayer spent his career defending a form of empiricism according to which claims about a soul surviving death, even when they are not straightforwardly refuted by experience, fail the test of verifiability and therefore lack literal sense. He was also a resolute and public atheist who treated death as the extinction of consciousness. Late in life, after a choking incident stopped his heart, he had a much-discussed near-death experience; he wrote about it afterward and, as he described it, the episode left his naturalism essentially in place, though he conceded that it had slightly weakened the firmness of his expectation that death is the end. I am drawn to him for my essay because he is one of the few well-known twentieth-century philosophers who addressed what death is and whether anything of a person can be said to survive it, without any prior religious commitment.

I would like your careful reconstruction of what Ayer would have said about three developments that postdate his death in 1989. First, cryonic preservation, in which people arrange to have their bodies or brains frozen after legal death in the hope of future revival. Second, mind uploading, understood as the claim that a person's survival could consist in the continued operation of a sufficiently detailed computational model of their brain. Third, griefbots: LLM-driven chatbots trained on a deceased person's letters, messages, and diaries, which survivors use to continue something like conversation with the dead. For each, I want you to reason through the question Ayer would have pressed: is the proposed sense in which the person survives one that can be cashed out in terms of verifiable identity and continuity, or does it only deliver a copy, a substitute, or a linguistic confusion? Please separate the parts of your reconstruction that follow fairly directly from Ayer's documented commitments from the parts that require interpretive choices about him, and flag where scholars plausibly disagree about how to read his verificationism or his late-life remarks rather than presenting one reading as the consensus. Where the evidence about what he did or did not claim is thin, say so.
