I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas beat, and I'm planning a piece that pokes at the old chestnut that history is written by the victors. I want a sidebar that helps readers figure out where they actually stand on how power and perspective shape historical knowledge, rather than just picking a label they like. Audience is thoughtful but non-academic.

Could you build me a decision tree, six leaves, with no more than five branch questions? Hard cap of 900 words for the whole thing — it'll probably run as a pullout box, so tight matters more than exaustive.

Here are the six positions I want as leaves, with rough glosses to work from:
1. Rankean objectivism: the past is a fixed object; a good historian just reports it; distortion from the writer's own moment is an avoidable error.
2. Critical realism: there is a determinate past, but every access to it is perspectival; good method disciplines perspective rather than promising to eliminate it.
3. Standpoint realism: some subordinate positions in a social order are epistemically better placed to see how power actually works, so history from below can be truer about domination, not merely different.
4. Narrative constructivism: histories are made, not found — the plot-structure and categories come from the historian — but some constructions are still better than others by shared scholarly standards.
5. Strong relativism: historical accounts are just expressions of power; none is truer than another; the victors slogan is more or less the whole story.
6. Presentist pragmatism: what matters about a historical account is its usefulness for present struggles; whether it is true in some neutral sense is secondary or misleading.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the names. Each branch node should be a yes/no question about a concrete case or claim a reader can answer without already knowing the labels — so nothing like "are you a relativist?" The angle I care most about is keeping two thoughts seperate: (a) all historical writing is shaped by the writer's position, and (b) all historical accounts are equally valid because of that. The tree should pull those apart instead of letting them collapse together.

Include at least one question that uses the American Civil War / Lost Cause case, or another actual case where the defeated side's version dominated for a long time, so the tree doesn't quietly assume the victors slogan is true. For each leaf, after the one-sentence definition, add one sentence on the most awkward commitment someone who lands there has to own.

Finally, flag any pair of leaves the tree can't cleanly separate, and note any combination of answers that looks empty — either because nobody holds it, it's incoherent, or it's just underexplored. Don't worry about citations or a reading list. If presentist pragmatism cuts across your first branches, don't force it: either add a final purpose question or say so outright, I'd rather have the honest note than a fake leaf.
