Next month I'm giving a colloquium paper at a philosophy department, and I'm defending the view that we've got moral biography backwards: it's the morally mixed lives — not the cleaned-up ones — that do the actual moral-educational work. The audience is mostly virtue ethicists who think moral learning happens through admiring and imitating exemplars, plus two medievalists who work on saints' lives and will bristle at my claim that hagiography, even in its modern secular forms like the plaster-saint physicist or the plaster-saint activist, is bad for moral formation because it holds up unreachable ideals and teaches people to hide rather then work on their own flaws. My argument, compressed, is: moral improvement is mostly learning to handle your own mixed motives; a sanitized life models none of that, so it teaches either despair or hypocrisy; Plutarch's paired lives — where a man's vices sit next to his virtues and he's then compared with another flawed man — are a better model.

So I need my Q&A prep sheet as a numbered list of exactly five objections, ordered by how likely and how damaging they'll be, each phrased the way a virtue ethicist or medievalist at this colloquium would actually say it out loud. For each one add one more sentence giving my best two-minute reply, the point where that reply is softest, and what I should just concede. No introduction, no conclusion — just the five items. And make one of the five the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
