I have spent most of the past year turning over a question that began with the Greek gods and ended up in the local magistrates' court: what does an oath actually do? In the old stories, when even the gods needed a promise they could not go back on, they swore by the river Styx, and a god who broke that oath was struck down as though the river itself had the power to enforce it. That has always struck me as strange. The gods, of all beings, have no one above them to punish them, so why should an oath over a river hold them? And yet we do the same thing in our own way, in courts, at weddings, and when someone takes public office, usually without being able to say who would punish us if we broke it.

I would like you to build a decision tree that sorts a person into the account of oaths they actually hold, based on what they say about concrete cases rather than which label sounds best to them. Build it from the logical space up rather than from a list of names: first the branching questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the real positions in that space, and note any answer combinations nobody occupies, with a one-sentence diagnosis of why each is empty (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). Flag any leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate rather than papering over the gap.

The tree is for a general reading group, so it should assume no academic background. Aim for seven to ten leaves and no more than six branching questions. Use the Styx case as the anchor example, and somewhere in the questions include all of the following cases: an oath sworn by someone who does not believe in the deity or thing invoked; an oath extracted under duress; an oath whose breaking would harm no one and whose only witness is the swearer; a false oath taken with a mental reservation or a clever loophole in the wording; and an oath of office to uphold a law the swearer privately thinks unjust. The leaves must include at least these positions, named in plain language: the witness-and-punishment account, the self-curse account, the promise-with-added-solemnity account, the institution-or-practice account, the integrity-or-self-betrayal account, and the deflationary account that an oath adds nothing of its own.

Please return the whole instrument as a JSON object with these fields, using British English throughout:

- `"instrument_title"`: a short title for the decision tree.
- `"opening_prompt"`: one short paragraph introducing the exercise to a taker.
- `"branching_questions"`: an array, ordered as the tree is traversed. Each element has:
  - `"id"`: a short string identifying the question.
  - `"question"`: the question itself, phrased so a layperson can answer honestly without already knowing which position it leads to.
  - `"case"`: one sentence setting out the concrete situation the question asks about.
  - `"options"`: an array of objects, each with `"id"` (a short string) and `"text"` (the answer as a taker would give it).
  - `"what_it_tests"`: one sentence on the underlying divide the question is meant to expose.
- `"leaves"`: an array. Each element has:
  - `"name"`: the position's plain-language name, or `"unnamed"` if nobody seems to defend it.
  - `"path"`: an object mapping each branching question `"id"` on the route to the selected option `"id"`.
  - `"one_sentence_position"`: what the view claims.
  - `"most_awkward_commitment"`: one sentence on the hardest thing the view must accept.
- `"unoccupied_combinations"`: an array. Each element has:
  - `"path"`: an object mapping question `"id"`s to option `"id"`s, as above.
  - `"diagnosis_type"`: one of `"incoherent"`, `"unattractive"`, or `"unexplored"`.
  - `"explanation"`: one sentence saying why that combination is empty.
- `"inseparable_pairs"`: an array of objects, each with `"leaf_a"` and `"leaf_b"` (matching names from `"leaves"`) and `"why_the_tree_cannot_separate_them"`: one sentence.
