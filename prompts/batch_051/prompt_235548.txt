An old friend wont stop telling me that Kierkegaard already diagnosed social media more than a century early. On his telling, Kierkegaard thought the daily press produced a thing he called the public — an anonymous, everybody-and-nobody abstraction that turns people into spectators with opinions rather than actors with commitments, levels qualitative distinctions out of envy, and lets everyone feel involved while no one in particular is answerable. His conclusion is that Twitter/X, comment sections, and platform virality are just the same mechanism with a more efficient machine, so any serious critique of online discourse should begin with Kierkegaard rather than media studies. I want an audit of this analogy, not a history lesson. Can you give the correspondence table — what maps onto the public, what maps onto the daily press, what corresponds to leveling, and what counts as the individual who only likes, shares, and retweets — and then say where the mapping breaks? Which features of 1840s Copenhagen does the analogy quietly import that the modern internet lacks (or vice versa), and does his conclusion that the internet is best read as the latest installment of Kierkegaard's diagnosis survive once those disanalogies are made explicit? Keep me honest about what's well-established in readings of Kierkegaard versus what's genuinely contested among people who study him, and don't present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
