I'm a first-year PhD student TAing an undergrad philosophy of language course, and I've got a pile of midterms to grade. The language-change unit landed close to home this year: I spent my childhood hearing my nonna switch between dialect and Italian in a single sentence, and I never really registered untill recently how strange it is that we talk about one language "turning into" another. One of the exam questions was:

"Is there a fact of the matter about when Latin stops and Italian begins? In your answer, draw on at least two of the following: mutual intelligibility, standardization, and speaker self-identification. Discuss what it means to say that Latin 'became' Italian."

Here's what one student wrote:

"Latin and Italian are phases of a single continuous tradition rather than two discrete objects, so there is no date on which Latin became Italian. The spoken Latin of the late empire (Vulgar Latin) already differed from the conserved literary Latin of Cicero, and after the empire fell the regional variants drifted further apart. The change was also insensible from inside any one generation: each generation could understand its parents and its children, and since mutual intelligibility is transitive, the whole chain holds together as one language unless we impose an arbitrary cut somewhere. But there is still one real moment of division, in the sixteenth century, when Bembo and others fixed a literary standard on fourteenth-century Tuscan and Dante was retroactively crowned the father of Italian. Before that, 'Italian' just named a family of dialects with no privileged member; after that, Italian was a real, distinct language. So Latin never died — what happened is that one segment of the continuum got promoted to a national standard for political and literary reasons, which is the old point that a language is a dialect with an army."

I want to grade this carefully on the course's ten-point scale. Could you give me a grade and, more importantly, the point-by-point comments I'd write in the margins? I'm looking for where it earns credit, where it subtly goes wrong, and how each comment should be phrased so the student sees the distinction.
