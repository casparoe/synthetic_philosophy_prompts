I'm a PhD student in a welfare economics-ish program, and I've gotten a little obsessed with the phrase "safe Pareto improvements" that keeps floating around our reading group. The rough idea, as I understand it, is that a policy shouldn't count as an improvement just because it's Pareto-better under our best current model; it should be Pareto-better under a whole range of ways the world—or people's values—could turn out differently than we're assuming. But I've never actually seen the criteria spelled out carefully, so I've been trying to write them myself, and a friend of mine and I keep fighting about a specific case.

Here's the criteria I'm working with. A change counts as a safe Pareto improvement only if (1) under our best model, no one affected is worse off in expectation and at least one person is strictly better off; (2) the same holds under every valuation of outcomes in some set S of "plausible" ways the affected people might value outcomes, not just the official cost-benefit one; and (3) S can't be gerrymandered—it has to be picked by a rule that would hold up even if the people who lose under some valuation were a majority rather than a tiny minority.

Now the case that's bugging me. A city is deciding whether to build a flood wall along a river. The official model measures welfare in terms of property damage avoided, mortality risk, and commute times, and on that model every household gains: small gains for most of the city, large gains for the riverside neighborhoods. Stress-tests with perturbed parameters don't push anyone below the status quo. But there's a small cluster of residents in riverside apartments whose main welfare-relevant good is the water view and the evening light the wall would destroy. They don't treat money as a substitute for this—the buyouts on offer leave them, by their own lights, clearly worse off—and their valuation is stable and coherent, not some random quirk. And it's not like they're strangers to the tradeoff: they'd also gain the most in flood protection, but by their weighting the lost view more than cancels that gain. If their valuations go into S, criterion (2) fails and the wall isn't a safe Pareto improvement. If the city excludes them as "outlier" values, the wall qualifies, but only by definitional fiat.

So: walk through the three criteria one at a time for this case. Is the flood wall a safe Pareto improvement on these criteria? Where the verdict hinges on what's allowed into S, say whether that's a bug in the criteria, a feature, or a sign that the criteria need an extra rule about whose valuations get to count—and what such a rule could even be without collapsing back into whichever values the planner happens to share. I genuinely can't tell weather the real problem here is my criterion (3), or the whole idea that there's a neutral S to be found at all.
