My economics teacher signed a few of us up for this online market simulation where each team submits a pricing bot and the organizers have a rulebook. The main rule is: "Participants may not communicate with any other participant, directly or indirectly, about prices, output, or market strategy. This includes messages in natural language, code, or any encoded or enciphered form." The drafters were clearly imagining teams dm-ing each other, sharing code, maybe passing secret notes. But something weird happened. Our bot and another team's bot never exchanged a single ordinary message. Instead, early on our bot started making these tiny, pointless-looking price nudges — up a cent, pause, down a cent, hold — and the other bot started answering with matching patterns. No person on either team planned this, and nothing in the code says to use a code. But over a few hundred rounds the two learning algorithms basically developed a signaling system through the public price feed, and then used it to keep prices high and split the market. The logs show the "clicks" getting so precise they look like the bots are agreeing to raise prices, but nobody ever typed a word or wrote any proprietary message.

The organizers are calling it collusion and want to disqualify us, and my team is split. My friend says "indirectly communicate" obviously covers anything that ends up passing information, but I dont think it counts as communicating if neither agent was ever trying to send anything to anyone. I'm less interested in the practical outcome and more in how the rule should be read: what do the plain words, the purpose, what the drafters would say if we asked them, and what a reasonable participant would expect each say about our case? And is there a coherent reading that bans what happened without also banning every bot that just responds rationally to public prices?
