I keep finding myself pulled between two things I believe, and I want them forced into the same room.

On one side I'm fairly convinced by the case for open borders — or at least by the argument that the burden of proof lies overwhelmingly on any border restriction. The world I was born into is pervasively shaped by where people happened to be born, and the standard line that a liberal state may exclude outsiders just because they're outsiders doesn't look very different to me from arbitrary discrimination I'd reject anywhere else. If I wouldn't accept a law saying people from Ohio can't move to California, and the empirical claim that immigrants are a net drain is weak or at least very mixed, then a lot of the case for immigration control starts to look like a rationalization for a morally arbitrary status quo. I also like that the open-borders view has a clean structure: one principle of equal freedom, applied to movement like everything else, with only narrow, clearly-specified grounds for restriction.

But I have an intuition I can't shake, and I want it taken seriously rather than waved off as xenophobia or status quo bias. It's roughly this: a democratic welfare state is a bounded scheme of cooperation. The taxes that fund it, the decisions that run it, and the solidarity that sustains it are built on the sence that the people involved are, in a fairly thick way, in it together, and that the terms of the scheme can legitimately be renegotiated only by those already inside it. If the scheme is open in principle to everyone who can move there, then the thing that was supposed to be a joint enterprise run by a particular demos turns into something else — maybe a global insurance scheme operated from one lucky location, or a scheme with no real owner. Notice the point isn't the empirical claim that immigrants make the welfare state unaffordable; my intuition survives even on the assumption that migrants are net fiscal contributors. The point is about membership: who has standing to decide the terms of cooperation, and whether there's any sense left in which "we" decided them. Letting someone in isn't exactly like admitting a new member to a club, because the state already exercises coercion over everyone on its territory; but it also isn't nothing like that.

So here's what I actually want. Give me the strongest version of both sides, then tell me where the pressure goes and why. In particular: (a) is my intuition about the welfare state really an intuition about the normative importance of a bounded demos, or is it a disguised empirical fear about costs and cultural change that would dissolve if the fear turned out false? (b) If the open-borders view can be amended to accommodate the intuition — say by distinguishing a right to enter and work from a right to share in the full social minimum, or by concluding that what should be globalized is the welfare state rather than protected — is that a genuine fix or a retreat? (c) Here's the federalism analogy I keep returning to: we don't put border controls between U.S. states to protect their tax bases, and we mostly regard that as fine, even when it means people move toward high-benefit states. If that's fine, what exactly is supposed to be different at the international level; and if it isn't fine, why don't we say so? Don't just pick a side — say what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that gives, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that gives, and whether there's a version of each that can survive unchanged.

Write in American English, please.
