I spent most of my working life doing systematics — marine invertebrates, mostly, but systematics is systematics — and retired about two years ago, which is when I finally got around to reading some cognitive science and philosophy. I've noticed I have pretty firm, immediate verdicts about what belongs in what category, but when I line the verdicts up I can't see the principle behind them, and the stuff I've read on prototypes and exemplars only gets me partway. So I'm hoping for help extracting the principle, or failing that, a short list of candidate principles with an honest note about which of my verdicts have to go.

Here are the verdicts.

A robin and a penguin are both birds. But a robin is a better example of a bird than a penguin; I'd never deny a penguin is a bird, yet if you gave me one example to teach someone what a bird is, I'd pick the robin. And "birds fly" still sounds true to me, penguins notwithstanding.

A whale is not a fish. More precisely: someone in 1650 who called a whale a fish was wrong, not just working with a different concept I have no business correcting. Yet from my own field I know "fish" isn't a real clade — a tuna is more closely related to us than it is to a shark — so I can't say the sailor violated the true essence of fishhood, because there isn't one. That's the first thing that bothers me.

A tomato is a fruit, botannically, and a vegetable, in the kitchen, and I'm not tempted to declare either one the real answer. So I do allow that the same object belongs in different categories in different contexts; but I won't grant the 1650 sailor the same courtesy with "fish." Why the difference?

Pluto is not a planet. I accept the current definition, and I'd gently correct a child who said it was one. But I recognize the line was drawn by a vote, and I feel fine saying that "planet" is partly stipulative, so its strange how settled the correct verdict feels.

A virus is not alive. Like the planet case, I think the line was drawn by us, but I'd also defend drawing it where we did rather than somewhere else; it's not pure convention.

Ice and steam are both water. The fact that water is normally a clear liquid plays no role in my willingness to call a cube in my glass water.

A piece of driftwood that happens to be shaped exactly like a chair is not a chair, unless someone starts using it as one. The tree stump outside my house that I've sat on every morning for years: at some point it became a chair, or at least a seat, and I don't know when. So for artifact terms, use and history seem to do the work that appearance doesn't.

My vague hunch is a principle something like: for natural kind terms, membership is fixed by hidden structure or history of origin, and typicality only affects how good an example something is; for artifact terms, actual function and history of use do the work; and for terms that belong to different practices, like botany and cooking, the term is practice-relative and there's no context-free truth. But I can already tell that hunch has problems with the whale/fish verdict, and probably with Pluto.

So: is there one principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures these verdicts? Please state it precisely, say which clause is motivated by which case, and tell me how it handles a diamond grown in a lab: diamond, mineral, both, neither? And be straight about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. If the hunch fails, I'd rather hear that than get a patchwork that saves every verdict at the cost of being ad hoc.
