I run a small workshop on comparative privacy cultures—about 20 people, mostly grad students in political theory, STS, and a couple of law students, and we have two hours. Next session is on how citizens relate to state visibility, and we're contrasting three settings: the German constitutional tradition of informational self-determination (where privacy is framed as a condition for developing your personality, not just a defense against harm), the American pattern of sectoral privacy laws and general openness, with suspicion of government databases but comfort with corporate data, and Chinese social credit pilots, where legibility—being scored and sorted on the basis of aggregated data about your behavior—is supposed to make trust scalable. The group keeps falling back on a lazy "freedom vs. control" story, so I want an activity where they run into the actual tradeoff themselves. The takeaway I'm after: hiding from the state is not obviously the good option, because concealment has real social costs (it can wreck coordination, reputation, and trust), while legibility has real vulnerabilities (sorting, sanction, anticipatory conformity, and not knowing which of your behaviors actually counts). The three regimes should come out as different ways of pricing that tradeoff, not as good and bad.

I'm imagining a repeated game with three tables, one per regime, each table a kind of mini-polity with it's own official role. Each round, players would have a stack of "fact cards" about themselves and choose how many to reveal to the official; the public-good payoff for everyone rises with what's revealed, but individual risk rises too, and the rules about what the official may do with revealed info differ per table. I'd want, at the German table, hiding to be individually attractive and protected but to produce coordination failures players will audibly complain about; at the American table, openness to be attractive and made safe by procedural rules but leave players exposed in ways they don't notice until late; at the Chinese table, give the official broad discretion to reward or penalize on the basis of a score—and, this is the part I especially want, don't give players their own table's full rule sheet, so the opacity of not knowing what's being scored is felt rather than explained. The insight has to come from playing, not from a debrief speech.

Can you design this properly? I need concrete roles or rules, the exact scorekeeping, a timeline for the two hours, a setup script, predictions for what will go wrong in each round and how to handle it, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the ideas. Be honest about what the game can't reproduce—real fear of the state, historical memory, the role of corporate data brokers, the fact that real social credit systems are inconsistent across localities. Please no lecture up front; maybe two minutes of instructions max.
