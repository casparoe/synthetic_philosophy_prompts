I've been trying to sort out whether I'm a Stoic or a romantic about the emotions, and I've realized the labels aren't doing much for me. The Stoics treat the passions as disturbances, false judgments, basically noise in the system that the wise person works to undo. The romantics treat feeling as the actual substance of a good life, so a life that ran smoothly but felt nothing would be barren, not flourishing. The problem is that when I go case by case, my gut verdicts don't line up with either slogan, and I want to know what principle, if any, is behind them.

Here are my gut reactions, with a little background on each.

1. Crying at my mother's funeral and feeling the full weight of the loss. This feels valuable, not noise. A version of me who felt nothing there would be missing something.
2. Still being flattened by that grief six years later, unable to work or enjoy anything. Noise. Even though it started from the same grief as case 1, something has gone wrong.
3. Anger when I learned my employer was quietly underpaying the newest hires. It kept me up, but it also made me gather evidence and organize a complaint. That anger felt like it belonged.
4. Anger about a bad offside call in my Sunday league game, still stewing about it hours later. Noise. I'm embarassed I even feel it.
5. Missing my broke grad-school years, even though they were worse in almost every objective respect than my life now. I like that feeling, and it seems to add something.
6. Envy when my friend got the fellowship I wanted. I never acted on it, and I still think it was pure noise, something I'd be better off without, even though it was "apt" in the sense of tracking a real shortfall.
7. A surge of fear before a scheduled surgery. Not noise. It's just taking the threat seriously.
8. Jealousy about my partner's harmless friendship with someone. Noise. Nothing good in it, even though it's "about" someone I love.
9. The joy of hearing a toddler laugh hysterically at nothing. Not noise — almost the opposite — even though it does no practical work.
10. The little flash of pleasure when a disliked colleague flubbed a public talk. I enjoyed it, and I think that's exactly what was wrong with it. Noise.

Two pairs bother me most. Fresh grief and envy are both painful, both track something real, and both could inform or motivate action, but I think the grief is part of a good life and the envy isn't. And the two angers look like they differ only in stakes, so maybe the principle is just "fitting object, fitting intensity, fitting response" — but if fittingness is the whole story, envy should come out fine, or at least no worse than fear before a surgery, and I don't think it does. Missing grad school also seems to flunk any simple "fits reality" test, since I wouldn't actually go back.

Can you state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, as precisely as possible, and say which of my verdicts it can't accommodate? I'd also like to know what the account you settle on says about a case I keep going back and forth on: feeling sad for days about something terrible happening to people on the other side of the world, where I can't do anything for them and it won't affect my life. Is that closer to fresh grief, closer to the Sunday-league anger, or neither? And please don't just tell me the Stoics would say one thing and the romantics the other — I already know that. I want the best principle that fits my actual verdicts, even if it ends up more qualified than either camp's slogan.
