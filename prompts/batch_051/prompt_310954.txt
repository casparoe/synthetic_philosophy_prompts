I am a novelist, and the problem I need help with is conceptual, though it begins in my working life. I write fiction in which racists speak in their own voices, and I render their thinking from the inside, in dialogue and in free indirect style. This has left me unable to settle a question I need to answer before I can know what my novels are saying: what makes a statement racist?

I am not asking for an account of blameworthy speakers or a taxonomy of prejudice. I am asking for a working definition of "a racist statement" — a criterion precise enough to be applied to the sentence, to the act of uttering it, to its context, or to some combination of these — and I want that definition run through the following cases. A few strike me as clear; several do not.

The cases:

1. A character at a dinner party says of a Black family that has just bought the house next door: "I don't want those people in this neighborhood." I have no doubt that this is a racist statement, whatever the character's private feelings happen to be.

2. A character tells another that the pharmacy closes at noon on Sundays. I have no doubt that this is not a racist statement.

3. A politician in the novel campaigns on phrases like law and order and protecting the character of our suburbs. His audience hears them as promises about keeping Black residents out. The phrases themselves are colorless, and the speaker can deny any racial meaning. I want the definition to handle this dog-whistle case: if these are racist statements, I need to know in virtue of what they are racist, and why the same words are innocent in another mouth and another context.

4. In a historical novel, an otherwise decent character remarks, as a matter of course, that a colored man should know his place. The character feels no awareness of wrongdoing, and the novel plainly invites the reader to find the remark appalling. Is the statement itself racist, or only the attitude it reveals?

5. A character defending a school-discipline policy says: "Black students here are suspended at three times the rate of white students, and that is simply a fact." The sentence is true, and the character intends nothing beyond the fact. I am genuinely unsure whether this is a racist statement, and I do not know whether the answer changes if he goes on to draw a racist conclusion from it.

6. A white colleague says to a Black colleague, meaning it as praise: "You're so articulate." I cannot decide whether this is a racist statement, a racially insensitive one, or neither, and I cannot state the difference precisely.

7. A Black character uses a slur affectionately to another Black character. I am confident this is not the same thing as case 1, but I need a definition that explains which parameter does the work: the speaker, the intent, the history of the word, the relationship, or something else.

8. My third-person narrator, in free indirect style, renders a character's thoughts in language the character would use, including a racial epithet. The novel's overall perspective is ironic and dissecting. Is the narrated sentence a racist statement? Does it matter that every published word is ultimately mine, even when it is presented as a character's idiom?

I want more than a verdict on each case. Propose the definition, run it against all eight cases, and say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer. Then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. If the right move is to split the concept — one definition for the content of a sentence, another for the act of uttering it in a context, another for its predictable effects — then give me those definitions, explain how they interact, and show me which cases move between them.
