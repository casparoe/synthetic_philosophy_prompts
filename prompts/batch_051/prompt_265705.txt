You are a professional philosopher specialising in personal identity and the theory of rational choice, and you are used to making your background assumptions explicit before arguing. Please think through the question slowly, begin by brainstorming the different readings of the practice described below, and distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely happens to be compatible with.

Cryonics organisations typically offer members a choice between whole-body preservation and the cheaper neuropreservation option, under which only the head is preserved. Providers go to considerable lengths to begin cooling and processing the body as soon as possible after legal death, on the grounds that the relevant neural structures have not yet degraded beyond repair; yet the same organisations do not publish any operational criterion for deciding whether a preservation has succeeded, and they leave revival to the discretion of unknown future people whose interests may differ from those of present members. The standard arrangement is financed through life insurance and held in a trust that pays for long-term storage, and the member is told only that revival has some non-negligible probability, without any specified mechanism or base rate for estimating it.

What theory of personal identity, and what theory of prudential rationality, does this practice presuppose?

In particular:
(1) Does the choice of neuropreservation over whole-body preservation strictly presuppose that a person's identity is carried by the brain's informational or computational structure rather than by the whole living organism? Is the practice instead compatible with several rival accounts of identity, and what would an animalist or a psychological-continuity theorist have to add before they could endorse it?
(2) What view of welfare across time, and of the rationality of low-probability, high-payoff gambles, is presupposed by paying now for a future service that cannot be verified, cannot be assigned a base rate, and may never be delivered? Is the practice an application of expected-value maximisation, or does it need a distinct principle such as a precautionary rule or a Pascal-style wager?
(3) Is there a single coherent theory under which all parts of the practice are rational at once — preserving only after legal death, privileging the brain over the rest of the body, funding the arrangement through life insurance and trust, and deferring revival to unknown future actors? Or does the practice quietly presuppose inconsistent commitments, and if so, which part would a consistent provider have to revise, and what would the revised practice look like?
