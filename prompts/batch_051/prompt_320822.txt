I follow the published case rulings of a comparative psychologist, Dr. Arden, who applies a strict reading of Morgan's canon: she attributes a higher mental faculty to an animal only when she cannot see how a lower associative or innate mechanism would produce the behavior, and she treats the availability of such a lower account as decisive. Her rulings on six past cases are these. (a) A digger wasp whose provisioning routine is disturbed mid-sequence and which then restarts the whole fixed sequence: no higher faculty, a fixed action pattern. (b) A rat that learns to press a lever under a tone-shock schedule and transfers the response to a visually similar box but not to a rearranged one: no higher faculty, simple association. (c) A cichlid fish that learns to exit a net through a hole after many reinforced trials and adopts a novel exit route only after being coaxed through it repeatedly: no higher faculty, trial-and-error learning. (d) A crow that, on first presentation of unfamiliar materials, bends a wire into a hook to lift food from a tube, and later switches to a different tool when the hook is no longer usable: higher faculty, means-end reasoning. (e) An octopus that, on first exposure, opens an unfamiliar container and then adjusts its method when the closure is swapped for a different type: higher faculty, flexible problem-solving. (f) A chimpanzee that, after a delay with no training on the materials, stacks crates to reach suspended fruit and succeeds on the first attempt: higher faculty, insight.

A colleague now brings a new case to the lab. Bumblebees were trained to roll a small ball to a goal in exchange for sugar water. After watching a trained demonstrator, some novices solve the task from a starting position they were never rewarded from, and a few take a shorter route than the one demonstrated. Throughout, the ball and goal remain visible, and a plausible lower account decomposes the performance into reinforced motor routines plus perceptual generalization.

Predict how Arden would rule on the bee case if she stays consistent with her record, and diagnose the rule she is running. I want exactly five things: (1) the candidate rules that fit all six of her verdicts; (2) what each candidate rule requires for the bee case; (3) your best prediction of her verdict, with the closest earlier case named; (4) the reason for preferring that prediction over the alternative; (5) one further case that would cleanly separate the leading rules. Reply with exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
