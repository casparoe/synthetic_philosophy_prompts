I have been trying to get my views about higher-order evidence into a stable package, and I would like an audit of whether the following six commitments hang together. I hold all of them; I am not sure where the strain is, and I would rather you locate it than defend the individual items.

1. Enkrasia. It is always a rational defect to have a doxastic state that is misaligned with one's own assessment of what one's evidence supports on the whole. Being 0.7 confident in a proposition while thinking that the evidence supports only 0.5 is a failure of rationality, not merely an awkward psychological condition.

2. Higher-order evidence counts. If I learn that a judgment of mine was formed under conditions known to impair judgment—severe fatigue, intoxication, a recognized bias—I should reduce my confidence in that judgment's content, even when a careful re-examination of the original first-order evidence leaves my reading of that evidence unchanged.

3. Conciliationism. Peer disagreement is a special case of higher-order evidence. Learning that someone I regard as my equal in intelligence, information, and conscientiousness has reached a different verdict should make me substantially less confident in my verdict, for the same kind of reason that learning my judgment was impaired should.

4. Self-trust at the limit. There is a limit to self-discounting. A rational agent cannot be driven to the point of treating her own best judgment as merely one more possibly corrupt instrument among others; at some point the deliverance of my own careful reasoning must be the stopping point, because there is no further court of appeal.

5. Bayesian closure. The rational response to new information—including information that bears on my reliability rather than on the world—should be representable as probability updating. A piece of evidence's being higher-order should not call for a separate kind of epistemic rule.

6. A considered case verdict. If I work through a problem while mildly oxygen-deprived and then learn of the condition, even though the reasoning still strikes me as compelling on review, I think the rational response is to become substantially less confident in the answer while continuing to find the first-order reasoning compelling.

Please go through these six and identify which pairs are in genuine tension and which merely look as though they conflict. For each genuine tension, say how severe it is, which commitment would have to give, and what the cheapest repair would be and what it would cost. For each merely apparent tension, say briefly why it dissolves. Discriminate: do not flag every pair that can be made to sound inconsistent.
