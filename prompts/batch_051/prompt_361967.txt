I follow a standing epistemic rule for how I handle metaphorical speech when I am deciding what to believe: I do not update on a metaphor until the speaker is willing to say, without the metaphor, what she is claiming. When a friend says her depression is a heavy fog, or an economist says the housing market is a bubble, I let the image move me — it changes what I notice and which questions I ask — but I add to my beliefs only the literal content, and I treat everything else as prompting rather than as asserted information. My colleague says this is not epistemic hygiene but a kind of tone-deafness: metaphors, she argues, carry cognitive content of their own, often true and often organized into systematic patterns — arguments as war, time as a commodity, organizations as machines — that shape how we reason. By treating the metaphorical residue as mere effect, she says, I refuse to update on real information and I grant literal speech an unearned privilege. I cannot stop every conversation to demand a paraphrase, so any replacement rule has to be usable on the fly. Who is right? When is my policy defensible, when does it lead me astray, and is there a version of it that keeps the suspicion of a special metaphorical meaning without making me blind to what metaphorical speech teaches?
