I am a novelist, and I want to get the philosophy behind a plot device exactly right. The device is a variation on the standard Frankfurt-style case, and I would like you to work through how the standard analyses change under the variation.

Begin with the canonical case. Jones is deliberating whether to kill Smith. Black, a neurosurgeon, has implanted a device that monitors Jones's incipient decisions; Black can read the sign reliably and can override Jones if necessary. If Jones were about to choose to spare Smith, Black would intervene and cause Jones to decide to kill. In the actual course of events, Jones decides on his own to kill, and Black does nothing. The familiar compatibilist reading is that Jones is morally responsible for the killing even though he could not have done otherwise.

Now change one feature: the monitor is not perfectly reliable. It reads the incipient decision correctly with probability r, for 0 < r < 1, and gives the opposite reading with probability 1 − r. Black's policy is the same in form as in the canonical case: he intervenes and forces the decision to kill if and only if the monitor reads "about to spare"; he does nothing if the monitor reads "about to kill." In the actual run, Jones's incipient decision is to kill, the monitor reads "kill" correctly — as it does with probability r — Black does nothing, and Jones kills.

I want the analysis done separately for two versions:

- Version A: Jones knows nothing about Black or the device.
- Version B: Jones knows everything the reader knows about the device and the policy, including the exact value of r.

For each version, please give a stepwise analysis of these questions:

1. Does Jones, in the actual run, lack the alternative possibility of choosing to spare? Be explicit about which modal base you are using — the actual past and the laws, the agent's capacities, or the agent's available strategies — and about the two directions in which the noisy monitor can err, because the two errors do different work.
2. How robust is the standard compatibilist verdict? Should the case do the same work for every r, or does the strength of the claim that Jones could not have done otherwise degrade as r falls?
3. Does the incompatibilist fare better or worse than in the canonical case, and is the answer different in Version A and Version B?
4. In Version B, state the best available strategy for Jones if he wishes to spare, with its probability of success. Then say whether that probability is sufficient to count as "could have done otherwise" in the sense that matters to the principle of alternative possibilities, and why.

The practical stakes for my novel are these: if the safeguard that would have stopped a character was 92 percent reliable rather than perfect, can she truthfully say afterward that she never really had a choice? I am not looking for a verdict so much as for the modal and probabilistic structure that the trial scene should respect.
