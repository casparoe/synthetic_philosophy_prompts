I'm the chair of what our hospital calls the Access and Revenue Committee — basically the person who has to sign off whenever the business office are trying to take something patients currently get for free, by waiting, or by medical need, and turn it into something you can pay for. Every month or two they bring us a proposal: a concierge tier that lets people skip the line for primary care, a deal with a plasma company that pays donors, a scheme to sell priority scheduling for elective surgery, paying volunteers to enroll in clinical trials, things like that. I need a reusable decision procedure, not a fresh gut reaction each time that just ends up back-rationalizing whatever the CFO wants.

I know there's a whole philosophical literature on commodification and repugnant markets, and I've heard people throw around terms like corruption of a good, blocked exchanges, and noxious markets, but I can't turn any of that into a checklist. Please write me the procedure my committee could actually run on a proposal. It should ask its questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, say where the procedure stops with a hard no, say where it says "allow but regulate" rather than ban, and flag the steps that are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure — the places where a person just has to decide something about values. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle.

Constraints: the committee meets monthly for two hours, the proposals are usually lucrative and the system runs on thin margins, the general counsel and the chief medical officer both needs to sign off, and my memo to them has to fit on one page. The procedure should separate three kinds of objection: "this will predictably harm or exploit people," "this changes what the thing means or how the people involved are valued," and "this just feels gross but we can't say why." If those should be treated differently, the procedure should say so, and it should be honest about when a "no" is really a fairness objection rather than a commodification one.

Then run the procedure on two or three concrete proposals and show the answers. Use these: (1) a concierge primary-care tier where patients pay an annual fee for same-day appointments and longer visits, with a slice of the fee earmarked for charity care; (2) a policy where patients can pay to move up the queue for elective surgery, medical urgency always trumps payment, and the fee is waived when delay causes serious pain or disability; and (3) a program that pays healthy volunteers substantially above the usual rate to finish enrollment in an early-phase drug trial. Make sure at least one of these is a case where the procedure tells the committee to do something it would rather not do — that's how I'll know the procedure is doing any work rather than flattering our existing instincts.
