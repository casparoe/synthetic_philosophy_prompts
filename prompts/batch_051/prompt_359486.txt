I teach an undergraduate course in the philosophy of mathematics, and in two weeks I have a two-hour seminar for sixteen students. Most of them can follow a modest proof, but few have had the experience of watching a proof's concepts shift under the pressure of counterexamples. I would like you to design a structured, runnable classroom activity that lets them feel, before it is named, one of the central claims of Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations: that mathematical knowledge develops in a quasi-empirical way, with conjectures, proofs and counterexamples interacting so that a counterexample is often accommodated by stretching concepts or strengthening definitions rather than by simply abandoning the conjecture. Please build the session around a simple paper-and-pencil system with a fertile supply of apparent counterexamples — a combinatorial or geometric one is fine — but avoid the usual Euler polyhedron case, because I want to use that in the debrief as the historical point of comparison rather than as the live material. Specify the groups and roles, the sequence of moves, any low-stakes scoring that will make students invest in their definitions, the materials, and a realistic timeline for the two hours.

The crucial constraint is that the participants should run into the problem themselves through what the rules force them to do, not through what I explain afterwards; for example, they should be made to decide whether to exclude a troublesome example as 'not really' the kind of thing they meant, to retract the conjecture, or to modify the definitions, and they should have to live with the consequences before those options are given Lakatos's labels. The debrief should map particular moments in the exercise onto notions such as monster-barring, exception-barring, lemma-incorporation and proof-generated concepts, and should say what participants should come away seeing about mathematical rigour and change. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — for instance, students treating a refuted conjecture as settled too quickly and losing the productive tension — and how to steer those problems when they occur. Close with an honest note about which feature of Lakatos's picture a classroom simulation cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply. Please write the entire answer, including any student handouts, in British English.
