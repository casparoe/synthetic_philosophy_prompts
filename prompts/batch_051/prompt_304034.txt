Something has always bugged me. If two people get into a fight, everyone treats the question of who threw the first punch as important: the one who swings first carries a special kind of blame, even if the other had been provoking them for months. But move the question up the scale to the Cold War, and lots of historians will say “who started it” is the wrong question to ask. At that scale, they’ll tell you, the causes are structural, both sides misread the other, and hunting for a first punch is naive or just propaganda. And yet those same historians don’t write as if nobody did anything—they still single out certain choices, a speech, a blockade, a rigged election, as the points where the whole thing turned. So there’s a living asymmetry: between two people “who started it” is a perfectly respectable question, but between two superpowers it’s faintly disreputable. Does anything actually justify that asymmetry, or are we just inconsistent? I want the strongest principle that draws the line between the two cases, and an honest verdict on whether it holds up. Stress-test it against a bitter divorce where the causes are also structural, both spouses misread each other for years, and there were a dozen provocations before the final blow-up: is “who started it” still a good question there? And if the principle breaks, is the historian’s distaste for the question just a sophisticated way of declining to assign blame?
