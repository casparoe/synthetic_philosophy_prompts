I write ensemble novels, and I keep turning over a parallel between something from social choice theory and a problem I actually lose sleep over as a novelist. I want to know whether its deep or just a cute analogy.

Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, as I understand it, says roughly this: if every individual has preferences over lotteries that satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and society as a whole also has preferences satisfying those axioms, plus a Pareto condition — if everyone weakly prefers prospect A to B, society weakly prefers A to B — then society's preferences have to be representable as a weighted sum of individual utilities. Not maximin, not help-the-worst-off-first, not lexicographic concern for whichever character has been wronged most; any nonlinear way of aggregating breaks one of the axioms.

Here's the parallel. In an ensemble novel, each character comes with wants over how the plot could resolve — who gets the house, who ends up with whom, who dies. I, as the author, have to pick one resolution, and that choice reveals whose wants counted. Harsanyi's theorem seems to say: if the novel's overall fairness is to be coherent in the same way, and if it respects each character's preferences, then the novel has to assign weights to characters and choose the ending that maximizes the weighted sum. This matches my experience. A book that refuses to decide whose story it is, that tries to honor everyone without trade-offs, reads the way a social welfare function that's maximin in early chapters and utilitarian later would violate the independence axiom — it feels inconsistent even when each scene works on its own.

But the breaks in the analogy seem large. Characters don't rank probability distributions over plot outcomes; readers don't evaluate endings the way they'd evaluate gambles; and authors aren't bound by Pareto — a tragic ending can knowingly violate a character's preferences and still feel fair, maybe because narrative fairness tracks desert or dramatic necessity rather than ex ante welfare.

So: is this a real structural match, or am I just pattern-matching? If it's real, what exactly transfers from Harsanyi to craft — for instance, does it commit me to saying that an always-rescue-the-underdog narrative rule is structurally the maximin case and therefore unstable in the same way? And if it's superficial, where precisely does the mapping fail first: on the character side, on the author side, or on the idea that a novel has a single social-utility-like function at all?
