I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I've been pulled onto a working group that's redesigning how our university awards small internal research grants. I'm the only one with any social choice background, so a proposal that came up last week is giving me serious premise-versus-conclusion-voting flashbacks, but the group wants a practical read on it, not a literature review. Here's the scheme. Stage one: a panel of faculty reads each application and votes on exactly two things — P1, "if this project succeeded, the result would be genuinely significant for the field," and P2, "this project has a nontrivial chance of succeeding within the grant period." They are not allowed to attach any overall recommendation. Stage two: a different body (honestly, it could just be a script) receives only the tallies for each proposal — the yes/no counts on P1 and P2 — and applies a mechanical rule: fund every proposal where both premises passed a majority, in the order the applications were submitted, until the pot runs out. Nobody, at any point, votes on "should we fund this proposal?" The rationale is that asking for an overall verdict invites logrolling and grandstanding, and hiding the applications from the second stage stops them from second-guessing the experts. I'm suppose to write the memo on how this would actually behave. In particular: what do the stage-one reviewers do once they realize their two yes/no votes are the only levers, especially when the same people are also allowed to submit their own applications with a narrow recusal? What gets funded that a straight up-or-down vote would have killed, and vice versa — including cases where the majority that endorses P1 is not the majority that endorses P2? What will the stage-two people do with just a pair of tallies, and where does the scheme get gamed first? Is there a minimally invasive fix that keeps the anti-logrolling rationale without the whole thing collapsing?
