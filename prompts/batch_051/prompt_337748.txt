For a term paper I have been trying to work out whether Allais-type preferences can be rationally defended, and a supervisor has left me with an argument I cannot escape. Here is the setup, with the amounts made explicit so there is no ambiguity.

First pair of gambles:
A. $1,000,000 for sure.
B. 89% chance of $1,000,000; 10% chance of $5,000,000; 1% chance of $0.

Second pair of gambles:
C. 11% chance of $1,000,000; 89% chance of $0.
D. 10% chance of $5,000,000; 90% chance of $0.

Many thoughtful people prefer A to B and D to C; I do too.

The argument against that pattern runs as follows:

(1) The independence axiom — the sure-thing principle applied to risky gambles — says that if two gambles behave identically on one branch of the resolution and differ only on another branch, the choice between them should be settled by the branch on which they differ; the common branch is decision-irrelevant.

(2) Each Allais pair can be redescribed as a common branch plus an embedded choice. In pair 1 the common 89% branch pays $1,000,000 whichever option you pick; in pair 2 the common 89% branch pays $0 whichever option you pick. On the remaining 11% branch, A and C both deliver $1,000,000 for certain, while B and D both deliver a 10/11 chance of $5,000,000 against a 1/11 chance of $0.

(3) By (1), the preference between the certain $1,000,000 and the sub-lottery (10/11: $5,000,000; 1/11: $0) must be the same wherever that embedded choice occurs.

(4) But preferring A to B prefers the certain $1,000,000 in the pair-1 framing, and preferring D to C prefers the sub-lottery in the pair-2 framing — the opposite ranking of the same embedded choice, with only the common branch changed.

(5) The Allais pattern is therefore inconsistent in just the way rationality forbids: the common branch, which is by hypothesis irrelevant, flips the ranking. So anyone with the standard Allais preferences is irrational.

(6) That conclusion seems to prove too much. The pattern is not an error made once or twice; it is the most common response among thoughtful people, and each individual choice feels defensible. An argument whose conclusion is that a central class of intuitive risk attitudes is pathological looks like a reason to distrust the axiom rather than a discovery about the pattern.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If forced, I would rather give up the sure-thing principle than the Allais choices: tell me what I am paying for that, and in particular which argument or consideration would most likely change your verdict, or where your diagnosis is most likely to fail.
