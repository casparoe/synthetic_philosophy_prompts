Since retiring from a career in molecular biology, I have been educating myself in the philosophy of mind, and I recently joined a reading group whose current topic is the moral status of machine intelligences. In our last meeting, the question was whether a hypothetical, neuron-by-neuron emulation of a human brain—implemented on a silicon substrate and reproducing the brain's signaling patterns with perfect fidelity—would be conscious and would possess the moral status of a person. One participant ended the debate by insisting that the substrate doesn't matter: what matters is the organization of the information processing, and anyone who thinks otherwise is merely clinging to a carbon chauvinism. I have spent a lifetime in a field where the substrate of a process—the exact molecules, membrane environment, and local biophysical conditions—is very often doing real causal work, so the sentence struck me as either banal or astonishingly strong, and I could not tell which.

I would like you to dissect that one-liner for me. What are the distinct precise claims that "the substrate doesn't matter" might be expressing, arranged from the trivial to the radical? For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, or a claim we should reject, and say how much philosophical or empirical weight it can bear. In particular, identify which reading would be needed to support the move from "the emulation reproduces the same neural computations" to "the emulation is conscious and a moral patient," and indicate which readings, if any, would be vulnerable to what a biologist knows about how much substrate details matter in living systems. I am less interested in a single preferred interpretation than in a menu of readings with verdicts, so that I can return to the group and show which version of the slogan is carrying the argument.
