You are a researcher working on path dependence, increasing returns, and technology standards. Think carefully, and before giving any verdict, do some preliminary work: restate the argument in your own words, make its premises explicit, and identify which of them are load-bearing.

The classic lock-in argument associated with Paul David and W. Brian Arthur runs as follows. Where adoption of a technology exhibits increasing returns—network effects, compatibility with an installed base, learning-by-doing, coordinated expectations—early, small advantages can compound. Once a standard becomes dominant, each individual user often has a private incentive to stay with it even when a different standard would be collectively better: retraining and retooling are costly, complementary tools and files encode the old standard, and no individual can coordinate a mass switch. The result is that the market can become locked into an inferior standard, and the outcome is a product of historical accident rather than a reliable tendency toward the best technology. The canonical illustration is QWERTY, adopted in the typewriter era and often claimed to be inferior to later layouts; the claim of marked inferiority is itself empirically contested.

Now consider a specific development. The keyboard is now software-defined. On most devices the layout can be changed through a settings toggle or a downloaded package; the physical keyboard gives no special support to QWERTY; autocorrect, predictive text, swipe input, and dictation weaken the link between layout and typing performance; and a single platform vendor could, in principle, switch the default layout for hundreds of millions of users overnight or run large-scale randomized comparisons of layouts. Suppose that, despite all this, QWERTY remains overwhelmingly dominant for English-language text entry.

Does the classic lock-in argument survive this development? In particular:

1. Reconstruct the argument and state exactly what it must assume about switching costs and about who must coordinate for a switch to occur.

2. Which premise does the software-defined keyboard development actually put pressure on, and which premises does it leave untouched?

3. If QWERTY persists even when hardware switching costs are near zero, does that strengthen the lock-in argument—because lock-in now holds without physical capital—or does it weaken the argument, because the story may have been doing its work through physical switching costs, and what remains is a different phenomenon such as a focal convention, human-capital lock-in, or default bias? Defend one side.

4. Offer a repaired version of the argument if one is available, and be explicit about whether it still supports the strong conclusion that markets lock in genuinely inferior standards, or only the weaker conclusion that historical accident selects among roughly equivalent conventions. What evidence would distinguish the strong conclusion from the weak one?

5. Finally, assess the platform angle: when a single gatekeeper can change everyone's layout at once, is the coordination problem solved, or has it migrated from users to the gatekeeper's incentives?

Engage with the mechanism-level details rather than giving a generic verdict.
