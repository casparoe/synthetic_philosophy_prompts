I'm a software engineer, not a trained philosopher, and I help run a monthly "systems and ideas" workshop at my company. The group is 16 adults from engineering, design, and product; they're comfortable with adversarial games and technical material but have no philosophy background. We have two hours, a room with movable furniture, and the ability to print materials.

I'd like a runnable tabletop game for one of these sessions that makes people feel, before anyone names it, the force of a particular problem about the Turing test and behavioral criteria for mindedness. The problem I keep going back to is this. Fix a maximum length for a conversation, in terms of both number of exchanges and characters per message. Then the number of possible transcripts within that bound is finite, though astronomical. In principle, a finite lookup table could store a sensible response for every possible thing an interrogator might type in a session of that length. Such a table would be behaviorally indistinguishable from a thoughtful agent throughout that session. It would pass the bounded Turing test. Yet nothing in the table understands anything, and nothing it does is reasoning. The only thing standing in the way of extending the trick to longer conversations seems to be storage and search, not intelligence. I want the participants to collide with that fact rather than hear it, and to leave with a live sense that a bounded behavioral test can be passed by something mindless, and that scaling up the bound only demands more storage, so behavior alone doesn't settle whether any thinking is going on.

Please design the activity with that as the target insight. However you set up the rules, make sure the mechanics themselves embed two things: a hard bound on the length of the interaction and the message space, and a phase in which one team has to precompute all responses by writing an actual table or decision script before seeing any input from an interrogator, rather than responding live. A modest finite question pool is fine; the small scale is the point. The activity should rotate roles so that everyone spends time building the precomputed agent and everyone spends time trying to unmask one. Include a warm-up, one or two escalating rounds, a scoring system without real money, a materials and setup list, and a timeline. If you add a second round, one good escalation is to loosen a constraint and let the players watch what happens to the size of the table.

The facilitator should give only procedural instructions before and during play. No lecture, and no mention of Turing tests, lookup tables, minds, or the problem in advance. The idea should surface in what the players do: ideally they notice on their own that their agent isn't intelligent and yet still won or nearly won, and that patching a hole in the table starts to feel less like teaching it and more like adding rows.

The debrief should map specific moments in the game to the conceptual pieces: the moment an interrogator was taken in, the moment the builders realized they were filling in rows for inputs they hadn't anticipated, the escalation where the table blew up in size but not in kind. It should also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time we run it, for example, teams smuggling real adaptive skill into the precomputed responses, interrogators leaning on stereotypes about what humans sound like, or someone objecting that the precomputing team is the real mind behind the table, and how to handle each of those. And be explicit about the feature of the idea the exercise cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply: a table at the scale of a real Turing test is far too large for any team to exhibit, so the game trades on a miniature case, and the debrief has to make the leap in scale vivid without pretending the activity demonstrated it.
