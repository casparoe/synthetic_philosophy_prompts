I am drafting a scene in which a former military officer argues that deference to legitimate authority can never be a virtue. I want her reasoning to be genuinely strong rather than a straw man, because her interlocutor must answer it seriously. I have a nagging sense that one step is too quick, but I cannot locate it. Tell me whether the argument is valid, and if it fails, identify the exact step at which it fails and why.

(1) A legitimate authority's orders give the person subject to it a content-independent reason to comply: the reason arises from the order's source, not from the merits of the act ordered.
(2) When a person has a content-independent reason to comply, no assessment of the merits of the act is needed for her compliance to be rationally justified.
(3) If no assessment of the merits is needed for justification, then her own judgment about the merits—including the judgment that the act is seriously wrong—is rationally beside the point.
(4) If her judgment that the act is seriously wrong is rationally beside the point, she ought not to let that judgment override the order.
(5) But an agent who ought not to let her judgment that an act is seriously wrong override an order has surrendered her conscience, and the surrender of conscience is incompatible with virtue.
(6) Therefore, in complying with legitimate authority, an agent necessarily acts without virtue in that respect.
(7) Hence deference to legitimate authority is never a virtue.
