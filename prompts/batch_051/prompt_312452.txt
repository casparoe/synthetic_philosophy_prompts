You are a philosopher of mind who works on the unity of consciousness, and you have been asked to scrutinize an apparent inconsistency in how ordinary and legal thought counts minds and persons in neurologically unusual bodies. Think through the issue carefully, and begin by brainstorming the candidate criteria that might justify the judgments before you defend the best one.

Consider two well-known kinds of case. First, an adult with severe epilepsy undergoes a complete callosotomy. The surgery does not create a visibly new person; afterward, however, laboratory testing can reveal two streams of perception and intention that are imperfectly integrated. One hand may unbutton a shirt while the other buttons it, and the speaking hemisphere sometimes offers a fluent but apparently confabulated explanation for an action that was cued primarily to the silent hemisphere. Yet almost everyone—including those watching the experiment—continues to treat the patient as a single person: one name, one legal identity, one locus of responsibility, with disunified behavior treated as fragmentation inside one mind. Second, consider conjoined twins with two heads, two separately functioning brains, and one body shared from roughly the neck down. Here the ordinary judgment runs the other way. Each head has its own perceptions, temperament, and plans, and almost no one treats the pair as one person with a divided mind; they are regarded as two distinct people who happen to share organs and limbs.

This is the asymmetry. In the first case, bodily unity, a continuous life history, and continuity with the pre-surgical person lead us to resist multiplying persons; in the second, psychological distinctness leads us to multiply persons despite the shared body. But the one-body-one-person principle cannot cover both cases, and a one-stream-of-consciousness-one-person principle would seem to push toward treating the split-brain patient as two. Is there a defensible criterion—degree of neural integration, a threshold of unified agency, continuity of personhood over time, or something else—that actually explains and justifies the asymmetry? Or is ordinary thought merely counting heads and rationalizing afterward? Present the strongest candidate justification, test it against nearby variants (for example, conjoined twins whose two brains become progressively more integrated, or a split-brain patient whose hemispheres develop opposed religious commitments and life plans), and state clearly whether the asymmetry should be retained, revised, or abandoned.
