Karl Friston's 2010 Nature Reviews Neuroscience article, The Free-Energy Principle: A Unified Brain Theory?, claims that adaptive agents minimize the long-term average of surprise, and that this imperative unifies perception, action, and learning. The claim on which my question turns is Friston's account of action: he writes that action reduces to suppressing sensory prediction errors, because changing the world changes sensory input rather than changing the agent's internal model. Movement, in this framework, is enslaved by proprioceptive predictions; the brain does not send motor commands in the traditional sense but rather generates a prediction about the body's state, and the body then moves to abolish the resulting proprioceptive prediction error.

I am drawn to this theory because it is exactly the kind of systematic picture I usually want. One quantity, variational free energy, orders perception, action, learning, attention, and even certain forms of psychopathology under a single imperative. It yields a formalism, makes quantitative predictions, and unifies otherwise separate brain theories. But I also have a firm pre-theoretical conviction that my actions are guided by intentions and desires: I reach for the cup because I want the cup, not because my brain is minimizing a proprioceptive prediction error. The free-energy account seems to make the goal-directedness of action an epiphenomenon of my priors, while the phenomenology, and a long tradition in the philosophy of action, puts intention first. This is not a puzzle I am willing to settle by simply picking a side.

I want to know what it would cost for the intuition to be debunked and dropped, what it would cost for the theory to be amended or abandoned, and whether there is a reading of acting intentionally and a reading of minimizing prediction error on which both survive. In particular, evaluate whether the intuition is a scope-insensitivity artefact or something more like a datum that a theory of action must respect. Present the strongest versions of both sides, and then give a negotiated verdict that is not just a compromise.
