I'm a philosophy PhD student working on population ethics, mostly on the person-affecting/impersonal divide, and I co-run a small grad-run interview series in my department. In a few weeks we're recording an episode with a philosopher I'll call Dr. M, who has built a reputation on a deliberately bullet-biting line: she is a total utilitarian, and she accepts the repugnant conclusion not as a bitter implication she tolerates but as something we should actively affirm once we stop trusting the intuitions we form about very large populations. Her signature move when someone pushes the standard Z-versus-A case is to say the revulsion is just scope insensitivity plus an unrepresentative mental image of lives barely worth living, and she usually follows up by asking people to actually do the arithmetic of total wellbeing. I've watched her do this twice in Q&As and she is very good at turning every objection into a request to be more numerate.

I'd love to go in with questions that don't let that framing do all the work. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for me? I want them to build on each other so that her earlier answers constrain the later ones, and I want each question to come with a follow-up that anticipates the answer she's most likely to give, rather than a generic follow-up. The set should definitely include: (1) a question pressing on what lives barely worth living actually means — where the zero level of wellbeing sits, how we could ever know a life is above rather than below it, and whether total wellbeing is even the kind of quantity that can be added up across lives; (2) a question about the person-affecting move, the idea that it matters who exists and not just how much welfare the people who exist have, and why she thinks that move doesn't save the anti-Z intuition; (3) a question on same-number versus different-number tradeoffs, since a lot of the repugnance seems to track the difference between making people better off and making more people; (4) a question on the sadistic-conclusion style of case, where the better population is one in which every single person is worse off than every person in the smaller alternative, and whether she owns that verdict too; and (5) a question on continuity or lexical thresholds — whether there is any number of merely positive lives she would refuse to trade against a nontrivial improvement in one excellent life, and if she draws no such line, why she is comfortable with a view that never lets a quality improvement trump a sufficiently large quantity of mediocrity. Please end with a question about what could actually change her mind: what empirical finding or theoretical result would make her abandon the view, and if nothing would, is the view still evaluable at all.
