I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I help run a small public-facing philosophy blog. We want to make one of those "which theory of X do you actually hold?" quizzes, and the topic is origins: could you have come from a different sperm and egg, or would that just have been a different person (a sibling, or no one)? I want the quiz to be about people's actual reactions to cases, not about whether they recognize the terms "origin essentialism" or "animalism."

Could you build me an eight-to-ten question quiz with a scoring key? Each question should be a concrete scenario with two or three answer options, written so a smart non-philosopher can answer without knowing the labels. I'd like it to sort readers into something like these positions: (a) gamete essentialism (you essentially come from the particular sperm and egg you came from), (b) genetic-sequence essentialism (what matters is being that particular genome, however it got assembled), (c) an organismal view (you are the organism, so your origin is the zygote or early embryo you developed from, and later psychological changes don't touch your identity), (d) a psychological/narrative continuity view (origin matters little; continued memory, character, and projects do), and (e) a deflationary view (there's no deep fact about which origin would or wouldn't have been you). If you need to add or split a position, go ahead, but those five should appear.

Please include these cases, since I think they're what pushes the positions apart:
- an IVF mix-up where two couples' embryos are swapped and a child is gestated and raised by the non-genetic parents;
- monozygotic twins (same sperm, same egg, one zygote that splits) — make readers who said "the same sperm and egg is what matters" say whether the twins are the same person, and notice what that does to their view;
- a future clone made from one of your somatic cells (mostly your nuclear genome, but a different egg, womb, and childhood);
- the "different sperm won the race" counterfactual — if a different sperm had fertilized the same egg, would the resulting child be you, a sibling, or is the question itself confused;
- an embryo frozen for 25 years and then born (same zygote, much later birth);
- total amnesia plus a radical personality change;
- a rare tetragametic chimera with two cell lines, hence two different genomes in one organism; and
- a hypothetical person created with exactly your genome but from unrelated gametes (same genetic sequence, different origin).

The questions shouldn't just ask "is it the same person?" every time; vary whether the question is about whether someone is you, whether a different choice by the parents would have produced you, whether an origin is essential, etc. Keep the answer options plain-spoken, like "same person," "different person," "there's no determinate fact."

Then give the scoring key. For each position, give the pattern of answers that gets a reader there, and include a "leaning" rule so someone with one or two off-pattern answers still gets classified as leaning rather than as incoherent. Also flag the pairs the quiz can't reliably seperate (for instance, if the gamete and organismal views mostly produce the same answers, tell me that and say which question is the separator). Note any answer combinations that look logically empty or unstable rather than occupied by a real view.

And one more thing, which matters to me: after the scoring key, say which of your assignments are steadiest and which are most likely to misfire — e.g., if a large chunk of respondents end up with a mix that matches no named position, or if the clone case does ambiguous work. Then say what concrete pattern of answers or what argument would change your mind about where to draw one of the dividing lines (for example, merging the deflationary view with another position, or moving the line on the genetic view).

Audience is blog readers with no philosophy background, so keep the question text jargon-free, and don't cite anything or lean on Kripke unless it's truly necessary (and then explain it in a clause).
