I'm the records secretary for my state's rare bird records committee, which is the group that decides whether a reported sighting goes into the official state checklist. We get fifty or sixty reports a year, we meet four times a year, and every call matters: a confirmed breeding pair can get a site protected, and researchers use our records, so a false 'yes' and a false 'no' are both bad, definately. Nobody on the committee has any real training in probability or statistics, and our current method is basically gut votes.

I've been thinking about this on my own for a couple years now, and I've come to trust two ideas. One is that almost no evidence is all-or-nothing — a birder saying 'I saw one' shouldn't push us to 100% and shouldn't leave us exactly where we started; we should move partway toward the claim, by an amount that depends on how much the report should change our minds. The other is that when a piece of evidence doesn't discriminate between two possibilities — like a blurry photo that's exactly as consistent with the rare bird as with a common lookalike — it shouldn't change their relative odds. My committee mostly thinks I'm overcomplicating this and we should just vote.

Please write me a decision procedure my committee could actually run. It should be a checklist in plain English that a bunch of tired birders can get through in one evening. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to move on, where the procedure should stop with a hard no or a hard yes, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the step is a judgment call and no rule can settle it. Be honest about which steps feel mechanical but are really guesses. In particular, I suspect the procedure will need a number for how much to trust a report, and the thought of us just picking that number feels like the old gut vote wearing a fake mustache — so please say outright where that number comes from, and if the honest answer is that we have to pick it ourselves, say that too.

Then run it on three reports: (1) a one-line report of an extremely rare bird from a careful observer, no photo, no other witnesses; (2) a blurry photo equally compatible with a rare species and a common lookalike; (3) a crisp audio recording that a respected expert says is probably — but not certainly — a species never recorded in the state before. Make sure at least one of these comes out in a way we'd rather not accept, and tell me which step forces that.

At the very end, after everything else, put one final line that summarizes the whole procedure in a single sentence.
