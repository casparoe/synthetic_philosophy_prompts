An individual acquaintance of mine recently described acting on an inarticulate hunch — she accepted a job offer — and then, months later, when pressed to explain herself, reconstructing a meticulous cost–benefit analysis that made the decision look demonstrably correct. Asked whether that analysis was her reason for accepting, most of us would say no: the decision was not made for those reasons, and the reconstructed rationale is a rationalization rather than the real basis of her choice. We would hesitate to call the decision rational under that description until the rationale could be tied to something actually operative in her thinking at the time she chose.

Now apply the same standard to a group. A hiring committee votes first and writes the justification for its shortlist into the minutes afterward, assembling the rationale from scattered score-sheet comments and institutional priorities in a way that corresponds to no single member's actual reasoning. An appellate court issues an opinion whose reasoning is the product of drafting and negotiation among chambers and clerks, not the deliberative process of any one judge. In these cases most people are comfortable saying that the committee or the court had reasons for what it did, even though no individual reasoner ever held those reasons in that form. The rationale floats free of any reasoner — a reason without a reasoner, so to speak.

That looks like an asymmetry in ordinary judgment: individuals are credited with reasons only when the rationale was theirs in the relevant sense, while collectives are credited with reasons that no one ever actually thought through. Is there a principle that justifies the asymmetry, or is it an inconsistency we live with? The strongest candidate justifications I can imagine appeal to the absence of a group consciousness, the division of cognitive labor, or the different purposes served by attributing reasons to persons and to institutions, but each runs into trouble in nearby cases — a committee small enough for face-to-face agreement on a post hoc narrative, or an expert individual whose decision is genuinely shaped by considerations she could not have articulated until later. I want the strongest statement of the principle you can give, tested against such cases, and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or dropped.
