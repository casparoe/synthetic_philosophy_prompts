I'm throwing together a lecture on whether unending life would actually be desirable — the heaven/immortality unit — and I want to open with a poem instead of another slide. The problem I keep running into is that students read Williams on the Makropulos case as saying heaven would be boring in the way a bad party is boring: too many harps, nothing to do. But the stronger point is that the trouble is not any particular bad experience inside the endless life; it's that the sort of categorical desires that make a life worth continuing — a project, a person, a score to settle — are the kind of thing that can be used up, and they probably depend on the background fact that we don't have forever. I'd like a villanelle from the point of view of someone who has lived for several centuries, in the Makropulos situation, to get at this.

Here's the structural ask. The two refrains should begin as near-opposites: one expresses the old appetite for more life, the other expresses the flatness of having already lived it. Through the repetitions, the reader should gradually lose track of which one was which — the line that first read as hunger should start to read as weariness, and vice versa — so the villanelle's machinery, its returning lines, is itself doing the argument rather than decorating it. In the final quatrain, when the two refrains finally sit side by side, I want the ambiguity left unresolved: the speaker cant tell whether they are asking for more life or just can't find a reason to end it. That unresolved thing is, I think, the actual state the Makropulos case is meant to illuminate. Don't let the last two lines solve it; let the form hold the tension. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows Williams would nod, not wince.
