I'm a third-year undergrad taking a course on happiness and public policy, and I also sit on my university's student wellbeing committee, which advises the administration on how student services money gets spent. At our next meeting we're supposed to respond to a proposal from the administration to launch an annual student wellbeing survey modeled roughly on the World Happiness Report: students would rate their overall life satisfaction on a 0-10 ladder and answer questions about social support, freedom to make choices, generosity, and so on. The administration wants to set a public target of getting us into the top 20 of some national student wellbeing ranking by 2028, and tie part of the student services budget to year-on-year improvement in our score.

I get to draft the short memo that the committee will debate and vote on, and I'm supposed to come in with an actual recommendation. My gut is that once money is attached to a survey score, we'll start optimizing the number rather than student happiness — questions will get coached, students who are annoyed at the university will use the survey to vent, and the 0-10 scale probably tracks something like "how students feel about their life on the day they fill it out" rather than whether their wellbeing is actually improving. A friend of mine in the committee thinks I'm overcomplicating it: imperfect measure, but at least it forces the admin to pay attention, and right now there's no pressure on them at all. I dont fully buy that, but I also don't want to be the person who writes a memo that just shoots the whole thing down.

Could you write the memo for me? It should recommend a concrete course of action — adopt the survey as proposed, reject it, or adopt it with specific changes — with a timeline for whatever you recommend, and it needs to engage seriously with my friend's position rather than just restating the measurement critique. Keep it short enough to circulate before the meeting and clear enough that the committee can actually act on it.
