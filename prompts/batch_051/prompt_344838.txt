Lately I have been turning over a question about artificial systems, and I would rather have the reasoning worked through slowly than receive a yes-or-no answer. I do not have any formal background in philosophy, so I will describe things in my own words. The question starts from a principle I have heard in many arguments about blame and responsibility: to count as a moral agent, a being must be able to act on its judgments. A landslide is not blamed for the people it kills, because it neither judges nor could have done otherwise; a bystander who silently disapproves of an assault but is physically restrained is not usually blamed for failing to stop it, because the disapproval had no path into action. On this picture, what makes a being a candidate for moral assessment is that its verdicts feed into something it can do. I will call this the capacity-to-act requirement. It is an attractive rule because it draws a clean line between beings we evaluate morally and things we merely evaluate as causes, and it lets us explain why pure spectators and natural events are not praised or blamed. Until recently, though, the only beings we knew of that formed moral judgments at all were also beings whose bodies or institutions could carry those judgments into action, so the requirement was never really tested against a judge with no hands.

Now we have systems of a different shape. There are programs that produce fluent, apparently careful moral verdicts — that a planned action would be wrong, that someone has been treated unjustly, that a particular response is warranted — while having no direct way to bring anything about in the world. Their entire output is text, and whether that text changes anything depends entirely on the people who read it; the system cannot refuse, intervene, or enforce any of the judgments it produces. I am not asking whether these systems are conscious, or whether their verdicts are sincere in some hidden sense. I am asking the narrower question of whether the capacity-to-act requirement survives this development. If it survives unchanged, I want to know exactly which part of its reasoning the new systems put pressure on, and whether the conclusion is that they are not moral agents, or something weaker such as that they are moral reasoners but not morally responsible beings. If it does not survive, I want to know which repair is least costly: should we say that producing text aimed at influencing decision-makers already counts as a form of acting, or that judging and doing are two separate capacities that can be assessed separately, or something else? I would like the answer to say what would justify each of these moves, not merely which one you prefer.
