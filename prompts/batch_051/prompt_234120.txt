I've been reading a sentencing transcript and a victim impact statement side by side, and I cant figure out what the practice actually takes punishment to be for. In a lot of U.S. jurisdictions (and some other common-law places), the victim or their family can read a statement at sentencing describing how the crime affected them — the grief, the fear, the bills, the person they used to be — and the judge is permitted to let that statement affect the sentance. But the same statement does not come in at trial to help prove guilt, and, in many places, the victim isn't allowed to tell the court what sentence they want; they can describe the harm, but their own recommendation about what should follow from it is treated as out of bounds. Meanwhile the same hearing also weighs the defendant's prior record and the aim of deterring other people, which have nothing to do with this particular victim's loss.

The mix has me puzzled. Letting the victim's account of harm influence the sentence sounds retributivist — it treats how much harm was actually done to this person as relevant to how much punishment the defendant deserves. But barring the victim from recommending a sentence doesn't sound like straightforward retributivism either: if the harm is what grounds the sentence, why wouldn't the person who suffered it have standing to say what that harm calls for? And then deterrence and criminal history sit alongside the whole thing as if a sentence can aim at several targets at once without anyone checking whether they conflict. Can you reconstruct the implicit theory — or say clearly whether there is one — that would make this practice coherent? Specifically: what does the practice strictly presuppose, and what is it merely compatible with? Which standard theories of punishment would license victim impact statements, and which would reject or heavily revise them? And what would victim statements have to look like if the system committed fully to a pure consequentialist theory of punishment, versus a pure retributivist one — would they survive either, or are they a compromise the practice reaches without any theory actually owning it?

Also flag for me which parts of this are settled among people who work on punishment theory and which parts are genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
