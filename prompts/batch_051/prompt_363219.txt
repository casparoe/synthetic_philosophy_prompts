I'm a novelist — not a professional philosopher — but the chair of the philosophy department at the local college read one of my books, which has a philosophy professor as a supporting character, and decided that that made me qualified to teach a summer section of intro to philosophy. So here I am, grading short papers on infinite regresses. I set the students this question:

"Explain the difference between a vicious infinite regress and a benign one, and evaluate the following claim: a regress is vicious exactly when the demand that generates it is one the regress itself cannot satisfy, so that each step merely defers the needed explanation. Illustrate with at least two examples, one of them being the truth regress."

One student turned in this (I've cleaned up the spelling but kept everything else):

"The usual distinction is drawn in terms of whether the regress undermines what it was supposed to explain. In a vicious regress, the same problem reappears at every step: the thing invoked to do the explaining is of the very kind that stood in need of explanation, so the explanatory debt is never discharged. A standard case is the regress of instantiation: you posit a universal to explain what makes two things the same with respect to some property, but then you need a further universal to explain how each thing is tied to the first universal, and so on without end. By contrast, in a benign regress each step is fully fixed by an operation or rule, and the infinity is a harmless side effect rather than a blocked explanation. The truth regress — from 'p is true' to 'it is true that p is true' to 'it is true that it is true that p is true' and so on — is usually called benign, since every higher-order truth claim is generated automatically from the one before it and p's being true is already settled at the bottom.

Now, the claim in the question says viciousness is a matter of the regress being generated by an explanatory demand that the regress can't satisfy. I used to think this was right. But I now think it's equivalent to something simpler: a regress is vicious exactly when it does not terminate. An explanation that never reaches a stopping point is an explanation that has not been finished; so infinity itself is the problem. On this criterion, the truth regress is actually vicious, even though it is usually called benign. The fact that each step is automatic doesn't matter, because there is no last higher-order truth in virtue of which the truth of p is finally grounded. So the proposal in the question is too weak: it would let a non-terminating regress count as benign as long as the offending explanatory demand seems to be met at the first step. The right view is stricter — no final term, no explanation."
