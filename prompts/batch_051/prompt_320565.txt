I spent forty years doing physical chemistry, mostly reaction kinetics, and I've lately been reading the fairness-in-machine-learning stuff, especially the impossibility results. Here's a thought I cant get rid of. I've been calling it the 'bias invariant,' though I'm sure that name is taken or misleading. The idea is that the three-way tension between calibration, equalized false positive rates, and equal positive predictive value is really a conservation law. The conserved quantity is what I'd call surplus group signal: the part of the group label's association with the outcome that isn't already inside your legitimate features. If a model tries to satisfy any two of the three criteria, the surplus group signal doesnt go away—it just gets pushed into the third criterion, or into feature selection, or the score distribution, or which errors get made. I don't mean 'signal' literally, but the proofs have the shape of a balance equation: a few inequalities that only line up as equalities in degenerate cases, like equal base rates or a perfect predictor. So my question is whether this is a real concept or just a picture. Is there an actual conserved quantity in the math, with something like a symmetry behind it, or am I smuggling in the conclusion by calling it 'surplus'? Where does the conservation picture make false predictions—for example, does it wrongly forbid collecting better features from improving things for everyone at once? And is the 'degenerate case equals no net flow' analogy doing any work, or just decoration?
