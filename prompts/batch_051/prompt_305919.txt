Here's a practice I keep noticing and it bugs me. A city or national government will approve a megaproject—a subway line, an Olympics, a high-speed rail link—on the strength of a detailed engineering plan with a cost estimate and a schedule. The same government's own auditor or treasury sometimes has data showing that comparable projects in comparable places overrun by something like 40 percent on average and open years late. But the reference-class data never gets used to adjust the estimate. Then, when the overrun happens, the government dosent switch to the base-rate method; it commissions a new, even more detailed plan for finishing the rest, on a revised budget, based mostly on the same project-specific reasoning. And yet the same officials will happily use actuarial tables, flood maps, and other base-rate methods in other parts of their jobs.

So what theory of knowledge—or at least of how we know the future—does this practice presuppose? Does it only make sense if each project is seen as a unique case whose future is best read off its own internal logic, with similiar projects treated as not much evidence? Or is the practice actually compatible with the view that you should start from the reference class, and the real explanation is just incentives (consultants get paid for detailed plans, politicians want a low number to announce)?
