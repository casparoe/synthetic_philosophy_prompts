I teach philosophy, and every year my graduate seminar spends a week on infinite ethics. I've been fiddling with a variant of the standard toy case, and I'd like someone who works on this stuff to tell me how the usual moves hold up.

Here's the standard case as I teach it. A world is an infinite sequence of one-person lives indexed 0, 1, 2, ... . In world A every life is at welfare +1. In world B life 0 is at 0, and every later life is +1. Intuitively A is better than B: it's B with one life improved and nobody made worse. But the two simple ways my students reach for can't say that. The total sum diverges in both worlds, and a welfare-level count ties them: both have countably many +1 lives, and the single extra 0-life in B doesn't change the cardinality. Meanwhile pointwise dominance (X is better than Y iff X is at least as good as Y at every index and strictly better at some index) says A > B, and truncated sums (compare the sums of lives 0 through n) also put A ahead of B at every n.

Now my variant. Let C = (-1, +1, -1, +1, ...), with -1 at every even index. Let D = (+1, -1, +1, -1, ...), which is C shifted left one place, i.e. C with its first -1 life removed and everyone else moved down a slot. That looks like exactly the same kind of improvement as A over B. But if I compare C and D index by index, neither pointwise dominates: D is better at index 0, C is better at index 1, and so on. Truncated sums also go wonky: D's partial sum through index n is never less than C's, but they're equal at every odd n, so the truncated-sum rule gives only a weak preference for D, not a strict one. And welfare-level cardinality says C and D are tied, since both have countably many -1 lives and countably many +1 lives. So the rules that were friendly to the original case become shy in the variant.

I'm not after a survey of the liteature. Act as an expert on infinite ethics and formal aggregation, think carefully, and before committing, brainstorm the three or four candidate criteria a sharp student might propose (pointwise dominance, truncated sums, welfare-level cardinality, and anything else you think is natural), saying exactly what each one outputs in the original case and in the variant. Then, in several paragraphs: does the analysis that gave A > B actually survive the variant? What hidden assumption does the variant force into the open (I suspect it's something about a privileged way of lining up the lives, but I want it made precise)? And should we conclude that the original verdict was never fully earned, or only that we need to restrict which alignments between worlds count?

After the prose, give me a JSON summary with exactly these fields:

- "candidate_rules": a list of objects, each with "name", "verdict_on_A_vs_B", "verdict_on_C_vs_D", and "rationale"
- "best_analysis": a short string naming the rule or family of rules you think handles both cases best
- "hidden_assumption": a short string stating the assumption the variant exposes
- "revised_question": a one-sentence question the seminar should take up next

The JSON is a summary, not a substitute for the reasoning.
