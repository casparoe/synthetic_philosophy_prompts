It has often puzzled me that readers of fiction are in one respect almost infinitely flexible and in another respect strikingly rigid. We accept without hesitation a story that stipulates that in its world cats speak, that time runs backwards, or that every fourth day is simply missing, even though we believe none of these things is possible. We also accept stories in which characters hold moral views we find monstrous: a novel can depict a society that treats ritual human sacrifice as an honourable duty, and we follow it easily as a story about such a society. But when the narration itself appears to assert that the practice is right — that in this world the killing really is good, and not merely believed to be good by the characters — many readers refuse to go along. They say the story is badly written, or that the narrator must be unreliable, or that the fiction cannot be entered at all. The same readers who refuse this moral stipulation will happily accept, sometimes in the very same story, that gravity pulls upward.

I want to know whether this asymmetry is defensible. Is it a genuine feature of how fiction works, something about the special place of moral concepts in the imagination, or is it only a parochial unwillingness to imagine moralities unlike our own? Consider a nearby case: a story stipulates that a painting is ugly, or that a joke is not funny, and readers again resist taking the stipulation on board. Does the same explanation cover both resistances, and does it show that the asymmetry is principled rather than parochial? If the asymmetry cannot be defended, which side gives way: should we learn to imagine alien moral truths as readily as alien physics, or should we admit that the fiction with alien physics is not, after all, fully imagined?
