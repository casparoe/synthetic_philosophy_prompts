I'm a PhD student in evolutionary biology, and I've reached the point where my supervisor's gene's-eye view of everything is starting to grate. Every time I mention a field observation he reminds me that organisms are temporary vehicles and the replicator is what really matters. For our department's end-of-year magazine I want to write a satirical piece, and I think the best form would be a deadpan 'annual report to shareholders' written by an allele whose host has just died at a decent age, leaving behind a fair number of offspring and grand-offspring. The allele should review the host's life in corporate terms: birth as an IPO, growth and reproduction as return on investment, altruistic or grief-stricken behaviour recast as profit-seeking strategy, and death as an orderly liquidation once the body has stopped being useful for propagation. I'd like the comedy to come from getting the biology right—inclusive fitness, kin selection, parent-offspring conflict (the offspring's manipulative begging should read like a hostile takeover), perhaps senescence as depreciation or planned obsolescence—rather than from cheap personification. Could you aim for about 700 words, formatted as numbered items, in the mildly self-congratulatory voice of a middle manager in a genome? The satire should target the gene's-eye view itself, specifically the way it can make organisms disappear from their own stories, but it should be the kind of thing a Dawkinsian would recognise as fair and occassionally painful.
