I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am working on a feature about what it means to understand a proof rather than merely to execute the procedure the proof justifies. Here is the case I want you to reason through. Suppose that, starting next academic year, every university mathematics department adopts the following rule for its undergraduate major: for the first two years, students are trained exclusively to apply theorems and carry out techniques, and whenever a student asks why a theorem is true, the instructor gives only the scripted reply that this comes later; in the third year, proofs are introduced from first principles in courses that assume no prior exposure to proof. Course content, examinations, and total instructional time are otherwise the same as in a standard proof-early curriculum. Give me your committed prediction of how a cohort graduating under this rule differs from a standard cohort both in what the phrase understanding a proof comes to mean to them and in how well their understanding transfers to unfamiliar problems. Present exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. In each item, state one definite predicted difference, identify the mechanism producing it, and say whether the prediction is robust or speculative by noting the condition most likely to overturn it.
