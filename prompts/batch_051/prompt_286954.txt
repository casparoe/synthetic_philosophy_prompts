I'm a high school student preparing for a mock legislative hearing on the governance of brain–computer interfaces, and my team has drafted a mechanism that we will need to defend against adversarial questioning. Our coach keeps warning us that a policy can look principled in the abstract and still fail in practice, so rather than asking whether the proposal is a good idea, I want to understand how it would actually behave if enacted.

The proposal works as follows. Every commercial BCI provider must hold a graded "cognitive liberty permit" corresponding to the deepest level of neural processing it performs. Tier 1 permits, covering motor-command and overt-choice decoding, are cheap. Tier 2 permits, covering real-time inference of attention, fatigue, or workload, cost substantially more. Tier 3 permits, covering inference about memory, emotion, belief, preference, or anything plausibly read as a particular mental content, are by far the most expensive and additionally require an opt-in from each user that expires after thirty days and must be actively renewed. Raw neural data are held in an escrow run by a public agency rather than by the provider, and an independent auditor funded by permit fees samples models and data flows to detect "reconstruction" of Tier-3 material from lower-tier signals; a provider found reconstructing is decertified for a year. Finally, users may sell their Tier-3 opt-ins, but only through a centralized public exchange on which prices and aggregate purchases are published.

Please walk through how this scheme would behave in practice. I would like to understand: how providers would adapt what they build, buy, and classify as lower-tier processing; how the auditor's incentives would develop once its funding and its decertification decisions are both at stake; how the opt-in market would operate, including which users would enroll and how prices would transmit information about them; where the system would be gamed first; and whether the most likely failure modes are fixable with adjustments to the proposal or are reasons to abandon it.
