I spent thirty years in analytical chemistry, where if a measurement couldn't be tied to an operational definition we wouldn't publish it. Since retiring I've been reading literary philosophy, and the debate over whether the author's intention determines the meaning of a text strikes me as having the same problem: the main variable never gets operationalized. So give me a committed working definition of "the meaning of a text" — not a menu of possible definitions — and run it against the cases below. (If you think "meaning" is ambiguous, commit to the sense that does the most work in this debate and say which it is.) I have stubborn intuitions about cases 1, 4, and 7, and I'm genuinly undecided about the other four. Where your definition gives an answer that offends my intuitions, say so and defend it rather than hedging.

Cases:
1. (fridge-list) A fridge list that reads "eggs, flour, yeast." The writer wanted eggs, flour, and yeast, and everyone in the house reads it that way.
2. (parking-note) A note left on a badly parked car that reads "Thanks for taking my parking spot." The writer and reciepient both understand it as sarcasm; the literal words say the opposite.
3. (email-typo) An email that says "I'll be out of the office until May 13," but the rest of the thread and the sender's calendar make it clear she meant May 12. Does the email say May 12 or May 13?
4. (constitution) A clause in a late-18th-century constitution places limits on the punishments a state may impose. The drafters had a specific list of punishments from their own century in mind. A modern judge applies the clause to months of solitary confinement, which the drafters never imagined. Does the drafters' private intention settle whether the clause covers it?
5. (novelist-interview) In an interview, a novelist says the dragon in her novel was always a stand-in for her father. Nothing in the published text pushes a reader toward that. Is the father-resemblance part of the novel's meaning?
6. (coded-message) A spy's message reads "the package is in the garden at noon," but a prearranged code among its recipients converts it to "the minister will flee by boat at midnight." Which one is the meaning of the message?
7. (language-model) A language model outputs "I miss her more every day." There is no human author. Does the sentence have a meaning, and if it does, what fixes it?

My prior is that (fridge-list) is fixed by the author's intention, that (constitution) is fixed by the public meaning the legal words had at the time rather than the drafters' private aims, and that (language-model) can't be intention-fixed because there's no author, though I can't say what does fix it.

After you argue for the definition and run all seven cases, return a JSON object with these fields:
- "definition": a string, your committed working definition of the meaning of a text in one or two sentences.
- "case_verdicts": an array of objects, one per case, each with fields "case" (the short label from my list), "verdict" (one of "intention fixes the meaning", "public meaning fixes it", "both", or "neither/no determinate meaning"), "confidence" (one of "high", "medium", "low"), and "rationale" (one sentence).
- "surprises": an array of case labels where your definition conflicts with what you'd have said before testing it.
- "least_secure_verdict": the case label where you're least confident.
- "revised_definition": a string, the definition after the cases forced any changes, or the same definition if none were needed.
- "bottom_line": one definite sentence stating your overall position on whether authorial intention determines textual meaning.
