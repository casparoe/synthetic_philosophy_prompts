Until recently I had spent my career making probabilistic forecasts about external outcomes: markets, weather, elections. Last year I took over a small research program at an institute that builds decision-support tools, and one of our tools now asks users to assign probabilities to their own future choices — whether they will remain in a job, follow a treatment plan, or end a relationship. When I began using the tool on myself during a decision about whether to take on a new role, I encountered something I did not expect. The probabilities I wrote down did not simply track my choice; they entered into it. A high number felt like a promise I might either honor or defiantly break, and a low number felt like a verdict I was accepting or refusing. My self-forecasts never settled into anything I could straightforwardly believe.

I now suspect the tool is resting on an unresolved conceptual problem. There appears to be at least a tension between the deliberative stance — treating one's options as open — and the predictive stance, in which one's own eventual action is just another event to which one might assign a probability. Some would put the underlying worry by saying that deliberation crowds out prediction. I do not yet know whether the tension is genuine, whether it makes assigning probabilities to one's own choices merely difficult or rationally incoherent, or whether it is mostly a psychological artifact of how people think about themselves.

The program I direct can support about three postdoctoral researchers for two years. Its practical goal is to determine, before the tool is scaled up, whether and how decision-support systems may defensibly elicit or rely on people's probabilities about their own choices. I want a defended agenda, not a survey of the literature.
