In my labor law class we've been doing the "collective bargaining is a fundamental right" story, and I keep tripping over one thing. If a bunch of us agree we won't work for less than $21/hr and threaten to walk out if management won't meet that, that's protected concerted activity — the proudest part of US labor law, basically. But if the two gas stations on Route 9 agree not to sell below $3.40 a gallon, that's horizontal price fixing, per se illegal under the Sherman Act, and people can go to jail for it. Both groups are sellers of something getting together to raise their price above whatever competition would give. The legal answer, I know, is the "labor is not a commodity" line. What I can't figure out is whether there is an actual principle underneath that slogan, or just a carve-out we happen to like.

I can generate some candidate justifications: workers usually face one employer with the real bargaining power (a kind of monopsonist), labor is stuck to a person in a way a barrel of oil isn't, and unions move money from capital to labor while cartels move it from consumers to firms that are already rich. But these all feel shaky once I think about hard cases. Airline pilots and NBA players are not exactly oppressed, and their unions exist in part too keep wages above the competitive level — should I, on the principle, want them treated like cartels? And if the real justification is unequal bargaining power, why shouldn't a few tiny hardware stores be allowed to collude against Walmart? Build the strongest version of the distinction you can, and then tell me honestly whether it holds: do we keep this asymmetry as-is, narrow it, or drop it entirely?
