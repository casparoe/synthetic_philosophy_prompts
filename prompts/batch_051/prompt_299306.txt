I'm a software engineer on a team that trains language models to produce multi-step solutions for math and coding problems. Until recently we scored model outputs the simple way: a solution got a positive label if the final answer was correct and a negative label otherwise. We then switched to per-step scoring, following a paper everyone on the team read, Hunter Lightman and colleagues' "Let's Verify Step by Step." They draw the contrast like this: "Outcome-supervised reward models (ORMs) are trained using only the final result of the model’s chain-of-thought, while process-supervised reward models (PRMs) receive feedback for each step in the chain-of-thought." In our current pipeline, human labelers mark each line of a model's generated reasoning as correct or incorrect, and that per-step signal trains the reward model we use to rank and fine-tune solutions.

The engineering case feels intuitive—more precise feedback, easier to inspect, no reward for a wrong derivation that stumbles into the right answer. But the more I think about it, the more the design seems to be quietly committing us to a picture of what good reasoning is. It seems to presuppose that an intermediate step has a correctness value all by itself, before we know where the solution is headed; that reasoning can be carved into discrete moves, each assessable in isolation; and that the quality of the whole is built up out of the quality of the parts. Is that actually a specific, contestable theory of reasoning or justification, and what exactly does process-based supervision commit us to? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of reasoning that this design presupposes, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and flag where the design is philosophically underdetermined. Then tell me what would have to change—in the training pipeline, or in the claims we can make about it—if the presupposed picture were false, for instance if whether a step is a good one always depends on the larger argument it ends up serving.
