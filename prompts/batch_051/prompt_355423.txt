Here are the criteria I have been using to call a theory of truth deflationary:

1. It denies that truth is a substantive property or relation, such as correspondence to fact, coherence, or warranted assertibility.
2. It holds that the content of the truth predicate is exhausted by the equivalence schema: for every proposition p, the proposition that p is true if and only if p.
3. It holds that the truth predicate's function is expressive: it lets speakers generalize over, endorse, and indirectly assert propositions they cannot enumerate.

Now consider a position I will call normative minimalism. Its defenders accept all three criteria. They deny that truth has an underlying nature, and they say that every fact about truth is fixed by instances of the equivalence schema. But they add that truth-talk also marks a standard of correctness: to assert that p is to present p as what one ought to accept, and calling a claim true is the canonical way of holding it to that standard. They insist that this addition does not reintroduce a substantive property or a further set of truth-facts, because the standard generates no explanations beyond those already available through the schema.

Is normative minimalism a deflationary theory of truth? Go through the three criteria one by one and say whether the view satisfies each, violates each, or whether the criterion is too coarse to settle the question. Then say whether the case shows that the criteria need revision — for instance, whether the first criterion is doing the real work and the third is not independent of it, or whether a fourth condition is needed to separate a harmless normative gloss from an inflationary commitment.
