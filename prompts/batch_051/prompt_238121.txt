Ok so I've been reading a lot of the learning-sciences stuff about naive physics and conceptual change, and I want a sanity check before this turns into a long forum post. Pretend you're a cognitive scientist who genuinely knows both the intuitive-theories camp and the knowledge-in-pieces camp, not just their names. Start by brainstorming all the possible tension-points between the commitments below, then prune — I don't want a list where everything is flagged as equally bad.

Where I've landed:

1. Students' errors in physics are systematic, not random gaps, so something organized must be generating them.
2. But the organized units are tiny — p-prims (phenomenological primitives) like "closer means stronger" or "motion requires a cause" — not full intuitive theories of mechanics.
3. So conceptual change is mostly reorganizing, re-weighting, and sorting out the contexts for those pieces, not swapping one big naive theory for a big scientific one.
4. That's why it's completely normal when the same student get's a problem right and then flubs a near-identical one where a different feature of the setup is salient: pieces fire in contexts.
5. Which is why teaching should treat prior ideas as resources to refine and redirect, not as misconceptions to root out.
6. But I still want to hold, on a coarser grain, that learning Newtonian mechanics is analogous to theory change in the history of science, where something like phlogiston or caloric really did get replaced.

Audit this for me. Which pairs are actually in tension, and which only look like they are once you disambiguate "systematic" and "theory"? Rank the real tensions by severity, give the cheapest fix for each and what it would cost, and say which single revision cleans up the most.
