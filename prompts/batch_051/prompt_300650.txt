I'm setting the take-home final for an upper-level undergraduate seminar called 'Ownership Without an Owner,' which sits at the boundary of property theory and intellectual-property policy. Draft one 90-minute essay question, with an expected answer of roughly 1,200 words and worth 20 marks, on the difference among orphan works, abandoned software of the sort often called abandonware, and works that are genuinely ownerless because their term has expired.

Structure the question around a short concrete scenario rather than an abstract topic — for example, an archive that wants to digitize (a) a photographer's letters and prints whose owner cannot be found after a diligent search, (b) a 1980s scientific software package produced by a company that dissolved and whose code has no clear current rightholder, and (c) a text whose copyright has expired. The question should ask students to argue how the law ought to treat the three cases rather than merely what current law happens to say, and should force them to draw on at least two of the standard justifications for exclusive rights — incentive, labor-desert, and personality — while also engaging the conceptual difference between abandonment, lapse, and unlocatability.

The crux should make the question unanswerable by reciting a summary of those justifications: it should separate students who can explain why a right might survive even when the owner has disappeared, is indifferent, or is silent from those who have only memorized the labels. Then provide a model answer and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, the marks each is worth (total 20), and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each wrong. Do not require students to cite specific statutes, directives, or cases, and do not make the model answer depend on any jurisdiction's positive law; the question should test conceptual command and be answerable without outside legal research.
