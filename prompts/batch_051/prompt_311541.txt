Last night my two housemates spent an hour arguing about a dumb mobile game, and by the end I genuinely couldn't tell who was right. The game has a weekly gamble: you can stake any fraction of your current balance, a fair coin is flipped, and on heads you get your stake back plus an extra 50% of it, while on tails you lose 40% of the stake. So the expected value per dollar staked is positive — fifty cents one way, forty cents the other, a nickel of edge. Dan argued that settles it: each dollar has positive expected value, the bet repeats weekly, so the rational policy is to stake the biggest fraction the game allows every week. Priya said that policy is how you end up worse off than you started with high probability over time, because the results multiply, and what matters is not the expected value over all possible parallel histories but the growth rate of the single path your balance actually takes.

Dan came back that she was confusing a bad outcome with a bad decision: the typical path may look grim, but the rare big wins more than make up for it in expectation, and refusing a positive-expectation gamble because the typical path is grim is just risk aversion — fine as a preference, but not a demand of rationality. Priya said it isn't risk aversion: even a risk-neutral person should distinguish maximizing expected wealth from maximizing the long-run growth rate of a repeated multiplicative gamble, and only the second tracks what will actually happen to the account. Dan replied that if the game is played only finitely many times, 'long-run growth rate' is a claim about limits, and for a finite horizon a risk-neutral person is exactly right to maximize expected final wealth; she can't use infinity to judge a finite game. Priya answered that she didn't need infinity: she offered to bet him that his all-in strategy would end below his starting balance after twenty weeks, and said she'd win that bet most of the time, which shows the real issue is the gap between the average outcome and the typical one, not limits. Dan said that side bet is a different gamble with its own payoffs, and asked why it should override the original expected values. I was asked to referee and couldn't.
