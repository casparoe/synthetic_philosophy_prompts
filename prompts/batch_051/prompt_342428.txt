A recurring charge in debates over anti-aging medicine is that much of the opposition to radical life extension is 'deathism' — reasons produced after the fact to rationalize an affective attachment to the status quo, in which human beings age and die on roughly the present schedule, rather than a stable view about what actually makes a human life good. I want to know whether that charge can be tested, as opposed to merely asserted.

Let me make the claim precise. On the deathism hypothesis, typical objections to life extension — that an indefinitely long life would become boring, that mortality is what gives life meaning, that we would lose urgency or ambition, that natural limits deserve respect — are confabulations generated after an initial aversion, and they will shift or crumble when the aversion is dampened or the status quo is reframed. On the reflective-valuation hypothesis, those objections survive debiasing and reframing largely intact, because they track the person's considered values.

How would one test between these hypotheses? Give me the strongest concrete design you can — survey experiments, framing manipulations, debiasing protocols, incentive-compatible measures, longitudinal or cross-cultural comparisons, or some combination — and specify in advance which result pattern would favor deathism and which would favor reflective valuation. Then give the residue report: what would even a clean result on either side still not settle? In particular, can any behavioral test distinguish a reason that was rationalized at birth but has since become genuinely endorsed through reflection from a reason that was genuine from the start, or is that distinction doing more work than any measurement can reach?

I want the answer to be self-critical about this. If the very term 'deathism' loads the case, or if rationalized and reflectively endorsed reasons are not a clean dichotomy, say so plainly and tell me what could be salvaged; if no feasible study would move the question much, I would rather be told that than be given a design that overclaims.
