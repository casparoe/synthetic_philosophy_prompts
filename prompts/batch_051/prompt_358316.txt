I am a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on moral psychology, and an argument from last week has been nagging at me. A classmate defended what she called an uptake view of promising: on her account, a promise by itself creates nothing. The obligation comes into existence only when the promisee actually receives it, understands it, and forms some expectation or begins to rely on it. If I promise to meet you for lunch and you laugh, say you would never rely on me, and walk away, then, she argued, I am under no obligation to show up — not because you released me, but because no obligation ever came into being. My professor disagreed from the other direction. On his view, the binding force of a promise lies in the promisor's deliberate act of self-commitment, so I can bind myself by promising even if the promisee is not listening, does not care, or forms no expectation at all — in something like the way a private vow can still bind. The two of them treated this as a purely conceptual dispute, but it seems to me that it has empirical fingerprints: the two views should differ, at least in tendency, about when people feel bound, when they feel released, when observers blame them, and what kinds of repair or excuse people accept.

Spell out how you would operationalize "binding force" in observable terms, specify the conditions and measures, and say exactly which result patterns would favor each reading. Then say what such a study would not settle — in particular, how far findings about ordinary judgments, felt obligation, and blame can go toward establishing what actually binds, as distinct from merely describing the psychology that surrounds promising.
