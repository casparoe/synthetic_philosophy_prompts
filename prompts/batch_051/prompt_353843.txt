I chair a state parole board, and I need a single, reusable decision procedure for deciding whether an eligible prisoner should be released. Each month we hear roughly fifty cases, most in under an hour; the board members are lay appointees, not philosophers or statisticians, and our written reasons are reviewed by courts and occasionally by legislative oversight. Assume the legal side is settled for present purposes: a prisoner before us is eligible, the statute instructs us to weigh public safety and suitability, and we must be able to explain ourselves. Our decisions currently lurch between two rationales. Some members treat the only question as whether the person still poses a risk; others treat it as whether the person has really served enough. Please write us a procedure, grounded in the distinction between consequentialist and retributivist theories of punishment, that a lay board could actually apply.

One distinction must survive the translation into practice. Retributivism can be read as requiring the state to impose deserved punishment, or only as forbidding punishment that outruns desert. The difference is decisive for parole, because nearly every release is a decision not to carry out the full authorized sentence. On the first reading, early release for good prison conduct is an injustice unless desert itself has changed; on the second, early release is permissible whenever it serves the future, provided no one is punished beyond what they deserve. Your procedure should say which reading it adopts, or explain on what sorts of cases the two readings diverge and how the board should then decide.

Give us the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than fact. Tell us how to handle the actual material in a file: actuarial risk scores, institutional behavior, participation in programs, expressions of remorse and acceptance of responsibility, age and health, and victim statements. In particular, I need to know whether remorse and good prison conduct bear on desert or only on risk, and whether a low risk score may ever be treated as evidence that the offender has already been punished enough.

Then run the procedure on these three cases. First, a seventy-two-year-old man who has served forty-eight years of a life sentence for a murder committed during a robbery when he was twenty-four. His disciplinary record has been clean for three decades, he works as a hospice orderly, and his actuarial risk score is in the lowest category. He has never admitted the killing; he says he drove the car and never used the weapon, and he will not express remorse for a murder he denies. The victim's family opposes release. Second, a thirty-four-year-old woman who has served three years of a five-year sentence for embezzling four hundred thousand dollars from a small nonprofit. She has expressed remorse, completed the financial-rehabilitation program, and has a stable home with her sister, but her actuarial risk score for new fraud is high because she has two prior fraud convictions and the instrument treats early fraud and repeated breaches of trust as strong predictors. The nonprofit's director opposes release as a signal that stealing from a charity carries no real price. Third, a sixty-year-old man who has served nine years of a twelve-year sentence for embezzling from his employer's pension fund. He has been a model prisoner, paid restitution by surrendering his assets, and now has early heart failure that makes continued imprisonment unusually burdensome and expensive; his risk of new fraud is low and he is quietly remorseful. The original sentencing judge said white-collar offenders should serve the full term.

At least one of these cases is built so that a consistent procedure will recommend something the board will not like; do not soften that. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot settle and where the board is left with an irreducible moral decision, however much it would prefer to treat the decision as a technical one. Do not cite legal cases or scholarship; I need the reasoning, not authorities. Write your answer as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
