I am a novelist who writes about the culture of self-improvement, and next month I am giving a lecture at an interdisciplinary institute that brings together developmental psychologists, psychometricians, and philosophers working on character education. The thesis I intend to defend is this: the contemporary language of grit and resilience is not a neutral description of human capacities but a moralized ideology, because it relocates structural failures and unjust constraints inside individual character and, in practice, chiefly trains people to bear conditions they should be helped to alter; this is not the old virtue of fortitude, which was embedded in a substantive account of the good life, but something thinner and more disciplinary. My audience will be mostly educational psychologists and psychometricians who study constructs such as grit and resilience as predictors of academic success, plus a minority of neo-Aristotelian virtue ethicists who worry that I may be discarding the virtue of fortitude along with the buzzwords. Please act as an expert on both the empirical psychology of self-regulation and the virtue-ethical tradition, and think through the task slowly before drafting: begin by mapping the audience's likely commitments, then identify the weakest and most load-bearing assumptions in my view. Then produce a Q&A preparation sheet, not a defense of the lecture: the objections I am actually likely to receive, phrased in this audience's vocabulary, ordered by how probable and how damaging they are, each paired with a short reply, a note on what I should concede and what I should hold, and a clear flag where the reply is soft. End with the question I should most be dreading in the Q&A.
