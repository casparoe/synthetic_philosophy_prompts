You are a philosopher of mathematics who has worked on how concepts get their exact definitions; think carefully and show your reasoning. I keep running into the same unsatisfying story: either a definition is presented as a near-mystery ("precisely the right abstraction") or as pure convention that we are free to swap out, and I would like the full specification before I read any more. What must a definition deliver in order to count as the right notion in its part of mathematics, rather than merely a workable stipulation?

Use the open-set axioms for topological spaces and the modern group axioms as the two running test cases. For the record, several candidate criteria came up with a friend and I do not yet know which are genuine: capturing the pre-formal idea (continuity/nearness, symmetry); making the central theorems clean and their hypotheses natural; generalizing the core examples while keeping genuinely different structures apart; being closed under the standard operations such as products, subspaces, and quotients; and being minimal rather than a hash of individually motivated clauses. For each requirement on your final list, say what it demands, why it is a requirement, and how open sets and groups respectively satisfy or strain it. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any adequate account and which are preferences of a particular school or research program; flag any pairs that can pull in opposite directions or fail to be jointly satisfiable; and finish by identifying the single requirement that excludes the most wrong or merely accidental definitions while still admitting both open sets and groups. Begin with a short brainstorm of candidate criteria before you commit, and a table is fine.
