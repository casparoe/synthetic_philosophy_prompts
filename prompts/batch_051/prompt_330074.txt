Suppose an international conservation body adopts the following mechanism. It will maintain a registry of protected lineages. For listing and funding purposes, a lineage counts as a species if it satisfies at least one of four familiar candidate species concepts — the biological, phylogenetic, morphological, and ecological species concepts — as operationalised by a fixed scoring rubric applied by a rotating panel of systematists. Petitions to list or delist may be filed by any state, an accredited conservation NGO, or any group of three professional systematists. If the rubrics disagree about whether the lineage is a species, the lineage is listed but only as provisionally protected, and an objector then carries the burden of showing that its species status is unstable across concepts. Conservation finance is distributed to states in proportion to the number of listed lineages whose known range falls within that state's territory, and a successful delisting reduces the state's allocation. Panel members serve rotating three-year terms and are chosen by lot from a public register in which systematists declare their primary species concept.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral or conservation programme: the task is to predict how it would actually behave — what it incentivises, who gains, how it gets gamed, where it fails, and whether the failure modes are fixable.

Structure the answer in exactly these named sections, in this order and at the indicated lengths:

1. The mechanism as a set of incentives (about 150 words). Restate the scheme as constraints and incentives, with no evaluation.

2. What systematists will do (about 250 words). Consider the incentives around describing new lineages, lumping versus splitting, designing the operational rubrics, concept shopping, and gaming the panel lottery.

3. What states and NGOs will do (about 250 words). Consider petitioning, competing range claims, delisting fights, and gaming of the funding formula.

4. What happens to the species concepts themselves (about 200 words). Consider rubric drift, capture of the public register, and whether pluralism is preserved or collapses under the scheme's pressure.

5. Failure modes and their fixes (about 250 words). Identify the first two places the scheme gets gamed and why, and say which failures could be repaired without making the scheme worse and which could not.

6. Upshot for the species problem (about 200 words). Say what the scheme's predicted behaviour reveals about whether the dispute among species concepts is verbal, metaphysical, or partly an artefact of the uses to which classifications are put.

Write the answer in British English throughout.
