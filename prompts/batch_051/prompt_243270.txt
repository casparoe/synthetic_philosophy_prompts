Popular accounts of the meme idea, going back to Dawkins and carried on by a long line of writers on cultural evolution, lean heavily on the claim that culture evolves in a Darwinian way. On this picture the meme is to culture what the gene is to biology: a unit that is copied, that varies, and that is selected. Tunes, catchphrases, fashions, and religious beliefs are said to spread because they are good at getting themselves copied, not because they are true or useful to the people who carry them; just as genes build bodies as vehicles for their own replication, memes are said to shape minds and practices for their own propagation. I find the analogy attractive, but I cannot tell how much of it is doing explanatory work and how much is the analogy running ahead of the evidence.

I would like an audit of the meme–gene analogy. Lay out the correspondence explicitly: what maps onto the gene, the phenotype, the organism, the environment, the generation, mutation, selection pressure, and the fitness criterion. Then identify where the mapping breaks down, and — just as important — separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusions people draw from the ones that are merely superficial. I especially want to know what the gene side quietly imports that the culture side may lack: particulate inheritance with high copying fidelity, a stable genotype–phenotype distinction, blind rather than directed variation, predominantly vertical transmission, and the possibility that the replicator's interests diverge from the vehicle's. Finally, say whether the conclusions the analogy is used to support — that there can be a genuinely Darwinian science of culture, and that cultural items should be expected to evolve for their own spread rather than for the benefit of the humans who carry them — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether a different analogy, such as epidemiology, does the same work with less distortion.
