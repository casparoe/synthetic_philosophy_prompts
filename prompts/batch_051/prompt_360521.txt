I work as a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and each year I allocate a fixed portion of my income to charitable giving. I keep a spreadsheet that scores candidate options by the near-term, legible effects I can actually estimate: bed nets distributed per dollar, tons of carbon dioxide kept out of the atmosphere, hours of maintained open-source software, and so on. I know that every option also has long-run and diffuse consequences—shifting norms, changing institutions, influencing the research trajectory of a field, affecting what other donors do. I have adopted a standing policy for these consequences: when I cannot trace a causal chain from an action to a downstream effect with at least rough quantitative confidence, I assign that effect zero weight in my estimate. I do not assume the effect is literally absent; I assume it is not usable in a decision, because speculative benefits and speculative harms often seem equally plausible and should not be allowed to drown out the effects I can see. I have applied this especially when choosing between global health, climate, and more recent projects aimed at reducing risks from advanced AI; the second-order arguments in each direction all sound important, but they also sound mutually incompatible and nearly impossible to falsify.

A friend has challenged this. She argues that the policy is not neutral; it systematically favors interventions with legible, short-horizon metrics, and it gets things exactly wrong in the cases where the largest effects are the least visible. She notes that many of the most consequential choices in ordinary life—joining a small company, moving to a new city, choosing a partner—are ones where the dominant effects cannot be traced quantitatively in advance, and that demanding a traceable causal path before counting any effect is a way of selecting for what is easy to measure rather than what is good. Her proposed alternative is to keep long-run effects in the decision but discount them only modestly, and to reason qualitatively about probable direction even when magnitude is unknown. I find this attractive in the abstract, but I worry it opens the door to every speculative argument and to whichever story is told most vividly.

My question is whether my zeroing policy is a defensible epistemic shortcut for someone who cannot do better than rough estimates, or a bias that dresses up a preference for measurable near-term impact as intellectual humility. Is there a principled way to distinguish the long-run effects that deserve nonzero weight from those that should be set aside? Would it be better to discount them continuously rather than truncate them at zero, and if so, how would I choose the discount rate without sneaking my conclusions in? Does the answer change when the choice is between radically different causes rather than between similar projects within one cause?
