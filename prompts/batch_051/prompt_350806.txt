I'm a novelist who somehow ended up on the board of our town's food co-op (I write the newsletter, and apparently that qualifies me as "the word person"). We're deadlocked over a proposal from our general manager, and I've been tasked with drafting the decision memo we'll vote on.

The proposal is a store remerchandising. Move the chips, candy, and soda to knee-level shelves along the back wall; put fruit at eye level by the registers; reorder the grab-and-go case so the salads and hardboiled eggs hit you first instead of the maple-glazed scones. The GM is clear: we change nothing about what we stock and nothing about prices. We only change what's salient. He says every supermarket already does this, and our own sales data predicts a real shift in what members buy.

Two board members are livid. One says it's worse, not better, that the nudge works on the part of your mind that doesn't notice it — at least a price hike you can see and argue with. The other points out that the current layout isn't some neutral state of nature: it already pushes impulse junk food, so "keep the store as it is" isn't refusing to nudge, it's just endorsing yesterday's nudge. And the motive point: if the exact same layout change were being done to maximize margin, we'd call it a dark pattern; does the fact that the motive is benevolent turn the same technique into something the board should vote for?

I need a memo with an actual bottom line — adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions (and if conditions, tell me which and why they don't just make us feel better while leaving the real worry untouched). Give the strongest version of the loosing side, not a straw man. Most of the board has never read a word of philosophy, so write it as something practical people can argue about and then vote on.
