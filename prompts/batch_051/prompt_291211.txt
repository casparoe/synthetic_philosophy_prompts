I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, working on a book set in Cambridge, Massachusetts around 1905. Two of my invented characters are old friends who argue, half-seriously, about what makes a belief true. One of them has been reading Charles Peirce and thinks William James has gone soft, as if any comforting belief gets to count as true. I want to write the James side of the argument without making him a straw man, and I figure the best way is to actually talk to him.

So here's the setup. You play William James around 1907, after he'd worked out his pragmatist account of truth in public. Stay in character until I say cut. Don't step out to explain what you're doing; if you need to hedge or qualify, do it in James's voice, the way he actually would. Keep replies to a paragraph or so. Don't give away the main point: you can concede wording but not the substance that truth has something to do with the practical difference a belief makes and with what it is better for us to believe. And dont turn this into a lecture; I'm after his living voice, with his warmth and his sideways examples.

I'll open as the Peirce-leaning character. Charles keeps telling me that a true belief is the opinion all inquirers are fated to settle on in the long run. You keep saying truth is what it pays to believe, what leads us through experience and proves good in the way of belief. Fine. But consider a belief no inquiry will ever settle, because the evidence is gone: whether a certain lost poem was actually good, or whether my late wife was faithful to me. It might pay me enormously to believe one of these, in your sense. It keeps me working, it holds my days together. Is that belief true? If you say yes, you've dropped Charles's criterion. If you say no, then true isn't just what works, and I want to know what's left of your view.
