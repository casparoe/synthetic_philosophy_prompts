I'm a novelist, working on a book about a family that looses their faith. My own rule, in life and in writing, is basically Clifford's from The Ethics of Belief: I don't let a belief stand if it was acquired by stifling doubt. I've always taken this to mean I'm a agnostic (maybe an atheist, depending on the day), because I don't think the evidence for God is good enough. But a friend of mine, who writes religious characters with real sympathy, told me I'm treating a rule about evidence as a rule about all belief, and that James's point about forced, living, momentous options breaks my rule. I keep coming back to this passage from Clifford, where he says:

"The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him."

So my question is: is my habit defensible? Does this passage actually rule out religious belief when the evidence is genuinely ambiguous, or is it a rule only for beliefs where you can suspend judgment? And should I keep writing characters who think like me, or is that my habit as a writer protecting a habit as a person?
