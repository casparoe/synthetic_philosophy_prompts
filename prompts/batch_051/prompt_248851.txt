I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for a decade I have run my personal charitable giving on a fixed rule. I fund only interventions backed by randomized or rigorous quasi-experimental evidence and a credible cost-effectiveness estimate, and I concentrate the money in the one or two programs that look best per dollar. The rule has a second clause, and that is the part now bothering me: I assign an expected value of zero to any intervention whose central claim is that it changes norms, institutions, or policy, because neither I nor, I suspect, its practitioners can verify such effects ex post.

Lately I have come to see the second clause as possibly a sampling bias wearing ordinary clothes. The interventions I can measure are those whose outcomes become legible within a short time horizon, while some of the largest historical changes look in hindsight as though a few advocacy campaigns and institutional reforms were extraordinarily cheap per life affected, even though their ex ante success was radically uncertain and no study would have captured the mechanism. I also see that the measurable slice of the charitable world is exactly the slice that attracts donors like me, so its marginal dollar may be worth less than a marginal dollar in neglected, harder-to-evaluate areas. Yet when I imagine relaxing the clause, I find no stopping rule: every advocacy group can tell a plausible leverage story, and I will drift back to giving on narrative.

Is the zeroing-out policy defensible as an individual epistemic rule, or is it measurability bias? If it is not defensible as stated, is there a revised version that keeps its protection against narrative while allowing less measurable interventions a non-zero hearing? And am I entitled, as a donor with limited time and no special evaluative skill, to keep the policy even if I suspect it misses better opportunities?
