I run the anomaly-detection side of a condition-monitoring system at a wastewater treatment plant. I came up through operations, not a classroom, but Ive been reading about root cause analysis on my own and this one question has really got under my skin: when are two anomalies "the same mechanism"?

Here's the situation. Every week we log anomalies — pressure dips, turbidity spikes, chlorine residual drifting, vibration alarms. Whenever two happen near each other in time, on the same system, or on the same sensor, I have to decide whether to write them up as one underlying problem or two different ones. That call drives whether maintenance treats it as a single open issue or duplicates, whether we escalate to engineering, and whether something gets called "recurring." If I get it wrong one way we waste a crew on a duplicate; if I get it wrong the other way we miss that one bad component is failing in two places and the fix didn't actually take.

The hangup is that "looks the same" and "same sensor" both fail. Two different problems can produce the same-looking anomaly: a chlorine residual dip from a bad dosing pump and one from a valve left partway open trace almost identical. And one problem can produce a bunch of different-looking anomalies: a failing pump seal will throw a vibration alarm, a pressure drop, and a turbidity blip downstream. So Ive been going around on what "same mechanism" should even mean. Same root cause? Same chain of physical parts? Same fix makes both stop? Same "if we'd fixed the first one, the second would never have happened" test? These come apart and I cant keep them straight.

Please write me a decision procedure my team could actually run, written for operators and junior techs, not philosophers. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and says "not the same mechanism," and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a judgement call rather than a checkable fact. Also say, honestly, where the procedure will misfire and what it cannot settle. We usually have about half an hour per call, the person running it might not have seen the equipment, and whatever we conclude has to be written up so a plant engineer and a compliance officer will sign off on it.

Then run the procedure on three cases:
(a) Two chlorine residual dips on the same sensor two days apart. First one we traced to a faulty dosing pump and fixed it. Second one had the same shape and size, but the pump checked out fine; after a while we found a valve left partway closed by a contractor. So: same signature, different mechanism (or is it?).
(b) A pressure drop at the intake and a turbidity spike at the filters, an hour apart, on what look like different systems. The crew wanted to log them as unrelated, but it turned out a clogged intake screen made the pump cavitate and pull sediment through, so both traces came from one mechanism. So: different signatures, same mechanism (or is it?).
(c) A turbidity spike three weeks ago that cleared before we could inspect, logged "cause unknown," and a similar spike yesterday that is still sitting on the chart. There's no good data on either beyond the trend. Say what the procedure can and cannot conclude, and where exactly it hands the call to a person.

Make sure at least one of the worked cases comes out in a way I am not going to like, and say which feature of the case is doing the work in that verdict.
