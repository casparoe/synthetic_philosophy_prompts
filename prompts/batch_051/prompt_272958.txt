I teach a third-year epistemology course, 22 students, one 80-minute block. I want to run a live Sleeping Beauty game, but I don't want to announce that name until the very end and I don't want to front-load any theory: the students should get caught in the disagreement first, then have it named and dissected. Can you design the whole session for me?

The core setup is the standard one. A fair coin is flipped Sunday night. If heads, the sleeper is woken exactly once, Monday. If tails, the sleeper is woken Monday and Tuesday, with the memory of the Monday waking wiped, so that from the inside the two wakings feel identical. At each waking the sleeper has to set a fair price for a $1 ticket that pays if the coin came up heads. Stakes should be real but tiny, nickels or quarters. By the end I want participants to feel the pull of both 1/2 and 1/3, and to see that the fight is really about which space of possibilities a particular wake-up is drawn from (the reference class problem) rather than about anyone being bad at fractions.

Now the constraint I want you to actually solve, not wave at: you cannot drug anyone. I don't want the usual cop-out where students are just told to pretend they forgot. Give me a multi-role protocol that substitutes for amnesia. My hunch is that each potential waking slot gets played by a different student who genuinely does not know which slot they occupy (the lone heads-Monday slot, the first tails slot, or the second tails slot), but you should improve on that. The self-locating uncertainty has to be real for the person setting the price, not narrated into existence.

Also build in a rotating bookie or market-maker role so there is pressure to give prices rather than just opine. And I want a settlement rule that is deliberately ambiguous between settling per trial and settling per waking, so the debrief can show that halfers and thirders are often not disagreeing about the fairness of the coin but about which bet is being made in the first place. Then give me exact rules, materials (cards, envelopes, slips, schedule, suggested wording), a minute-by-minute plan for the 80 minutes, and a seperate debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the halfer, the thirder, and the reference class problem, with the names held back until that debrief.

End by being straight about two things. First, what predictably goes wrong in the room: someone blurts out Sleeping Beauty, one student dominates the pricing, people think the lesson is that coins are weird, people confuse risk aversion with credence. Tell me how to handle each. Second, be honest about which feature of the real puzzle the live version cannot reproduce even with the slot-per-waking trick. I suspect it is genuine diachronic identity and the actual memory wipe, but say how much I will have to supply by fiat when I debrief.
