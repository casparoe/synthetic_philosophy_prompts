I'm a novelist and I've been keeping a log of words I coin for the worlds I build. A pattern keeps showing up. Whenever I need a term for a new material, a social role, or a piece of tech, I almost always reach for Latin or Greek roots: a memory-device becomes a mnemostat, a ruling council becomes an archigerous, whatever. If I try the same from, say, Old Norse or Coptic or even a living language like Yoruba, it lands different — it feels either too embedded in a real culture or just not authoritative enough. So I have this obvious asymmetry in my practice and in the genre generally: dead classical languages are treated as a legitimate source for new coinages, wheras other dead or living languages are mostly treated as quarry for names and flavors, not for technical vocabulary.

Is there a real justification for that, or is it just prestige and path dependency wearing a rationalist costume? I can see a few candidates: the classical languages are morphologically regular and familiar to a lot of English-speakers, so the meaning is semi-transparent; they're dead but not too dead, so they don't drag living people into your neologism; Latin and Greek supplied scientific vocabulary for centuries so there's a learned-practice reason. But I'm not sure any of these survive a closer case. Suppose I coin a term from Sumerian or Aramaic with the same morphological clarity and the same gloss; would I be making an error of taste, a political choice, or just a less useful word? If there's a defensible asymmetry, I want the strongest version of it. If not, should I deliberately break the pattern and start coining from a rotating list of languages, even if it makes my prose feel less serious to editors? What I want is a principle for when a language counts as fair game for new technical terms, and whether the Latin/Greek monopoly is something a good craftsman should keep, bend, or deliberately break.
