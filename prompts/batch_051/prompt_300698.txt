So I was in my study group last night and two of my friends, Priya and Jon, got completely stuck on a thought experiment they came up with, and I genuinely can't tell who's right. The scenario was this. Imagine a drug (call it a grief-damper) you can take right after someone close to you dies. It doesn't touch your memory of them, doesn't change your belief that they're gone, and doesn't stop you caring about them in the normal sense — it just removes the felt ache of grief. No hollow feeling, no lump in the throat, no crying jags. Everything else stays. The question was whether the life where you take the pill is better than the life where you ride out the grief, all else equal.

Jon said it's obvious the pill-world person is better off. If the love and the memories are intact, the sadness is pure cost. It isn't information — it doesn't tell you anything you don't already know — and the dead person isn't helped by your suffering. He compared it to swelling after an injury: inflammation is an appropriate signal that you've been hurt, but once you know you've been hurt you don't have to keep the swelling out of respect for the injury. So he thought the world where everyone takes the pill is genuinely better, with maybe some reservations about social pressure.

Priya disagreed hard. She said the pill leaves a mismatch between your evaluative state and your emotions: you'd be looking at the death of someone you claim to love and feeling, basically, fine. That's a failure to be in touch with what you value, she said — like a person who has the judgment that the tiger is about to kill them but feels no fear at all. The fear isn't just a cost; it's part of what it is to register the threat as a threat for you. Same with grief. The feeling is how the loss shows up as yours, not just as a fact to be filed away. She also said the "same love minus the pain" picture is confused, because part of what made the attachment love in the first place is that this person was the kind of thing whose loss could wound you. If you know in advance you'll chemically strip the wound away, the attachment becomes more like a pleasant association you'd like to keep, not a love that makes claims on you.

Jon came back that this is just the worship-of-pain move. Fittingness shows an emotion is correct, he said, not that it's good to have. A panic attack during a real house fire is a fitting response, but he'd take a pill that gave him clear-headed accurate fear — the appraisal without the panic — in a heartbeat. So her tiger example cuts the other way: accurate appraisal plus no terror beats accurate appraisal plus terror. As for love making you woundable, he said that's a fact about the disposition before the loss. The vulnerability did its work in structuring the relationship, and the pill doesn't retroactively undo it. Being miserable in July doesn't retroactively make your love in June deeper.

Priya's answer: no, it doesn't retroactively make it deeper, but going forward the felt pain is the repair work. The ache is the self reconfiguring — from a self built around someone being there to a self that knows they aren't. If you skip that, your plans and habits and hopes stay silently configured for a person who's gone, and you don't notice because the feeling that would have forced the reassembly is absent. The pill doesn't remove a cost of love. It removes a part of love — the part that makes what you value settle into your body, if that isn't too vague a way to put it.

Then Jon said, fine — maybe in the first weeks the ache does repair work, but what about the person six months later who still wakes up sick every morning? By Priya's lights you'd have to say that's still meaningful work, which would make her position collapse into opposing any antidepressant for the bereaved, and that's absurd. The line between acute functional grief and prolonged damaging grief is just a matter of time and degree. Priya said he'd changed the subject: they were asking about the ideal life and whether fully felt grief is worth having, not clinical policy for people whose grief is spiraling. Jon said no, the original question said "all else equal," and her whole case is that all else never is equal — so she changed the rules, not him.

At that point I had to leave, but they're picking it up next week and told me I had to referee. What I actually want is a point-by-point adjudication: for each of the specific moves Priya and Jon made, who's right about what, where does each argument overreach, and what (if anything) are they both missing? A few of the points I'm most stuck on: is Jon right that fittingness can't distinguish fear from panic and so can't carry Priyas side; is Priya right that the attachment itself is changed by knowing the wound will be chemically stripped away; and does she dodge the acute-vs-chronic question at the end? I'm less interested in a final winner than in a move-by-move breakdown of which arguments actually land.
