I've been developing an idea I call the future-reader effect, and I want an honest assessment of it. The core claim is that whether a person ends up in the historical record depends not only on what later archivists choose to preserve but also on whether that person, while alive, could picture a future reader for the traces they left behind. People who expect to matter historically keep diaries, preserve correspondence, and organize their papers; people who have internalized that people like them are not recorded rarely produce such material, not because there was less to record but because no imagined audience gave the recording a point. On this view, an archive can create the very silence it later reports: it teaches some people that no one will read them, and those people then fail to generate the evidence of their lives, an absence that later readers treat as evidence that there was little worth recording. I think this explains a pattern I keep noticing, where underdocumented groups are described as having left no records when their silence may reflect a reasonable prediction about how little of them would have been kept. Is this mechanism coherent, or is it just a metaphor dressed up as causal explanation? Does it already exist under a better name? And where does it break, for example by wrongly counting as self-silencing what is actually outright exclusion or destruction?
