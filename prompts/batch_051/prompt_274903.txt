A friend in my formal epistemology reading group tried to convince us the other week that Jeffrey conditionalization is just strict conditionalization in disguise, and that there's no distinct problem of uncertain evidence. I want to resist it but I can't located the mistake.

Here's the argument she gave. (1) You see a sock in dim light and your credence that it's blue (B) moves to 0.7. (2) Right then it's true of you—and you know it—that your new confidence in B is 0.7; call that proposition U. (3) When a proposition is learned for certain, rational updating goes by strict conditionalization, so your posterior should be Pnew(·) = Pold(· | U). (4) Since U reports your own current confidence, and you're never wrong about that, U is learned for certain. (5) Moreover, your old credences should satisfy a reflection-like principle here: conditional on U, your credence in B should be 0.7, and your conditional credences given B and given not-B should be unchanged, since U only records your response to the sock and carries no information about the fabric itself. (6) But that's exactly the distribution Jeffrey's rule would give for the partition {B, not-B} with inputs 0.7 and 0.3. (7) So every Jeffrey update is really a strict conditionalization on a proposition about the agent's own posterior, and uncertain evidence is a distinction without a difference.

Every step seems fine when I read them one at a time, but the conclusion feels too strong. If the argument has a hole, tell me which step is doing the work to cover it, and how to sharpen the claim so the trick becomes visible. If it's valid, which premise should I give up, and what does rejecting it cost a Bayesian who wants to keep both strict conditionalization and Jeffrey's rule?
