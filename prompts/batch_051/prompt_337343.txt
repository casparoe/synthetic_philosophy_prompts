im a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and the reading group ive been in has spent way too many threads relitigating whether majority rule has anything like the guarantees people claim. weve got two camps: an epistemic democrat who thinks the Condorcet jury theorem still does real work outside its textbook assumptions, and a skeptic who thinks those assumptions are exactly where real-world information aggregation dies. we want to stop arguing past each other and write an adversarial collaboration instead, and id like you to draft the document they should produce.

Both camps accept the clean version of the theorem: n voters, binary choice, each voter independently right with the same probability p > .5, then the probability the majority is right increases with n and tends to 1; p < .5 tends to 0; p = .5 stays put (ties aside). They also agree the interesting question is whether actual juries, electorates, and crowds are close enough to those assumptions for the theorem to carry weight, and both agree the naive slogan more-voters-are-always-better is not what the theorem says.

Please format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table with columns: the crux, the epistemic democrat's position and prediction, the skeptic's position and prediction, and the test or case both sides accept would bear on it. Make the wording tight enough that each side would sign, no strawmanning, and if you think some of their fight is verbal (say, one of them means a voting rule and the other means a forecasting heuristic), say so explicitly.

A few things the document has to handle. First, the common-shock problem. They once agreed on a toy model where each voter's signal is X_i = θ + u + e_i, with θ the truth, u an error everyone shares, and e_i independent noise, and asked what majority accuracy does as n grows with the variance of u held fixed. They agree on the math: the shared shock doesnt wash out, so the majority stays capable of being wrong as a block no matter how many voters you add. Where they split is how much of real political disagreement is the shared u versus the independent e_i, and both have said theyd update on an estimate of that ratio from crowd forecasts of binary events, with predictions recorded in advance. Put that in the table.

Second, heterogeneous competence. If p_i varies by voter and every p_i is above .5 but some can sit arbitarily close to .5, both camps agree the folk theorem is under-specified. The democrat bets theres a clean condition or weighting scheme that rescues the asymptotic result; the skeptic bets the needed conditions end up so strong or uncheckable that the theorem does no practical work. Both would accept a closed-form result for a two-point distribution of p_i as evidence, with their predictions about what it would show listed beforehand.

Third, the strategic-voting problem. The skeptic keeps bringing up the result that sincere voting isnt generally an equilibrium once voters have different preferences over the two outcomes, and the democrat concedes the math but says it bites hardest in adversarial elections and less in common-interest bodies like juries. They both want that disagreement made precise: what exactly does strategic voting threaten in the theorem, and does it change the sign of the aggregation result or just the magnitude.

Write the thing they should have produced. If there are other cruxes theyre missing beyond the three above, add them, but keep the table honest about which side gets which burden.
