I have caught myself following a stable but unusual policy about how far I let deduction extend my knowledge, and I want an honest assessment of it. In ordinary cases I endorse closure under known entailment without hesitation: if I know that a meeting is in the east wing and I know that this entails it is not in the west wing, I will say I know it is not in the west wing. But I make one standing exception. When the conclusion of a deduction is simply the denial of a radical skeptical scenario, I stop treating the deduction as knowledge-conferring. I know that I have hands, and I know that my having hands entails that I am not a handless brain in a vat being fed an illusion of hands; yet when someone runs that argument on me, I still will not assert that I know I am not the brain in the vat. I do not think I might actually be one, and I do not reject the entailment. I merely treat this particular kind of inference as not transmitting knowledge. A philosopher friend says this is Nozick's rejection of epistemic closure rendered as a personal rule. Is my policy defensible? If it is, what principled line distinguishes the deductions I accept from those I refuse, and how does the policy avoid licensing me to quarantine any inconvenient conclusion by recasting it as a skeptical denial? If the policy is confused, or if my question rests on a mistaken picture of inference or knowledge, say so plainly rather than trying to clean it up. Where your verdict genuinely outruns the considerations, I would rather you flag that than present a false decisiveness.
