I keep finding myself defending the view that naked statistical evidence shouldn't be enough to convict someone—or even find them civilly liable—even when the numbers are excellent. You know the cases: the blue bus one (80% of buses on the route are Blue Bus Co., a witness says a bus hit her but can't identify the colour) and the gatecrasher one (999 out of 1000 people at the rodeo didn't pay, we grab a random attendee). The probability the guy is guilty is .999 but almost nobody wants to convict on that alone, and I think that reluctance is tracking something real, not just a probability-illiteracy bug a good Bayesian should be embarrassed by. I know this is really a claim about what proof beyond a reasonable doubt requires. But I've been trying to steelman the other side, and I want to know what would actually count against my position. What would have to be true—empirically, conceptually, normatively—for the claim that statistical evidence is different in kind from individualized evidence to be wrong, or at least not doing the legal-epistemic work I think it is? I especially want to know whether the view is making predictions a pure Bayesian-in-the-legal-system account doesn't make, or whether it's just dressing up a policy preference about error costs and incentives in epistemic language. What would a defender of the distinction concede as a genuine strike against it, and what should she just shrug off? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. And be straight with me about whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability—and whether that would be a bug or a feature for a view like this.
