I am working on a set of questions about how a text can carry authority across languages, and I keep returning to two practices that seem to reject the usual assumption that only the original-language wording can be authoritative. The first is the ancient standing of the Septuagint: the Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures, which some Jewish communities of the Hellenistic period and many early Christian writers treated as Scripture in its own right rather than as a mere rendering, to the point that New Testament writers often appear to cite the Greek wording where it diverges from the Hebrew. The second is the modern King James Version-only movement, which treats the English of the KJV as the inspired and preserved word of God for English-speaking Christians and assigns the Hebrew and Greek texts behind it a merely subordinate role. I would like you to reconstruct the theory of language, meaning, and inspiration that these practices presuppose — not the theology I could look up elsewhere, but the implicit philosophy of language. Specifically: does treating a translation as inspired commit one to the view that inspiration attaches to the wording or verbal form rather than to the content, or can inspiration attach to one and the same meaning as it passes from language to language? What would have to be true about meaning for a translation to count as the same text as its source, and would the Septuagint tradition and KJV-onlyism give different answers to that question?

Then I want the same question pressed in the other direction. If one instead holds a strict original-only view — no translation is inspired, only the Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek originals are — what does that view itself presuppose about meaning? Does it covertly assume that wording is meaning, or that semantic content cannot survive translation except imperfectly? And what would actually have to change in practice if a community moved from one presupposed theory to the other: in how it reads, argues, corrects, and teaches the text? I would like you to distinguish what these practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with, to say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and to be candid about the points at which the practices and the philosophy of language come apart.
