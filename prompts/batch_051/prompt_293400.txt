Suppose a jurisdiction introduces a new appellate remedy, the Gettier appeal. A convicted defendant may move to vacate the conviction by showing, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the verdict was Gettiered: the prosecution's case relied essentially on at least one false intermediate claim, with the result that the factfinder's beliefs about the elements of the offense were both true and justified, but only luckily connected to the truth. The defendant need not show innocence, and the reviewing court is not to ask whether the verdict was factually correct; the only question is whether the chain of reasoning from the evidence to the verdict passed through a false lemma. A successful appeal vacates the conviction, and the prosecution may retry the case only if it does not rely on that false lemma.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than as an abstract normative proposal. Analyze how it would actually behave: what incentives it creates for prosecutors, police investigators, expert witnesses, defense counsel, and appellate judges; how litigation would come to define "essential" and "false"; where the rule would be gamed first; what secondary effects it would have on plea bargaining, testimony, and the presentation of evidence; and whether a materiality threshold or a harmless-error exception would repair its failure modes or merely relocate them.
