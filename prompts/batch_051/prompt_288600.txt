For a 110-minute guest session in a graduate software-engineering course (18 students, no legal background), design a structured role-play that makes participants feel — before the facilitator names it — why mandatory arbitration clauses in consumer and developer terms of service get called a privatization of justice. The participants are the kind of people who would ship such a clause inside a product, so the activity should put them on both sides of a dispute and let the structural asymmetry of private adjudication emerge from the rules, the incentives, and the text of the agreement rather than from an opening lecture. By the end, participants should be able to state in plain terms what changes about a claim when it moves out of a public court into a confidential, repeat-player-dominated private forum, and why that shift matters for enforcing small-value rights. The deliverable must be runnable as written and include: (a) the arbitration clause participants must navigate, written in plausible consumer-facing language; (b) roles with conflicting objectives and a clear set of allowed moves; (c) small real stakes — points, a pooled prize, or equivalent — so that choices fall where they actually cost; (d) a timed run of show; (e) at least a 20-minute debrief that maps concrete moments and decisions in the activity onto the mechanics of private adjudication, lets participants name the asymmetry they encountered before the facilitator explains it, and closes by stating the general claim the activity is meant to make felt; (f) an honest note on which features of real mandatory arbitration cannot be reproduced in a room and what the facilitator must supply at the end; and (g) a prediction of what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run, with concrete redirects. As gameplay mechanics, incorporate at least three of the following: a class-action waiver; arbitrator selection and payment by the platform; a confidentiality requirement; a repeat-player arbitrator whose prior rulings are visible only to the platform; a severability ruling that strikes one provision and then lets the platform amend the clause on short notice; an opt-out window almost everyone misses; and mass arbitration as a counter-move that makes per-case fees ruinous.
