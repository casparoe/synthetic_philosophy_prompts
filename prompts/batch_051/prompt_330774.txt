I am a novelist, and I am planning a book in which several chapters are narrated from the inside of systems that are not obviously conscious: a honeybee, a small free-living nematode whose nervous system of a few hundred neurons has been mapped in its entirety, and—in one deliberately experimental chapter—an old mechanical thermostat. Before I commit to this architecture, I want a philosophical stress test of the conceit, and I want it run through a variation on a familiar thought experiment.

Take Nagel's bat as the baseline. On the standard reading, the argument runs something like this: a bat has experiences; there is something it is like to be a bat; and no amount of objective description of echolocation, neural activity, or behavior can capture that subjective character, because it is accessible only from the bat's point of view. Now vary the thought experiment in one respect: instead of a single richly conscious mammal, run the same question down a series of decreasing complexity—bat, honeybee, nematode, thermostat—and do not assume in advance where, or whether, the series bottoms out.

Work through how the standard analysis changes under this variation. In particular:

(i) Which premises of the original bat case carry over to the honeybee and the nematode, and which begin to fail? Is the failure best described as reaching a point where the answer is simply no, or a point where the question—whether there is something it is like to be that thing—stops having a determinate sense?

(ii) Does treating the bat as the top of a continuum, rather than as a standalone case of alien sentience, strengthen, weaken, or reframe the original conclusion that objective description leaves something out? Does the gap between subjective and objective look sharper or blurrier?

(iii) Does what-it-is-like admit of degrees? If it does, is the thermostat the zero limit of the scale, or is it off the scale entirely? If it does not, where along the series does the threshold fall, and what feature—integration, valence, learning, having a point of view, something else—does the threshold track?

(iv) Briefly: does moral status move along the same gradient as what-it-is-like, or does it obey a different logic, such as a sharper threshold or a different kind of consideration altogether? This matters to me because the novel's stakes depend on which of these interiors the reader is asked to treat as morally real.

Do not resolve the empirical facts about bee or nematode cognition by assertion; I am asking for the conceptual analysis. I want a defended verdict on the conceit: is there a principled line below which writing from the inside is not a more difficult imaginative exercise but a category mistake, and, if so, where does that line fall for the chapters I am planning?
