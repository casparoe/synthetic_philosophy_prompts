I have been thinking on my own, without any formal background in decision theory, about a puzzle I know as the absent-minded driver, and I would like help with a version of it I have not seen discussed. In the version I know, a driver is on a road with two identical exits. When he reaches an exit he cannot remember which of the two exits he is at. At each exit he either continues or exits. Exiting at the first exit gives 0; continuing at the first exit and exiting at the second gives 4; continuing through both exits gives 1. Before the trip he can settle on a rule: at each exit, exit with probability p. The best such rule from the ex ante point of view is p = 1/3, with expected payoff 4/3. The puzzle is that, when he is actually at an exit, if he treats the two exits as equally likely to be the one he is at, then exiting has expected payoff 2 while continuing under his p = 1/3 rule has expected payoff 1.5, so he wants to depart from the rule he chose. One standard reply, as I understand it, is that equal probabilities are the wrong belief: he should condition on the fact that he is following the p = 1/3 rule, which makes it more likely that he is at the first exit than at the second, and once he does that the two actions are indifferent at p = 1/3.

Now I want to know how the analysis changes when there are three identical exits rather than two. Keep the same idea: exiting at the first exit gives 0, exiting at the second exit gives 0, exiting at the third exit gives 4, and missing all three gives 1. Before the trip the driver can commit to the rule that at each indistinguishable exit he will exit with probability p. Please work through the following. First, derive the ex ante optimal p and the expected payoff it gives. Second, at an exit, suppose the driver gives each of the three exits probability 1/3. Given that he otherwise follows his ex ante optimal rule, does he then prefer to exit or to continue, and what are the two expected payoffs? Third, instead suppose he conditions on the fact that he is following his ex ante optimal rule; what probabilities should he assign to being at the first, second, and third exit, and do exiting and continuing come out indifferent as in the two-exit case? Fourth, say whether the consistency resolution of the original puzzle carries over to the three-exit version unchanged, or needs adjustment, and whether the move from two exits to three changes anything qualitative about the puzzle.
