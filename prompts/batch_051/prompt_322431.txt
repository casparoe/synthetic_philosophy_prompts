I have been reverse-engineering a friend's implicit taxonomy of the emotions. She has no philosophical vocabulary and resists theorizing, so I have fallen back on case elicitation: I present a mental state, she rules on whether it is an emotion, and her rulings prove stable across repeated testing. After several months I have a corpus of settled verdicts and want to know what decision boundary underlies them before I collect the next batch.

Her settled verdicts are as follows:
- Fear upon seeing a snake on a hiking trail: an emotion.
- A startle jump at a door slamming behind her: not an emotion, just a reflex.
- A diffuse, objectless bad mood lasting an entire day: not an emotion, a mood.
- A fast flash of anger at a slight, arising before she could have articulated what the slight was: an emotion.
- Caffeine-induced jitteriness with no target: not an emotion.
- A wholly affectless preference for tea over coffee: not an emotion.
- Months-long regret over a career decision, returning episodically with a heavy, sinking quality: an emotion.
- Felt revulsion at a foul smell, with a recoil, before she could identify the smell: an emotion.

The next cases I would like predictions for are:
(a) She reads a dispassionate audit of a small organization, concludes that a director acted wrongly, and reports this in a flat tone with no felt or physiological response whatever. Is the evaluative conclusion itself an emotion?
(b) She develops a felt hunch that a new colleague is being dishonest — a queasy wariness — though she can articulate no evidence and does not judge that he is dishonest. Is the hunch an emotion?
(c) She spends a week in quiet but felt grief over the death of a fictional character whose story she has followed for years, while never once believing that any real person has died. Is the grief an emotion?

Predict her verdicts on (a) through (c). Then state the rule she seems to be running and identify the other rules that fit her earlier verdicts just as well — the live candidates strike me as some form of appraisal or judgment theory, a James-Lange-style feeling theory, and a perceptual or embodied-appraisal view. For each candidate rule, say what it predicts for the three new cases and which of (a) through (c) does the most work separating the candidates. If her settled verdicts underdetermine the rule, say so explicitly, and name the single cleanest follow-up case that would settle what remains open.
