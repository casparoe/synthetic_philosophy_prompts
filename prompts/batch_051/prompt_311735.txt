I'm preparing a handout for a working group on hinge epistemology, the family of positions inspired by Wittgenstein's On Certainty according to which some commitments function as hinges: they are not justified by evidence, but they make evidential assessment possible in the first place. The objections I am already covering are these. First, the truth objection: if hinges are not propositional or not truth-apt, it is unclear how they can enter into logical relations with the ordinary claims that rest on them, while if they are propositional, their possible falsehood seems to reopen the skeptical questions they were meant to close. Second, the arbitrariness objection: if hinges are held without evidential grounds, nothing non-circular distinguishes them from prejudice, ideology, or epistemic bad luck. Third, the closure objection: if I know something as ordinary as that I have hands, and that claim entails the hinge that there is an external world, then familiar closure principles either turn the hinge into something I know after all or force the hinge theorist to abandon closure. Fourth, the conversion objection: people sometimes revise or abandon apparently bedrock certainties through conversion, trauma, or theory change, so treating hinges as fixed endpoints of justification is suspect. Fifth, the mental-state objection: it is obscure what kind of psychological state a hinge commitment is if it is not an ordinary belief, and calling it a certainty or an attitude risks being a placeholder rather than an account. I want an objection that is not in that list and not a rewording, special case, or combination of anything in it. State the objection precisely, explain why it is not already covered by the five above, and then honestly assess whether it is any good. Answer in American English.
