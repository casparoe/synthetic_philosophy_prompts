Please give a careful intuitive explanation of a point about Bayesian convergence that tends to get flattened in the slogan that priors wash out. Consider two Bayesian agents who observe an indefinitely long sequence of observations drawn from the same unknown process. They share a likelihood, update by conditionalization, and hold priors that are mutually absolutely continuous. Under these conditions, a merging-of-opinions theorem tells us that their predictive distributions over future observations will merge. It does not tell us that their posterior probabilities over hypotheses will converge to one another. Explain, in plain but precise language, why the theorem concerns the merging of predictions rather than the merging of full posteriors. Your answer should do three things. First, state what mutual absolute continuity of priors amounts to and what work it does: what breaks when one agent's prior assigns zero probability to the parameter region that is actually generating the data. Second, give an intuitive example—for instance, a model with one identified parameter that drives the likelihood and a second parameter that leaves every observable distribution unchanged, where the agents' priors over the two parameters are independent—in which the agents' predictive distributions over future observations converge while their posterior probabilities over the second parameter remain exactly as far apart as their priors. Third, use the example to state a precise limitation on the washing-out idiom: along which dimensions of the model are priors forced to become irrelevant as data accumulate, and along which dimensions do they remain free indefinitely? Do not give a formal proof; the ordinary-language explanation should track the mathematical facts rather than merely gesture toward them.
