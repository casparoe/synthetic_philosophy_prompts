I'm writing the final for a small upper-level seminar, Recognition and Statehood in a Decentered World (it's cross-listed with political theory), and I want one essay question that tests whether students can actually do the recognition debate rather than just define it. Last year half the class turned in near-identical restatements of the declaratory/constitutive distinction and never explained why the choice matters, so this year I'm building the question around two invented cases and asking them to make the theories do real work. I'd like you to produce the complete exam item: a cleaned-up version of the question, a model answer of about 800 words, and a marking scheme. The exam block is 90 minutes and the question is worth 20 points, so please build the marking scheme around five must-hit points with the marks assigned to each, plus a one-line note on the mistake students typically make at each point.

Here's what I have so far. Cape Meridian declared independence from a larger federation fourteen years ago; it has defined borders, a settled population, courts and police that actually function, and it negotiates its own trade agreements, but it has received zero formal recognitions because its parent state and all the major powers find it politically convenient to look the other way, even though no one disputes that it runs its own affairs day to day. The Republic of Arva used to be an ordinary, widely recognized state, but for the past eleven years it has had no effective control over anything: its recognized government operates from exile in a neighboring state, a foreign military occupies most of its territory, two local militias hold the rest, yet more than a hundred states still recognize it and it keeps its seat in several international organizations. The rough question I keep tinkering with is something like: Using one or both cases, argue whether recognition is best understood as declaratory or constitutive, and say what your view implies about whether each entity is a state. In particular, confront the fact that a constitutive view seems to make statehood relative to whoever is doing the recognizing, while a declaratory view seems unable to explain why non-recognition plainly changes what an entity can actually do internationally.

On the model answer, please seperate what is genuinely settled in this literature from what is still contested among people who work on it, and don't present a contested position as the consensus; if the strongest defense of one view is itself controversial, it should read as one defensible position among others rather than the answer. Keep everything in American English.
