My friend Dana and I have been arguing for months about whether anything done to a character inside a video game could be morally wrong in its own right, as opposed to being wrong only because of how it affects the player or other real people. She rejects both simple extremes: she does not accept that the fact that they are only pixels settles every case, but she also does not treat game characters like persons. I have kept a record of her rulings on a batch of cases, and she insists that a single consistent rule lies behind them, even though I cannot extract it.

Her earlier rulings are as follows.

1. In a photorealistic action game, a human NPC with scripted objectives and relationships is tormented for fun; the character pleads, sobs, flinches, and shows wounds. Dana says this is clearly wrong, and treats it as about as serious as a game action can be.
2. In a 1980s-style arcade shooter, a player destroys rows of abstract alien sprites that blink on and off and then disappear, with no faces, voices, or behavior beyond moving down the screen. Dana says this is not a moral issue at all.
3. In a farming simulator, a cartoon animal companion is coded to have needs and to seek the player out; a player deliberately withholds food while it trembles, whimpers, and its health bar drains. Dana says this is wrong, but moderate, and much less serious than the photorealistic NPC case.
4. A simulated robot vacuum emits a flat low-battery tone when its charge runs out, but it has no face, no expressions, and no interests; it simply follows a mechanical routine. A player repeatedly switches it off mid-cycle for no practical reason. Dana says this is not a moral issue.
5. In a text-only interactive fiction game, a character with no image or sound has plans, relationships, and fears that the game tracks; the player destroys its home even as the character pleads through prose. Dana says this is wrong.

The new case Dana must rule on next week is this. In a horror game, a human-shaped training dummy flinches, whimpers, and appears to sob every time it is struck, with unusually realistic animation and audio. The game tracks no goals, memories, interests, or relationships for it; it is a stationary prop whose only behavior is recoiling from blows. A player sits there striking it over and over, for no in-game purpose and with no one else watching.

I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the rule behind it, so please do the following. First, lay out the most plausible rules that would fit all five of her earlier rulings. Second, say what each rule predicts about the dummy. Third, give your best guess about Dana's verdict, with your reasons for preferring one rule over the others. Fourth, name one further case, real or invented, that would cleanly settle which rule she is actually running, and say what each rule predicts for that case.

End your answer with a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{...}, where ... is your predicted verdict for the dummy, either wrong or not wrong. If you must qualify, keep the qualifier to three words or fewer.
