I'd like a reconstruction of the philosophical commitments implicit in psychiatric practice as an institution, because the practice looks to me as if it is trying to be a branch of medicine and a system of social control at the same time. Take the standard sequence: someone receives a DSM diagnosis on the basis of a checklist of reported behaviors and states, with no biomarker or lesion identified; that label authorizes insurance payment for treating an "illness"; it can justify involuntary commitment on the grounds that the person is dangerous to themselves and too impaired to decide; and in criminal law the same label can be used to remove or reduce responsibility, while in ordinary life the diagnosed person continues to be held fully responsible. What theory of mental disorder does this apparatus presuppose? Does it strictly require the view that mental disorders are diseases in roughly the sense that infections are, or is it compatible with a deflationary view on which a diagnosis is a practical label for a cluster of problems in living? Where does the practice become genuinely inconsistent, relying on a strong medical-realist picture in one setting and a pragmatic, norm-laden picture in another? And what would have to change in diagnosis, insurance, and the courts if one took the Szaszian view that mental illness is a myth — that the term is applied to behavior judged problematic rather than to any discovered bodily lesion, and that the cases so labeled are really either bodily disease or problems in living?
