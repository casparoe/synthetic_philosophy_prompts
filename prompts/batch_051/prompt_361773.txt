I'm a few years into an environmental econ PhD and I keep running into the same problem: everyone doing intergenerational justice and climate policy treats one move as obviously right — a discount rate, or a shrug at the non-identity problem, or weak sustainability as if it follows from justice, or the assumption that future people will value roughly what we value. Before I read another paper that builds a normative theory on one of those moves, I want the spec. What does a theory of intergenerational justice actually have to get right, for climate decisions specifically, to count as adequate?

Write the desiderata: the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse, the results from economics or climate science it can't contradict. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preference. At a minimum, the list has to say something about:

- the non-identity problem: if a policy changes who comes to exist, can it still wrong the people who end up existing, or does the theory have to go impersonal at that point (and is it allowed to)?
- pure time preference versus discounting for growth or opportunity cost. Which one is a genuine desideratum and which is a modeling convenience, and can the normative theory get away with not answering?
- deep uncertainty: about the shape of future welfare, about future people's preferences, about adaptation. What does respecting future people mean when there are no determinate people with determinate preferences yet, and their likely values might drift far from ours?
- the asymmetry between making existing or near-future people worse off and failing to create different, better-off future people. Does an adequate theory have to be person-affecting, impersonal, or something weirder? What breaks if you pick wrong?
- aggregation over an open-ended future: small harms to each of very many future people against large costs to a few present people, plus huge numbers of merely possible people. Does the theory stay sane if the future is indefinite?
- non-reciprocity: future people can't consent, compensate, threaten, or reciprocate, so what makes them more than a modeling artifact in a framework built for reciprocal agents?
- substitutability: natural versus produced capital, weak versus strong sustainability. Does a theory of justice have to rule on substitution possibilities, or is that an empirical question to keep separate?

Then do the two things I can't do myself. First, flag any pairs of these requirements that you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — I have a hunch that no-pure-time-preference plus aggregation over an indefinite future with nonzero catastrophic risk either explodes or hides arbitrary horizon choices, but I'd like to see it worked out. Second, say wich single requirement, if taken seriously, does the most to knock out the standard views.
