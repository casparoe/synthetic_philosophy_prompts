I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am drafting a column about the standards that govern flat-out assertion—when a person is entitled to state something as fact rather than hedge it or attribute it. Before I write, I want an audit of the working position I have settled into, because I can feel that parts of it pull against one another but I cannot locate the problem, and I suspect that at least one apparent conflict is only apparent.

My commitments are these:
(1) Assertion is governed by a knowledge norm: one is properly positioned to assert that p only if one knows that p.
(2) A reporter who publishes a carefully sourced claim that later turns out to be false has not violated any norm of assertion, though the record should be corrected.
(3) It can be entirely proper to report a claim on attribution—for example, that an official said that p—even when the reporter does not know whether p is true.
(4) A statement of the form 'p, but I do not know that p' is always improper to assert, even when p happens to be true.
(5) One cannot properly assert flat-out that one's lottery ticket has lost merely on the strength of the odds, however overwhelming those odds are.

Do these commitments hang together? Please give me exactly five numbered findings, ordered from the most severe tension to the least, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. For each finding, identify the commitments involved, say whether the tension is real or merely apparent, and state what would have to be given up to resolve it.
