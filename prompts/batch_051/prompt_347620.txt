I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I work on generative systems that produce images, music, and conversational agents. I recently came across this passage in G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica, Chapter VI:

“It is, therefore, possible that we cannot discover what the Ideal is. But it is plain that, though this possibility cannot be denied, no one can have any right to assert that it is realised—that the Ideal is something unimaginable. We cannot judge of the comparative values of things, unless the things we judge are before our minds.”

I understand that Moore goes on to treat the enjoyment of beautiful objects and personal affection as among the chief intrinsic goods. My question is whether his argument in this passage survives a development from my own field. Today a single generative model can put before my mind an effectively unlimited number of novel aesthetic objects: images, musical passages, written texts. A conversational agent can also put before me something that behaves like personal affection, though it is not another human. On one reading, this dissolves part of Moore's problem: the “before our minds” condition is now easier to satisfy, so we can sample a far larger space of candidate goods and compare them. On another reading, it makes his argument more urgent or more dangerous, because what is before my mind is generated in a way that may bias the comparison or flood it with superficially varied but structurally homogeneous candidates. I want to know what happens to the argument, not to any policy question. Does Moore's argument still go through under this development? Does the development undercut a specific premise, repair it, or leave it standing? In particular, does his treatment of beauty and friendship still have force when the beautiful objects and the affectionate-seeming companion are synthetic? Please identify which premise the development bears on and whether the argument can be restored in some modified form.
