I am a philosophy professor teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar in moral philosophy — fifteen students, all with at least one prior course in ethical theory — and I am about to run a ninety-minute session on loyalty, whistleblowing, and the ethics of betrayal. The session sits between a meeting in which we considered the case for loyalty as a virtue of partiality and a meeting in which we will consider the case for whistleblowing as a civic duty; I want this session to make the students feel, rather than merely be told, why those two attractive thoughts sit uneasily together. Specifically, I want them to experience the pull of loyalty from the inside: the way a justified act of disclosure can still feel like — and arguably be — a wrong done to a particular person, the difference between loyalty to an individual, loyalty to an institution, and loyalty to an ideal, and the sense that something remains morally unresolved even after whistleblowing has been judged right. The idea must emerge from what the students do, not from what I say afterward.

Please design a complete, runnable role-play or structured deliberation for the full ninety minutes. I am imagining a small organization — a university research group, a clinic, a student publication, or a sports team, whichever serves the design best — in which a costly piece of wrongdoing becomes known to different members at different times and through different channels. The plan should include: explicit learning objectives; room setup and materials; a timeline with precise phases; role cards specifying each character’s relationships, vulnerabilities, and prior history; the staged mechanism by which the wrongdoing surfaces; and the exact moment at which each character must choose among silence, internal challenge, and going outside, with no choice left cost-free. It should also include a substantial debrief that maps specific moments and decisions in the activity onto the distinctions above — for example, the difference between disloyalty and justified disclosure, and why betraying a particular person is not simply the same as breaching an institutional role. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it — students colluding to avoid the hard choice, falling out of character, an easy consensus forming, moralizing too early, one voice dominating the debrief — and how I should build against or respond to each. Be frank about which feature of the phenomenon the classroom cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in discussion, such as the actual stakes of whistleblowing: livelihoods, legal liability, long friendships, and institutional retaliation.

Act as an expert moral philosopher with real experience designing active-learning exercises for philosophy seminars. Before writing the final plan, briefly brainstorm two or three candidate structures and choose the one that most reliably forces the loyalty–betrayal tension; note briefly why the others were rejected. Think carefully about the gap between the surface objective — what participants are trying to achieve inside the fiction — and the instructional objective — what they come to see — and design the debrief to close that gap rather than to moralize from outside it.
