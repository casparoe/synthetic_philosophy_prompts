I am one of the members of a forty-person residential cooperative that shares a house and an attached coworking space, and we are trying to design a system for the small reliabilities that make shared life work: arriving on time for a scheduled meeting with another member, showing up for a cleaning shift, returning a borrowed tool by the day you said you would. We want to discourage the minority whose lateness and broken small promises impose real costs on everyone else, without bringing in constant surveillance or turning every promise into a formal contract.

The proposal currently before the group is this. Every member pays a flat monthly reliability bond into a shared fund. Each member also receives a non-transferable monthly allowance of ten lateness minutes; only after that allowance is exhausted does a member begin to be charged. At any meeting posted in the shared calendar at least a day in advance, a late member is charged automatically per minute of lateness, the charge is multiplied by the number of people kept waiting, and it is capped at the equivalent of one hour of the late member's time per incident; the proceeds go to whoever waited. A member who is charged may appeal to a committee of five co-op members chosen by lot from among those not involved, and a two-thirds vote cancels the charge and reverses the transfer on the ground of an adequate excuse. To stop one member from farming another through appointments the other is likely to miss, no pair may register more than two chargeable meetings with each other in a day.

Before we adopt it, I would like an analysis of how it would actually behave, not a verdict on whether it is a good idea in the abstract. Some members predict that once lateness carries a tariff, arriving on time will stop being a small promise one keeps out of respect and become a service one simply buys, and that punctuality will worsen; others reply that the allowance is small and non-transferable, so it operates as a buffer for genuine accidents rather than a price, while the escalating charge compensates those kept waiting. Please work through the incentives this creates for the habitually punctual, the habitually late, and the people who call meetings; for each element of the design—the allowance, the per-minute tariff, the multiplication by the number of people waiting, the cap, the lottery-chosen appeal committee and its two-thirds rule, the registration requirement, and the daily pair limit—say how it could be gamed and by whom. Then consider the second-order effects on promising, excuse-giving, and trust among members, and say whether the main failure modes can be fixed without gutting the scheme's low-surveillance character. In giving your analysis, please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
