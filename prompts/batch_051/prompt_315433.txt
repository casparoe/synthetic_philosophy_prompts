I am working through the standard threefold taxonomy in the Christian theology of religions and keep tripping over a case that seems to sit exactly on one of its fault lines. As I was taught it, positions about salvation can be sorted by four questions. First, saving origin: is salvation ultimately brought about by the person and events of one particular tradition — for Christians, the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth — or can it originate through more than one revelation or agency? Second, actual scope: can some people be saved who never explicitly affirm the truth of that one tradition? Third, explicit faith: is conscious assent to that tradition's central claims a necessary condition of salvation? Fourth, status of other traditions: are other religious traditions ever genuine, divinely intended vehicles of salvation, or at most settings in which something salvific happens to them incidentally? On the textbook use of the labels, exclusivism answers yes to the explicit-faith question, no to the scope question and to the vehicles question, and takes the saving origin to be a unique particular revelation. Inclusivism keeps a unique saving origin but answers yes to scope, no to explicit faith, and grants other traditions at most a derivative or instrumental role. Pluralism denies that the saving origin must be unique, answers yes to scope, no to explicit faith, and yes to other traditions as genuine vehicles.

Now consider a position I will call Logos-identity universality. It holds that the divine Logos that was later incarnate in Jesus has never been absent from creation and has always been present in the religious life of every culture. Other religions, in their own practices of prayer, self-discipline, and love of neighbor, can really unite people to this very Logos; they are not merely preparations for the gospel. A Buddhist or a Platonist can, on this view, be saved through the Logos as mediated by her own tradition. At the same time, the position insists that there is only one saving agent, namely the Logos that became incarnate in Jesus, and that everyone who is saved is saved by that same agent, and in that sense through Christ — even though most are not saved through anything Jesus of Nazareth did or taught during his historical life and do not know the agent under that name.

My question is whether, on the four criteria above, Logos-identity universality is best classified as inclusivism or as pluralism, or whether it is a stable intermediate view that shows the threefold taxonomy needs reworking. Work through the criteria one by one, say explicitly where they underdetermine the verdict, and conclude by saying whether the difficulty lies in the case or in the criteria. In doing so, keep separate what is settled about how these labels are standardly applied from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this material, and do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus. Treat this as a conceptual exercise; I am not asking for sources. After the table, give a short paragraph stating the overall classification you would defend and whether the taxonomy needs a revised or fourth category.
