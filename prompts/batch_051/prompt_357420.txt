I have been working through Anselm's Proslogion alongside Gaunilo's reply, and I am trying to put together a complete map of the objections to Anselm's ontological argument, rather than just the two or three that get repeated in class. Here is the passage from Gaunilo of Marmoutiers, In Behalf of the Fool, section 2, that I keep returning to, because it isolates the step of the argument I find hardest to defend:

Unless indeed it is shown that this being is of such a character that it cannot be held in concept like all unreal objects, or objects whose existence is uncertain: and hence I am not able to conceive of it when I hear of it, or to hold it in concept; but I must understand it and have it in my understanding; because, it seems, I cannot conceive of it in any other way than by understanding it, that is, by comprehending in my knowledge its existence in reality.

As I read him, Anselm's proof moves through these steps: (i) even the fool understands the phrase for the being than which nothing greater can be conceived; (ii) whatever is understood is in the understanding; (iii) it is greater to exist in reality than in the understanding alone; so (iv) if that being existed only in the understanding, we could conceive of something greater than it, namely the same being existing in reality; therefore (v) it exists in reality.

The list of objections I have collected so far is this:

(1) Gaunilo's unreal-objects objection, the one stated in the quoted passage: on Anselm's principle, every false or imaginary object that can be mentioned and understood would likewise be in the understanding, so merely being in the understanding cannot single out God from a fiction.

(2) Gaunilo's perfect-island parody: if Anselm's form of reasoning were sound, it would equally prove a greatest conceivable island; so the reasoning either is invalid or rests on a suppressed premise that applies to God but not to islands.

(3) The equivocation objection pressed in Gaunilo's painter example: a thing that exists in the artist's understanding before it is painted exists only as knowledge in the artist's mind, not as a picture; Anselm appears to slide from conceptual existence to real existence without a bridge.

(4) Kant's objection that existence is not a real predicate: existence adds no new content to the concept of a thing, so it cannot be one of the properties in virtue of which one thing is greater than another.

(5) The objection that the proposition is not self-evident to us because we do not know the divine essence — Aquinas's sort of point, whether or not aimed directly at Anselm: the fool can have the words in mind without having in mind a content rich enough to make the denial visibly contradictory.

(6) The no-greatest-member objection: for many scales there is no possible maximum, as with numbers or islands, so Anselm owes us an argument that the description of the being than which nothing greater can be conceived picks out a possible object rather than an incoherent superlative. This is related to the island parody, but I count it separately because its target is the standing of the initial description rather than the validity of the inference.

Give me another objection to Anselm's argument, one that is not on this list and not a restatement, specialization, or combination of anything on it. I would like the strongest version of it you can give. Then do two things with it. First, identify which of Anselm's steps it attacks — the step Gaunilo targets in the quoted passage, or a different one — and say how. Second, tell me honestly whether it is any good: can Anselm, or Anselm's own reply to Gaunilo, meet it, or does it expose something the other objections do not?
