You are a professional philosopher with expertise in vagueness, philosophy of mind, and animal ethics. Please think this through slowly: begin by brainstorming the possible ways of blocking the argument, then settle on the response you regard as most defensible, and say why.

I have been arguing with a colleague about whether moral considerability could really be graded all the way down, and our dispute kept taking the shape of the sorites paradox. In its heap form: one grain is not a heap; adding a single grain cannot turn a non-heap into a heap; so no number of grains makes a heap. Now run the same form of argument on moral status. Imagine a sequence of 10,000 possible systems. System 0 has an information-processing architecture in the same rough class as an adult human's; system 10,000 is a thermostat. Each neighboring pair differs by one ten-thousandth of the total functional distance between those endpoints—for instance, a slightly shorter window over which information is held together, a slightly sparser network of pain-detecting pathways, a slightly weaker learning rule—so that along the way the systems pass through stages broadly comparable to a dog, an octopus, a honeybee, and a simple worm. It seems arbitrary to say that system k is a moral patient while system k+1, differing only in one such micro-change, is not a moral patient at all. But then induction forces the thermostat to count as a full moral patient—which seems absurd—and refusing to take one more step seems no less arbitrary.

What I want you to assess is whether transposing the sorites from "heap" to "moral patient" changes the costs of the standard solutions, and which solution emerges in the least bad shape. Work through at least these four: epistemicism (there is a sharp but unknowable cutoff); supervaluationism (borderline cases are neither determinately true nor false, but correspond to multiple permissible sharpenings); degree-theoretic or many-valued logic (truth, and perhaps moral status, comes in degrees); and contextualism (the standard for "sentient" or "moral patient" shifts with the practical context). Is a sharp hidden threshold more or less plausible for consciousness and moral status than for heaps? Does supervaluationism leave us with genuinely indeterminate obligations—and if so, is that tolerable in ethics the way it may be in philosophy of language? If a degree view simply says the thermostat has an infinitesimal, practically negligible degree of moral status, does that dissolve the paradox or only relocate it into problems of aggregation, where many tiny statuses might sum to outweigh one large one? Finally, consider the strongest resistance to the whole setup: rejecting the tolerance premise outright, either because sentience might emerge as a biological phase transition rather than by degrees, or because moral patiency is not one linear dimension and any single ordered chain is a contrivance. Which block is strongest, and what does it cost the rest of the view? End with a definite verdict.
