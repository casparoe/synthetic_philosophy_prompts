I have been trying to settle whether relief theory can be absorbed into benign violation theory. Here is an argument that it can.

(1) Consider a paradigmatic case of relief humour: laughter after a perceived danger turns out to be harmless, such as a menacing shape in a dark room that is revealed to be a coat draped over a chair.

(2) The situation, as it is experienced at the moment of laughter, is appraised as a violation: the lingering sense that something was wrong, threatening, or taboo remains part of the content of the experience.

(3) That same situation is simultaneously appraised as benign: the subject now sees that she is safe, that the apparent danger is harmless, and that the tension can be released.

(4) Benign violation theory says that humour occurs when a situation is simultaneously appraised as a violation and as benign.

(5) So, by (2) through (4), the relief case satisfies the two conditions that benign violation theory treats as jointly responsible for humour.

(6) Therefore, relief humour is a special case of benign violation humour, and relief theory does not pick out a genuinely distinct kind of humour.

I suspect the argument is too quick, especially at the step where a violation appraisal and a benign appraisal are assigned to one and the same situation, but I cannot say exactly where it goes wrong. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails? Give the strongest reading of that step and say whether the argument goes through on that reading. If the argument is valid, is the reduction as damaging to relief theory as the conclusion says, or could a relief theorist accept the whole argument and still hold that relief theory explains something benign violation theory does not?
