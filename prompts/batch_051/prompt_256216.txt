I chair the three-member accountability panel of a small, worker-owned software cooperative. We must decide before next week's general assembly whether to expel one of our worker-owners, M., for disclosing closed-session material to a journalist. The facts are not in dispute among us. The cooperative's principal product schedules home-care visits for a municipal agency; its travel-time logic, though legal and common in the sector, systematically underestimates time spent driving between clients, so the agency's home-care workers are underpaid for the time they actually work. The leadership group knew this, voted in closed session not to revise the logic before contract renewal because it would weaken our bid, and decided to keep the full membership in the dark until the renewal was securely ours. M., who sat on our ethics review circle, pressed the matter internally for two months, was told the membership would be informed only after the renewal, judged that delay tantamount to another two-year term of underpayment, and sent the minutes and her internal memos to a reporter. The resulting story led the agency to freeze the renewal and reopen bidding; we lost the contract and several of our worker-owners are being laid off; and the vendor that replaced us appears to use the same travel-time convention, so the home-care workers M. meant to protect are no better off.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our discussion. It should recommend a concrete course of action — expel M., retain her with a formal finding that she breached her obligations to the cooperative, or fully vindicate her — together with any conditions and a rationale the general assembly could actually vote on. Do not settle into a flat whistleblower-versus-traitor framing. I want the opinion to work through three harder questions: whether loyalty to fellow owners keeps moral weight even if the disclosure was justified, and whether an act can be both the right thing to do and a genuine betrayal; whether we should judge M.'s decision as it appeared when she made it or in light of how it turned out; and whether her having bypassed a democratic body's own, admittedly slow, accountability procedure cuts in either direction. Give the strongest case for whichever position loses its due, and do not rest the decision on what whistleblower-protection law requires — that is not what we are deciding.
