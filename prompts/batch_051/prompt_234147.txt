I teach an introductory evening course for adults titled The Stoic Revival, eighteen students, seventy-five-minute sessions. We are about to read the Enchiridion of Epictetus. In the translation I assign, the opening chapter denies what most of the class will bring through the door: it says, in paraphrase, that some things are up to us—judgment, impulse, desire, aversion, and in a word whatever is our own doing—while body, property, reputation, and office are not up to us; and that mistaking the second class for the first is what makes people obstructed, aggrieved, and ready to blame gods and men. I want the students to hit that distinction in their own behavior before I let them read it. Design a seventy-five-minute activity for eighteen adults, with no opening lecture, in which a game or structured exercise leads them to try to control, trade, or protect something Epictetus classifies as not up to them, and to discover through the failures and friction of the game where their actual room for action was. The rules should be simple enough to run without a projector and with only paper, index cards, and a whiteboard; stakes can be points, roles, or status in the room, not money. The crucial requirement is that they feel the collapse of a control claim before the distinction is named.

Then, in the debrief, map specific moments in the game back to the Epictetus passage: where participants mistook what was not up to them for what was; where impulse and aversion remained in their power even when the external object did not; where they blamed another player for something that was, on the passage’s terms, already foreign. Be explicit about what a group activity cannot reproduce: public opinion, bodily vulnerability, and long-term status are not really in the room. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—students treating it as a pure competition, one person spectacularly confusing the categories and dominating discussion, or the exercise producing cynicism rather than recognition—and how I should handle those failures without turning the debrief into a sermon.
