I'm not an academic or anything, but I've been turning this over for a couple years and I'd like outside eyes on it, because I cant tell anymore whether it's a real idea or just a tidy list.

I lived in Japan for a while and now I work mostly with Dutch colleagues. The two styles of handling difficult truths keep messing with my head. In Japan a bad presentation would never be called bad to my face; instead I'd get indirection, something like "perhaps you'd like to reconsider the middle part," and I learned to hear it. My Dutch colleagues just say "the middle part didn't work." It stings, but afterward I know exactly where I stand.

Here's my working idea. When we ask "are white lies wrong?" we treat white lies as one thing, but I think they're at least four different jobs:

1. Protecting someone from a truth they can't use at all (telling a woman with advanced dementia that her husband has just stepped out when he died years ago).
2. Protecting someone from a truth they could use but aren't ready to hear yet ("your writing isn't publishable yet" softened into "it's almost there").
3. Protecting the speaker while pretending it's for the hearer ("I'm busy," when the truth is that I don't want to go).
4. Ritual fictions that nobody takes literally ("no, no, it was no trouble").

I think Dutch-style directness and Japanese-style indirectness aren't more or less honesty on a single dial. They put the politeness in different layers. Dutch directness is still polite, but the politeness is in the framing: I'm telling you this because I respect you enough not to waste your time. Japanese indirectness puts the politeness in the content and in the silence around it. So Dutch bluntness isn't honesty minus politeness; it's a different politeness.

The part that nags at me is that the two styles might be good at different jobs. I suspect indirectness handles job 2 better, because it gives the hearer room to save face and come to the hard truth on their own schedule. Directness handles jobs 3 and 4 better, because self-serving disguises and ritual fictions are harder to hide, and easier to drop, when everyone is committed to plain content. And neither style has a special advantage on job 1, since in the dementia case you aren't really lying to the person in the normal sense at all; you're living inside their reality.

So the essay I'd write would argue that "should we be more Dutch or more Japanese about difficult truths?" is the wrong question. The styles specialize in different jobs, and the best norm would be a deliberate mix: direct content with indirect timing, say, or directness by default but indirectness for face-threatening truths the hearer can't be expected to metabolize on the spot. I'm not sure any real culture has that mix.

I'm worried about two things. One, the four-way split might be the kind of thing that feels deep because it's a list, not because it cuts reality where it matters. Two, I'm treating two huge countries as single personalities, which is exactly the thing I hate when people do it about my background.
