I cover evolutionary biology's long-running conceptual disputes, and I have been sitting in on an editorial-board argument at a major ecology and evolution journal. The board is debating a submission rule aimed at the tautology objection: the worry that fitness, when it is measured by which organisms actually survive and reproduce, is then invoked to explain why those very organisms survived and reproduced, leaving the explanation circular. The proposed rule says that any manuscript offering fitness or natural selection as an explanation must state, before presenting its outcome data, an independent operational definition of fitness as a propensity—a measure of expected reproductive success derived from the organisms' traits, life history, and environment—and must show that this prospective measure, rather than the observed survival and reproduction in the study, does the explanatory work. The editors concede the rule is far easier to state than to satisfy; one faction believes it will force genuine predictive content into adaptationist work, while another believes it will simply make authors dress the same reasoning in more algebra.

I am trying to understand how the rule would actually play out if adopted, rather than whether it is admirable in principle. Where would it bite hardest, and which kinds of studies—long-term field projects that infer selection from recapture rates, laboratory evolution experiments, comparative phylogenetic work—would be forced to change most? How would researchers game or quietly evade it, for example by gerrymandering trait-based proxies, by relabeling fitness as viability or performance, or by choosing study systems whose life histories make propensity estimates tractable? Would the original circularity be solved, or would it simply reappear at the level of how the propensities themselves are estimated and justified? And what would the rule's characteristic failure mode be after a few years?
