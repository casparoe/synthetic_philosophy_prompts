For the last couple weeks my partner and I have been making Bongard problems for our kid on index cards — you know, rows of little diagrams where the left-hand ones all fit some concept and the right-hand ones all dont, and the point is to guess the concept. It started as a car-trip game, but it's got me actually puzzled. Last week I drew one where the left cards all had three shapes touching each other and the right cards had three shapes separated; our kid got it immediately. Then I made a harder one where the left cards were all 'fuzzy' in a way I couldn't pin down — something about the shapes looking like they'd been drawn left-handed, or slanted, or not quite parallel — and all three of us could sort new cards correctly while completely failing to say what the rule was. That started a fight with a friend of mine. His line is that finding a Bongard concept just is extracting a hidden rule, a necessary-and-sufficient condition, and if you can't state the rule you havent actually got the concept yet. I think that's backwards: I think we anchor to a few examples and judge new ones by holistic resemblance, and the clean rule you state afterward is usually a rationalization layered on top of something messier.

I'm writing an essay for a small philosophy zine I contribute to, and I want it to actually take a side. So: write an essay on what Bongard-style problems reveal about how we induce concepts from a few examples. Weigh the rule-based/definitional picture against the similarity-to-exemplars/prototype picture. Use concrete mini-cases where they pull apart — like cases where someone sorts correctly but can't state a rule, or where a confidently stated rule mispredicts a new card. Don't end with 'both sides have something right' or 'it's just two descriptions of the same thing.' Defend a definite verdict, even if it's a qualified one (like 'explicit rules are a late, optional overlay on a mostly similarity-driven process, except in these domains...'). I'd rather you overstate slightly than leave it as a standoff.
