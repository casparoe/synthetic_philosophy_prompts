I'm in a historiography course and a classmate handed around an argument that seems to show Ranke's ideal is empty and that no historical account is more objective than any other. The conclusion strikes me as much too strong, but I couldn't find the flaw while reading it, and I want to know exactly which step to reject and what I would be paying. The argument runs as follows. (1) Ranke's dictum sets the aim of history as presenting the past as it actually was, free of later interests. (2) The past, even a single event, contains indefinitely many true statements, so every finite history must select some facts and omit others. (3) Which facts get stated cannot be settled by the facts themselves, since the omitted truths are just as true as the included ones. (4) Selection therefore has to be guided by something outside the facts: the historian's present standards of significance. (5) Those standards vary with the historian's period, commitments, and narrative conventions—this is where Carr's point about the historian selecting facts and Hayden White's point about emplotment are supposed to apply. (6) So what enters a history is always shaped partly by the historian's present standpoint rather than by the past itself. (7) Therefore no history presents the past as it actually was; and since every history is equally a product of some standpoint, there is no fact of the matter about which history is more faithful to the past. Ranke's ideal is empty.

I would much rather keep the Rankean aim in (1) than accept (7), but I can't tell whether the fault lies in (3), in (4), or in the move from (6) to (7). Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? And what does rejecting the relevant step cost respectively for Ranke, for Carr, and for White?
