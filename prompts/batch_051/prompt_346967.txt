I'm a retired physicist who's been reading political science for the last year or so, and there's something in Fearon's rationalist explanations for war that I can't get straight. My understanding is that Fearon starts from the observation that war is always ex post inefficient: once the fighting ends, there was always some division of the disputed pie that both sides would have preferred to the war itself. So if both sides are rational, they should be able to find that bargain beforehand and avoid the fight. Doesn't that make the whole framework a theory of why war is irrational, in the sense of states failing to take a deal they'd prefer?

If that's right, then I'm confused about why the same framework is so often used to predict that states or firms will rationally race on AI, possibly all the way to disaster. People say, roughly, that even if everyone would prefer a pause, no one can afford to pause while rivals keep going — and they call that a rationalist story. But if rational actors can't be counted on to take the bargain they'd prefer over war, why would we predict a race at all? Isn't the AI race arguement either misapplying Fearon or smuggling in irrationality? What am I missing?
