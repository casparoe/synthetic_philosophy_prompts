Two people in my philosophy of mind study group got into it after someone told a hiking story. Ana said she'd been certain there was a snake on the trail, and that the stick genuinely looked different to her in that moment — it seemed to move. Ben said that isn't cognitive penetration; it's fear making her interpret an unchanged visual experience as a snake, the way anyone startles at a rope in a dim garage. Ana replied that he can't anounce from outside what her experience was like, and that his view would predict expectations never change how things look. Ben said that's not his view: expectations obviously change where you look and what you conclude. The interesting claim is narrower — that with the same stimulus, a belief can change the experience itself, without sneaking in through attention or judgment. Her snake case doesn't show that, because fear changed her attention, not her visual system. Ana said the 'same stimulus' clause is rigged to make her view unfalsifiable, and once you admit beliefs shift attention and attention shifts what it's like to see, you've already admitted cognition shapes seeing. Ben said that just collapses her claim into the truism that people look before they leap. Ana said the real question is whether perception can be a neutral check on belief, and if it can't, then his 'truism' is exactly the point. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Go through their exchange and adjudicate move by move: who is right about which point, and what are both of them missing?
