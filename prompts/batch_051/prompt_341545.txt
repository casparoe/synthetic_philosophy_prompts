OK so I'm a high schooler in this summer philosophy reading group and we just did William James's essay "The Will to Believe." I can't stop thinking about one line in it:

> He who refuses to embrace a unique opportunity loses the prize as surely as if he tried and failed.

He's talking about how passing up a one-shot thing is equivalent to trying it and failing — the outcome is the same loss. But then he has that whole bit later about the two great laws of the knowing mind: believe truth and shun error, and he says they are not the same commandment. In actual life though, we absolutely use them differently. If a person believes something false with weak evidence, we say he's gullible or irrational. If a person refuses to believe something true with the same weak evidence, we say he's cautious, maybe a good critical thinker. I was thinking of court cases: a juror who wrongly convicts vs a juror who wrongly acquits. Or medicine: we freak out if someone takes a fake supplement too seriously, but if they dismiss a real treatment early on, no one calls them epistemically blameworthy. Now in accuracy-first epistemology, the only epistemic value is accuracy. So a false belief and a missed true belief are both just failures of accuracy: they should get the exact same score. But our practices don't act like that. We treat accepting a falsehood as a much bigger epistemic sin than rejecting a truth. Is there something in epistemic utility theory that actually justifies this asymmetry, or is it just leftover Jamesian "will to believe" psychology that we've dressed up as rationality? And if you defend the asymmetry, don't just say practical stakes differ — hold the practical stakes fixed and tell me why the epistemic charge is different.
