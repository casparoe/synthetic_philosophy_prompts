In Some Thoughts Concerning Education, Locke seems to endorse two different pictures of the child. On one hand, he maintains that children should be treated as rational creatures and that the central aim of education is to bring them under the direction of reason. On the other hand, his practical program builds early morality through repetition, habit, the management of esteem and disgrace, and deference to parental authority before the child is in a position to appreciate the underlying reasons. Give an account of how these two strands are supposed to fit together. In particular, explain whether the early non-rational formation should be understood as merely a necessary stage on the way to rational self-governance, or whether it introduces a standing tension in Locke's view: if the tutor's authority shapes the child's habits without the child's rational endorsement of that process, what makes the resulting character one of rational self-government rather than successful conditioning?
