I’m working through Aristotle’s On the Soul, book 3 chapter 3, the section where he’s trying to pin down phantasia, usually translated as imagination or appearance. My translation is clunky and I don’t trust it, so I’ll paraphrase the paragraph I’m stuck on instead of quoting it. He seems to say phantasia is neither perception nor belief/judgment, but it is the thing in virtue of which an image comes to be present to us. Then almost immediately he says something like: thinking cannot happen without phantasmata, or the thinking soul never thinks without images. I can’t tell whether he means that as a strong requirement or just a frequent accompaniment.

Here’s what confuses me. If phantasia is a kind of perceptual residue, something like an after-image or a stored sensory imprint, why would conceptual thought need that? Is he saying thought literally needs images the way perception needs objects, or is he saying that whenever we think, some sensory appearance is just along for the ride? And he also says elsewhere that phantasia is up to us, something we can produce at will, unlike perception—but how can that be true if phantasia is itself caused by prior perception? I feel like I’m missing the exact dependency structure he’s drawing between perception, phantasia, and thinking. Help me work out what he can consistently be saying about how images get into thought without making thought just a fancy kind of perceiving.
