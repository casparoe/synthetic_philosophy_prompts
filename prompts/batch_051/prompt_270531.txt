I'm in a bit of an argument with a friend about statutes of limitations, and I keep coming back to the standard faded-evidence defense of them. The argument, roughly, is that the state is permitted to let prosecutions lapse because time corrodes the evidentiary basis of a fair trial: witnesses forget, memories get contaminated, documents go missing, alibis become impossible to verify. So a trial held long after the fact is no longer reliable enough to justify punishment, and forcing someone to defend against a stale accusation is unfair for the same reason. That rationale felt really compelling when I first learned it, but it also seems like it was built for a world where memory and paper were the primary storage technologies. Now a huge share of our lives is logged in forms that don't decay the way a witness does: texts, emails, location histories, financial records, cloud backups, photos, metadata. Some of this even gets easier to search as the tools improve. So I'm trying to figure out what happens to the faded-evidence argument. Does the development actually kill the argument, leaving statutes of limitations to be justified only by other considerations? Or does the argument survive with a repaired premise, for instance because digital evidence has its own failure modes and the unfairness of stale accusations was never purely about reliability? Or does it survive largely unchanged because what "timely" punishment protects is something social rather than evidentiary? I want you to work through which premise the new development really bears on, and whether the argument should be declared dead, repaired, or left standing in another form.
