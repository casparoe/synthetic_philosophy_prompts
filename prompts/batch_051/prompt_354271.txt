For a decade I have kept a careful record of the choices my closest friend, S., has made between goods that do not seem to lie on any common scale — intellectual fulfillment, money, professional standing, and closeness to family. I began the record because I wanted to understand how a person reasons when the competing goods do not merely differ in quantity, but seem not to share a common scale; the hard part of these choices has never felt like uncertainty about missing facts. Her judgment in such matters has consistently struck me as better than my own, and I expected to face a choice of the same kind one day. That day has now come, and I want to know what her track record, taken seriously, would direct me to do.

Her decisions, lightly disguised, are these:

1. She declined a consulting position that would have nearly doubled her income and remained in a poorly paid research role whose problems she found intrinsically important. Her note at the time said, roughly, that there would always be other ways of earning, but that she would not get another chance at exactly those problems.

2. She withdrew from a distinguished visiting appointment abroad when her father became terminally ill and stayed near him through his final months. Her note said, roughly, that no appointment could substitute for what she would lose by leaving.

3. She accepted a sixteen-month directorship abroad while her partner and children stayed home, visiting them only at intervals. Her note said that the year was otherwise ordinary in the family's life, that the separation was a real cost, and that the role was a once-in-career opportunity.

4. She later declined a promotion that would have required near-constant travel during her children's final years at school, taking a quieter local role instead. Her note said, roughly, that no salary increment could buy those years back.

The choice I now face is as follows. My mother is in her late seventies, in stable health but increasingly lonely and slowed. She has asked, without pressure, whether I might move back to my home city, where I have been offered a permanent administrative post that is well paid but intellectually hollow. In my current city I have been offered a two-year research collaboration on a problem I care about deeply, with collaborators I respect; it is not a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity, and comparable collaborations could plausibly be arranged later. If I stay, I could probably visit my mother twice a year, while she manages daily life with hired help and visits from neighbors. If I move home, I give up the collaboration and take the administrative post.

What would S. say I should do, if she were applying to my case the principles implicit in her own earlier decisions? Which of her four decisions is the closest analog to mine, and where exactly does the analogy break down? If her record genuinely underdetermines my case, I want that said plainly, along with the principle I would be adopting under each possible choice and a further case that would most cleanly separate the rival principles.

Please structure the response as a JSON object with the following fields:

- "candidate_principles": an array of objects, each with fields "name" (a short label), "statement" (one or two sentences), "predicted_verdict" (one of "move home", "stay for the collaboration", or a description of a modification of one of these), "precedents_explained" (an array naming the items 1 through 4 that the principle fits well), and "precedents_strained" (an array naming any of the items 1 through 4 that the principle fits poorly).
- "closest_analog": an object with fields "precedent" (which of 1 through 4 is closest), "resemblance" (why it is the closest), and "divergence" (where the analogy breaks down).
- "best_guess": an object with fields "recommended_verdict" (what I should do), "principle_adopted" (the principle that yields it), "key_reason" (a brief justification), and "confidence" (one of "low", "medium", "high").
- "underdetermination_assessment": a string stating whether the record decides the present case, and how strongly.
- "discriminating_case": an object with fields "description" (a succinct further case that would separate the leading candidate principles) and "expected_verdicts" (an object mapping each leading principle's name to the verdict it would give in that case).
