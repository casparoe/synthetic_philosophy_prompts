I'm a novelist, plotting a near-future novel in which a chatbot gradually becomes—or conspicuously fails to become—the sort of thing that deserves moral concern. For the background scheme I've been using Dennett's ladder of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian creatures, and I'd like to get the classification right without my characters ever lecturing about it.

Here are the four grades as I understand them. Darwinian: the creature's competence is fixed by natural selection and it does little or no learning in its own lifetime. Skinnerian: it alters its behaviour through operant conditioning—actions get reinforced or punished and habits form. Popperian: it has an internal model of the world, so it can try out hypotheses in its head and let the bad ones die there rather than in the world. Gregorian: it thinks with cultural tools such as words, diagrams and notations, so its cognitive system extends into the artefacts it inherits from others.

The thing I'm trying to classify is the central system in the book. It's a large language model, pretrained to predict text and then fine-tuned on human preference comparisons. At run time, before it answers, it's prompted to write a private working-out in natural language and then base its public answer on that. It can notice a contradiction in its working-out and revise; the working-out lives only in its context window and is discarded after the reply. It has no body, cannot act in the world between replies, and the only feedback available after training is whatever the human says next, or a rating if the company collects one. My instinct is to call it Gregorian becuase it's using language as a tool for its thinking, but I can't tell whether the working-out is better read as a Popperian inner model made visible, or whether the system isn't really a mind at all and the taxonomy refuses to get a grip on it.

So: is the system as described a Popperian creature, a Gregorian creature, both, or neither? Please go through the four grades one by one and say where my description leaves the classification underdetermined. For the fifth point, say whether the case suggests that the inner/outer distinction behind Dennett's grades needs redrawing for systems like this.

Format it as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. And one other thing: where philosophers genuinely disagree, mark it as contested rather than presenting one reading as the consensus; I care about which bits are settled and which are open.
