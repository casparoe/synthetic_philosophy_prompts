I cannot settle a dispute in myself between a theory of love that seems to me to have the better architecture and an intuition that refuses to be redescribed away. The theory is a bestowal account, in the line that runs through Frankfurt: love is not the recognition of a value the beloved already possesses; it is the creation, or constitution, of value through the fact of caring, the investment of will, or the making of the other into someone to whom one is bound. Its virtues are architectural. It explains why love does not transfer to the next person with more of the same admired qualities, because love was never a ranking of qualities. It explains constancy without ad hoc epicycles: the beloved can change in almost every empirical particular and the love does not thereby lose its ground, because its ground is not an empirical property but an act or a history of caring. And it explains particularity in the right direction: that I love this person and not that one is not a puzzle to be explained away but the central fact, since love is precisely the making of this person matter in a way no one else does.

The intuition is that love is answerable to its object. When I try to articulate why I love someone, I reach for their qualities—a sense of humor, a kind of courage, a way of noticing things—and it does not feel like rationalization after the fact. It feels like giving the grounds. I also cannot stop myself from thinking that love can be misplaced, formed under a misapprehension, or sustained only by a refusal to see the person as they now are. If every trait I admired in someone turned out to be a performance, I would think my love had rested on a false picture of the world, not merely that I had withdrawn a value I once conferred. And we do make ordinary normative criticisms of love: that it is based on a misreading, that it has outlived its reasons, that it is a kind of self-deception. A pure bestowal view seems to me to have difficulty making sense of these as anything other than changes in the lover's will.

I am not asking you to tell me which side wins. I want a negotiated verdict. Tell me what I would need to accept for the intuition to be the thing that gives way: what sort of error theory about the phenomenology of loving, about the point of the ordinary question why one loves someone, and about our practices of criticizing love would be required, and whether that error theory is itself credible. Tell me, equally, what would have to be weakened for the theory to give way: whether one can hold onto the idea that love creates reasons or value while conceding that it does so only under conditions—that not every act of bestowal succeeds, that some create value and some merely pretend to—and whether that concession lets the appraisal tradition's core thought back in. And tell me whether there is a reading on which both survive, perhaps by distinguishing the reasons for falling in love from the reasons of love itself, the value of the person from the value of the relationship, or the epistemic dependence of love from its constitutive structure. Which outcome do you in fact find yourself accepting, and what is the single consideration that most moves you?
