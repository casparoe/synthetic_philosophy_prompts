I am a novelist, and I have settled into a rule for reading historical scholarship that I now want to test. When a historian explains a large event—a revolution, a war, a market crash—by stacking up broad structural causes, I treat the explanation with suspicion, because I have come to believe that the true triggers are usually small, accidental-looking details that broad accounts erase. If a source records a delayed message, a misread order, or a meeting that nearly did not happen, I give that detail more causal weight than the surrounding analysis invites. I adopted this habit after reading Henri Poincaré's Science and Method, where he argues, in the chapter on chance, that a cause too minute to be noticed can nevertheless produce a large effect, and that we then attribute the result to chance even though it is fully determined. I took this to be the intellectual seed of the butterfly effect. But I now suspect I have overread it. Poincaré is saying that an unobserved small cause produces the appearance of chance, not that every significant event must have a small cause. My question is whether my rule is defensible as a heuristic for a novelist who cannot investigate everything: is it reasonable to discount large-cause historical explanations and privilege small-trigger ones, or is this a temperamental bias disguised as science? Please answer in American English.
