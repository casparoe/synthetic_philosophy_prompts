Last night I argued for several hours with a friend about whether people who hold deeply opposed value systems should seek compromise in politics, and I came away unsure which of his objections actually damaged the position I had been defending. My position was that compromise between rival value systems can be justified as a kind of trade: each side surrenders something it values comparatively less in order to secure something it values comparatively more, so the resulting package can leave both sides better off by their own lights than the no-agreement alternative, in the way that two countries with different endowments and preferences both gain from trade without sharing any common standard of value. My friend rejected the analogy. He argued, among other things, that a moral conviction is not an asset one can exchange at a favorable rate; that the status quo is not a neutral fallback but already encodes one side's advantage; that a justified compromise can still leave a moral remainder with no analogue in a commercial transaction; and that the very values by which a party judges whether it has gained may be altered by entering into the compromise. I felt the force of these points, but I could not tell which were fatal to the analogy and which were merely rhetorical.

Before I rely on this analogy again, I would like an audit of how far it holds. Give me the explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the trading parties, their initial endowments, their preferences, the terms of trade, the no-trade point, and the gains from trade when the analogy is applied to compromise between value systems. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and sort the disanalogies into those that are load-bearing for the intended conclusion, that both sides can be made better off by their own lights, and those that do not matter for that conclusion. Say what the economic picture quietly imports that the moral case lacks, such as stable and exogenous preferences, divisibility, enforceability, the absence of third-party effects, or the neutrality of the status quo. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument from the start, and if it was, state the weaker conclusion, if any, that the analogy can still support and whether a better analogy is available.
