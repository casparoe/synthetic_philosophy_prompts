I am a PhD student in formal epistemology, and I have a standing policy about how I express my degrees of belief. When colleagues ask how confident I am in a proposition, I refuse to give a single number; I give only an interval, such as "between 0.4 and 0.6," or I say that I have not yet settled on a precise credence. My reason is Ramsey's argument in "Truth and Probability." As I understand the passage, Ramsey argued that if an agent's degrees of belief did not satisfy the probability axioms, it would be possible to construct a set of bets against him such that he would lose no matter how the world turned out. I worry that if I state a precise number, I am committing myself to a betting rate, and if my other commitments are even slightly incoherent, I open myself to exactly that kind of sure loss. By stating only an interval, I avoid having to defend any single rate.

But I am not sure whether this policy is genuinely rational or a way of protecting my beliefs from scrutiny. It seems to me that Ramsey's point is not really about bets; it is about the structure of partial belief itself. If my real degrees of belief are incoherent, refusing to state them as numbers does not make them coherent. On the other hand, perhaps there is a legitimate view that precise credences are not required of rational agents, and that my interval policy is the honest expression of a genuinely imprecise state of belief.

- "The policy restated" (roughly 100 words)

- "The case against the policy" (roughly 300 words)
