I chair the general education committee at a mid-sized university that still has a “science for understanding” requirement, and we’re about to vote on a proposal to replace our second natural science course—currently picked from a list of mostly non-applied subjects like cosmology, evolutionary biology, or geology—with a required course in “quantitative reasoning for real-world decisions.” The main argument in favor is that its more useful, students complain the pure science courses don’t have any obvious payoff, and we could market the change to parents. Some of us are uneasy but cant articulate why without sounding elitist. I’d like you to draft the short written opinion I’ll circulate before the vote. It should take a concrete position—keep the pure-science requirement as is, adopt the substitution, or some hybrid—and it should engage the strongest arguments on the other side seriously, not just dismiss them as philistines. In particular, I want the memo to work through this passage from Aristotle’s Metaphysics (Ross translation), which we discussed in our last meeting:

“But as more arts were invented, and some were directed to the necessities of life, others to recreation, the inventors of the latter were naturally always regarded as wiser than the inventors of the former, because their branches of knowledge did not aim at utility. Hence when all such inventions were already established, the sciences which do not aim at giving pleasure or at the necessities of life were discovered, and first in the places where men first began to have leisure. This is why the mathematical arts were founded in Egypt; for there the priestly caste was allowed to be at leisure.”

How should this bear on our vote? Is Aristotle giving a historical explanation of why such sciences arose—leisure, Egypt, priestly caste—that we should not mistake for a justification, or is he also making a normative claim about the value of knowledge that doesn’t aim at use? And does that claim survive if his historical story is shaky? Write it as a memo the committee could actually act on, with a clear recommendation and a timeframe.
