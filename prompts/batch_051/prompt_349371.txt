When I sign up for a website, I am shown a dense block of terms in a small scrollable box and a button labeled "I agree." I click it without reading, everyone involved knows I did not read it, and I am now bound to whatever is in there — mandatory arbitration, automatic renewal, a clause letting the company change the terms later. Courts will generally enforce those terms against me. Yet courts will also sometimes refuse to enforce a particular term on the grounds that it is too one-sided or that no reasonable person would have expected to meet it there.

Now compare a different setting. If a doctor proposes a procedure, the law does not treat my signature on a form as enough. I have to be told the material risks, alternatives, and likely outcomes in terms I can understand, and my consent counts only if it is knowing and voluntary. If I signed without understanding, something went legally wrong even if the form is complete.

These two practices seem to be built on different ideas of what agreement is. The click-to-agree practice behaves as if a click is a full commitment regardless of what I knew. But then it borrows from the other idea when it steps in and says that some terms are so far outside what a person could reasonably be taken to have accepted that they are not binding after all. I find it hard to say what coherent view of agreement, consent, or voluntary commitment could license both moves at once.

What theory of agreement does the practice of enforcing click-to-agree contracts actually presuppose? Is that theory coherent, or is the practice trying to hold two incompatible ideas at the same time? And if we took the medical model of informed consent seriously, what would have to change in how software is designed, how terms are written, or how we think about the act of clicking "I agree"?
