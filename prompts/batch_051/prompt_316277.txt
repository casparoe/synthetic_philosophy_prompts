I keep coming back to a parallel between Braudel's three timescales and the way you'd analyse a time series, and I can't work out whether it's deep or just cosy. Braudel splits history into the longue durée (geography, climate, the almost stationary stuff), a middle band of institutions, economies and cycles that shift over decades, and the short term of battles, treaties and individual lives, which he mostly treats as froth on top. Time-series analysis does something similar: you decompose the series into a slow trend, medium-frequency cycles and a high-frequency residual, and you treat the residual as uninformative noise once the model's structure has been removed. The mapping looks clean — longue durée/trend, cycles/cycles, events/residual — and Braudel's whole polemic, that events are the least explanatory, looks like the claim that the noise is where the least information lives. But the signal-processing version only works because the components don't feed back into each other; you can subtract the trend and the noise is just left over. In history that seems false: a revolution or a single epidemic can rewire the slow structures, so the 'noise' actively shapes the lower frequencies. Is the analogy doing real work or am I just importing the word 'frequency'? If it is real, what would history have to be like for filtering or detrending to be a legitimate historiographic method, and what does the analogy predict that then turns out false? If it's superficial, say exactly where the structures come apart. I'd like the answer in British English.
