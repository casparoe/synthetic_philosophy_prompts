I want a critical reading of Peter Shaffer's Amadeus—either the stage play or the film—through Rawls's treatment of envy in A Theory of Justice, and in particular through his category of excusable envy. Rawls seems to want two things at once. He treats envy proper as a vice: it is being pained by another's good even when that good takes nothing from you, and unlike resentment it does not rest on any claim that a wrong has been done. But he also allows that some envy is excusable: there are circumstances in which it would be unreasonable to expect a person to overcome the feeling, because it is a predictable response to arrangements that are themselves unjust or that systematically erode a person's self-respect. On that picture, the moral diagnosis of an envious person turns on what the envy is answering to: the vice can belong to the person and the excuse to the arrangement.

Amadeus is built around Salieri's envy of Mozart, and it keeps the two diagnoses in play. Salieri is the devout, industrious, socially established court composer; Mozart is the vulgar, effortless prodigy in whom Salieri sees the gift he bargained with God for as a boy and was denied. Salieri narrates his own fall with unusual self-awareness: he knows his own music is competent rather than great, and he concludes that the injustice is cosmic rather than social—that God has mocked his devotion by placing genius in an unworthy vessel. That immediately complicates a Rawlsian reading, because the distribution Salieri protests is one of natural talent, and the arrangement he arraigns is the divine one, not the basic structure of society.

I want a reading I can use with a philosophy and literature class. It should do the following. First, apply the distinction carefully: which features of Salieri's feeling look like envy proper, which look like excusable envy, and whether the category still applies when the alleged injustice is cosmic rather than social. Second, work through concrete dramatic material—the confession frame, the boyhood bargain with God, Salieri's encounter with Mozart's manuscripts, and his decision to destroy Mozart and claim the Requiem—and say what each scene shows or complicates about the distinction. Third, be honest about where the lens fits and where the play resists it: does Amadeus ultimately present Salieri's envy as a moral failing, as a response to a genuine injustice, as a mixture the philosophical categories struggle to hold together, or as something the work leaves genuinely undecidable? Along the way, use Rawls's contrast between envy and resentment to ask whether Salieri is ever allowed to pass off the first as the second: does the play let him borrow the moral standing of someone who resents a wrong while displaying the profile of someone who envies a good? In your account of Rawls, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers who work on this, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
