Immanuel Kant, in the first preface to the Critique of Pure Reason (Meiklejohn translation), writes:

"But it quickly discovers that, in this way, its labours must remain ever incomplete, because new questions never cease to present themselves; and thus it finds itself compelled to have recourse to principles which transcend the region of experience, while they are regarded by common sense without distrust. It thus falls into confusion and contradictions, from which it conjectures the presence of latent errors, which, however, it is unable to discover, because the principles it employs, transcending the limits of experience, cannot be tested by that criterion. The arena of these endless contests is called Metaphysic."

I hold a generalized version of Kant's claim: philosophy, at least in its metaphysical core, does not make the sort of cumulative progress that logic, mathematics, and the natural sciences make; philosophical work can be locally acute without the central disputes ever being closed across generations. What would have to be true for this to be false? I want the strongest arguments and evidence against it, ranked by how decisive they would be, including developments that have arguably already occurred. Distinguish what would refute the position from what would merely embarrass it, and say what a defender of the position would themselves concede counts against it, not only what critics say. Then assess honestly whether the claim has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind. I would personally be most moved by demonstrated convergence: a historically live philosophical dispute that was settled by argument in a way both sides now accept, rather than merely transposed into new vocabulary.
