I am a retired biologist now serving on my former institute's animal research oversight committee. We have before us a neuroscience protocol that would involve invasive procedures on octopuses, including surgically implanted electrodes and terminal experiments. Our current policy requires full committee review and welfare conditions for vertebrate animals only; cephalopods, like other invertebrates, are treated as outside our mandate. Several committee members argue that octopuses show the behavioral and physiological markers of sentience, and that drawing the line at vertebrates is a vestigial form of speciesism rather than a defensible criterion of moral status. Others reply that the evidence is not firm enough to justify extending protections, and that once we move beyond vertebrates there is no principled place to stop. I have been asked to prepare a short written opinion for the committee recommending a concrete decision: protect cephalopods, refuse, or protect them under specified conditions. The opinion should state criteria we can apply when the next invertebrate case arrives, say whether the vertebrate line can be defended as more than administrative convenience, and give an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side.
