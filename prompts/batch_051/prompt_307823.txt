I'm a philosophy professor, and I've caught myself following an epistemic rule that I'm not sure I can defend. The rule is that naked statistical evidence can move my credence, but it can't take me all the way to flat-out belief about a particular event unless I also have some individualizing evidence that ties this specific case to the claim. If I know 70% of the buses in town are operated by Blue Bus, and I hear that a pedestrian was hit but nobody saw the color, I'll say it's likely the bus was blue, maybe very likely, but I won't believe "the bus was blue" full stop. If I know that 900 of 1000 people at the rodeo paid and one person is singled out as a gatecrasher, I'll say the evidence against him is overwhelming, but I'll stop short of believing he actually crashed unless I get something more specific — a witness, a torn stub, anything that connects him to the act. I do this in everyday life too: if I know most crows around here are black and a bird flys by too quick to identify, I'll say "probably a black bird," but I won't believe it was black.

I suspect some of this is bleed-over from the law. Courts have their own reasons to set the bar above "more likely than not" — proof beyond a reasonable doubt is supposed to protect defendants, not to track the truth exactly. Belief, though, is supposed to track the truth. So am I defending a reasonable epistemic policy, or have I just dressed up a base-rate aversion as a principle? Under what conditions would a rule like mine make my beliefs more accurate, and under what conditions would it make them systematically underconfident? Is there a version of the policy that keeps whatever it's good for without turning me into someone who never, in practice, believes a particular claim on statistical grounds alone? And does the fact that it feels worse to say "he did it" on statistics than to say "it's overwhelmingly likely" tell me something about the difference between belief and credence, or is the real lesson about blame and accusation rather than knowledge?
