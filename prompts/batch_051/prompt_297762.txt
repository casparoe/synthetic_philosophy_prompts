im taking a philosophy of science course right now and we just spent a few weeks on Woodward's interventionism — the view that X causes Y roughly if there's some possible intervention on X that changes Y, with all the fine print about the intervention being surgical, invariant under changes, and the circularity worry that you cant even define "intervention" without already using causal concepts. anyway it finally clicked for me, which is bad timing because my internship at a small ed-tech startup found out im the philosophy person.

The data team there runs A/B tests pretty much every week, and the product manager signs off in a friday ship review. The stakes are medium-ish: we sell to school districts, so if we roll out a feature based on a bad causal read of a test, a bunch of paying classrooms get a change that might quietly hurt engagement or waste teacher time. The team has no philosophy background, and per test they have maybe 15 minutes to decide wether a result is real enough to act on. They keep saying things like "the test was statistically significant so the feature caused the lift," and I want to hand them something better.

So here's what I want: turn Woodward's interventionism into a reusable decision procedure for the data team — a plain-language checklist they can run on any A/B test to decide whether it licenses a causal claim of the form "[feature] caused [metric] to change," as opposed to just an association. dont assume they know the theory; put everything in product-analyst language. Make it capture the main conditions an intervention is supposed to satisfy — exogeneity/no backdoors, affecting the outcome only through the thing youre testing, and invariance — but translated into checks someone can actually do with a real experiment log. And be honest about the circularity worry: in the real world nobody has the full causal graph, so there will be places where the procedure has to say "stop, you need an extra assumption here" rather than pretending it can verify everything from the data.

Then apply the procedure to these three cases, one of which should give the team an answer they will not like:

1. We tested a new onboarding checklist. Users in the treatment arm also got a push notification as part of the launch; the team wants to conclude the checklist (not the notification) caused the 7-day retention lift.
2. We randomized a new progress dashboard, but only about 40% of treated users ever opened it. The team still wants to say the dashboard caused the lift in weekly sessions.
3. A cleanly randomized test showed a reminder feature increased sessions, and the product manager wants to claim the feature will improve end-of-term course completion rates, even though the test only ran for two weeks and only measured sessions.

Finally, say explicitly what the procedure cannot do — what background causal assumptions it has to take on faith, and what kinds of conclusions it should never be used to license.

After the procedure and worked cases, give me a compact JSON summary I can paste into our internal wiki, with exactly this schema:

- "checklist_name": string, a short plain-language name for the procedure
- "steps": array of objects, each with "step_number" (integer), "question" (string, the check phrased as a yes/no question), and "fail_action" (string, what to do if the answer is no)
- "judgment_calls": array of strings, the places where the checklist cannot decide and a human has to supply an assumption
- "worked_cases": array of objects, each with "case_label" (string), "verdict" (string, one of "causal claim licensed", "causal claim not licensed", or "judgment required"), and "unwelcome" (boolean, whether the verdict is one the team would rather not hear)
- "known_limits": array of strings, what the procedure cannot establish no matter how carefully it is followed.
