Next week I'm pitching a governance norm to my org and I want to be ready for the question period, because I already know they're going to come at me. The org is a mid-sized AI safety research outfit, about twenty-five people, mostly ex-engineers who hate process, plus two policy people who came out of government and care about acountability, and one ops person who will fact-check every mechanism I propose. Everyone has at least skimmed the unilateralist's-curse stuff, so I don't need to explain the basic idea.

The position I'm defending: instead of the standard fixes — requiring N independent approvers before someone can unilaterally publish, deploy, or start a possibly load-bearing line of research — we should adopt a freeze norm. Any single member can, with a short written justification, put a two-week freeze on another member's planned action. After that, a panel of three people who'd actually be affected hears it, and the default is yes: the action goes ahead unless the person who called the freeze makes a case that lands. My argument is that the usual approval-threshold fixes treat inaction as neutral, when it isn't. If one cautious person can block things cheaply, you've just relocated the unilateralist's curse to the veto — the unilateral act is now inaction, and a risk-averse minority can impose costs on everyone. But my freeze is deliberately cheap and temporary, so I claim it doesn't create the reverse curse while still catching the classic case where one person is way more optimistic than everyone else. Better than thresholds because thresholds quietly kill weird-but-good projects by selecting for the median researcher; a freeze with default-yes preserves the weird ones and only slows them down.
