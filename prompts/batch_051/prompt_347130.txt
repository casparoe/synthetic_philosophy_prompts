I’m a retired organic chemist and only started reading philosophy after I stopped working, mostly with a reading group of other retirees. We’re going through Schopenhauer’s On the Basis of Morality alongside Nietzsche’s The Antichrist. A phrase keeps getting thrown around in our discussions—something to the effect that compassion is the sole basis of moral worth, or the only genuine moral motive. People use it to settle arguments about whether someone’s position is morally decent or just self-serving. But when I actually read Schopenhauer’s passage, he seems to me to be making a more restricted claim: he says that when we act from immediate participation in another’s suffering, rather than from prudence or abstract principle, only then does the action have moral value; he contrasts this with egoistic and duty-based actions. Nietzsche, in The Antichrist, then attacks almost the opposite view—that making pity the center of ethics is a symptom of decline, something life-denying rather than virtuous. I can’t tell whether the slogan is a trivial claim about motives, a substantive ethical theory, or a policy proposal. What precise claims could it be making? Which of those readings are platitudes, which are false, and which reading were the people in my group actually relying on when they used it to call a position heartless?
