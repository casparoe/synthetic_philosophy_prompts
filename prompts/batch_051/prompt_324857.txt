I'm a TA for an intermediate philosophy of mind class, and this week's unit was on self-deception and the puzzle of whether it's even coherent. The exam included this question:

"Self-deception seems impossible: how can one person both know the truth and get herself to believe the opposite? Discuss the main paradoxes this idea generates, and assess whether a non-intentional account of self-deception solves them."

One student wrote:

"Self-deception is puzzling because if we model it on ordinary interpersonal deception, the self-deceiver would have to intend to deceive herself and also know the truth she is hiding. That creates two paradoxes. The static paradox is that she would have to believe p and not-p at the same time. The dynamic paradox is that if she knows her own plan to make herself believe p, the plan can't work. Most philosophers now reject this traditional intentional model as incoherent. The standard fix is a deflationary account like Alfred Mele's, on which self-deception is just motivated false belief: the person's desire that p biases how she searches for and evaluates evidence, and she ends up believing p without ever intending to deceive herself or believing not-p. This dissolves both paradoxes because there is no dual belief and no intention to deceive. A further virtue of this view is that it still explains why self-deception is blameworthy: the conscious self is kept in the dark while an unconscious subsystem knows the truth and stage-manages the bias, so there is still a kind of internal deception going on. One might object that this collapses self-deception into wishful thinking, but that's an acceptable consequence."

The exam is closed-book and I'm not expecting citations. Could you grade this answer and give me the specific comments that justify the grade? I'm looking for a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression. As part of the comments, please seperate what you consider settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers working on this topic, and don't present a contested position as the consensus.
