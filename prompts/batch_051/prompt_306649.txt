I'm a junior and I sit on my school's student government. Last month the student government passed a new rule, by a narrow vote and following every step of our normal procedure, requiring every club to submit weekly reports on each member's level of participation; those reports go into a file that the teachers consult when they write college recommendations. I voted against it because I think it rewards students who make their effort visible and quietly punishes students who work hard without performing it, but I lost, and the vote was done exactly the way our bylaws require: it was announced ahead of time, debated in open session, passed by the required majority, and recorded. So there is no procedural objection to it.

Now I have to deal with the fact that I'm expected, as a member of the student government, to help enforce the rule and explain it to students who complain about it. My principal says the procedure is what matters: the rule was properly passed, so it is the rule, and everyone is bound by it whether or not they agree with it — work to get it repealed if you don't like it, but follow it in the meantime. A close friend says the opposite: a rule this unfair, whose whole effect is to sort students into performers and non-performers, isn't really a rule at all; it's just a majority using the forms to impose its will, and nobody has a real obligation to go along with it.

I know the rule is bad; that isn't what I'm asking. What I can't settle is whether its badness matters for whether it is actually a rule and whether we have to obey it. Does a rule passed through the proper process automatically become a real rule that should be followed, or can a rule be so unfair that it stops being a rule no matter how properly it was passed? Who is right — my principal or my friend? And how should I, as someone who has to either enforce it or push back against it, decide what to do?
