Two students in my philosophy of mathematics tutorial got stuck on whether 2 ∈ 4, and I would like an adjudication of their argument as it actually went, not just a general discussion of the issue.

The first began from the von Neumann reduction: 0 = ∅ and each successor n+1 = n ∪ {n}, so 4 = {0,1,2,3}, which makes 2 ∈ 4 true. The second replied that under Zermelo's reduction, where each successor n+1 = {n}, 4 = {3}, so 2 ∉ 4. The first then said this shows the question has no coding-independent answer: two equally good reductions of the natural numbers to sets diverge on it, so 2 ∈ 4 is only true or false relative to a reduction. The second objected that this is too quick, because numbers are not sets; set membership is not the sort of property a number could have, so asking whether 2 is an element of 4 is a category mistake rather than a question to be relativized. The first answered that this is exactly what structuralism says, and it cuts the other way: if a number is nothing over and above its position in the natural-number structure, then any set-theoretic question not definable from that structure has no answer for the structuralist, which is why different reductions are free to diverge on it. The second insisted the first is conflating having no answer with having many answers, and that treating 2 ∈ 4 as determinately false better respects the fact that arithmetic itself never gives membership a meaning.

Who is right about which specific points, and what are both of them missing? Please indicate how confident you are in your claims and single out the point you are least sure of. End your answer with a line beginning "Verdict:" giving a one-sentence summary of your adjudication.
