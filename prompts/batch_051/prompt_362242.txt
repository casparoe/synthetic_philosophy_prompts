I'm a physics PhD student, but this is a philosophy-of-science-ish question about a pattern I keep noticing and don't have a word for. I know it sits somewhere near talk of "emergence" and "reduction," but those words seem to be doing too many jobs at once, so I'm hoping for something more precise. Here are two instances. When you model a fluid, you use a handful of bulk quantities—density, viscosity, temperature—and you can get extremely accurate predictions for things like the drag on a pipe or the flow past an airfoil. The equations never mention molecules, and if you tried to put in all the molecular positions and collisions, you wouldn't improve those predictions; you'd just be swamped. The fine details seem to wash out, and the washing-out is itself systematic: the coarse theory has its own rules for how the coarse quantities relate, and those rules stay reliable across a huge range of molecular situations. The other instance is less physics-y: a chess engine evaluates something like "king safety" without representing the silicon or the machine code running the search, and adding that detail wouldn't make the evaluation better.

Is there an established name for this specific feature—not just "levels of description differ," but the fact that a higher-level description can be complete for a class of predictions while the lower-level details are not merely unknown but irrelevant, and that the higher-level theory seems to shield itself from those details? If there are several nearby names, I want to know how they carve the space differently: is the "details wash out" part the same thing as the "higher-level theory is self-contained" part? And does this feature show reductionism is false, or only show that reduction is a claim about what exists rather than about what predicts? I can live with "there is no single name," but if you think that, please coin one and say where the phenomenon gives out—what kind of fine-grained detail would fail to wash out, and what that failure would tell us. Also, tell me which part of your answer you're least confident in, and what argument or example would make you change it.
