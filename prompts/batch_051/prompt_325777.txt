Here is an argument I keep failing to refute cleanly. Let f be an irreducible cubic with rational coefficients. Its three complex roots are not given in any preferred order, and any labeling of them as α, β, γ is as legitimate as any other. Now consider the standard algebraic expression "the field obtained by adjoining a root of f to Q."

(1) An expression is well-defined only if its referent is independent of every arbitrary choice made in specifying it.
(2) "The field obtained by adjoining a root of f" makes an arbitrary choice: whether to adjoin α, β, or γ.
(3) These choices do not always give literally the same object. Regarded as subfields of the complex numbers, Q(α), Q(β), and Q(γ) can differ; for f(x) = x³ − 2, one of them lies inside the real numbers while the other two do not.
(4) Therefore the expression fails to pick out a single object, and so is not well-defined.
(5) Hence the standard notation Q[x]/(f) — usually glossed as the field obtained by adjoining a root of f, constructed without choosing among the roots — is suspect, and there are really three distinct root fields corresponding to the three possible labelings.

My suspicion is that the error has to do with what "the same object" must mean in (1), but I am not sure whether that suspicion is sound or just special pleading on behalf of standard algebra. Is this argument valid? If it is valid, that would be a serious result about standard algebra. If it is not, identify exactly where it goes wrong — a premise, an inference, or an equivocation — and say how "well-defined," "the field obtained by adjoining a root," and Q[x]/(f) should be understood so that standard algebraic practice is in order.
