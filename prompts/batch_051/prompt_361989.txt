I have been going back and forth with a colleague about a case we both regard as clean as such cases get: a neighbor has undertaken to water a friend's plants during a vacation, fails to do so, and the plants die. My colleague insists that, in the most literal sense, the neighbor's failure to water caused the plants' death — no less literally than a drought would have — and that denying this is just squeamishness about ascribing causation to non-events. I am not convinced that the "caused" here is anything more than a compact way of assigning fault. I would like your actual credence — a number from 0 to 1 — in the proposition that omissions are genuine causes: that at least some singular causal claims of the form "the failure to φ caused E" are literally true, rather than normatively useful shorthand for responsibility or blame. I currently sit around 0.55; my colleague is around 0.85.

Please decompose the estimate rather than defending a bare number. I anticipate something like the following factors, but correct the list if it is wrong-headed — in particular, you may want to add a term for the possibility that "cause" is simply indeterminate here. (a) The probability that the correct metaphysics of causation is fundamentally difference-making or counterfactual, rather than productive or process-based. (b) Conditional on the correct account, the probability that absences or omissions are admissible relata of the causal relation, rather than being disqualified by the demand for a concrete event, process, or conserved quantity. (c) The probability that the description under which an omission is picked out — not-watering, not-watering-with-clean-water, not-arranging-a-substitute — is objectively constrained rather than selected by our interests and background expectations. (d) The probability that our causal locutions in such cases are tracking causation itself rather than culpability or some other normative-pragmatic relation. Give each factor a number with a one-line justification, show how you combine them, and identify the term to which your final credence is most sensitive. Then say which sub-question most likely explains the difference between someone at 0.55 and someone at 0.85, and what argument or evidence would move you most on that sub-question.
