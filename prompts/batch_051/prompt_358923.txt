I have recently begun sitting on a review board that must recommend sanctions for people who committed the same outward violation under markedly different states of mind, and I keep returning to the claim associated with Abelard that the sin lies solely in the consent, while the external act adds nothing to guilt. I would like to think through what would have happened if that principle had actually become institutionally central.

Counterfactual: suppose that at the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215, the Latin Church had made an Abelardian account the explicit standard for confession and penance. In particular, a sin is imputed only to the degree that the will consents; the outward act and its worldly consequences add nothing to guilt; and an act performed without consent, or in ignorance, is not a sin at all. Assume the decree is widely taught to parish priests and becomes embedded in pastoral practice within a generation.

Give me your reasoned prediction of how European moral psychology, confessional practice, and the Church’s role in social control develop over the next two centuries. I am less interested in a final verdict than in the mechanisms: what happens to confession when priests must adjudicate inner consent rather than outward acts? Which sins become harder to discipline pastorally, and which become harder to hide? Does the system drift toward laxity, toward scrupulosity, or does it split between different classes of penitents? How do ordinary habits of self-examination change when deeds lose their independent moral weight? Please distinguish between predictions you regard as robust structural consequences and those that depend on contingent assumptions about how priests and penitents actually adapt.
