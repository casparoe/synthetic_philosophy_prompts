I keep noticing a move in arguments about wrongdoing and I can't tell whether there's a clean concept for it or whether a few different things get lumped together. The move isn't denying the accusation, and it isn't exactly "you do it too." It's when someone responds to criticism by saying, in effect, that the critic has no business making it — not because the critic is wrong about the facts, but because the critic is implicated in the same kind of wrong. The critic might not do the thing herself; she might just benefit from it, or have helped create the conditions for it, or have kept quiet when speaking up would have cost her something.

Two examples. A landlord who has never personally evicted anyone but lives off rental income criticizes a particular eviction as predatory; the reply is that he can't say that, since his income depends on the same system. A citizen of a wealthy country blames a corporation for labor abuses in its supply chain; the reply is that she's wearing the products, so she's in no position to blame anyone.

I get that this is somewhere near hypocrisy, but it seems different: the hypocrite's flaw is a gap between his own conduct and the values he preaches, whereas these critics may be perfectly consistent, just compromised. It's also near tu quoque, but tu quoque usually concludes that the accusation is false because the accuser is just as bad, while here nobody is saying the original criticism is false — they're saying this particular critic shouldn't be the one to voice it. I'm trying to pin down exactly what is supposed to be lost in these cases: a right, a permission, a kind of authority, or just credibility. And does losing it mean the criticism is false, or only that it shouldn't come from them? Is there an established concept for this specific loss, distinct from plain hypocrisy, and if it has no clean name, how would you define it and where would you draw it's edges? A bare label won't help me much; I want to know what the thing is.
