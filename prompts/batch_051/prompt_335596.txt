I’m in a course on ancient and modern ethics, and we keep encountering the golden rule as if it were a single principle, but I’m starting to suspect the phrase covers several distinct rules that come apart under pressure. The negative formulation says, in effect, that you should not do to others what you would not want them to do to you. The positive formulation says that you should do to others what you would want them to do to you. Some versions appeal to what you as a particular individual would want, while others appeal to what any reasonable person would want, or to what a person’s dignity demands. Kant, in the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, argues that the golden rule cannot serve as a universal moral law, because it gives no ground for duties to oneself—someone might be willing to have everyone neglect him, and so it would not forbid him to neglect them—and because it makes beneficence optional for anyone willing to do without help from others. I find this objection hard to assess because I don’t know exactly which formulation of the rule it targets.

Please give a working definition of “the golden rule”: a single, precise statement of the principle, formulated so that we can test whether it is a plausible moral rule. Then test your definition against these cases: (a) a dentist who gives her patients exactly what she would want done to her own teeth, though her dental preferences are unusual; (b) a person who refuses to help a struggling coworker because he would not want or expect help in the same situation; (c) a judge who sentences a defendant to prison while knowing she would not want the same done to her; (d) a person who ends his own life, where the only plausible object of the rule would be a duty to himself; and (e) a parent who takes away a child’s toy as punishment, though the parent would not want someone to take away her own possessions.
