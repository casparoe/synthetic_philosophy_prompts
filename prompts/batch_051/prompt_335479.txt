Okay, I've been going back and forth on whether conciliationism undermines itself, so I wrote the argument out in steps to see where the trick is. It feels like it proves too much, but I cant find the sleight of hand. Heres the argument:

(1) On most controversial topics — god, whether UBI would work, free will — there are smart, well-informed people on both sides, and at least sometimes the person on the other side is your epistemic peer: same evidence, same competence, same track record.
(2) Conciliationism says that when you learn an epistemic peer disagrees with you about p, you should substantially lower your confidence in p. Not necessarily all the way to 50/50, but enough that you stop asserting p flat out.
(3) Conciliationism itself is exactly the kind of controversial philosophical claim that has smart, well-informed opponents — the steadfast people think it's wrong. So by (1), some of those opponents are your epistemic peers.
(4) So by (2), you should substantially lower your confidence in conciliationism itself.
(5) But a rule that, applied to itself, tells you to lower your confidence in the rule is self-undermining: you can only keep applying it by not fully taking its own advice.
(6) And a self-undermining rule can't be rationally adopted.

So conciliationism can't be rationally adopted. I suspect step (5) is sneaking from "lower your confidence" to "stop relying on it," but maybe I'm just looking for an escape hatch. Tell me straight: is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, why it fails, and what the corrected version of that step would say. If it is valid, does the conclusion really damage conciliationism, or can the conciliationist just bite the bullet and keep going at lower but still liveable confidence? I want a definite verdict, not a menu of options.
