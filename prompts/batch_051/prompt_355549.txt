So here's the setup. A national health service decides it needs a single hedonic currency, because it funds treatments on the principle of "quality-adjusted pleasure per dollar" and has to compare, say, the pleasure of tasting a ripe peach for twenty seconds, the pleasure of finally understanding a proof, and the pleasure of being reunited with a friend after years apart. The philosophers on the committee are worried about the heterogeneity problem: what do these pleasures even have in common that would make them commensurable? Instead of answering that question, they design a calibration scheme to sidestep it.

They choose one Reference Pleasure (RP): the feeling of a warm, unscented footbath at 39°C, water kept still, lasting exactly ten minutes. One "bath-minute" is defined as one minute of RP. To price any other pleasure, trained calibrators run subjects through a forced matching task: the subject adjusts either the duration of the RP or the duration of the target episode until the two feel "equally worth having over the next hour." Everything then gets priced in bath-minutes, and treatment funding goes to whatever has the lowest cost per bath-minute.

Analyze how this scheme would actually behave rather than whether it sounds good. What does it assume about pleasure by dodging the heterogeneity question instead of solving it? Who would game it, and how—patients, doctors, calibraters, politicians? What artifacts does the footbath-as-reference introduce (think very intense, very brief, very "cold," or very social pleasures)? Are the failure modes fixable by changing the reference pleasure or the matching task, or do they point to something deeper about hedonism? Be specific about the second-order effects.
