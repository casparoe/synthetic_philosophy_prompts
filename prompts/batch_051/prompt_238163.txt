You are a philosopher of biology who works on the organism concept. I am preparing a paper on whether holobionts (a host plus its resident microbes, such as a human with a gut microbiome), eusocial insect colonies, and superorganisms count as organisms, and before I evaluate any particular account I want the specification of what such an account is for. Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of organismality: a list of requirements any such theory must meet, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and each marked as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp. Organize the list under: (1) cases it must get right — a siphonophore (a Portuguese man o' war is a colony of physiologically connected zooids with divided labor), a lichen, an ant or honeybee colony, a clonal aspen grove, a human-plus-microbiome holobiont, and a single worker ant; (2) distinctions it must not collapse, such as organism versus biological individual versus physiological individual, and being one organism versus being a unit of selection; (3) biological results it must respect, such as evolutionary transitions in individuality, immune tolerance of resident symbionts, and host–microbe metabolic interdependence. Then flag any pair of requirements that classify the same case differently or look jointly unsatisfiable — metabolic integration and shared reproductive fate often pull in opposite directions for holobionts — and say whether the tension is real or only apparent. Finally, identify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding the main candidate theories, and score the leading families (functional-integration, Darwinian/evolutionary individuality, immunological) only briefly or in a table. Think carefully and brainstorm candidate cases before settling on the final list; spend most of the answer on the criteria and their motivation rather than the scorecard.
