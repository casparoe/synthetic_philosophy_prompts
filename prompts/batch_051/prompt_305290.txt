I am the lead analyst for a small private foundation that ranks grant proposals by explicit expected value, mostly long-horizon interventions whose value is concentrated in low-probability scenarios. Four or five times a month I must decide, in a few hours per file and from models that do not supply full probability distributions, whether a proposal's headline expected value is a usable number or an artifact of how its list of scenarios is ordered. Seven-figure grants are at stake, and the trustees who must sign off on my recommendations will not read decision theory. I have become convinced that some of our estimates have the pathology of the Pasadena game: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads, a first heads on toss n pays (−1)^(n−1) · 2^n / n dollars, so the probability-weighted payoff terms are the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2 in its natural order but can be rearranged to converge to any real number or to diverge — the standard reason the game is said to have no well-defined expected value. That toy case is not the problem; the problem is that the same failure can hide in an ordinary scenario-weighted cost-benefit spreadsheet, and while I have read enough about expected-value reasoning to know the displayed total should not be trusted without further checks, I do not yet have a checkable procedure I can hand to an analyst and defend to the trustees.

The principle I want operationalized, without being explained back to me, is this: a quantity whose value depends on the order of summation is not a true expected value, and the relevant test is not whether a displayed sum has some limit but whether the probability-weighted payoffs are absolutely summable — and, more broadly, whether the estimate is dominated by low-probability tail scenarios whose contribution has not been pinned down. Turn that principle into a checklist an analyst who has never read decision theory can execute on a grant file. Set out the checks in the order they should be run, say what each check catches and what it cannot catch, and specify what my memo should conclude at each failure. The checklist must function when a proposal gives no well-defined probability distribution over scenarios, and it must explicitly flag every step at which a judgment call is being dressed up as a computation. Then apply the checklist to three worked files: one whose estimate survives; one whose estimate is Pasadena-like in a way that forces a rejection or large downgrade of a proposal the committee was inclined to fund; and one on which the checklist cannot determine whether the number is meaningful. Close by stating what the procedure cannot do — including whether it can distinguish a genuinely undefined expectation from a merely poorly estimated one, and what I should do when the two look alike in practice.
