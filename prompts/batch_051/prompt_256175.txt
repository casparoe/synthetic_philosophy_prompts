I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind with sixteen students, and I have a single ninety-minute session to spend on Dennett's multiple drafts model and its target, the Cartesian theater: the stubborn intuition that conscious experience gets assembled at one place and time and then shown to an internal audience that witnesses a finished, canonical version of events. Instead of beginning with the theory, I want a runnable classroom activity in which students run up against the limits of that intuition before anyone names it. The activity should be built around live temporal illusions in perception — the color phi phenomenon, in which two differently colored dots flashed in quick succession are reported as one moving dot that changes color midway through its path; the cutaneous rabbit, in which taps at the wrist and elbow produce phantom taps along the forearm; and, if you judge it workable, the flash-lag effect. The aim is for students to notice that what they report having seen at a given moment can depend on information that arrived only later, as if the "finished" experience had been revised after the fact. Please supply concrete roles or rules, a materials list that requires no specialized lab equipment, a ninety-minute timeline, and a written-report protocol that prevents students from contaminating one another's descriptions before discussion. The facilitator should offer no theoretical framing up front, so also specify exactly what to say at the start and what to withhold.

The debrief should map what happened onto core features of the multiple drafts model: that there is no single canonical moment at which a perception becomes conscious, that multiple interpretations are continuously generated, selected, and edited in parallel, and that asking "when did you actually see the green dot?" may be a misguided question rather than a deep one. It should introduce the Stalinesque/Orwellian contrast — roughly, whether a revision happens before an experience reaches awareness or an earlier conscious experience is overwritten afterward by false memory — and explain why the activity alone cannot decide between those descriptions. Students should leave able to state in their own words why a report of experience is weak evidence for a central inner witness. Since this is a first-run design, be candid about at least two predictable failure modes (for instance, uneven susceptibility to the illusions and report anchoring), how to handle each, and which aspect of the multiple drafts model a perceptual illusion can only gesture at and must therefore be supplied explicitly in the debrief.
