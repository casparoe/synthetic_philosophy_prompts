I am pulled between a theoretical account of moral judgment and an intuition about a familiar kind of antisocial person, and I want you to arbitrate the conflict rather than simply choose a side.

The theory is motivational internalism about moral judgment. In its strongest form it says: to judge that an act is wrong is not merely to place it under a learned predicate; it is, in part, to be disposed to be moved against doing it, at least when self-interest, fatigue, and other interfering factors are absent. A subject who reliably sorted acts into wrong and not wrong but was left entirely cold by the sorting would be like a color-word user without color experience: behaviorally fluent but conceptually incomplete. The theory is attractive because it explains the otherwise puzzling intimacy between moral belief and motivation, and because it makes sense of why the figure of the amoralist, the person who announces that she knows an act is wrong and simply does not care, always sounds as though she has not quite got the concept.

The intuition is about the psychopath: a person with intact practical reasoning and an accurate command of the local moral and conventional rules, but with severely blunted empathy, guilt, and other-directed concern, who harms others as a matter of course. When such a person says of his own action that it was wrong, and can say why, citing precisely the features of the victim, the rule, and the harm that ordinary moral agents cite, it seems false, even morally convenient, to deny that he knows what he did was wrong. He is not ignorant; his knowledge is exactly what makes his conduct intelligible as wrongdoing rather than a mistake.

So the theory yields one verdict and the intuition the opposite. The theory says the psychopath has at most a shell of moral knowledge, because the motivational element is missing. The intuition says he has the knowledge, and that is precisely what makes him culpable.

Your task is to present the strongest possible version of each side, and then work out which should yield. Specifically: (1) state the best internalist argument that the psychopath cannot be said to know, being precise about the kind of knowledge that is supposedly missing; (2) state the best case for the intuition, including a diagnosis of what the internalist is conflating; (3) give a negotiated resolution that distinguishes as many grades as are needed, for example, recognition of a rule's applicability, propositional knowledge of wrongness, evaluative understanding, and full moral competence, and say which grades the psychopath possesses, whether any grade is legitimately reserved for agents with the relevant motivation, and whether that reservation illuminates the phenomena or just gerrymanders the concept to save the theory. Conclude by explaining what would justify each available move: abandoning the intuition as a debunked artifact of how we picture antisocial minds, amending the internalist account so that it does not imply the psychopath is ignorant, or holding that the two were never in direct conflict because they concern different grades of moral knowledge.
