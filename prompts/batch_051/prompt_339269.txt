Hey — I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language. I was reading a popular logic book and it summarized the indicative/subjunctive distinction like this:

"Indicative conditionals, like 'if it rains, the match will be cancelled,' are not truth-evaluable claims about how the world is. They express a conditional credence: how strongly you accept the consequent conditional on the antecedent. That's why the probability of an indicative conditional equals the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent. Counterfactual conditionals, like 'if it had rained, the match would have been cancelled,' are truth-evaluable, because they make claims about possible worlds: a counterfactual is true just in case the consequent is true in the closest possible world where the antecedent is true. That's why counterfactuals have truth conditions and indicatives don't, and why the probabilistic treatment can't be extended to counterfactuals."
