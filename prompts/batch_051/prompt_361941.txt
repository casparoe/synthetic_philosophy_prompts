In preparing a course on early Chinese political thought I have returned repeatedly to the dispute between the Legalist and Confucian traditions, and I am struck by how little the underlying argument has moved even though it now appears in modern dress: performance targets and audit culture versus professional judgement, deterrence versus legitimacy in criminal justice, extrinsic incentives versus intrinsic motivation. The Legalist side, as one might put it in Han Feizi’s idiom, holds that officials and subjects are moved chiefly by interest and fear, that sound institutions must therefore be designed to anticipate self-interested behaviour, and that any order depending on the virtue of particular officeholders is fragile and unreproducible. The virtue side replies that institutions built on this premise merely call forth the very behaviour they assume: they corrupt the dispositions that make officials trustworthy and subjects governable without force, and durable order requires the cultivation of character, the rites, and the example of those above. Each side has its favoured cases, and each side treats the other’s counterexamples not as evidence against the principle but as failures of implementation or as exceptions that prove the rule.

My working hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but several conducted under a single heading: an empirical question about how people respond to institutions; a normative question about what a state may legitimately demand of its officials and subjects; and a further question about what should count as good order in the first place — compliance and stability, or the quality of the relations among ruler, officials, and subjects. I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the dispute substantive or at bottom verbal, and if verbal, where exactly is the equivocation? Do the two sides differ over facts, over standards of success, or over starting intuitions about human nature that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — an experiment, a distinction, a change of question — or should we conclude that no progress ought to be expected, and why?
