i've been chewing on herding models lately and i think i might have come up with something, but i need someone to tell me if it actually pays rent. in a standard binary cascade, once a couple people pick A, the public history starts to dominate private signals, so the next guy rationally follows even if his own evidence says B. fine. but here's the part i haven't seen stated flat out: when he follows, his action reveals almost nothing about his private signal to everyone downstream, because he would have picked A either way. so each conformist choice makes the public history a little less informative for the people who come after, in way that compounds. i want to call this 'informational overgrazing' or maybe 'signal erosion': rational herding as a tragedy of the epistemic commons. conforming is individually rational given the history, but it imposes a negative externality on later observers by diluting the aggregate signal, the way overgrazing depletes shared pasture. the cascade is the social trap, not the sheep.

that reframing feels like it explains why "people should just be independent" is useless advice—it's asking everyone to pay a private cost to maintain a public good. my questions: is this already a known result, or does it have a name i'm not remembering? does the externality framing do any real work beyond what the fragility stuff already says, or am i just relabeling "cascades can be wrong and fragile"? where does the commons analogy break down, given that information isn't literally used up the way grass is? and does the obvious fix actually follow—a norm where public disagreement is prosocial and cheap agreement is mildly taxed, red teams, devil's advocate roles—or is that me importing my temperament into the model? lead with the bottom line on whether the concept earns its keep. and please no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just continuous prose like you're writing me an email.
