I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, mostly math and the philosophy of math, and I'm working on a piece about what it means for a mathematical object to be given rather than chosen. I've been reading around Galois theory and structuralism, and I've ended up with a set of commitments I want checked for consistency before I build an essay on them. I honestly don't know where the weak spot is, so please don't tell me it all hangs together unless it does.

Here's where I've landed:

(1) I'm a structuralist: mathematical objects are what they are in virtue of their structure — the relations they enter into — not in virtue of what there elements 'are.'

(2) The two roots of x^2 + 1 = 0, which we call i and −i, are two different things, but nothing in the structure of the complex numbers as a field extension of the reals tells them apart: complex conjugation swaps them and is an automorphism that fixes every real number.

(3) So defining i as the square root of −1 is not well-defined; it hides an arbitrary choice. The usual ways of pinning down which root you mean — the one in the upper half-plane, say — already presuppose the choice.

(4) But I also think it's completely fine, mathematically, to just pick one of the two roots and call it i. Anything you prove about your chosen root that doesn't lean on the arbitrary choice will be true of the other root as well.

(5) More generally, I think canonicalness is graded: an isomorphism between two objects is a real identification only if it's unique, or at least explicitly specified; if it's not, the objects are 'the same' only in a weaker sense.

(6) And I think this isn't just philosophical fussing. A finite-dimensional vector space is isomorphic to its dual, but not canonically; treating them as literally the same object leads to real trouble.

Which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and which single revision would clean up the most? Rank the tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix is. And be committed: if you think one of these has got to go, say so and defend it, rather than laying out my options.
