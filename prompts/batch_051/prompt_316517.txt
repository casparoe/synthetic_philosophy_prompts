Lately I've been reading philosophy of mind, and I keep coming back to a passage in Clark and Chalmers's “The Extended Mind” where they say: “It is not just the presence of advanced external computing resources which raises the issue, but rather the general tendency of human reasoners to lean heavily on environmental supports.” I’ve been asked to give the after-dinner remarks at a small workshop my old field station is hosting on “natural history in the digital age.” The audience will be maybe thirty people: field biologists who knew me, two philosophers who work on extended cognition, an archivist, some donors, and a few spouses. I want the speech to use that Clark and Chalmers passage as the real structural backbone, not just as decoration. I’d like to open with the quote, use it to frame three short stories from my fieldwork where the notebook did something I would have called thinking if it had happened in my head, and then close with the idea that the station’s archive is a kind of extended memory for the whole place. But I don’t want it to turn into a philosophy lecture, and I don’t want to overclaim the metaphysics. Could you write the speech for me, roughly eight minutes, warm and concrete rather than abstract, and help me see where the philosophers in the room would likely push back so I can decide whether to leave a line out or keep it in and take the heat?
