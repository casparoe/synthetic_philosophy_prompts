I am a novelist, and for the past year I have been working on a book about a city that has just emerged from a period of prolonged political violence. The plot centers on a municipal commission charged with deciding what to do with the city's inherited monuments, street names, plaques, and public inscriptions. I have found that the real difficulty is not plot but vocabulary: characters who agree on every historical fact nonetheless disagree about whether a given statue or inscription commemorates someone, and that disagreement carries the book's central moral argument. I want to know whether the word has enough structure for these disputes to be reasoned about rather than merely felt.

So I would like a working definition of commemoration, one that tracks the line that matters to my characters: the line between public acts that hold a person or event before a community in an evaluative way, and acts that merely document, preserve, or leave the past in place. Please propose the definition, run it through every case below, tell me where it delivers a verdict I did not expect, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Here are the cases, arranged roughly by my own confidence:

1. An equestrian statue of a long-dead military commander, erected by the state after his victories and still standing in the main square.
2. A war memorial carved with the names of the city's dead soldiers, with no other text.
3. A minute of silence observed each year for the victims of a flood that destroyed a neighborhood.
4. The preserved basement where political prisoners were executed, opened to the public with informational panels and officially called a memorial.
5. A street renamed after a novelist who once lived on it, chosen primarily to attract literary tourism.
6. A small plaque on a building stating only that prisoners held by the occupying army were executed there in 1943, with no names, images, or interpretive text.
7. The commander's statue removed from the square and placed in a museum's off-site storage, catalogued but never exhibited.
8. A festival in which residents reenact the city's medieval guilds in period costume.
9. A bridge named after a corporation that paid for its construction.
10. A textbook chapter that describes a massacre in neutral, documentary detail.

My own instincts are that the first two are clearly commemoration and the last two clearly are not; about the rest I have only hunches, and I would rather the definition settle them. End by saying which of your final verdicts is least secure, and why.
