I have been turning over a thought experiment — call it the careenium — that seems to expose a fault line in how I think about mind and mechanism. Imagine a large frictionless plane covered with thousands of small magnetic spheres, call them simmballs, sliding and colliding. Because of their magnetism, some of them stick into clusters, and the clusters themselves slide, collide, merge, and split. Now suppose the plane is large enough and the simmballs numerous enough that these clusters form stable higher-level structures. Some of those structures begin to behave like symbols: one cluster's shape reliably tends to produce another cluster, rather as one token in a computation produces another. Eventually there is a structure that behaves like a self — it has a model of its table-world, states of wanting, deciding, and acting. At the low level, everything that happens is ball-by-ball physics: each simmball's motion is fixed by local forces, and the cluster as a whole exerts no extra push. At the high level, however, it is almost impossible not to describe the clusters as doing things: the symbol for danger causes the self to swerve; a remembered plan sends the clusters in a new direction.

The theory I find compelling is bottom-up physical causal closure: the high-level patterns are real but they are not additional causes; talk of symbols pushing balls is just a useful compression of ball-level dynamics. This theory has the virtues of simplicity, unification with physics, and a clear answer to every causal question: the substrate always runs the show.

But I have an intuition I cannot shake. When I myself decide to raise my hand because I understand a request, it really seems that the content of the request — a symbolic matter — is what does the work. If the claim is that the meaning of a message never exerts pressure on the substrate, then my own reasoning appears to be epiphenomenal in a way that undermines itself: the fact that I am giving reasons would be a story the substrate tells without the reasons having anything to do with it. So I am torn between a theory that makes excellent sense and an intuition that seems to be the condition of taking my own reasoning seriously.

Present both sides at their strongest and give me a negotiated verdict. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes — that is, what kind of debunking explanation would show that the apparent downward push of symbols is an artifact of how I model high levels? What would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes or gets amended — for example, is there a respectable notion of downward causation, or of different levels of explanation being incommensurable, that would let symbols genuinely push without violating ball-level closure? Or is there a reading on which both survive, and if so, what exactly does the word push mean on that reading? I do not want a party line; I want to know which move is least costly and why.
