Many people who would confidently say that a dog yelping and cradling a burned paw is in pain deny that a fish struggling on a hook is, even when the behavioral indicators are parallel: nociceptive responses, wound-directed guarding, learned avoidance of the injuring stimulus, and a return to normal behavior under analgesia. The same divergence shows up in scientific practice, where behaviors taken as evidence of pain in a rat are often dismissed as mere reflexes in a fish, and in animal-protection law, which extends safeguards to some taxa but not others along what appear to be purely lineage-based lines. The evidential standard, in other words, seems to track ancestry rather than anything the animal does. Is there any principled justification for demanding stronger evidence of conscious pain from taxonomically distant animals, or is this an unexamined vertebrate bias that ordinary judgment and the law should give up? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the case in which two animals exhibit exactly the same behaviors and differ only in their lineage.
