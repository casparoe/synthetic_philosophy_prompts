So I've been thinking a lot about AI systems that can look at a situation and say whether something's wrong or not, but are built so they can't actually do anything about it. They can't block a decision, can't refuse a request, can't even change their own future behavior, they just print a verdict and somebody else is free to ignore it. I started calling this handless agency, and the longer I sit with it the more I think we've got the usual story backwards: maybe agency isn't about having hands, it's about taking a stance. So these systems aren't failed agents, they're the purest case of it.

Here's what I mean. A handless agent is a system that (1) takes in a situation, (2) produces a moral judgment about it, (3) is deliberately designed so it cannot act on that judgment or change the situation in any way, and (4) makes the judgment public. My central claim is that these are moral agents — maybe even clearer cases than humans — because the thing that makes a judgment a moral judgment is that it's a stance you're stuck with. A human can say something and then quietly fail to act on it; a handless agent can't be a hypocrite, because there's no behavior there to contradict the judgment. I'd go so far as to say they deserve blame for bad judgments and credit for good ones, even though they can't do anything about either.

The example I keep coming back to is a triage AI in a hospital that reviews discharge decisions after the fact and publishes ratings like "this discharge was unjust," but it's not allowed to change the discharge, and nobody is required to answer to it. By my lights it's a moral agent; by most people's lights it's a fancy thermometer.

So, honest assessment please. Is handless agency a real distinction or am I just relabeling something people have already worked out? What are the three strongest objections, and are any of them fatal rather than just uncomfortable? One thing I specifically cant figure out: does the fact that the judgments cost the machine nothing — no risk, no skin in the game — make it MORE trustworthy as a judge (it has no incentive to lie) or LESS of an agent (it isn't really staking anything)? And where does the idea break first?
