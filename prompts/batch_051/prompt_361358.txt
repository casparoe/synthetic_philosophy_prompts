I'm leading a two-hour session on Dennett's taxonomy of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures for a mixed third-year seminar of philosophy and cognitive science students. They will have no serious background in evolutionary biology or behaviorism, and the goal is that they leave able to classify an unfamiliar behavior by the floor, or floors, doing the work rather than merely reciting four definitions.

Don't explain the taxonomy to me; plan the explanation. Work out the explanatory arc: what has to be in place before the first floor is introduced and why; the order in which the four floors should be introduced and what each later floor conceptually depends on from the earlier ones; and the single carrying example that runs through all four floors. Say what that example must have — expressible at every floor, with the same recurring move, the relocating of trial and error one step inward, visible at each transition — and what it must lack, such as any pressure to conflate the Gregorian floor with literal tool use.

Then map the misconceptions. I expect, at minimum, that students will read the floors as an evolutionary timeline or as a ladder on which each species sits on exactly one rung; that they will take 'Skinnerian' to mean Skinner was right about the mind; that they will equate 'Popperian' with conscious imagination; and that they will take 'Gregorian' to mean simply using tools. For each, specify where in the sequence to head it off and how. Finally, give one end-of-session question that would distinguish a student who understands the Popperian-to-Gregorian transition from one who has only memorized the labels, and state what should be explicitly left out at this level.
