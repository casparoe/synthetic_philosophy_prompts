I am putting together a short philosophical profile for a workshop on early modern accounts of happiness, and before I circulate it I would like a consistency check on the position I have ended up with. I have been working through the eighteenth-century philosopher Émilie du Châtelet's essay on happiness, and here, in compressed form, is what I now think.

(i) Happiness is not tranquillity, the absence of pain, or the extirpation of the passions; it is the felt satisfaction of one's passions, so that a life without strong desires and attachments cannot be a happy life however undisturbed it is.
(ii) The love of study is the most reliable route to durable happiness, because its objects are always available, its pleasures are renewable, and it depends far less than love or ambition on the unpredictable conduct of other people.
(iii) Happiness requires illusion: several of our most important passions — romantic love, ambition, hope for particular projects — depend on our not regarding their objects with complete dispassionate accuracy, and a fully disabused person would find the sources of happiness drained.
(iv) Yet happiness also requires lucidity: one must be able to recognize an illusion as an illusion when it begins to do harm, and to refuse to become its dupe.
(v) Happiness requires virtue, understood at least as being able to approve of one's own conduct under self-scrutiny; a person whose passions are satisfied but who cannot bear their own examination is not happy in the relevant sense.
(vi) Because whatever depends on fortune or on other people can be lost through no fault of one's own, the prudent person maintains only a moderated attachment to external goods and grounds happiness chiefly in goods that cannot be taken away.

I do not want a summary of Du Châtelet, and I am not asking which of these faithfully reproduces her. I want an audit of the set. Please go through the commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, rank those tensions by severity, and say for each what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be. Also flag any apparent tensions that dissolve on closer inspection, so I do not invent problems where there are none. If there is a single revision that would clean up the most, identify it and say what it costs.
