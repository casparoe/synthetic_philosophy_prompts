I'm a PhD student in moral psych, and I've been going back and forth with my advisor about what the cross-cultural stuff actually shows. You know the WEIRD problem: most of our classic findings come from Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic samples, and when you actually go to small-scale societies you find people who don't organize moral judgment the same way—different weights on harm versus purity, different views about whether certain violations are morally wrong at all, and so on. Coming out of undergrad I was a fairly straightforward moral realist: I thought there were stance-independent moral facts and that ordinary moral judgment tracks them imperfectly. But reading the anthopology side of things has made me think the variation is real and deep, not just noise or divergence about non-moral facts. I keep flip-flopping on how much this should actually move my metaethics.

So here's what I want: what's your credence that there are no stance-independent moral facts—i.e., that moral realism is false—in light of the cross-cultural variation lit? Decompose it. I'd want at least: (1) the probability that the reported variation is mostly robust and not an artifact of translation, vignette framing, or sampling; (2) the probability, conditional on its being robust, that the disagreement is genuinely moral rather than driven by non-moral background beliefs; (3) the probability that variation this deep and persistent would be surprising if stance-independent moral facts existed; and (4) whatever other terms you think carry weight. Give a number for each and say how you combine them. Then tell me wich sub-question the final number is most sensitive to and what evidence would shift you most.
