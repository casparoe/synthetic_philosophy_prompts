I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind course, and one unit presents four ways a mind can keep its behavior aligned with its environment. A Darwinian creature improves only by the differential survival of whole organisms across generations: its behavior is fixed at birth and cannot be revised within its lifetime. A Skinnerian creature can revise its individual behavior within a lifetime, but only through actual reinforcement: rewarded actions are retained, punished or unrewarded ones are extinguished. A Popperian creature has an internal model in which candidate actions can be tried out before they are performed, so that unsuccessful candidates are eliminated as hypotheses rather than as behavior or as organisms. A Gregorian creature can transmit tested designs through language or other representations, so that improvements accumulate across individuals without each learner having to recreate them from scratch.

I have a single 90-minute session with twenty-four upper-level undergraduates. Design a class activity, most likely a set of progressive rounds built around one task, in which students are assigned to the four levels and made to feel, not just be told, what each level can and cannot do. The task should be concrete enough to be run from printed materials: a repeated two-armed bandit with shifting payoffs, a small gridworld with hidden traps, or something equivalent. The constraints have to do real work. A Darwinian run should have no within-lifetime learning but should allow rapid cycling of generations of fixed strategies. A Skinnerian run should permit only reinforcement and prohibit verbal hints, explicit planning, and note-taking. A Popperian run should allow planning from an internal model but prohibit transmitting that model to the next player. A Gregorian run should allow the passing of instructions, so that a fresh player inherits what earlier players paid to learn. Use a point-based payoff scheme rather than money.

Then give me a debrief that maps what actually happens to the underlying distinctions: the death of whole organisms, the death of overt behavior, the death of hypotheses, and the outsourcing of design into the wider culture. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, for example students smuggling in language where their assigned level forbids it, confusion over how to stage generations within one class period, or the task being too easy to solve by unguided reinforcement, and explain how to handle each failure. Finally, be explicit about which aspect of the four-level distinction cannot be made to appear in a one-session game and will still have to be supplied by the instructor.
