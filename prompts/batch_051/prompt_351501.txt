I've been trying to sharpen what should count as an explanation of a system's behavior rather than a mere reproduction of it, and I've settled on three criteria: an explanation must be substantially shorter than a step-by-step simulation of the behavior it covers; it must let someone predict outcomes for cases they haven't already tabulated, without running the full system; and it must identify some feature of the system that is responsible for those outcomes rather than simply reporting them. I'm now testing these criteria against two cases from the study of cellular automata. The first is a complete lookup table for a one-dimensional binary cellular automaton on a fixed 20-cell ring (2^20 possible initial configurations), with a rule that is simple to state but has no obvious shorthand for its long-run behavior; every initial configuration is listed next to the configuration it reaches after 100 time steps, all computed by brute force. The second is a proof that a certain elementary cellular automaton is computation-universal, together with a general construction that encodes an arbitrary Turing machine into the automaton's initial configuration so that the automaton's subsequent states carry out that machine's computation. Is either of these an explanation of the automaton's behavior under my three criteria? Does either case show that one of the three criteria is not really necessary, or that the criteria themselves need revising?
