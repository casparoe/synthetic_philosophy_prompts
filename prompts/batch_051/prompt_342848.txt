Consider Adam Smith’s account of sympathy in *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*. Here is one passage, early in Part I, Section I, Chapter I, where Smith confirms that we have no immediate access to other people’s feelings:

As we have no immediate experience of what other men feel, we can form no idea of the manner in which they are affected, but by conceiving what we ourselves should feel in the like situation. Though our brother is upon the rack, as long as we ourselves are at our ease, our senses will never inform us of what he suffers. They never did, and never can, carry us beyond our own person, and it is by the imagination only that we can form any conception of what are his sensations.

I’m trying to figure out how far Smith’s “impartial spectator” actually parts ways with the ordinary process of sympathy described in this passage. If all I can ever do is imagine myself in another’s shoes, then when the spectator is impartial it seems like the main shift is just whose shoes are imagined and which features of the situation are held constant. Write the joint statement an adamant Smithian and a committed care ethicist could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. It should include: the claims they can both endorse about what sympathy does, the precise point where they diverge over whether the impartial spectator corrects ordinary bias or quietly smuggles in a demand to stop caring about proximity and partiality, the cruxes, and for each crux a concrete case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side’s prediction written down in advance. Don’t resolve the disagreement—just make it so exact that both sides would say, yes, that’s where we are stuck.
