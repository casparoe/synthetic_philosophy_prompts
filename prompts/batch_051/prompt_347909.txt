I am a novelist, and the phrase "the tragedy of the commons" has begun to do a great deal of work in my vicinity, not always honestly. In the shared studio building where I keep a desk, a painter invokes it whenever the communal kitchen fills with unwashed dishes, as though the phrase itself proved that no shared space can be cared for and that the only remedy is private lockers. While researching a novel set in a fishing town, I heard an economist use the same phrase to explain how individually rational fishing decisions can deplete a fishery on which everyone depends. A neighbour applies it to the building's recycling bins; a television essayist applied it to the atmosphere; a friend used it to explain why no one replaces the toner in the shared photocopier. I suspect that several distinct claims are travelling under this one phrase, some trivial, some substantial, some likely false as stated. I would like you to separate them. Please lay out, as a list, the distinct precise claims that "the tragedy of the commons" could be making in these uses. For each, state the claim exactly, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or a contentious or probably false one, and identify which of the uses I listed that reading would actually support. In particular, I want to know whether any respectable reading of the phrase, by itself, establishes that shared resources cannot be well governed, or whether that conclusion requires further premises the phrase does not contain. I intend to rehearse this before taking it up with the painter, so precision about what the phrase can and cannot prove matters more to me than a single tidy definition.
