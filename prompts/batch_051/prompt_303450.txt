I'm a novelist, and I'm trying to write a character who has a very strong, almost physical sense that some acts are wrong not because they hurt anyone but because they feel desecrating—like they dirty something that should stay clean. Recently I've been hanging around some people in a moral-psychology reading group and they keep reaching for a smoke-detector analogy to explain this. Their line is that moral disgust is like a smoke detector: it's loud, crude, and it sometimes goes off for burnt toast, but you'd be a fool to rip out the batteries. The idea seems to be that disgust, however unpleasant and however bad it is at giving reasons, is a cheap, pre-rational detector of something real in the moral domain—sacredness, contamination, something like that—and that we should treat it as a fallible but usefull warning signal. In their arguments, this is what makes repugnance count as a legitimate moral consideration in debates about cloning, organ markets, ritual purity, and the like.

Please reason through this carefully and take the analogy apart as a moral psychologist or philosopher who works on moral emotions. Start by brainstorming all the moving parts of the smoke-detector story before you commit to a reading. Then give me an explicit correspondence table: in the target, what plays the role of the smoke, the fire, the detector, the battery, the false alarm, the missed alarm, the person who installs the thing, the building code? After that, walk me through where the analogy breaks, and separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the conclusion from the ones that are just surface-level. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion survives—that moral disgust should be treated as a defeasible signal of genuine moral wrongness rather than dismissed because it can't articulate its reasons—and if the analogy can't carry that weight, tell me what a better analogy would be. Please write in American English, and don't be afraid to be technical.
