I am a philosopher of science organizing an adversarial-collaboration project between two colleagues who have spent the past term arguing about unrealistic assumptions in scientific models. I'd like you to act as the expert drafter of the document they should have produced. One colleague holds the predictivist position associated with Milton Friedman, often called the F-twist: models are to be assessed by their predictive success in the intended domain, and the falsity or unrealism of their assumptions is, by itself, no defect; demanding realistic assumptions is a methodological error. The other holds a causal-isolationist position: idealizations are legitimate when they isolate, exaggerate, or abstract away factors that are genuinely causally relevant, so a model whose false assumptions are mere computational conveniences may predict well while failing to explain, and is to that extent deficient. The final document should be something both could sign, not a compromise and not a verdict: the claims they share, their residual disagreements made exact, and the questions on which their dispute actually turns.

Please format it as follows. First, a numbered agreed-claims section covering the nature of models, the difference between idealization and abstraction, the role of predictive success, and shared readings of two test cases: the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium model and the Lotka-Volterra predator-prey model. These claims should be neither vacuous nor slanted. Second, a disagreements table in which each row gives a neutrally worded question and then the exact wording each side accepts for its own position. Third, a crux list: the sub-questions such that settling one would reasonably move at least one side. I suggest you weigh at least these four candidate cruxes, though you may add or replace them with argument: (a) whether the predictive success of a model is enough when no answer can be given to the question why the model works in its domain; (b) whether, given two models equally accurate on all available data, there is any non-aesthetic reason to prefer the one whose assumptions more closely track known mechanisms; (c) whether robustness of a result across models with mutually inconsistent idealizations raises confidence or instead shows that the result is insensitive to truth; and (d) whether behavior under domain shift — perturbing the target system beyond the range in which the model was fitted — should be counted as a further prediction or as evidence about representational accuracy. For each crux, record both sides' predictions in advance, keyed to a concrete case or class of results both accept as evidentially relevant, using the Hardy-Weinberg and Lotka-Volterra cases wherever they help.

Throughout, think carefully about which disagreements are substantive and which are verbal. In particular, the two sides may use "realistic" to mean different things — literal truth, everyday resemblance, or faithfulness to causal structure — and a good draft should introduce neutral vocabulary wherever that dissolves a merely apparent dispute. Before you write the document, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming candidate formulations each side would accept, and then note where a formulation rests on a contested reading. End with a brief meta-note saying how confident you are in the wording you have drafted and identifying the single formulation or interpretive claim you are least sure of.
