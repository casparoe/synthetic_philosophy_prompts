I've been reading a popular book on how institutions and technologies change — one of those crossover books that draws on economics and economic history — and a single paragraph near the end has been nagging at me. The author is summarizing what the 'path dependence' literature supposedly shows, and as best I can reconstruct it from memory, the claim goes like this. QWERTY still dominates our keyboards not because it is the best layout but because it got locked in early and no one could coordinate to switch; VHS beat Betamax for similar reasons of timing and cumulative advantage rather than technical merit; and the location and later preeminence of great cities often turn on small accidents of early settlement that then become self-reinforcing. From these cases the author draws a sweeping lesson: history is deeply arbitrary. Where a system ends up is not explained by any underlying causes or by the superior properties of the winners; it is explained mainly by chance and happenstance. Any story that makes the actual outcome look inevitable — or that explains the success of an institution, a technology, or a city by saying it was simply better — is therefore a just-so story told after the fact, and we should distrust it.

I find the conclusion attractive but a little too strong. I'm inclined to believe that many 'why the winner won' accounts are post-hoc rationalizations, and I know the QWERTY case is famous, so the examples feel credible. But I don't know the actual research well enough to tell whether this summary is fair, or whether it is quietly collapsing several different claims into one. Is this a fair account of what path dependence shows? Please separate what is accurate, what is genuinely contested, and what a careful defender of the research would say has been overstated or conflated. I'd also like you to say how confident you are in each part of your assessment and to single out the point you are least sure about.
