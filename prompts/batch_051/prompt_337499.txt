I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been trying to understand what makes a definition viciously circular. The usual starting point is a sentence that attributes falsehood to itself. Curry's paradox then seemed to show that even negation is unnecessary: let C be a sentence that says that if C is true then P, for arbitrary P, and the truth predicate appears to deliver P anyway. That still looked self-referential in the obvious sense. Then I encountered Yablo's paradox, which is supposed to produce the same kind of trouble from an infinite sequence of sentences, none of which refers to itself. Since I reason about recursive data structures and fixed points for a living, I want to know whether the argument below actually goes through and where the circularity, if any, is hiding.

Let S_1, S_2, S_3, ... be a countably infinite sequence of sentences, where each S_n says that for every m > n, S_m is false. Here is the argument that this list cannot be consistently assigned truth values. (1) Suppose some S_n is true. (2) Then, since S_n says that every later sentence is false, every S_m with m > n is false. (3) In particular, S_{n+1} is false, and every S_m with m > n+1 is false. (4) But S_{n+1} says exactly that every sentence after S_{n+1} is false, and a sentence is true when what it says is the case; so S_{n+1} is true. (5) That contradicts the claim in step (3) that S_{n+1} is false. Since n was arbitrary, no S_n is true. (6) By bivalence, every sentence is either true or false, so every S_n is false. (7) But if every sentence in the list is false, then for any n, every sentence after S_n is false, which is exactly what S_n says; so S_n is true. (8) Contradiction.

I cannot find the bad step. Is the deduction to contradiction valid under the usual assumptions about truth and classical logic? If a step fails, identify it and say under which reading it fails. If the argument is valid, which structural assumption does the real work — bivalence, the unrestricted principle that a sentence is true exactly when what it says is the case, or the assumption that the infinite sequence of sentences is well-defined at all? And finally, does Yablo's construction genuinely avoid self-reference, or is the self-reference smuggled in by defining the whole list at once?
