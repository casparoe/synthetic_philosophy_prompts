Take the familiar puzzle that the two roots of x² + 1 are structurally indistinguishable: swapping i with −i preserves every statement in the language of fields, so nothing internal to the complex field designates one of them. Now vary the case: over the rationals, x³ − 2 has the three roots α, αω, and αω², where α = ∛2 is the real cube root and ω is a primitive cube root of unity satisfying ω² + ω + 1 = 0. In the field obtained by adjoining these roots to Q, there are symmetries of addition and multiplication — field automorphisms fixing every rational number — taking any one of the three roots to any other, so algebraically all three are on a par even though exactly one is real; in particular, complex conjugation fixes α and swaps ω with ω². Work through how the analysis of "it doesn't matter how you label the roots" changes under this variation. Specifically: (a) Is defining α as the real cube root a canonical choice, and how does that differ from choosing i rather than −i? (b) Once α is fixed, are ω and ω² still genuinely interchangeable, or does the sign of their imaginary parts give a non-arbitrary labeling? (c) If a computer algebra system must list the three roots in some order, what exactly is arbitrary about that ordering, and does the arbitrariness have mathematical consequences? Be precise about what "canonical" amounts to in each case and about which facts are invariant under the relevant symmetries.
