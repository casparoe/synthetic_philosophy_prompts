Please argue for the following position: accepting the simulation argument as a serious trilemma should substantially dampen fanaticism about the far future. By the simulation argument I mean the claim that at least one of the following holds: (i) almost no human-level civilizations reach a technologically mature stage capable of running large numbers of detailed ancestor simulations; (ii) almost no such civilizations have both the means and the desire to run them; or (iii) we are almost certainly living in an ancestor simulation. By fanaticism about the far future I mean the tendency in expected-value reasoning to let minuscule probabilities of influencing the very long-run future—centuries, millennia, or longer than that—dominate all other moral considerations. In constructing the argument, do not rest it solely on the thought that a simulated future is unreal or that our actions cannot reach outside a simulation. Engage with the strongest counterarguments, including at least these two: first, that the trilemma supplies no precise probabilities and therefore cannot justify any particular rate of discount; second, that a simulation, if we are in one, may itself contain a far future whose trajectory our present choices can still influence. End by stating how, if at all, a dampened longtermism should differ from one that has abandoned the far future as a source of reasons.
