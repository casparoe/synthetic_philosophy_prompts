I chair the governance committee of a professional standards body. Our rules make certain short written messages in our official channels count as acts, not just as reports of attitudes: a committee member can resign, recuse themselves, nominate a candidate, second a nomination, withdraw a motion, or accept an office just by typing the right kind of message to the right list. Our committee members are volunteers who write quickly and often on phones, and every week we receive dozens of messages that might or might not be the act in question — "I'm basically out," "consider this my no vote on the whole thing," "fine, I'll chair it, but I'm not happy about it," "you can count me as a second," "I already said that in the team chat." My staff has to decide, usually within fifteen minutes and without philosophical training, whether the act was performed at all, whether it binds the member, and how to log anything marginal. The stakes are real but not dramatic: a wrong call changes who can vote, who has standing, and who is in office, and contested cases come to my committee for a final call.

Please write us a reusable decision procedure, in plain language, grounded in what the theory of speech acts and performative utterances can actually support rather than in local intuition. The procedure should run through questions in order: whether the message invoked a convention our body actually has; whether the speaker, audience, channel, and circumstances were appropriate; whether the conventional procedure was executed correctly and completely; whether the required commitment (and, where our rules require it, the accompanying conduct) is present; and what role uptake by the addressee or the committee plays. It should use the distinction between a misfire, where the act was void and never occurred, and an abuse, where the act occurred but was hollow or insincere, and it should say which kinds of failure do and do not void the act. It should also say when a message can be retracted before or after uptake, and when "I was joking" or "I didn't mean it like that" should matter. At each step, mark what is fixed by our written rules, what the speech act analysis can determine from the message and its context, and what remains unavoidably a judgment call. Where the real problem is that our rules never specified who must be addressed or what wording counts, the procedure should flag that as a gap in the rules rather than pretend it is a fact about the message.

Then run the procedure on three representative cases: an explicit nomination sent by an ineligible member to the wrong list; an angry resignation typed in a side channel ("I'm done with this committee"); and a written acceptance of an office that the member then ignores. At least one of the three should come out in a way my committee will not like, and you should say so plainly. Finally, state what the procedure cannot decide, where we will still have to make the call ourselves, and why.
