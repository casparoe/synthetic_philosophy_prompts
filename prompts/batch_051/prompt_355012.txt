I keep running into a clash in epistemology and I want someone to actually adjudicate it, not just lay out both sides and wave.

The theory I'm drawn to is simple reliabilism about justification: a belief is justified just in case it's produced by a reliable cognitive process. I like it because it unifies perception, memory, testimony, and inference under one heading, and because it doesn't demand that I know my faculties are trustworthy before they can justify me — a kid or a dog can know things without having views about their own reliability. It also gives definite verdicts in most cases, which is more than I can say for a lot of views.

Here's the intuition that won't leave me alone. I look at a wall, it looks red, I believe it's red; let's grant my color vision is reliable. Now I run the little argument: the wall is red; my color vision reports it's red; so this time my color vision got it right. Do that across enough walls and lighting conditions, and on the reliabilist picture I seem to have built a justified inductive case for the conclusion that my color vision is reliable — using only outputs of the very faculty whose reliability is at issue. That feels like cheating. It's a free lunch: my eyes vouch for my eyes. The belief "my color vision is reliable" doesn't seem justified when it's formed that way, even though every step looks licensed by the theory. And there's a milder cousin: I already seem to know that the wall isn't a white wall under red light just by knowing it's red, and that in turn underwrites trusting my vision — a similar too-easy knowledge.

So the clash is between a theory with real systematic virtues — simplicity, unification, no self-certification requirement, tidy verdicts — and the firm intuition that you can't pull yourself up by your own epistemic bootstraps. Which yields?

I want the strongest form of each side. For the theory: why the bootstrapped conclusion actually is justified, or why the sense that it isn't is a debunking artifact — maybe I'm confusing being justified with being able to offer a non-circular dialectical defense, or overgeneralizing from circular arguments to circular belief-forming processes, which might be fine. For the intuition: what exactly the defect is, beyond "it feels gross" — is it that a source can't confirm itself without independent corroboration, or that induction/deduction can't transmit justification to a conclusion that asserts the reliability of the very source of the premises, or something else? Then give a negotiated verdict. Should the intuition be dropped? Should reliabilism be amended, and what restriction would do the work without wrecking the virtues that made it attractive? Or is there a reading on which they're compatible — for instance, the bootstrapped belief is justified but defeated once I notice the provenance — and what test would tell that apart from a face-saving relabeling? Mostly I want to know what we stand to loose either way.
