I'm a software engineer without a formal philosophy background, but Bayesian epistemology keeps coming up in the forecasting and decision-making systems I work on, and a few of us at work have a standing discussion group on diachronic rationality. I've written down the set of commitments I've ended up with over the past year, and I'd like a consistency audit before I present them. I've deliberately listed them without flagging which ones I'm most attached to, because I want to find out where the stress points are rather than guide the answer.

Here is the set:

(1) Rational credences obey the probability axioms and update by conditionalization on new evidence.

(2) Reflection: my current credence in any proposition should equal my current expectation of the credence I will have in that proposition at any later time. Speaking loosely, I should defer to my future self's credences.

(3) A related but narrower claim: when I know that at a later time I will conditionalize on one member of a known partition of possible observations, my current credence is already the weighted average of my possible future credences, so I cannot predict the direction of my own update.

(4) I also think a rational agent can know she is about to lose information. If I know I will take a drug tonight that erases my memory of a diagnostic test result, then tonight my credence that the diagnosis is positive is high, but I know that tomorrow, having forgotten the result, I will rationally fall back to a lower prior-based credence. Tonight-me and tomorrow-me both seem rational, and neither seems to be making a mistake.

(5) I think rational agents may make binding precommitments that constrain a later self. If I set my phone to lock me out of an app after midnight, and I do this precisely because I know my midnight self will choose to keep scrolling, the precommitment is not thereby irrational.

(6) In the Sleeping Beauty problem---a fair coin is flipped Sunday night; Beauty is woken Monday if heads and both Monday and Tuesday if tails, with her memory of any earlier waking erased, and she knows the setup---I side with the thirders: when she wakes, her credence in heads should be 1/3.

Please structure the response as a JSON object with the following fields:

- "tensions": an array of objects sorted by severity in descending order, each with "commitments" (the labels of the conflicting claims), "severity" (an integer from 1 to 10, where 10 means outright contradiction or near-total overhaul is required), "explanation" (the nature of the conflict), "cheapest_revision" (which commitment to modify or abandon and how), and "cost_of_revision" (what is lost by taking that fix).
- "apparent_tensions": an array of objects, each with "commitments" and "why_it_dissolves" (the distinction that removes the conflict).
- "least_costly_repair": a string describing the smallest overall revision that makes the surviving set consistent, and indicating which original commitments survive intact.

Rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating every pair as equally problematic, and say clearly where the apparent conflicts only look like conflicts.
