Suppose you are advising the research-ethics policy committee of a large academic medical center. The committee is rewriting its guidance for when the institutional review board may approve a randomized controlled trial that compares an established treatment with a promising but unproven alternative. The committee has provisionally accepted that such trials are permissible only while there is clinical equipoise, understood as a state in which the relevant community of expert practitioners is genuinely uncertain about, or honestly divided over, which arm would be better for the patients enrolled. The members disagree, however, about which ways of spelling out that requirement should actually govern their guidance, and which disputes are refinements that would rarely change an approval decision.

They have asked you to produce a short agenda: the three or four questions about equipoise they most need to resolve before the guidance is issued, in priority order, and the questions they should explicitly set aside as overrated. The committee has a small policy staff and a six-month deadline.

Among the issues on the table are these.

Whether equipoise is a property of the trial as a whole or of each enrolling physician individually, and whether a physician who has lost personal equipoise may still ethically refer a patient to a trial about which the expert community remains divided.

Whether the relevant uncertainty covers only the comparative merits of the two arms, or also the surrounding care setting — for example, whether protocol-driven care inside the trial is as good for a given patient as individualized treatment outside it.

Whether equipoise should be understood as a claim about the balance of evidence or the balance of expert opinion, and what the guidance should say when the two come apart, as when the published evidence is thin but expert practice has largely settled.

How to handle designs that strain the standard picture: non-inferiority trials, adaptive and platform trials that change arms or allocation ratios mid-course, and trials whose control arm is fixed by regulatory requirements rather than by genuine clinical uncertainty.

Whether equipoise is only a condition on starting a trial or a continuing requirement, and how much of the work should instead be done by independent data monitoring and by consent.

Whether the point of equipoise is to protect the treating physician's duty to the individual patient, or to protect something that a rigorous consent process could secure just as well, and whether consent can carry the moral weight if equipoise is weakened.

For each question you prioritize, explain what kind of approval decision it would actually change, how often that situation arises in the center's portfolio, how tractable it is to turn into workable written policy, and what argument or evidence would move it up or down the ranking. For each question you set aside, say why resolving it would change little in practice. I am more interested in the defended ordering than in a survey of positions, and I would rather you introduce a question I have not listed than pad the list.
