I've been listening to a bunch of AI safety people argue about "low-impact AI," and one analogy keeps coming up that I think is doing more work than they admit. They say a helpful AI should be like a good houseguest: use the guest room and the coffeepot because those are offered, but don't rewire the house, don't repaint the living room, don't eat the last of something clearly not meant for you, and leave the place at least as close as you can to how you found it. The move they make from this is that "avoid side effects" can be operationalized before we solve value alignment: you just penalize the system for making the world diverge from the basline of what would have happened if it had never acted (or had stayed shut off), and that supposedly gives you a target that doesn't require a full specification of human value.

I'm not sure it survives contact with the details, and I want an audit rather than a vibe. Can you lay out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the host, the invitation, the guest, the furniture, the state of the house when you arrived, and the final walkthrough? Then go after the breaks: what does the houseguest analogy quietly import from the human case that the AI case lacks? In particular, try to pin down whether a world has a well-defined "how you found it" baseline, whether long-term resource use is a side effect or just the unavoidable cost of doing anything, whether painting one wall counts as a side effect even if the host would have liked it, and whether a divergence penalty can work without somebody already deciding which divergences matter and which to ignore. Then give me the bottom line: does the conclusion — that "avoid side effects" can be defined and measured without a full specification of value — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument? If there's a better analogy that would actually support the conclusion, say what it is.
