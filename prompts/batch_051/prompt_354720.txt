I am a cognitive neuroscientist who works on the sense of self: body ownership, sense of agency, multisensory integration, the rubber-hand and full-body illusion paradigms, and predictive-processing accounts in which the self figures as a high-level object in a generative model of the body and its environment. That vocabulary is the one I actually reason in, and I have read almost no medieval philosophy with any care. I would like you to take Avicenna's flying man argument and restate it in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. As I understand the argument, it runs roughly this way: imagine a person created all at once, fully developed, but suspended in perfectly uniform air with eyes covered and limbs spread so that no part of the body touches another; such a person would have no sensory contact with anything, not even the pressure of limb on limb, and yet he would still affirm that he exists while not affirming that he has any body part, any limb, or any internal organ. Avicenna takes this to show that what the person affirms — the self — is not identical with the body or any bodily part. Please restate each step of that reasoning using my concepts: body schema versus body image, exteroception and interoception, efference copy and predicted sensory consequences, the distinction between the sense of ownership and the sense of agency, and the notion of a minimal first-person perspective or subjective frame that can remain even when the body model is unavailable.

Then give me the residue report. Which of Avicenna's moves translate cleanly into claims that my literature could in principle test or model — for instance, the idea that a subject can represent its own existence without a current body model? Which moves only survive if you distort them — for example, if you quietly reinterpret Avicenna's soul as a predictive model, or his claim that the self is known directly as a claim about introspective reliability? And which moves have no counterpart in my framework at all, because they presuppose a metaphysics of substances and essences that my vocabulary simply cannot express? I want the translation to be honest in both directions: do not smuggle in the assumption that the self is a neural representation and then announce that Avicenna was groping toward predictive processing, but also do not pretend my framework is defective merely because it speaks a different ontology.

The question behind all this is whether my standing intuition — that the flying man is really an intuition pump for a core self that is distinct from body representation, and that neuroscience eventually vindicates the distinction — is a fair reading, or whether the argument only looks plausible because it equivocates on something my vocabulary cannot even state. Tell me whether the residue points to something my framework is genuinely missing, or to something Avicenna was less clear about than he seemed.
