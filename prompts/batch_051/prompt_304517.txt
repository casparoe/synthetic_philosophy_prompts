I can't stop thinking about an argument I had after a dinner party. One friend, Lena, has basically reorganised her life around caring for her elderly father: she left a better job, moved back to her hometown, and spends enormous amounts of time and money on him, and everyone, me included, calls it devotion. Another friend, Amir, was just passed over for a promotion because the hiring manager gave the job to his own daughter. The same people who praised Lena called that manager corrupt, or at least disgraceful. But when you line the two cases up, in both situations someone gives a relative what they have to give rather than letting it go to whoever might need it more or do the job better. The only difference I can see is that Lena is spending her own life and money, whereas the manager was spending a company's payroll. I can't help feeling there's a principle in here somewere, but I can't make it stick.

This looks to me like the old fight between the Confucians and Mozi. As I understand them, Confucians like Mencius defended graded love, the idea that care naturally begins with family and radiates outward, and that this ordering is part of what makes a person decent. Mozi attacked exactly this, and taught impartial care: you should be as ready to benefit a stranger's father as your own, because partiality is what fuels conflict. I keep wondering whether my friends' split reactions are just Confucian common sense with a Mozi-shaped hole in it, or whether there is a principled line between Lena's devotion and the manager's nepotism.

I'd like the take of an expert moral philosopher who works on Confucian and Mohist ethics. Please think carefully, and before committing to an answer, brainstorm at least three candidate principles that could justify the asymmetry. Then give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Point 1 should be the single strongest principle you can find; point 2 a nearby case where it works; point 3 a nearby case where it strains or breaks; point 4 the strongest Mozi-style objection to your principle, fairly put; and point 5 your honest verdict on whether the asymmetry between praised family partiality and condemned nepotism should be kept, revised, or given up. Please write in British English.
