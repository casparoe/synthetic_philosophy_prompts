I want to practice arguing against a committed accuracy-first epistemologist. Play that role: an unnamed proponent of the accuracy-first program in formal epistemology, also called epistemic utility theory. Your core commitments are these. The sole fundamental epistemic value is accuracy, understood as closeness of credences to truth; a credence function is evaluated by how inaccurate it is at possible worlds. The norms governing rational credence—above all Probabilism, the claim that rational credences obey the probability axioms—are not primitive but derived from the pursuit of accuracy. You accept the standard machinery: strictly proper scoring rules such as the Brier score or the log score as admissible measures of inaccuracy, an additive structure across the propositions in the agenda, and arguments by accuracy dominance or expected accuracy. You are sharp, not a strawman: you will concede local technical problems and make refinements, but you will not give up the central claim that accuracy is the fundamental source of epistemic normativity. You may draw on familiar moves from this literature, but present them in your own voice as your own reasoning, without citing titles or authors. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to a single paragraph.

My opening challenge is this. Your dominance argument for Probabilism seems to presuppose that the truth values of the agenda's propositions are fixed independently of the credence function being scored. But put a self-referential proposition S in the agenda: S says that my credence in S is less than 0.5. For any candidate credence function c, S is true just in case c(S) < 0.5, so the truth value of S co-varies with the very credence function under evaluation. As a result, not every pair of a world and a credence function is admissible: a world that makes S false cannot be paired with a credence function that assigns S a credence of 0.4, and so on. Your theorem, by contrast, compares candidate credence functions against a common space of possible worlds. How do you run the accuracy-dominance argument over an agenda like this without changing the subject or quietly ruling out self-referential propositions? And if you cannot run it, does the vindication of Probabilism hold only for artificially restricted agendas that no reflective agent—one who reasons about her own credences—ever actually has?
