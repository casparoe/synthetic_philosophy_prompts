Much of my working life in the natural sciences was spent with models that describe a system by a few parameters and then predict its behavior; the vocabulary of "will," "self-control," and "resolution" always struck me as, by contrast, descriptively idle. Now that I am retired and reading philosophy in earnest, I find myself drawn to the account of weakness of will that runs through hyperbolic discounting precisely because it supplies the mechanistic, quantitative story I was trained to expect.

On that account, the baseline is exponential discounting, whose constant rate makes an agent time-consistent: a plan that is optimal now remains optimal later, so there is no room for acting against one's own standing judgment. Hyperbolic discounting changes this. It weights near rewards disproportionately, so that a smaller, sooner reward can overtake a larger, later one as the moment of temptation approaches. What we call weakness of will is then not a failure of some inner faculty but the predictable working of a time-inconsistent discount function. The person who on Sunday resolves to run on Tuesday morning and on Tuesday stays in bed has not contradicted himself; he has simply made two different valuations from two different temporal standpoints. The view earns its keep by unifying a great deal: preference reversals in the laboratory, procrastination, addiction, the felt need to precommit, and the fact that penalties and commitment devices succeed exactly when a far-sighted self anticipates its own future reversal.

And yet I cannot make the phenomenology fit. When I switch off the alarm, I do not experience a new agent with a new discount rate arriving on the scene. I experience one agent who still holds the earlier judgment, who can think at that very moment that this is the worse choice and that he is making it, and who then acts against it. The experience is of conflict within a single person, not of a smooth succession between two valuations; the oldest description of the phenomenon — seeing the better and following the worse — captures it exactly. The hyperbolic model seems to dissolve rather than explain this, by telling me that the seeing and the following belong to different evaluative moments and that at the moment of choice I simply want the temptation more.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be amended, and whether there is a reading on which both survive. In particular: is there a sense of "wanting" in which, at the moment of capitulation, I still most want the earlier course, while the discount function merely describes the force of temptation as something other than wanting? Or is that exactly the sort of homuncular residue the model rightly teaches us to discard?
