Consider the following argument in the theory of animal communication, where an honest signal is one whose intensity reliably tracks some underlying quality of the sender, such as health, strength, or genetic condition. Is the argument sound as it stands? If not, identify the step (or steps) at which it fails, and explain how the standard account of the handicap principle corrects the reasoning.

(1) A signal can be honest only if low-quality senders cannot afford to produce it at the intensity that high-quality senders can.

(2) For a signal to be unaffordable to low-quality senders, producing it must be costly: it must draw on limited energy, time, or tolerance for risk.

(3) Therefore, other things being equal, the more costly a signal is, the more honest it will be; raising the cost of a display should make it a more reliable signal of sender quality.

(4) Hence, any costly display is an honest signal, and a cheap or costless display cannot carry honest information about sender quality.

This conclusion seems to overreach. Some honest signals are cheap in absolute terms — for example, an index such as body size in contest assessment, which is honest because it is physically constrained rather than because it is costly to produce — and some expensive displays could be produced by any individual with access to a common resource pool. If the conclusion is false, the argument must fail somewhere. Say exactly where, and explain why standard signaling theory treats honesty as depending on costs and benefits that are conditional on sender quality, rather than on the absolute cost of the display.
