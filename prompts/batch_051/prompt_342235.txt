I am taking a course on the ethics of deception, and while reading Augustine's treatment of lying I keep getting caught on an apparent inconsistency. He seems committed to an exceptionless prohibition: lying is always a sin, even when it is done to protect an innocent person's life, because the will to deceive corrupts the soul regardless of the beneficial consequences. I can see how one might square such a prohibition with the view that silence or withholding the truth need not count as lying; the hard case is his further practice of distinguishing various kinds of lies and ranking them by seriousness, treating lies about matters of faith as the gravest and lies told in jest or told to help another while harming no one as comparatively minor.

My confusion concerns what this ordering is supposed to do within an absolutist framework. If every lie is forbidden without exception, why does it matter how grave one lie is relative to another? And does the act of ranking lies by gravity not imply that the lesser kinds are at least partially excusable, or that the prohibition itself comes in degrees? I would like to understand what the taxonomy accomplishes in Augustine's account, rather than merely having the tension set aside.
