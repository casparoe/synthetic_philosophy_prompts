I'm a novelist, and the book I'm deep in turns on a defamation suit: a statistician called a state lottery's latest six-number draw "not random," and the lottery's lawyers answer that any finite sequence can come out of a fair machine, so the phrase is empty. I don't want the trial to run on a hand-wavy notion of randomness, and I don't want my characters to just be mouthpieces for whatever slogan sounds good. So before I write the cross-examination, give me the full spec.

Write the desiderata for a theory of when a finite sequence is random. What does an adequate account have to get right? I want:

- the clear cases it can't fumble: a trillion fair coin flips that come up all heads; the first million digits of pi; a sequence whose shortest generating program is about as long as the sequence itself; a sequence that passes every statistical test anyone has tried but was produced by a rule someone only finds later.
- the distinctions it has to respect and not collapse: random versus merely looking random; a property of the sequence, of the process that made it, or of what an observer knows.
- the conceptual constraints it can't wish away: every finite string has some short description relative to some machine; a fair coin can produce any finite string with positive probability; nobody ever gets infinite data to decide with.
- which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just camp preferences (frequency, unpredictability, incompressibility).

Then the part I actually want for the courtroom scene: tell me which subset of those requirements cant be jointly satisfied. If incompressibility and frequency can't be made to agree on a single draw, say so flat out, because that witness-box tension is the scene. Then briefly score the incompressibility view, the statistical-test view, and plain frequentism against the list — just which requirement each one bleeds on, not a full scorecard. Keep the weight on the requirements themselves.
