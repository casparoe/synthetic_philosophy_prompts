i've got a rule i use in a philosophy reading group i run, and it's become a running joke. whenever someone says a claim is neither true nor false---not becuase we don't know, but because it's a borderline case, a presupposition failure like the king of france being bald, or a future contingent like a sea battle tomorrow---i refuse to leave it there. i treat the gappiness as a sign the sentence just hasn't been sharpened, and i force a yes-or-no on the sharpened question that's actually doing the work. my line is that belief is bivalent, and 'neither' is usually a way of not committing. my co-organizer says i'm just denying real semantic phenomena and forcing false precision into questions that don't have it. is my rule defensible as an epistemic habit even if some semantics legitimately allows truth-value gaps? when does it help me, when does it mislead me, and is there a version of it worth keeping?
