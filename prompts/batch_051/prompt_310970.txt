Several democracies that refuse to condition the franchise on political knowledge for birthright citizens nonetheless require immigrants to pass a civics test about the country's political institutions as a condition of naturalization, after which they may vote. The same states typically repudiate literacy or knowledge tests at the ballot box as exclusionary. On its face, the practice pairs a strongly democratic principle—no epistemic gate to the vote—with a modestly epistocratic gate for newcomers. What theory of democratic competence does this asymmetry presuppose, if any? Does it strictly presuppose that the franchise should track demonstrated political knowledge, or can it be explained without epistemic assumptions, for instance by appeal to civic integration, a social contract, or the signaling of attachment? Is there a philosophically defensible reconstruction of the practice, or does it reveal that the state's rejection of voter knowledge tests is a matter of history and sentiment rather than principle?
