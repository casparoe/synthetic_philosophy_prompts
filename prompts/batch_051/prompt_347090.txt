Before I read any more candidate rules for decision under ignorance, I want the standard itself. I am working through the Rawls–Harsanyi dispute about what rationality requires behind a veil of ignorance, and the same setup keeps being made to do incompatible work: Rawls treats it as licensing maximin, Harsanyi treats it as licensing expected utility with equal probabilities, and minimax regret is brought in as a middle path that is less dour than maximin and less arbitrary than equal priors. Each side moves too quickly from a case it likes to the rule it likes, so I want the requirements fixed first. The purpose I have in mind is a single criterion that can cover both the original position and ordinary one-shot choices under complete ignorance, if one exists.

Write the desiderata: a precise list of what an adequate criterion for choice under complete ignorance must deliver, with the case or consideration that forces each requirement and an explicit verdict on whether it is non-negotiable or only a preference of one camp. At minimum, make the list respect — or argue against respecting — these distinctions: ignorance versus risk versus Knightian ambiguity; ordinal versus cardinal utility information; a rule's being well-defined versus its being well-motivated; and worst-case insurance versus sensitivity to what happens in non-worst states. Use these stress cases where they help: a society choosing principles of justice it must live under; a public health authority choosing among untested interventions for a novel pathogen with no base rates; and a one-shot choice whose state space the agent knows it cannot fully enumerate in advance.

Then flag any subset of the desiderata you doubt can be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most damage to the leading rules as a group. Close with a short scorecard — no more than a paragraph each — of maximin, minimax regret, and equiprobability expected utility against the list, keeping the weight on the criteria rather than the scoring.
