I've been arguing with a mate about whether satisficing is a genuinely different way of choosing or just maximising in disguise. He set out this argument and I half-buy it, but something smells off. It goes:

(1) A real choice includes not just the object you select (the flat, the job, the café) but also how much searching and deliberating you do before settling on it.
(2) A satisficing rule — e.g. take the first flat that scores 7/10 or more and stop looking — fixes how much search you do at each point as a function of what you've turned up so far.
(3) For any sane stopping rule of this kind, there is some preference ordering over the objects plus the costs of searching (time, effort, anxiety, missed opportunities) that makes the rule come out as expected-utility maximising.
(4) So a satisficer is really a maximiser over a bigger option set, where the options include 'keep searching' and 'stop now'.
(5) Therefore satisficing isnt an alternative to maximising; its just maximising for someone with particular tastes about search, and the whole bounded-rationality programme collapses into expected utility theory with extra steps.

I suspect the problem is either (3) or the leap from (3) to (4), but I cant make it precise. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails and why? If it is valid, does the conclusion actually undermine satisficing, or does it just redefine it away?
