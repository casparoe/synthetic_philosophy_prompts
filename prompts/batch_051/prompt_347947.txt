I'm a science journalist and I keep getting caught between editors who want every finding described as "X causes Y" and sources who say that's lazy. I've been rereading Russell's essay On the Notion of Cause in Mysticism and Logic. The bit I keep coming back to is where he claims that in advanced sciences like gravitational astronomy the word cause doesn't really turn up, and that the notion survives as a relic becuase no one thinks it does any harm. I want to turn that into something I can actually use on deadline. I write three of these stories a week, and a duff causal claim gets amplified by other outlets. Please write me a decision procedure—maybe a checklist or a set of tests in order—for deciding whether a science story can use causal language and, if so, how strong it can be. It has to be runnable in fifteen minutes before I file, it has to work when I only have a press release or an abstract, and it should flag which steps are genuinely Russellian and which are just my editorial judgment, including where my editor has to make the call. Then run it on three hypotheticals: a Mendelian randomisation study claiming a common habit raises depression risk, a lab study showing a molecule changes a pathway in cultured cells, and a population-level intervention that reduced traffic injuries after a law changed. I expect at least one case where the procedure tells me not to write the causal claim even though the researchers used the word in their abstract.
