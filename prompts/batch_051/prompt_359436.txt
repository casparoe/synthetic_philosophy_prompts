For my philosophy of language class I have to reconstruct Quine's argument from Two Dogmas of Empiricism, and I've ended up with the following numbered argument. I keep going back and forth on whether it proves to much, and my TA made a comment I didn't fully understand. Here it is:

(1) If there is a real analytic-synthetic distinction, there has to be a non-circular criterion that sorts arbitrary sentences into analytic and synthetic.

(2) The only plausible criteria are truth in virtue of meaning, synonymy, semantical rules, definition, and necessity.

(3) Truth in virtue of meaning is exactly the notion the criterion is supposed to explain, so it can't do the work.

(4) Semantical rules only tell you that certain sentences count as analytic for a particular constructed language; they don't say what it is for a sentence of a natural language, or a sentence in general, to be analytic by meaning.

(5) Definitions either report an already existing sameness of meaning or stipulate a new one, so they presuppose synonymy.

(6) Synonymy can only be tested by interchangeability salva veritate, but extensional interchangeability is to weak: two predicates can apply to exactly the same things without meaning the same, and if you require interchangeability in all contexts, including modal ones, you've already helped yourself to necessity.

(7) Necessity is no help, because on the standard story a necessary truth is just one whose truth is analytic, or reduces to logic plus meanings.

(8) So every candidate either is circular or collapses into another candidate in the same family.

(9) Therefore there is no analytic-synthetic distinction.

My question: is this argument valid? If not, where exactly does it break, and is it a false premise or a bad inference? My TA said something about step (1) demanding more than a distinction needs to demand, and about a slide from 'we don't have a criterion' to 'there's no distinction,' but I don't see why that's wrong if the distinction is supposed to do philosophical work. Pick the step that actually fails and give the reading under which it fails, or tell me the argument is fine.
