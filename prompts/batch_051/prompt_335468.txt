I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I keep running into the slogan "no causes in, no causes out" — most recently from a biostatistician who used it to shoot down a story I was pitching about mining electronic health records for causal effects. His line was that the data alone can't give you causes; you have to put causal assumptions in first or nothing causal comes out. But I've also heard people use the phrase to mean something much narrower (you can't tell which way a correlation runs) and something much more sweeping (all causal inference is just modeling your assumptions). I want to actually nail down what this quip could be claiming.

Could you give me a menu of the distinct precise claims "no causes in, no causes out" might stand for, roughly from the trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it's obviously true, substantive but defensible, or false? I'd also like to know where Pearl's causal hierarchy and the do-calculus fit in: if I specify a structural causal model — a DAG with arrows and functions — and use the do-operator to predict an intervention, that seems like putting causes in explicitly. Is that a counterexample to the slogan, a concession to it, or a refinement of it? And seperately, which reading would actually support this biostatistician's use of it against the health-records idea, and which readings would overstate what he could reasonably claim in that argument?
