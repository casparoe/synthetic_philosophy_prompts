An ethics consultant I work with takes firm positions on advance directives. She would not enforce a do-not-resuscitate order signed by a patient during an untreated major depressive episode, even though the patient is now unconscious and cannot speak. She would enforce a Ulysses directive: a patient with bipolar disorder wrote while stable that if he refused lithium while manic he should be hospitalized and medicated against his will, and he is now manic and refusing. She would honor a Jehovah's Witness's advance directive refusing blood when a car crash leaves her unconscious, with her family confirming the refusal never wavered. She would override a dying patient's written refusal of pain medication, signed while confused from sepsis, and would order analgesia.

Now she faces another case. At fifty-five, after watching her mother decline with dementia, a woman signed a directive stating that if she developed advanced dementia and could no longer recognize her family, she refused antibiotics for pneumonia and other life-prolonging treatment, and that she anticipated she might appear content in such a state but did not want to live that life. She is now eighty, has advanced dementia, no longer recognizes her family, appears cheerful, enjoys music and sweets, and has pneumonia. What should I expect the consultant to decide? The verdict matters less to me than the rule behind it: lay out the candidate rules that fit her earlier judgments, where they come apart on this case, which rule she is most likely running and why, and what further case would best separate them.
