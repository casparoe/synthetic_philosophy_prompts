I've been working through the many-worlds interpretation on my own, and I keep circling back to the familiar objection that it cannot make sense of probability. As I understand the objection, it runs like this. Many-worlds says the wavefunction never collapses: measurement entangles the observer with the outcome, and every branch allowed by the Schrödinger equation is real. But if every outcome happens, what can the Born rule mean when it assigns one outcome probability 1/3? There is no single future in which that probability could be realized, and if we simply count branches, each branch gets equal weight rather than the Born weight. So many-worlds seems unable to recover the probabilistic predictions of quantum mechanics.

What I cannot settle is whether this old objection still has force once two later ideas are added. The first is decoherence: branches rapidly stop interfering, so an observer inside a branch sees one definite, effectively classical result. The second is self-locating uncertainty: after a measurement but before looking at the result, I can know the entire wavefunction and still be uncertain about which branch I occupy. Probability can therefore be about my location among branches rather than about which branch becomes the world. Some writers then argue that a rational agent in that situation should assign self-locating credences to branches according to the Born weights — not as an extra physical law, but as a demand of rationality.

I want to know whether the original probability objection survives that move. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the derivation of the Born weights from rationality succeeds. Is the old objection dead, does it still go through in roughly its original form, or does it survive only in a repaired and weaker version? In answering, I would want to see which specific premise of the old objection decoherence-plus-self-locating uncertainty puts pressure on, which premise it leaves untouched, and whether the new move quietly imports the Born rule through the back door — for example, by assuming that rational agents must care about branches in proportion to amplitude. I am after a structural judgment about what the old argument needs in order to succeed and what the new development forces us to revise.
