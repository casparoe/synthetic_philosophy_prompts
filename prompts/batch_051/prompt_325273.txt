I retired a few years ago after a career in physical chemistry, and I have been spending part of my retirement working through the philosophy of probability and statistical inference. Recently I have been reading E. T. Jaynes's 1968 paper "Prior Probabilities," and I find myself both attracted to his argument and unsure how far it really goes. As I understand his position, he maintains that when one has only partial information, such as a known mean or variance, one should assign the prior that maximizes information-theoretic entropy subject to those constraints, because any other distribution would implicitly assume information one does not actually possess. He also argues that transformation groups can fix priors in a principled way: for example, invariance under changes of location or scale leads to certain standard forms, so that Jeffreys's rule emerges as a consequence rather than as an arbitrary convention.

I find this attractive because it seems to promise an objective substitute for the subjective choices that Bayesian practitioners so often have to make. Yet I am not certain how much it actually secures. The resulting priors appear to depend heavily on which constraints one chooses to treat as given, and on how the space of hypotheses or parameter values is described in the first place. I also wonder whether the argument establishes that there are uniquely rational priors, or only that, once one has accepted a particular model and a particular set of constraints, there is a natural default within that model. Could you please describe the most important implications of Jaynes's maximum entropy principle for the thesis that there are rational, non-arbitrary priors? I am especially interested in what it implies about the boundary between objective constraints and subjective modeling choices, and whether it can answer someone who insists that the choice of a prior is ultimately a matter of judgment.
