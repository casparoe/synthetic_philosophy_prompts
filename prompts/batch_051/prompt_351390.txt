I am a research officer on an independent commission that issues findings on claims about historical injustices between states and peoples, all of which are anonymised. Our working framework is the fourfold distinction Karl Jaspers drew after the Second World War between criminal guilt (individual legal liability for proven acts), political guilt (the liability citizens and their continuing institutions bear for the deeds of their state), moral guilt (an individual's own accountability for choices and omissions), and metaphysical guilt (a solidarity-based responsibility for standing by when one could have opposed injustice). The Commission has always held that criminal and moral guilt are not inherited, and we have never relied on metaphysical guilt alone to ground an enforceable order; the difficult cases are about what a continuing state or institution still owes.

Our previous findings include the following.
(1) In 2004, we endorsed a formal apology by the present government for a punitive expedition carried out by its colonial predecessor in 1905, while stating that no citizen living today bears criminal or moral guilt for those events.
(2) In 2010, asked by a woman whose great-grandfather had administered a forced-labour system to declare that she personally owed nothing, we declared that she bore no moral guilt but that her position did not dissolve the state's obligation to address the wrong.
(3) In 2013, we required a bank still operating and still owning buildings erected through forced labour to endow an educational trust.
(4) In 2016, we declined to order payment from a trading firm that had bought the assets of a slaving concern but had never held anything traceable to slave labour and shared no continuity of ownership or management with the original firm.
(5) In 2019, we directed a museum holding looted regalia to return them, describing this as an obligation of a continuing public institution rather than a verdict that individual curators or citizens were guilty.

We now face a claim for monetary reparations against a state that is constitutionally and juridically continuous with the colonial power that, in 1911, destroyed a town and seized its harvest. The seized goods were consumed within a few years; nothing taken at the time remains in public hands; and the state's present wealth comes from post-independence industries and more recent immigration, with no causal line back to the 1911 seizure. The claimants accept these facts but argue that a continuing state answers for its predecessor's actions regardless of whether any benefit persists.

What should the Commission decide if it wishes to be consistent with its own findings? Which of the five precedents is the closest analogy? If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the decision, spell out the competing principles our findings could be taken to embody, say what each requires here, and state which further case would most cleanly separate them. Please finish with a single line of the form \answer{...} giving your recommended decision as a short phrase, for example \answer{recommend reparations} or \answer{decline reparations}.
