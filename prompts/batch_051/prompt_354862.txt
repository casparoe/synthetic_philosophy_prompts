Two friends of mine, Dana and Tom, got into a long argument at a cafe last week, and I've been turning it over since. The subject was Dana's sister and brother-in-law, both atheists, who live a few blocks from the brother-in-law's very devout parents. The couple lets the grandparents take the kids, ages six and nine, to services and religious school every week, and the kids join family prayers and holiday rituals when they're over. At home the parents answer questions honestly: they tell the kids they don't believe the teachings, but that Grandma and Grandpa do, and that they go because they're part of this family and this practice is how the family holds together.

Dana said (1) the whole setup is quietly dishonest. The parents are having the children perform belief they've been taught to reject, so the kids learn religion as a manipulative social tool, not as something anyone actually commits to. Meanwhile the grandparents are being misled, since they obviously take the arrangement as evidence that the kids are being raised in the faith. Tom said (2) that's too crude. Participating in a ritual is not asserting every proposition the ritual contains. Belonging in a religiously mixed family requires what he called "ritual code-switching": you speak the family's religious idiom out of respect and membership, while being perfectly open with your kids about what you do and don't accept. The kids aren't deceived, because they know their parents' real views, and the grandparents haven't asked, so nobody's lied to them.

Dana came back (3) that the arrangement only works because the grandparents have been left with a false picture of what there grandchildren are being taught. If the parents really believed in Tom's code-switching ideal, they'd say plainly, "we're not raising them in the faith, but we'll happily bring them to services for the family's sake," and let the grandparents decide. Not doing that is exploiting a misunderstanding they've deliberately left in place. Tom answered (4) that full disclosure would be a kind of aggressive self-importance: nobody in a religiously divided family narrates their dissent at every wedding, funeral, or holiday table, and we don't usually call that lying. He said the parents are allowed to accept the goods of family belonging without turning every shared practice into a hearing about their own theology. At that point they were going in circles, and they asked me to referee, which is why I'm passing it to you.

Please act as a moral philosopher who works on honesty, assertion, and family ethics, and take this move by move. For each of the four numbered points, say what it gets right and wrong and how much it should matter relative to the others; then say who's more right overall, and what both Dana and Tom are missing. Start by brainstorming the possible principles about lying, deception by omission, participation without belief, and what parents owe their children and their extended family, before settling on your verdict.
