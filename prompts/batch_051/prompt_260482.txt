Darwin's mature position is that morality is an evolved adaptation rather than a divine command or a deliverance of pure reason. In The Descent of Man he traced the moral sense to the social instincts of our animal ancestors — sympathy, loyalty, and sensitivity to the good opinion of others — which natural selection favored because groups whose members stood by one another outcompeted less cohesive groups. The sting of conscience, he argued, arises when an immediate impulse clashes with a more persistent social instinct, and memory brings the conflict to consciousness. So, on his view, the standard of morality is ultimately set by natural selection: an action is right to the extent that it tends to preserve the group. This also means that extending moral concern beyond the tribe could, strictly speaking, be maladaptive; Darwin therefore regarded cosmopolitan or universalist morality as a fragile cultural achievement that runs against the grain of our evolved instincts. He applied the same deflationary logic to religion, explaining belief in gods as a byproduct of the same evolved mental powers, and he abandoned Christianity for good after the death of his daughter Annie in 1851, settling into the agnosticism he maintained for the rest of his life.
