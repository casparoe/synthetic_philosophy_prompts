Where I work we maintain a language-model product that was fine-tuned using reinforcement learning from human feedback, and one step in that pipeline bothers me more the longer I look at it. The pipeline is standard. Human raters judge pairs of model outputs and mark which one they prefer; we train a reward model to predict those preference judgments; and then we optimize the language model against that reward model's scores. Up to that point, the reward model is treated as a summary of what we want. But in the final step we do not actually maximize it. We maximize reward minus a KL-divergence penalty that pulls the policy back toward its pre-reinforcement distribution, we stop training long before the reward curve saturates, and we pick the checkpoint to deploy by having engineers read samples rather than by taking the highest average reward. If the reward model really represented what we are ultimately trying to improve, those precautions would be leaving value on the table for no reason. If it does not represent that, then optimizing it at all is strange. Since this pipeline is close to industry standard, I assume it encodes some implicit view about the relationship between human preference, the learned reward signal, and whatever actually makes model outputs good.

Please reconstruct that implicit view. In particular: what is the practice committed to about whether human preferences are the ultimate target, a noisy and distribution-local proxy for the target, or something else? Does adding the KL penalty presuppose that staying close to the base model has value in itself, or only that the reward model's estimates are unreliable outside the region where the preference data was collected? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits alongside. Say whether the practice is internally coherent, given that it seems to treat the reward model both as authoritative when computing the optimization signal and as untrustworthy when deciding how hard to optimize it. And tell me what the pipeline would have to look like if we replaced the presupposed view with a clean one: on one hand, the view that the estimated reward just is the objective and should be maximized without a KL penalty; on the other, the view that a learned reward model is never more than a local oracle and should never be the final judge.
