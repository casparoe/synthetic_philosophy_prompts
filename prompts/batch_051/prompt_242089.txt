In my economics class we studied the endowment effect. In the classic demonstration, a group of subjects is randomly given an inexpensive object such as a mug while another group is not, and the owners typically demand a much higher price to sell the object than the non-owners would be willing to pay to buy it, even though assignment to ownership was random. Our teacher said this result can be read in two ways. The first reading treats it as a bias: the owner's asking price is inflated by loss aversion, because giving the mug up is experienced as losing something and losses are weighted more heavily than gains. On this reading, the asking price overstates how much the owner actually values the mug. The second reading treats it as a legitimate preference: coming to own an object can itself change how much it is worth to you, because you learn what it is useful for, form plans involving it, or become attached to it. On this reading, the owner is not overstating anything; ownership has genuinely raised the object's value to that person.

I would like to know how one could test these two readings against one another rather than choosing between them on grounds of taste. Please design the strongest empirical test, or small battery of tests, you can for the claim that ownership genuinely changes a person's valuation, as opposed to merely inflating the prices they state because of loss aversion. Be concrete about the experimental setup: what conditions you would compare, whether you would elicit stated prices or actual choices, what the key measured variable would be, and which pattern of results would favor each interpretation. Then be equally concrete about what a positive result would and would not settle, including whether a test could show that the behavior it documents is rational or irrational outside the laboratory, and whether the line between a deeply entrenched bias and a genuine preference is fully empirical or partly a matter of how one defines a person's true valuation.
