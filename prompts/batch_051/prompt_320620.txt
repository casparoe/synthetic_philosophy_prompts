Exam question:

"In many religious and philosophical traditions, mystics claim that certain truths can be known but cannot be expressed in language. Some critics reply that this is self-defeating, because to say that some truths are ineffable is already to say something true about them. Is the notion of ineffable knowledge coherent? In your answer, distinguish at least these three senses of 'ineffable': (i) no proposition about the matter can be expressed at all; (ii) no statement captures the full content of the truth, although language can point to it; (iii) an experience has a qualitative character that cannot be conveyed to someone who lacks that experience. Assess the self-defeat objection and identify which sense, if any, would support the claim that there is ineffable knowledge of truths."

Student's answer:

"The self-defeat objection is decisive only against sense (i). If the statement 'there are ineffable truths' is true, then something true is said about the allegedly ineffable truths, and so they are not inexpressible in the strictest sense. But serious mystics are not committed to sense (i). They may mean sense (ii): the truth can be indicated even though no verbal formulation exhausts its content. Or they may mean sense (iii): the experience has a qualitative character unavailable to anyone who has not had it. Both are plainly coherent. Sense (iii) is especially solid: knowing what a mystical experience is like is itself a kind of knowledge, so a mystic who cannot describe union nonetheless knows something ineffable, just as I know what espresso tastes like without being able to convey the taste. Since this experiential knowledge puts her directly in contact with the very reality her ineffable truths are about, it gives her ineffable knowledge of those truths as well; the fact that she cannot state them does not make them less than propositional. Nor should we doubt this knowledge merely because third parties cannot check it. A person has first-person authority about her own experience, and an experience that presents itself as an awareness of a reality beyond words gives its subject adequate grounds to accept that reality. There is therefore no remaining conceptual problem with ineffable knowledge of truths."
