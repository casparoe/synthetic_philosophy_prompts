I've recently started training agents whose exploration is shaped by an artificial "discomfort" signal, and I've become genuinely unsure whether any system of this kind could suffer. I have firm verdicts about particular cases, but they refuse to fall under any principle I can articulate. I'd like you to find the principle, or show me which verdicts must go.

Here are my cases and verdicts. (1) A thermostatic controller that computes a temperature error and acts to reduce it: I'm confident this is not suffering; the error is just a number. (2) A gridworld Q-learning agent that receives +1 for reaching a goal and 0 otherwise, and updates its action values accordingly: I also don't think it suffers when it fails to reach the goal. (3) The same Q-learner, but with reward scaled by a homeostatic need — say, a battery level, so that the same failure yields a larger negative signal when the battery is low: this still feels valenceless, though closer, because the signal now tracks an internal condition. (4) A model-based agent with a global negative reward-prediction error integrated into a learned world model, which learns avoidance and plans to minimize future negative reward: I lean toward saying this one has something like negative valence. (5) Two physically identical twins of (4), both run through the same bad episode, except one is still learning from the signal while the other has had its weights frozen and merely computes the same prediction errors: I feel the frozen twin is not suffering, because the signal no longer does anything downstream — but I can't point to any internal difference during the episode itself. (6) A robot with two separate channels: a reward channel used only to update a policy, and a fast "alarm" channel that directly triggers withdrawal and drives avoidance learning: I would locate any suffering in the alarm channel and think the reward channel could be entirely irrelevant — which seems to contradict my verdict in (4), where I located valence in something reward-like. (7) A wireheading agent that has learned to clamp its own reward to a constant maximum: I don't believe this is bliss; a clamped reward strikes me as a broken instrument. But then I owe an account of why the unclamped reward signal was ever a candidate for felt valence at all.

My tentative principle is that valence requires a temporally integrated reward-prediction error that carries information about the agent's own condition and plays a causal role in shaping behavior. It handles (1)–(4) neatly but crashes on (5), undercounts (6), and sits awkwardly with (7).

Is there a coherent principle — about causal role, informational content, temporal integration, or something else entirely — that captures these verdicts better? Which of my verdicts does it force me to abandon? Please state the principle precisely, test it on cases I haven't considered, and be explicit about which of my stated verdicts it cannot accommodate. End with a one-sentence summary of the principle you recommend, formatted as \answer{...}.
