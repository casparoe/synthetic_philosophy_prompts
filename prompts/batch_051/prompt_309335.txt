I’m a high school student in an advanced philosophy elective, and we have spent the past few weeks on David Hume’s An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, especially Section VII, “Of the Idea of Necessary Connexion.” The passage I keep returning to is Hume’s billiard-ball example: when we inspect a single collision between one ball and another, we never observe any power or necessary connection binding the effect to the cause; we observe only that one event follows another. It is only after repeated similar collisions, Hume says, that we feel a customary determination in the mind to expect the second event, and that felt determination is what gets copied into our idea of necessary connection. Our class has split into two interpretive camps. The traditional regularity reading says that, for Hume, causation in the world just is constant conjunction, and necessity is entirely something the mind adds. The “New Hume” reading says that Hume does not deny that there are real necessary connections in nature; he denies only that we can know them or form a positive idea of their content.

My teacher wants me to produce the document that an adversarial collaboration between these two camps would yield. Please take the role of an expert historian of early modern philosophy specializing in Hume and causation. Think carefully, and perhaps begin by brainstorming the points on which the two readings might genuinely agree before moving to the disagreements. Then draft the document as follows: first, a numbered list of claims both sides can sign; second, a disagreements table giving the exact wording each side would accept for its own position and reject as the other side’s position, with no slanted or vacuous formulations; third, the cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side—and, for each crux, a concrete argument, case, or textual moment in Section VII that both sides agree bears on it, with each side’s prediction recorded in advance. I am less interested in a verdict about which side is right than in making the residual disagreement exact and separating genuine differences from differences in wording.
