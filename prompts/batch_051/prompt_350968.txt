I cover science and ideas for a general audience, so I keep finding myself in arguments where someone shuts the whole thing down with a version of "yes, but what if everyone did that?" It gets aimed at tax evasion, having another kid, throwing out a recyclable, not voting, taking three napkins, booking a cheap flight. The odd part is that it's treated as decisive even when the speaker knows perfectly well that everyone won't do it and that one person doing it makes no measurable difference. So it can't just be a prediction. Sometimes it sounds like a fairness complaint (you're free-riding on other people's restraint), sometimes like a consistency complaint (you couldn't will a world built on that rule), sometimes like a consequences complaint (if enough people did it, it'd be a disaster), and sometimes like a claim about the kind of person you'd have to be to act on that rule.

I know the move is Kantian somewhere in the background, and that later philosophers like Hare and Singer worked it into the more general idea of universalizability—something about being willing to prescribe the same act for any relevantly similar situation, not literally everyone doing the identical physical act at the same moment. But I don't have a clean map of the readings. I'd like you to lay out the distinct precise claims "what if everyone did that?" could be making, and for each say whether it's a platitude, a substantive claim that's probably right, or just false. Then I'd like to know which reading is actually doing the work in the usual free-rider cases (taxes, voting, emissions) versus the consistency-flavored cases, and where the slogan most overreaches. I'm less interested in the intellectual history than in knowing when the question carries a real argument and when its just rhetoric. Please finish with a final line that says "Summary:" followed by a one-sentence summary of the reading you think does the most legitimate work.
