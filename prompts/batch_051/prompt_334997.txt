I'm a PhD student in early modern philosophy, and last week my reading group got stuck on a passage from Locke's An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter XXVII, section 1. The line that started it was: "Another occasion the mind often takes of comparing, is the very being of things, when, considering anything as existing at any determined time and place, we compare it with itself existing at another time, and thereon form the ideas of identity and diversity." From there we went to Reid's brave officer, and two of my friends got into it. I couldnt exactly referee it, and the rest of us were split.

Their back-and-forth went roughly like this.

Mara: That passage commits Locke to identity being transitive as a matter of the basic relation he's describing: a thing compared with itself at two times. If A is B, and B is C, then A is C. But in the brave officer case, the general is the same person as the officer because he remembers being the officer; the officer is the same person as the boy because he remembers being the flogged boy; yet the general is not the same person as the boy because he does not remember the flogging. That's a reductio, and the earlier passage shows it cannot just be waved away.

James: But the passage is about identity of substances, or things whose identity is fixed by their beginning at a particular time and place. Locke turns to personal identity later and says person is a forensic term. The whole point is that personal identity is not one of those substance identities, so the transitivity of the earlier framework doesn't automatically transfer. Reid's objection only looks decisive if you quietly assume the person is a substance.

Mara: I think that relocates the problem rather than solving it. If "same person" is not transitive, then what does it mean when a court holds someone responsible for a past action? Either the general is the same person as the apple thief or he isn't. If he isn't, then the officer's remembered identity with the boy does not get you to the general's responsibility. If he is, the memory criterion gave the wrong answer. So either you drop transitivity and make forensic identity weirdly time-relative, or you keep transitivity and give up the memory criterion.

James: It's not time-relative; it's consciousness-relative. The question is always whether the present consciousness can appropriate a past action as its own. The general can appropriate being the officer, but cannot appropriate the officer's flogged childhood. That's simply what the view says. It isn't a contradiction; it's the verdict of the theory.

Mara: But then the very thing Locke starts with — identity as comparing a thing to itself at different times — ceases to apply to persons at all. You're saying there is no stable relation of being the same person over time, only a present consciousness's reach. That makes personal identity irreducibly epochal, and it leaves it unclear why we should call the officer and the general the "same person" beyond a present feeling.

Can you adjudicate their exchange move by move? Rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say who is right about what, including anything you think they are both missing.
