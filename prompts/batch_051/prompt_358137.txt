Modern wealthy societies have become irrationally risk-averse. They treat safety as if it were an absolute value, demanding zero risk from visible hazards such as nuclear power, chemical residues, and new technologies. But safety is not free, and it is not absolute. Every costly safety regulation makes a society somewhat poorer, and poorer societies have higher mortality, so regulation itself causes deaths, often among people who would never face the headline risk. The article calls this a risk-risk tradeoff: when we try to eliminate one risk, we create another. For this reason the precautionary principle — the principle that we should err on the side of caution when risks are uncertain — is self-defeating: it tells us to avoid the risk in front of us while ignoring the risks our avoidance creates. The only coherent standard for regulation is therefore cost-benefit analysis: regulate a hazard only when the expected number of lives saved by the regulation exceeds the expected number of lives the regulation costs. Judged this way, much environmental and health regulation does more harm than good.
