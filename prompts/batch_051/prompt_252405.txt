I have been working on a distinction I call "adversarial implicature," and I would like it checked before I try to do anything with it.

On the standard Gricean picture, conversational implicature is powered by the Cooperative Principle: a hearer works out what a speaker implies by assuming that the speaker is being cooperative and is either observing or flouting the conversational maxims for a communicative purpose. My proposal is that there is a further class of implicatures that run in the opposite direction. In these cases the hearer recovers the message only by assuming that the speaker is not being cooperative, and the inference is licensed by the visible violation of conversational norms together with the hearer's sense that the speaker is trying to evade, wound, or win.

Two examples. First, a minister is asked at a televised hearing whether she authorized a particular payment, and she replies, "I have always followed both the letter and the spirit of the law." No one present treats this as an answer. The message she gets across — roughly, that she authorized it and will not say so, or that the questioner should drop the matter — is recovered, I think, not by assuming cooperation but by recognizing the utterance as a move in a contest. Second, a colleague says, "You're surprisingly articulate." The hearer recovers the insult only by assuming the speaker's lowered expectations, not by assuming the speaker is helping the exchange along.

I realize this looks like it might just be ordinary flouting, with the Cooperative Principle still doing the work in the background. But I think the difference is real: in flouting, the hearer assumes the speaker is ultimately helping the exchange succeed; in adversarial implicature, the hearer assumes the speaker is trying to gain an advantage, and that assumption is what makes the inference go through. I also think many of these cases trade on plausible deniability: the speaker wants the hearer to take the point while leaving herself able to deny that she meant it, which puts pressure on Grice's claim that speaker meaning requires the hearer to recognize the speaker's intention.

I would like an honest assessment. Is "adversarial implicature" a genuine addition to the Gricean taxonomy, or does it collapse into flouting once we allow that arguments, hearings, and insults have their own shared purposes? Does this already exist under another name? Give me the three strongest objections to the idea and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. Also tell me where the concept breaks — for instance, how we would tell a real adversarial implicature apart from a hearer's paranoid over-reading that the speaker never intended.

Please keep the entire assessment under 700 words.
