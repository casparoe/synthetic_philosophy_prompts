Consider the claim, associated with the later Wittgenstein's remarks on certainty and with the family of views now called hinge epistemology, that some of the commitments we rely on — for instance, that the earth has existed for more than five minutes, that one has two hands, that there are other minds, or that memory is broadly reliable — are not empirical hypotheses that happen to be overwhelmingly confirmed. On this view, they are hinge commitments: they are held fixed while particular empirical judgments turn around them; they help determine what counts as a reason for what; and doubting them tends not to refine an inquiry so much as remove the framework within which the inquiry could proceed.

Take this as a claim about the actual structure of a person's cognition — about how their commitments are in fact organized — rather than merely as advice about what one may take for granted. Design the best test, or battery of tests, you can for determining whether a particular commitment in a particular subject is functioning as a hinge rather than as an ordinary empirical belief that has become very firmly entrenched. For concreteness, pick a specific target commitment, such as 'the earth has existed for more than five minutes,' and specify the subject and the probes, interventions, or observations you would use.

Then give an honest accounting: which results would favor the hinge reading, which would favor the ordinary-entrenched-belief reading, and which results would be compatible with both. Say explicitly where you would have to operationalize elusive notions — for example, what counts as genuinely doubting the commitment, what counts as revising it, and what counts as evidence-sensitivity — and state what your operationalization necessarily leaves out. Finally, identify what no empirical test could settle even in principle, and whether that residue is a limitation of our methods or a sign that the hinge-versus-belief distinction itself is underdescribed.
