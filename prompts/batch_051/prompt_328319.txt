I've ended up having to lead a discussion at my church's adult Sunday school next month, and I'm honestly a little out of my depth. The topic is whether the evolutionary history of life—something like hundreds of millions of years of animals killing, eating, and dying in painful ways—is compatible with the Christian claim that creation was originally very good and that death entered the world through human sin. I'm defending the view that it is compatible, but not through the usual moves. You know the standard line where somebody says death in that passage only means spiritual death, or that animals don't count, or that the Fall somehow reached backward and corrupted all of natural history. I don't find those fully honest. My actual position is closer to this: animal death and predation were part of the original good creation, not a flaw in it, because a self-sustaining, interdependent creation that brings forth life through the death and recycling of organisms displays something like God's own self-giving. The Fall, on my view, is about human estrangement from God and the introduction of human sin and violence, and the creation groaning in Romans 8 is about creation being frustrated by human sin, not about animal mortality as such.

The room is maybe twenty adults. It's a theologically conservative congregation but not a young-earth one: most people accept an old earth, and a good number work in medicine, engineering, or biology-adjacent fields, so they won't let me wave away the science. There are three or four people I'm especially worried about. One is a retired veterinarian who has actually read the relevant verses carefully and will press the difference between predation and cruelty. Another is a homeschool mom who keeps coming back to what this does to the hope of a redeemed creation—if animal death wasn't part of the curse, what exactly gets fixed in the new earth? And my most articulate opponent keeps saying something like, if a gazelle being torn apart by hyenas wasn't evil before the Fall, then God's goodness means something completely different than we thought, and maybe evil is just how things are.

Can you make me a Q&A prep sheet? I want the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this crowd, not the ones I'd get from a philosophy facutly or from an atheist pressing the problem of evil. Order them by how likely and how damaging they are, phrase each one the way that person would actually put it to me, and for each give a two-minute reply plus a note on where my reply is weakest. Tell me where I should concede ground rather than defend a bad line, and include the question I should be most afraid of—the one I'm quietly hoping nobody in that room knows enough to ask. Write the whole thing in American English.
