I'm a software engineer and I've been modeling my future self as a separate process that I can't push patches to after it starts, and it's got me trying to pin down what I actually think about whether your future self has any claim on you. You're an expert on this stuff—practical rationality, decision theory, diachronic whatever—so think carefully, maybe start by brainstorming a few candidate rules before you settle on one, and tell me which principle gets the cases below (or which of my verdicts it forces me to give up). Here's where my gut lands.

Binding yourself because you know you'll cave later seems completely fine: asking a friend to change your streaming password so you actually finish the project, the old tie-yourself-to-the-mast-because-you'll-hear-the-sirens move, taking the job with the brutal first six months because it's better afterward even though I know next-February-me will want to quit. I also think it's fine to just change your mind: agreeing Monday to take Friday's shift, then on Thursday genuinely wanting the day off and asking to swap back isn't a sin against Monday-me, even if its annoying to the coworker. But if someone offers me a little sequence of trades—pay a dollar to swap A for B, then next week pay a dollar to swap B back—and I say yes both times because my preference flipped, I think that's called a money pump, and I feel like something has gone badly wrong: not just that I'm out two bucks, but that my preferences themselves were defective. A smoker who takes a drug that makes cigarettes disgusting so they can quit? Rational, good. A person who takes a pill to permanently make themselves prefer their spouse's candidate? I want to say that's not rational, even though it's also just deliberately changing what future-them wants. My ad-hoc rule is that pre-commitments are fine when they tie your hands but leave your values alone, while editing your own preferences is creepy—but the smoker case kills that rule, and I can't find the replacement.

So: is there one principle that gets all of these, or do some verdicts have to go (and which ones)? And what does it say about the case I actually flip-flop on—setting my alarm across the room tonight so that 6 a.m. me, who will hate every step of it, has to get out of bed to turn it off?
