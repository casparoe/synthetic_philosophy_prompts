I've been reporting on a story about a woman who took a job at a nursing home and later found she couldn't bring a wage-theft claim in court because she'd signed an arbitration clause buried in her onboarding paperwork. That sent me down the rabbit hole: phone plans, credit cards, rideshare apps, even some doctors' offices now have fine print saying you wave your right to sue and agree to binding arbitration. The arbitrator is usually picked and payed by the company, there's no jury, no meaningful appeal, no class action, and the proceedings are kept confidential — yet courts keep enforcing these clauses under the Federal Arbitration Act. What's strange to me is that we treat the right to a public trial as almost sacred in criminal cases, but in civil disputes we treat it as a default you can opt out of with a click. The more I look at this, the less it seems like a technical question about contract law and more like a whole implicit philosophy of what a court is even for.

So here's what I want you to help me work through before I write the piece. What theory of adjudication or justice does a system of mandatory private arbitration presuppose? More carefully: does it strictly presuppose that the right to a public court is a private, waivable interest people can trade away like any other contractual term, or is the practice actually compatible with several different theories of what makes a legal process legitimate — including ones that see public adjudication as a public good the state owes everyone? Reconstruct the implicit view as fully as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say where it's philosophically underdetermined, and tell me what would have to change institutionally if you ran the system on the opposite presupposition — that access to a public, independent court is constitutive of the rule of law and can't be contracted around. I'm not after a policy brief so much as a map of the commitments: what someone is quietly buying into when they defend the current setup.
