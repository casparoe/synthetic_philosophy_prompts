We are a working group advising a mid-sized city on whether to adopt a "citizen trust score": a single continuously updated number computed from tax payment, traffic fines, utility arrears, planning-permit disputes, and eventually app and browsing metadata. The score would determine who receives expedited permits, preferred school placements, subsidized housing, and certain kinds of loans. It combines a record of past violations with a forward-looking risk model, so a resident can be marked down not for something she has done but for what the model predicts she might do. In a recent session, a proponent answered German colleagues' objections about data protection and informational self-determination with the sentence "If you've done nothing wrong, you've got nothing to hide." The Germans treated the sentence as obviously backwards, the American members divided between a transparency reading and a surveillance reading, and a member familiar with Chinese social credit noted that a version of the same thought does real justificatory work there, too. Before we deliberate, I want to know exactly what this sentence commits its user to. Disassemble it into the distinct precise claims it could be expressing: claims about whether collected data can harm the innocent, about what the state is entitled to look at, about who fixes the meanings of "wrong" and "hide," and about where the burden of justification lies. For each reading, state the principle in one sentence, classify it as trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and say whether that reading would actually justify building the trust score. Finally, identify which reading the pro-score argument genuinely needs, and whether that reading is among the defensible ones or only among the popular ones.
