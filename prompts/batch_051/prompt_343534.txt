Consider two cases. A friend announces that her goal is to save money; over the following year she keeps buying lottery tickets and overdrawing her account. Nearly everyone describes her as someone with a standing goal who suffers from weakness of will: her announced goal is taken as the real goal, and the contrary behavior is treated as noise around it. Now consider a recommendation system launched with a stated objective of promoting a healthy public conversation; it reliably surfaces inflammatory content anyway. Engineers and laypeople alike will say the system's behavior reveals the objective it was really optimizing, and that the stated objective was misspecified or little more than a cover. So in the human case, self-report wins over behavior; in the machine case, behavior wins over self-report. Yet both are cases in which a declared objective and an effective objective can come apart, and in both the actual objective has to be inferred from a finite record of choices made under constraints.

An adjacent asymmetry is no less odd. In the human case we take ourselves to know her option set—she could have bought a savings bond instead of the ticket—and we use that to say the failure lies in her choice rather than in her situation. In the machine case we are far more willing to infer not only its effective objective but also the shape of its effective search space from its observed behavior, and to treat the specification as revisable. What, if anything, justifies these two asymmetries? The strongest candidate principle might appeal to privileged introspective access, to the thought that declaring a goal partly constitutes a human goal, to a difference between training a system and trying to predict it, to a difference in the sense of the term objective across the two contexts, or to something else entirely. State the best principle you can find, run it against nearby cases—a human whose self-report is confabulated; a person whose objective an outside observer infers from her choices without asking; a machine that emits natural-language statements about its goals; a trained animal whose behavior and ethological evidence diverge—and then give an explicit verdict: should the asymmetry be kept, reversed, or narrowed, and where exactly does the line fall?
