I am putting together a two-page proposal for a philosophy of action workshop, and I want the proposal organized around the following passage from Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book III, chapter 3:

> Well then, nobody deliberates about things eternal, such as the order of the universe, or the incommensurability of the diagonal and the side, of a square. Nor yet about things that change but follow a regular process, whether from necessity or by nature or through some other cause: such phenomena for instance as the solstices and the sunrise. Nor about irregular occurrences, such as droughts and rains. Nor about the results of chance, such as finding a hidden treasure. The reason why we do not deliberate about these things is that none of them can be effected by our agency. We deliberate about things that are in our control and are attainable by action (which are in fact the only things that still remain to be considered; for Nature, Necessity, and Chance, with the addition of Intelligence and human agency generally, exhaust the generally accepted list of causes). But we do not deliberate about all human affairs without exception either: for example, no Lacedaemonian deliberates about the best form of government for Scythia; but any particular set of men deliberates about the things attainable by their own actions.

The question I want the proposal to address is this: when an agent is in the middle of deliberating about a choice, can the agent coherently assign probabilities to the alternative actions, and would doing so crowd out the very deliberation Aristotle takes to be about things in the agent's power? My half-formed thought is that a self-assigned probability is not a neutral observation: the act of assigning it becomes part of what is under choice, but I want the proposal to avoid simply assuming that and instead argue for it if plausible. Write the proposal so it includes: a precise statement of the central question, formulated so that it could in principle receive a determinate answer; an explanation of why existing discussions of the "deliberation crowds out prediction" problem leave this particular issue open; a method that uses the Aristotle passage as a diagnostic, sorting cases where self-assignment of probabilities is idle from cases where it conflicts with deliberation; a description of what a successful deliverable would look like (for instance, a criterion distinguishing first-person from third-person probability, or an account of why the two have different contents), and what would count as failure; and an honest assessment of what might push the project beyond six months. Do not include citations or bibliography; I will add them. End with a line beginning "Summary:" followed by one sentence that states the proposed question and method.
