I've got a set of gut reactions about the word "intentionally" and I can't tell whether there's one principle underneath them or whether some of them are just noise. Here are the cases.

A: a CEO approves a cost-cutting plan and says openly that she doesn't care about the river, the numbers come first. The plan dumps waste in the river as a side effect. Did she intentionally harm the river? I say yes.

B: same CEO, same indifference, but the plan happens to clean the river as a side effect. Did she intentionally help the river? I say no.

C: a surgeon performs an emergency operation that saves a patient's life, knowing it will leave a large ugly scar. Did she intentionally scar the patient? I say no.

D: a surgeon performs an elective operation the patient wants for cosmetic reasons and that the surgeon does mostly for the fee, knowing it will leave the same large ugly scar. Did she intentionally scar the patient? I say yes.

E: a manager reorganizes her team purely to cut costs. She dislikes one employee and would be happy to see him go, but the reorg saves his job as a side effect. Did she intentionally save his job? I say no.

F: a commander orders a strike on a munitions depot, knowing it will also destroy the school across the road. The strike is necessary to ending a war and will likely save thousands of lives. Did he intentionally destroy the school? My answer bounces around. If I force myself, I say yes, but I can't explain why he doesn't come out "no" like the surgeon and her scar.

My current rule is something like: good side effects never count as intentional; bad side effects count as intentional only when the person's main aim is itself not a good one. That rule fits A through E. But F looks like a counterexample to my own rule, and I'm not sure whether to fix the rule or change the F verdict. Is there a principle that captures all six—or five and a half—and what does it say about the commander? Also, which of my verdicts would I have to give up to keep the simplest version of the principle?
