I spent most of my working life as an atmospheric chemist and only started reading political philosophy after I retired, so forgive me if I'm missing a standard move here. The thing I keep chewing on is the old libertarian slogan that taxation is theft. I don't want to argue about the slogan in the abstract; I want to think through a world where the premise is taken seriously.

Counterfactual: in 2030, a wealthy, stable democracy of about 18 million people (think the Netherlands, but let's not get bogged down in Dutch specifics) amends its constitution so that no person can be compelled to pay any tax. The state can charge user fees where it can actually exclude non-payers — tolls, court filing fees, permits, tuition, park entry — and it can sell or lease assets, run lotteries, and accept voluntary contributions. But there is no legal penalty for refusing to pay a tax as such. Fraud, theft, and trespass laws remain in force, so if a fee is posted for a service, you can't just take the service without paying.

Walk me through how you think this country looks fifty years on. I want the mechanism, not a story: which functions of the old state (defence, police, courts, roads, public health, schooling, income support for the poor, environmental regulation) get funded somehow, which ones collapse or shrink, and what private or local substitutes appear. Be explicit about your confidence at each step: which effects are robust, which are guesses, and what would have to be true for your prediction to be wrong.

Then tell me what, if anything, this little thought experiment settles about the slogan that taxation is theft — or whether it just talks past it.

One thing I'd appreciate: seperate what's reasonably settled, empirically or conceptually, from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this, and don't present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
