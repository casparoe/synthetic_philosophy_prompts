Ok so this has been eating at me for a few weeks. I work on the ranking side of a video platform, and I recently noticed that my own opinions on a couple of public-health and political topics have drifted quite a lot over the last year, and the drift matches, a little too neatly, the stuff the recommendation system was feeding me. I could have predicted roughly where my credences would end up just from knowing what the system was going to show me. Im not proud of that. Anyway it made me think about Van Fraassen's Reflection principle, which I only remembered hazily from grad school. As I understand it, the principle says your current probability for some claim, conditional on the fact that your future self will assign it probability r, should itself be r. The idea is that your future self is you plus extra evidence and more time to think, so your present self should defer to them.

But in my case the future self I ended up being isnt really me-plus-evidence in the intended sense. Its partly a product of an engagement-optimizing recommender that had a model of me and selected which evidence I would see, in what order, with what framing. So now Im wondering whether the Reflection principle survives that. Does the development just undercut one premise in the usual argument, and if so which one? Can you repair the principle so it still says something weaker but still useful, something like defer only to future selves whose views were formed through a process you could now endorse? Or does the principle just fall silent whenever the formation of your future views is influenced by an outside optimizer, which might eventually be all the time?

Id like the answer in continuous prose only, like an email no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. And Im asking about the principle itself, not about what policy we should have toward recommender systems.
