my friend and me spent most of yesterday in an arguement about this and i still can't decide what the underlying view even is. A lot of how current chatbots get trained involves human raters choosing between two candidate answers, and the raters are guided toward preferring the response that's more helpful and easier to follow: it shows its work, spells out the reasoning in steps, doesn't jump straight to the conclusion. In practice that means a legible, calmly explained but slightly incorrect answer can beat an accurate answer that's compressed or confusing. But the same labs will happily ship a model that gets a state-of-the-art benchmark score through a process no one can audit, as long as the final number is high enough. And then a safety team will block deployment of a model because its chain of thought can't be inspected well, almost regardless of how accurate the outputs are.

So depending on where you look, the practice says legibility beats correctness, correctness beats legibility, and legibility is a precondition for treating correctness as trustworthy. What view of the relation between being right and being understandable does this actually presuppose? Is the implicit idea that an understandable answer is more likely to be right, or that correctness only counts when someone can check it from outside, or something else entirely? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology as charitably as you can, say whether it is coherent across those three contexts, and tell me what would have to change in how labs train and ship models if they instead held that correctness and legibility are completely independent of each other.
