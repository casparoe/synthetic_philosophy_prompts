I keep ending up in the same arguement with a friend about a decision she made years ago, and I think there's a testable claim buried in it. She turned down a good law-school offer to do a low-paying PhD, and she insists the choice wasn't hard because she lacked information, and wasn't hard because the two options were exactly equal overall. It was hard, she says, because the kinds of good at stake—the life she'd get as a lawyer versus the one she'd get as an academic—can't be put on a single scale; they're incommensurable. My standing objection is that 'incommensurable' is doing no real work: maybe she just didn't know the weights, or the weights kept shifting, and either way the word names a feeling rather than a fact about the options. There's also a middle view I've come across, that the options aren't strictly incomparable but are 'on a par': neither is better, they're not exactly equal either, and small improvements on one side don't budge the decision even though a big improvement would.

I want to get past the armchair. How could we actually distinguish those three stories about a hard choice—roughly equal value plus missing information, genuine incommensurability, or parity? Design the strongest concrete test you can: a choice experiment, a sequence of forced tradeoffs, adding small incentives to one side and seeing when people switch, whatever you think has the most bite. Use the law-school-versus-PhD case or replace it with a cleaner one if that helps. For each main outcome, say which story it favors. Then be honest about the leftover: exactly what a positive result for incommensurability would and would not show, and whether the word 'incommensurable' survives operationalization or whether we should retire it in favor of something thinner. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
