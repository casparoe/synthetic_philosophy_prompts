Act as a professional moral philosopher with expertise in normative ethics and the structure of consequentialist theories. Think carefully, and begin by briefly mapping the terrain in your own words before you adjudicate: distinguish a criterion of rightness from a decision procedure, a merely instrumental relation to value from a constitutive one, and derivative reasons from independent reasons.

Two members of my department's ethics reading group, Elena and Sam, argued on the walk home after we discussed consequentialism. The question was what place virtues and duties can have within a consequentialist view. I was the third person in the conversation and mostly listened. I wrote down the exchange as faithfully as I could:

Elena: "A consequentialist can happily make room for honesty, courage, and promise-keeping, but only as instruments. These traits and practices earn their place by tending to bring about the best outcomes; any disposition or rule that brings about better outcomes is just as good, and when a virtue or duty would fail to promote the good, it gives no reason at all. So their normative force is derivative through and through. Anything stronger is deontology wearing consequentialist clothing."

Sam: "You are running together two different things. Something can be derivative without being a mere tool. Health is part of a good life, not merely a means to one; a virtue like honesty might likewise partly constitute the valuable form of agency or character that a good life includes. If so, honesty is not a replaceable implement that you set down whenever it gets in the way. There is a third option: constitutive but derivative rather than instrumental."

Elena: "That is a distinction without a normative difference. If honesty is a constituent of the good, then its reason-giving force is still entirely explained by its contribution to that good, which is exactly the derivative status I claimed for it. You have refined the mechanism by which the virtue contributes, but you have not produced a single reason of honesty that does not bottom out in consequences. Show me that reason, or the virtue stays derivative."

Sam: "The real difficulty is that the good to which honesty contributes cannot be detached from the attitude of treating honesty as something you do not merely price against other gains. A state of affairs in which people are honest only while a case-by-case calculation says it is productive is not the same state of affairs, and may not be a good one at all. That is why the reason to be honest attaches to the settled disposition, not to the act's expected results. Once the virtue must be internalized as an end in order to secure the good, it is no longer merely instrumental in your original sense, and you cannot recover your position just by adding 'the good of having virtues' as a further entry in the ledger."

Elena: "I can grant most of that and still hold my ground. Indirect consequentialism has always said that the best strategy is often to cultivate firm dispositions rather than to calculate case by case, because we are biased, ignorant, and easily rationalize. But the justification for cultivating the disposition is still its expected results. If an unwavering disposition of honesty stopped producing good results in some world, it would stop being a virtue. That is what it means for a virtue or duty to be derivative. You have described good moral psychology, not an independent source of reasons."

Sam: "Then take a familiar kind of case. I promise a friend on her deathbed that I will water her garden each spring. Years later, no one knows about the promise, no one much benefits from the garden, and the water would do slightly more good elsewhere; the friend is gone and breaking the promise would never come to light. Plain act-consequentialism seems to say: break it. If you reply that the practice of promise-keeping would be eroded, you are appealing to a general effect that may not hold in this secret case. If you reply that fidelity is itself part of the good that should be honored even when it fails to produce the best consequences, then you have given the duty a weight that does not reduce to its role in producing outcomes. Either the theory gives the result you never wanted to own, or the duty is not merely derivative in the strict sense you defended."

Now, as the referee: assess the six moves individually, placing each on a spectrum from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each placement. Then say which of the two is more right overall, and identify anything important that both of them are missing or conflating. Do not take sides prematurely; work through each move on its own terms.
