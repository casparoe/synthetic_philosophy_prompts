A technology-policy group proposes a two-tier accountability scheme for AI systems that make judgments but cannot act on them. Such a system is assigned a status called "judgment agent": it may be praised, corrected, or sanctioned for the content and quality of its judgments, but it is never liable for downstream outcomes, no matter how foreseeable. Downstream liability always falls on the human operator who accepted the recommendation; operators may not cite the system's advice as a defense, and designers are liable only for independently proven design defects after certification, not for judgment errors. Treat this as a mechanism rather than a moral thesis: what does it incentivize among designers, operators, and users; how would the boundary between judging and acting be gamed or shifted; what second-order effects does it create for whether systems are kept deliberately impotent or given real authority; and which failure modes cannot be fixed by small amendments without abandoning the two-tier structure?
