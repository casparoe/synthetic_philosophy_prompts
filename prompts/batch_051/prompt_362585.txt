As a journalist covering science and ideas, I have settled into an epistemic rule that now shapes nearly every story I write. When I am trying to understand a contested question—whether a new study supports the causal claim made in the press release, why a guideline panel reversed its earlier advice, or what a well-known theory actually implies—I stop as soon as I have found an account that meets a modest internal standard: the mechanism is clear enough, the sources are competent and not obviously interested, and the account addresses the strongest objection I can think of at the time. I do not, as a matter of policy, keep searching for the best available account, the decisive test, or the interpretation I would converge on with unlimited time. One colleague says this is simply Herbert Simon's satisficing applied to inquiry, and that it is the rational response to bounded time and attention. Another says that satisficing makes sense when choosing among options such as jobs or flats, where the aim is a good-enough outcome and further search has costs, but not when forming beliefs, where the aim is truth and a merely adequate explanation can be systematically misleading.

I am less interested in who is right as a slogan than in whether the policy can be restated in a defensible form. I would like you to assess the habit as a policy: whether the standard justifications for satisficing under bounded rationality genuinely transfer from practical choice to belief formation and reporting; under what conditions stopping at a good-enough account will reliably serve accuracy and under what conditions it will predictably fail; what features of the science-journalism environment make it more or less trustworthy; how a defensible version of the rule would differ from the one I have described; and how I could tell from the inside whether I am satisficing because the costs of further inquiry genuinely outweigh the expected epistemic gain, or merely protecting a comfortable conclusion from further challenge. Please answer in British English.
