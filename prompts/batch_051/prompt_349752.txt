In a reading group on David Lewis's account of convention, one participant made a sweeping claim: the apparatus Lewis developed for cases like which side of the road to drive on also explains how much of our social order arises and stabilizes, from queuing and tipping to informal property boundaries and parts of ordinary morality such as promise-keeping and truth-telling. On the participant's telling, a norm begins as an arbitrary but useful regularity in a recurring coordination problem; once enough people come to expect others to follow it, each person acquires a conditional preference to conform, existing precedent makes the regularity salient, and the pattern locks in, eventually taking on the felt character of an obligation. They drew a quietist conclusion from this as well: because some other regularity would have served just as well, we should hesitate to treat the content of these norms as intrinsically correct.

I suspect this analogy is being asked to carry more weight than it can bear, but I want an audit rather than a dismissal. First, give the correspondence table: in the target cases, what is filling the role of the recurrent coordination problem, the competing equilibria, the precedent or salience, the mutual expectations, the conditional preferences, and the arbitrary initial choice? Then identify the places where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten the participant's conclusions from those that are harmless. Finally, say whether the descriptive claim—that these norms, including the moralized ones, evolved and persist as Lewisian conventions—survives the audit, and whether the quietist moral survives as well.
