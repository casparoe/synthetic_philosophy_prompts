I'm a high school student, and there is something I find puzzling about the design of my world history course, and I would like you to analyze it with me. The course's stated goal is to teach a genuinely global history. The teacher emphasizes that we cover China, India, the Islamic world, Africa, and the Americas, not just Europe, and our assessments reward comparing multiple regions in the same essay. But when I look at the actual architecture of the course, the unstated spine is European. After a unit on early river-valley civilizations, the year runs through classical Greece and Rome, the Middle Ages, the Renaissance and Reformation, the Age of Exploration, the Enlightenment and the Atlantic revolutions, industrialization and imperialism, the world wars, and finally decolonization and globalization. Non-European regions mostly appear as chapters about what was happening elsewhere during those European periods, or as places Europeans encountered. The implicit assumption seems to be that European turning points provide the universal clock, and that other regions become world-historical chiefly through contact with Europe. My teacher says this is not Eurocentric because the course includes everyone, but I suspect inclusion is not the same as symmetry, and that the order of units is doing philosophical work that the official goal does not acknowledge.

I am not asking for a summary of arguments for or against Eurocentrism, and I do not want a proposal to add more non-European content. What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of history that this course design presupposes: its claims about what drives historical change, what makes an event or period world-historical, how time should be divided, and how regions are related to one another. Then I would like you to separate the parts of that implicit theory that the course strictly presupposes, in the sense that the unit order and the comparative exercises would stop making sense without them, from the parts that merely fit the design, in the sense that several rival theories could also license them. In particular, I want to know whether the course's goal of genuine global coverage actually requires the European spine, or whether the design is philosophically underdetermined at exactly that point. Finally, tell me what a course genuinely built on the idea that the past can be told from everywhere at once would have to change concretely: not just which regions get more pages, but how the units would be ordered, what would count as a major turning point, how the comparison tasks would be written, and whether such a course could remain teachable to students like me within a single school year.
