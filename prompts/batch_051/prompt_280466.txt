Two people in my history-of-science group got into it over multiple discovery and whether it matters who gets credit. (1) Amin said Newton–Leibniz basically settles that discoveries are inevitable. Two people hit on the calculus within a few years; the famous priority fight was mostly publication timing and politics, and delete either one and someone else would have had a workable calculus soon after. So personal credit is a bit like praising a wave for being first onto the beach. (2) Dana pushed back that this reifies "the calculus" as a stable object waiting to be found. Newton's version was couched in fluxions and geometry, Leibniz's in the differential notation and rules that actually powered the next two centuries. Lumping them as "the same discovery" is the retrospective move that makes simultaneity look tidier than it was. (3) Amin replied that even at that finer grain the ingredients were all around—tangent methods, the inverse-area problem, work by Fermat, Barrow, and others—so the core idea was overdetermined even if the notation wasn't. (4) Dana then raised Darwin and Wallace: yes, they converged on natural selection independently, but Darwin had decades of evidence from breeding, distribution, and embryology, while Wallace's sketch was thin; those differences affected how persuasive the theory was and what got built on it. When you call it the "same discovery" you flatten a difference that does explanatory work. (5) Amin said that just relocates his point: the core idea was overdetermined, even if the evidential packaging wasn't. Could you referee? Go through (1)–(5) and say who is right about which point, where each overreaches, and who is more right overall.
