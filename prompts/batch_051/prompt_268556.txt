I'm an undergrad retaking electricity and magnetism after failing it once (don't ask), and the way my professor talks about gauge freedom has been bugging me all semester. Every week he insists the vector potential is "not real" — you can add the gradient of any scalar function to A and nothing physical changes, so the potential is just a mathematical crutch with extra unphysical degrees of freedom in it. Then he picks a gauge (Lorenz, Coulomb, whichever makes the math nicest), does the whole calculation in that gauge, and only at the end says the result is gauge-invariant so the choice didn't matter. The course's whole way of working seems to treat the potential as surplus structure that you're free to choose however you like.

But I can't square this with the fact that later in the same course we did the Aharonov–Bohm effect: an electron passing around a long solenoid gets a shift in its interference pattern even though the magnetic field is zero everywhere outside the solenoid, and the standard way to compute it runs through the vector potential, which can't be guaged away everywhere at once. Now the potential looks like its doing real physical work rather than just bookkeeping. And I've noticed people in quantum mechanics say similar things about the phase of the wavefunction — that it's unphysical — even though phases seem to be doing the same kind of work there.

So here's what I actually want to know: what philosophical commitments about what's real does the ordinary practice of gauge-fixing presuppose? Take the practice at face value — choose A freely, compute in a gauge, then declare the answer gauge-invariant — and reconstruct the implicit view of the relation between the mathematics and the world that this practice only makes sense under. Does it actually presuppose that only the field strength F (or line integrals of A around closed loops) is real and the potential itself is mere surplus? Or is it compatible with the view that the potential is real and gauge-fixing is just a coordinate-like choice of description? And is the practice in the Aharonov–Bohm case, where the potential is treated as essential, a different practice with different commitments, or is one of these two practices confused? I'd like a committed answer: pick the reconstruction you think is right, say what the practice strictly presupposes as opposed to what it merely fits with, and say what concretely would have to change in how a course or textbook presents the material if we swapped in the rival view.
