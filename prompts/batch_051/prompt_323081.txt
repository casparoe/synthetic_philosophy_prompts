I want a working definition of sentience in the sense that matters for moral status. I don't mean "is alive" or "is generally intelligent"; I mean the threshold at which a system's states can be better or worse for it, such that the system could in principle be owed something. Define the term precisely enough that it can be applied to artificial, partial, and slowed-down systems, and test the definition against these eight cases:

(1) a desk calculator executing an arithmetic algorithm;
(2) a convolutional network trained to detect damage to a robot's body and trigger protective avoidance behavior;
(3) a large language model predicting the next token on text it has never seen;
(4) a real-time digital simulation of the 302-neuron connectome of the nematode C. elegans;
(5) a human brain in deep, dreamless sleep;
(6) a human with blindsight, whose cortex extracts fine-grained visual information that guides reaching even though the person reports no visual experience;
(7) a cortical organoid exhibiting synchronized neural bursts but with no sensory or motor connections;
(8) a neuron-by-neuron simulation of an awake adult human brain, run so slowly that one simulated second of its dynamics takes a millennium of wall-clock time.

I'm confident the calculator is out and the awake human whose brain is simulated in (8) is in; on the rest I genuinely don't know. My suspicion, which you should not defer to, is that the ordinary term runs together a functional notion of information processing and a phenomenal notion of felt valence, and that only valenced states do the moral work. Propose a definition, run it through all eight cases, say where it gives surprising verdicts or no verdict at all, and then revise the definition or defend the surprises.
