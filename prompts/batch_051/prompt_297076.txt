I co-lead a discussion section for a first-year course on rational choice, and next week the schedule gives me the two-envelope puzzle for a single fifty-minute meeting. My students can multiply out expected values and have encountered dominance reasoning before, but they have no probability theory beyond finite, intuitive cases, and they tend to treat a paradox as a one-line trick: find the suppressed assumption, point at it, and the tension evaporates. I am not asking you to resolve the puzzle for me. I am asking you to plan the session so that the puzzle's real difficulty lands and survives the students' first attempts to dissolve it.

Please lay out the explanatory sequence in order of dependence. What has to be established early in the class before the puzzle can exert its full pull rather than sounding like a slightly odd gamble — for instance, is it a mistake to open with the version where you see $100 in your envelope, and should I instead earn the right to that version by working through a sealed-envelope case or a stripped-down case with only four possible contents? Identify the one example or case that should carry the hour, what it must make visible, and what it should not bring in. Then give me a misconception map in the order students will actually form these views: I expect some version of "the other envelope is equally likely to be higher or lower, so the expected-value arithmetic must contain a hidden error"; then "the calculation double-counts the same amount depending on which envelope you imagine holding"; then "there must be an upper bound on the money, and discovering that dissolves the problem"; and perhaps "the right response is to stop doing expected-value reasoning and fall back on the pre-opening symmetry argument." Tell me where in the sequence each should be intercepted, and explain how to stop the last move from ending the discussion prematurely — because my aim is for them to leave seeing that the puzzle is not merely missing a piece of information but exposes what expected-value reasoning presupposes about a betting setup before it can even be applied. Finally, give me one question for the last five minutes that would distinguish a student who can reproduce a resolution from one who has actually understood why the puzzle matters, and tell me what I should explicitly set aside as beyond this level.
