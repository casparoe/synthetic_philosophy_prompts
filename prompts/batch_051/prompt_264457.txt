A city council's code of conduct provides:

"Members of the Council shall not, in the course of their official duties, make any statement that demeans or expresses hostility toward a person or group on the basis of race, colour, or national origin. A statement demeans or expresses hostility toward a person or group if an ordinary, reasonable member of the public, hearing or reading the statement in its full context, would understand it as expressing contempt or hostility toward them because of that characteristic. A member may not avoid a finding of demeaning by showing that they intended some other meaning."

The clause was adopted after a series of public slurs, and the drafting record contains no discussion of coded or deniable language. During a council debate on the public-housing waiting list, a councillor says: "The list is not slow because of money. It is slow because the same communities treat the housing register as an extension of bloc politics. Until we end bloc politics in this district, families who have been here for generations will keep going to the back of the line." In the city, the phrase "bloc politics" has for years functioned as a recognized code aimed at one specific refugee-origin community, and members of that community consistently hear it as charging that their political participation is clannish and illegitimate; many listeners outside that community hear only a routine complaint about voting patterns. The councillor later insists they said nothing about any race or group: "if people want to hear race in that sentence, that's their reading."

Some colleagues argue that, under the text, the only question is what an ordinary, reasonable member of the public would understand, and such a listener would not hear racial hostility in the remark. Others reply that this reading makes the rule blind to exactly the kind of attack it exists to prevent, since a speaker can now signal hostility to the targeted group while keeping outside observers uninterested.

Does the councillor's statement violate the rule? Work through each of the following and say which should control when they differ: the text of the clause; the evident purpose of the rule; what its drafters would have said had they been asked about localized, deniable code words; and how a reasonable member of the public told only that the Code "prohibits demeaning statements on the basis of race" would expect it to apply. Then say whether an ordinary-listener standard can reach a dog whistle like this one without collapsing into "any offence taken by any group"; if it cannot, explain what the rule, or the standard, would have to give up.
