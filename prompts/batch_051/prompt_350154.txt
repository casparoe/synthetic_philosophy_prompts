I'm a software engineer without any formal background in philosophy, and I keep noticing a pattern in the research papers I read and in my own team's postmortems. The official story of the scientific method says you formulate a hypothesis, derive a prediction, run the test, and then accept or reject the hypothesis. But in practice the sequence is usually much messier. On one project I was close to, the key improvement came from a misconfigured training script that accidentally shared parameters between two modules; the paper later presented this as a hypothesis the team had deliberately set out to test. On another, a colleague kept pushing an approach that had failed in early experiments, and it eventually worked only because an unrelated change made those early failures irrelevant. Both projects are described, after the fact, as clean applications of the method.

What puzzles me is not that the practice is messy. It is that the method itself seems to get silently amended to cover each mess. If a discovery came from an accident, the method is said to have always included openness to surprises. If it came from an aesthetic preference for a simpler model, the method is said to have always included simplicity. If someone ignored a failed experiment and was later vindicated, the method is said to have always included judgment about when results matter. Each amendment is reasonable by itself, but together they make the method impossible to fail: any success can be redescribed, afterwards, as having followed the method, and no actual sequence of events seems to count against the method as a prospective guide. I am not doubting that science works; it plainly does. I am asking whether there is a name for this specific pattern, the way a supposedly prescriptive method is protected by being rewritten after the fact so that it tracks success instead of predicting it. I would want an answer to distinguish this from ordinary hindsight bias, from saving a theory with auxiliary hypotheses, and from plain post hoc rationalization, because it feels adjacent to all three but not identical to any of them. If there is no established name, what would you call it, and where exactly are its edges?
