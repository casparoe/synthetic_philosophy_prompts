I cover science and ideas for a weekly, and last month I was at a public festival event on freedom where two political theorists got into a proper row after the panel. I wasn’t involved, just took notes, and now I need to turn it into a short explainer. Here is roughly how it went.

Anna kept returning to a passage in Isaiah Berlin’s Two Concepts of Liberty where he says, in her loose rendering, that the extent of a person’s negative freedom is a function of how many doors are open to them, not simply how many doors they actually choose to walk through. She thought that image invited the republican objection: a door that is open only because a powerful person chooses not to lock it is not genuinely open, even if the lock is never turned.

She then said the distinction between negative and positive liberty misses the republican idea that you can be unfree even when nobody is actually blocking you. Ben jumped in: if nobody is blocking you, you are free, full stop; talk of domination without interference is just a prediction that somebody might block you later, and predictions are not unfreedom.

Anna came back with a thought experiment. A slave with a benevolent master who never says no is still in a certain condition, she said; you cannot describe him as free just because the master happens to be nice. Ben replied that the slave is not interfered with, so if you call him unfree you must mean he lacks psychological security or self-respect, which is something else, maybe a positive-liberty problem or a welfare problem, not liberty.

Anna said that misses the structure. The slave acts by permission, not by right, so even a wholly unexercised power creates unfreedom because any choice hangs on the master’s forbearance. Ben said this is exactly the move Berlin warned against: if you make freedom depend on the legal or social status of your relationships, you make freedom unusable as a standard for judging those relationships, since you have to know in advance which ones are legitimate.

Anna then asked whether Berlin’s own image of open doors doesn’t imply the quiet master is a problem rather than a counterexample. Ben said no, because the door is literally open if the master chooses not to close it, and liberal negative freedom only tracks whether it is open, not why.

I couldn’t tell where this had really moved the question. Can you adjudicate it move by move? Rate each of their moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say who is right about which specific point and what both are missing. Please write in British English.
