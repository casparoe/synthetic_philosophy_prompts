I co-run a small LessWrong/ACX-flavored reading group, and for next month I want to turn an argument that has been burning alot of our sessions into something worth keeping. Two regulars, call them N and R, keep fighting about the grit/fortitude/resilience cluster. N thinks these are real, measurable, trainable character traits, and that the school and workplace programs built on them are mostly straightforwardly empowering. R thinks the whole cluster is an ideology wearing virtue-words: it reframes structural failures as individual deficits, celebrates persisting in conditions you should instead leave or change, and smuggles a Protestant-work-ethic picture of the good life into curricula and HR. The pattern is predictable. N says R is doing a motte-and-bailey from the motte "the way grit is marketed is sometimes unfair" to the bailey "the trait itself is fake." R says N is doing the reverse, retreating from "teachable grit improves outcomes" to "grit is a real individual difference" whenever an intervention fails to replicate. R also says grit is mostly conscientiousness with better branding; N says that's only true if you make conscientiousness so broad it stops doing any real work.

Write the document they should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, not another debate transcript. I'd like a joint statement both can actually sign. Include:

1. A numbered list of claims both accept — about what the construct does and doesn't pick out, what the non-cherry-picked evidence does and doesn't show, what the interventions seem to change, and where values rather than facts enter.
2. A disagreements table: each remaining disagreement, each side's view in wording that side would accept, with the partly-verbal disputes flagged — the places where they mean different things by "grit" or "resilience" and would partly dissolve under a stricter definition.
3. The cruxes: sub-questions where a settled answer would genuinely move at least one of them. For each crux, describe a concrete study, natural experiment, or intervention both would accept as bearing on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance.

Don't adjudicate; that isn't the point. The point is to get them from talking past each other to a document where the residual disagreement is narrow, well-defined, and could actually be moved by evidence. If you think a fully joint statement is impossible because one side is just confused, write the closest thing a maximally charitable version of each side could live with, and tell me which sentences took the most translation.
