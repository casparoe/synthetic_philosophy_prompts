I work as a software engineer, so I have no formal background in philosophy, but a health-economics dispute is bothering me. In ordinary hospital care, if two patients need the same scarce treatment, we consider it unacceptable for a doctor to decide between them on the basis of expected years of life remaining or broader social usefulness; equality before the bedside is taken seriously. Yet in public-health planning and pandemic triage, we routinely compare expected quality-adjusted life years across groups, allow age or capacity to benefit to order access, and treat a life-year saved as a unit to be maximised. There is also a familiar pattern in which society spends heavily to save an identified person—a named child stuck in a well, a single patient given an experimental drug after a fundraiser—while declining far cheaper interventions that would prevent deaths among anonymous statistical people. These look like two expressions of the same asymmetry between the particular person and the aggregate. Are they really the same, and if you had to defend both, which principle would carry the weight? Or is at least one a form of inconsistency that we should give up? Lead with your bottom line; answer in no more than 400 words.
