I'm taking an upper-level seminar on death and the ethics of life extension, and I need to hand in a one-page position statement next week. The problem is I've been reading a mix of assigned papers and longevity-forum stuff, and the more I try to write out my views, the more I worry I've just bolted together claims that don't actually cohere. So here's roughly where I've landed, and I'd like an audit before I commit to it:

1. Death is bad, full stop, and aging should be treated as a medical problem like any other disease. I also don't buy the stagnation objection — I don't think an open-ended lifespan has to make people bored, risk-averse, or unable to care about anything.
2. But when I imagine my own life going on indefinitely, my projects start to feel less urgent, and I keep returning to the thought that deadlines and endings are a big part of what makes my writing, friendships, and long-term plans feel like they matter. I'm not sure I'd want to lose that.
3. I respect the right of anyone to refuse life-extension treatments, and I'd defend someone who wants to age and die on there own schedule as making a legitimate choice, not just one we grudgingly permit.
4. At the same time, I think most of the public resistance to radical life extension is status quo bias and sour grapes: people convince themselves that death is what gives life meaning because the alternative terrifies them, and the arguments they offer are mostly post hoc rationalizations (the longevity blogs call this deathism).
5. I have no intention of signing up for cryonics, even though I think aging is a solvable engineering problem.

Does this set hang together? Go through the commitments and tell me which pairs are in real tension and which only look like they conflict on a first pass. For each real tension, rank it by severity, say what the cheapest fix would be and what it would cost, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most across the whole set.
