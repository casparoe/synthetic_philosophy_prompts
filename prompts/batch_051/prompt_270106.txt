A medium-sized U.S. state is considering adding a desuetude clause to its constitution. Under current practice, statutes remain valid indefinitely even if no one has been prosecuted, sued, or cited under them for decades, which means dead-letter rules can be revived opportunistically and enforced selectively. The proposal is this: whenever a statute or regulation has gone twenty years without an enforcement action, citation, or adjudicated invocation, any resident may petition a new nonpartisan Office of Legal Obsolescence to certify the provision as lapsed. Certification becomes automatic unless the enforcing agency shows at a hearing that there is a concrete and foreseeable enforcement need within the next five years; a lapsed provision can be revived only by passing the legislature again from scratch, with no fast-track procedure. I want a behavioral analysis rather than a verdict on the principle. Walk through how this would play out in practice. What would it do to prosecutors' and agencies' charging and regulatory decisions, and would it create an incentive to bring token enforcement actions purely to keep a provision alive? How would regulated parties, interest groups, and repeat litigants game the certification process or the five-year "foreseeable need" hearing? Where would the scheme fail first, and which failure modes could be fixed by amendment without gutting the underlying idea? Please write the analysis in American English.
