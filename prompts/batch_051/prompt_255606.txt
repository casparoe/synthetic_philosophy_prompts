I got called for jury duty last month and the judge kept cutting off the lawyers with an objection I'd never heard before: a question can't assume facts not in evidence. The judge's example was something like "when did you stop taking money from the register?" — you can't ask that unless somebody's already established that the witness took money at all. Fair enough. But it got me wondering why the rule is even needed. In normal life, if I ask my brother "are you still borrowing mom's car without asking?", he just answer the "still" part, and somehow my question has quietly installed the fact that he used to do it. The court seems to treat that as a real danger — like a question can change what everyone is allowed to take for granted, just by taking it for granted in the asking. So what view of meaning and conversation does that courtroom practice presuppose? Does it commit the law to something like a running scoreboard of what's been accepted, where asking a loaded question is a move that updates the scoreboard unless someone objects? And if meaning were just truth conditions, and a question were just a request for information, would the rule make any sense? Also, ordinary conversation doesn't just tolerate this — it runs on it. Why does the courtroom have to police the very thing everyday talk depends on? Is the differnce the stakes, the official record, or what "common ground" is supposed to be in each setting? Reconstruct the implicit theory, but be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it's merely compatible with — and say what would have to change in the courtroom if we replaced it with a static, truth-conditional picture of language.
