For some years I have followed a policy when reading political and legal commentary: when a writer says that a proposal violates or erodes the rule of law, I discount the argument almost to zero unless the writer can translate the complaint into a concrete failure mode. My checklist is roughly the following: secret or unpublished rules; retroactive rules; contradictory or impossible-to-comply-with rules; rules that change too quickly to plan around; arbitrary or systematically unequal enforcement; adjudication by biased or non-independent tribunals; and officials acting beyond their lawful powers. If a writer cannot map the appeal onto one of those failures, I treat the phrase as something close to an applause light — an expression everyone nods to but which carries almost no information about what is specifically wrong — and I stop attending to the argument. I adopted this rule after repeatedly finding that invocations of the rule of law marked the emotional peak of a column rather than the place where any actual defect was identified.

A recent conversation with a friend who teaches public law has made me worry that the policy is too crude. She conceded that the clearest violations fit my checklist. But she argued that a government can satisfy every item on it and still undermine the rule of law, because the ideal also constrains how power is exercised: whether officials treat the law as a constraint they answer to, or as an instrument aimed at adversaries and waived for allies. Her example was a government that enacts a broadly worded statute through the proper procedures, with due notice and no retroactivity, and then uses it against political opponents on the strength of unpublished internal guidance. I wanted to dismiss this as an unverifiable appeal to something like a culture of legality. But I am no longer sure that I was disagreeing with her on the merits; it may be that my policy had quietly settled the underlying dispute in advance, by assuming a thin formal conception of the rule of law of the kind associated with Raz, against a thicker conception concerned with arbitrariness of the kind associated with Fuller's idea that legality has its own inner morality and with Waldron's emphasis on legal process. If so, the policy is doing exactly what it is supposed to prevent: deciding the substantive question under cover of a neutral triage rule.

Is the discounting policy defensible as a general epistemic habit? When is it reliable, and when does it throw away real information? Is there a restated version that keeps the triage benefit without silently legislating a thin conception of the rule of law — for example, treating a failure to specify the mechanism as a sign that the argument is underdeveloped, rather than a sign that the argument is wrong? Or should I bite the bullet and hold that any appeal to the rule of law that cannot be operationalised as a concrete failure mode has lost its content and may be discounted?
