You are a philosopher of physics who specializes in scientific methodology and theory choice. Think slowly and carefully before answering: begin by reconstructing, in your own words, the exact argumentative pattern I describe below; then work through each of its three components separately; then identify the nearest established concepts in philosophy of science and say precisely how each differs from the pattern; only then commit to a classification. A bare label will not satisfy me. I want to understand exactly what this phenomenon is, what it is not, where its boundaries fall, and how its parts should be evaluated.

I keep noticing, in defenses of string theory and of the inflationary multiverse, a pattern of argument for which I have no name. The research program makes no currently testable novel prediction, or its distinctive predictions concern regimes that may be permanently inaccessible to experiment. Yet its defenders treat it as the best available theory, and sometimes speak with considerable confidence. Their support tends to take three recurring forms.

First, the no-rivals move. The defender argues that no serious alternative framework exists: competitors are said to be dead, unmotivated, or already falsified, so the community would be rational to keep working within this framework even before it has passed direct tests. Second, the unexpected-coherence move. The defender points to connections that were not put in by hand: the framework, developed to solve one problem, turns out to shed light on another, or its internal consistency forces results that later look like explanations of otherwise puzzling features, even though those explanations cannot themselves be checked. Third, the history-favors-us move. The defender cites episodes from the history of science in which, so it is claimed, a theory was taken seriously or treated as the leading candidate long before its distinctive predictions became testable, and later developments vindicated that attitude.

These moves are often blended with one another and with whatever ordinary empirical support the program may have. Writers also seem to slide between using them to justify belief in the theory, using them to justify funding and attention, and using them only to justify continued pursuit. I cannot tell whether the slide is accidental, or whether the pattern itself is indifferent to that distinction.

Does this pattern, or any of its three moves considered separately, have an established name in the philosophy of science? If it has a name, what exactly does the named concept cover: all three moves, some of them, or something broader? Does the concept distinguish justification of belief from justification of pursuit or funding, or does it run these together? How is the pattern related to, and how is it distinct from, ordinary empirical confirmation, Popperian falsificationism, Lakatos's notion of a degenerating research programme, and mere sociological self-interest or wishful thinking? If the pattern as I have described it matches no single established concept, then say so, propose a label, define it explicitly, and state what would and would not count as an instance.
