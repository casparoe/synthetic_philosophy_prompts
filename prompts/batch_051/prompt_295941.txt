I've been pushing a little theory about climate model ensembles and I want it checked properly, because I cant tell whether it's actually new or just the standard point about model democracy in worse words. The theory: when an ensemble gives you a range of projections, that range is not a probability distribution over the future. It's a map of where the modeling community is currently confused — more like a poll of expert guesses than a measurement of a physical quantity. One consequence is that the multi-model mean has no special claim to be the most likely outcome; averaging a bunch of correlated, code-sharing models is averaging opinions, not estimating a central tendency of the climate. The second consequence is about decisions: skip the mean and either plan around the severe outcomes most of the models agree on, or bet on the models that have tracked the historical record best on the variable you actually care about (not global mean temperature, which is what skill usually gets measured on). Concretely: if six models say 3.1, 3.3, 3.4, 3.5, 3.6, and 5.2 degrees, I don't think the 5.2 deserves anything like a one-sixth vote — it's mostly information about one group's unresolved cloud feedback, not about the planet. So the question: is this distinction doing real work, or am I just relabeling the old point that grab-bag ensembles are not random samples? And where does it break — are there decisions where the ensemble mean genuinely is the right number to use, despite the category mistake?
