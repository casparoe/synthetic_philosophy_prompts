I'm a philsophy of science PhD student and I keep turning over what feels like a live puzzle. The usual story for why some fields accumulate knowledge is verifiability: math advances because a proof either checks or it doesn't, engineering because the bridge either stands or it falls, parts of medicine because the outcome is eventually legible. Fields where verification is slow, vague, or contested—large parts of philosophy, literary theory, macro—don't accumulate so much as relitigate. Call this the verifiability account of progress.

Now for the development I want to run against it. In the last couple decades, the slow-feedback fields got flooded with cheap, public, machine-generated verification: leaderboards and benchmarks in AI/NLP, citation scores, automated reproducibility checks, prediction markets on published claims. On the face of it, verification got vastly cheaper and faster in exactly the fields that supposedly needed it. But those fields don't look like they've started moving like math. Benchmarks saturate, people game the metric, leaderboards get hill-climbed, and the underlying disputes reappear one level up.

So the question is whether the verifiability account survives this development, is dead, or needs repair. I want you to isolate which premise is actually under pressure—is the original story smuggling in a distinction between verification that tracks the real target and verification that tracks a proxy?—and then defend the strongest repaired version you can, or say plainly why none is available if that's your verdict. Keep it under 700 words, and don't end on "it's complicated."
