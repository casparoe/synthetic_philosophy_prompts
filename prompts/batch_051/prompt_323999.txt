Molyneux's question: a man born blind can tell a cube from a sphere by touch; if his sight were restored, could he tell them apart by sight alone before touching them? I teach this most years, and the stock answers in my notes are:

(1) The Locke line: no—he can't match the two because the shape-ideas got by touch and sight are connected only through experience, and he hasn't had that experiance yet.

(2) The Leibniz line: yes—he can reason his way to it, because both senses track the same underlying geometry and he can figure out which visual presentation must be the cube.

(3) The Berkeley line: the question is ill-posed; visible figure and tangible figure are heterogeneous in kind, so there's no single 'shape' given in both senses for recognition to track.

(4) The empirical line: just try it—the best-known modern work with newly sighted children found initial visual matching near chance, usually read as support for Locke, though the follow-ups are messy.

I want a fifth answer to the question. It has to be genuinely different—not a rewording, special case, or combination of (1)–(4), and not just another way of saying the question is confused, since (3) already corners that market. Tell me what motivates it independently and whether you'd actually defend it.
