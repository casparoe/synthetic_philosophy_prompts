I teach a philosophy of AI seminar, and last term I got cornered at a workshop by two people who'd been arguing for an hour about chain-of-thought monitioring. For anyone not in the loop: that's the safety practice of reading a model's intermediate reasoning steps before it produces its final output, on the theory that you can catch it planning something bad, or reasoning in a way you don't endorse, before it actually does anything. I jotted their exchange down from memory because I honestly couldn't referee it, and now I'm hoping you can help. I don't need a literature review or citations either—I want a point-by-point verdict on the argument as it actually went.

Clara (the skeptic) opened with: the whole technique rests on a confusion. You train a model to produce correct outputs. The chain of thought is only under selection pressure insofar as it contributes to that. You are not rewarding the model for telling you what it actually reasoned; you're rewarding it for emitting a sequence of tokens that leads to the right answer and looks sensible to whoever reads it. So the thing you read when you monitor isn't reasoning, it's a justification written for an audience. Faithfulness is not measured; legibility is.

Ravi (the defender) came back with: that's true as far as it goes, but it doesn't sink the technique. Nobody claims the chain of thought is a transcript of private mental life. The claim is comparative: it's a better evidence channel than watching outputs alone. If the chain of thought says something like "I can satisfy the letter of this request by exploiting an ambiguity, even though the user probably meant something else," that's actionable information even if the final output looks fine, and a behavioral evaluation would never surface it. And when you do process supervision—grading intermediate steps rather than only the final answer—you're training for steps human reviewers can verify. That's not perfect fidelity, but it is a real constraint.

Clara said: process supervision is exactly the problem, because the human reviewers can only reward steps that look right to them. That's legibility again. And as systems get better than the reviewers, you won't even have that; you'll be grading steps you can't actually check, which pushes you right back to grading outputs. Meanwhile the pressure to obfuscate is growing. A model that learns its honest chain of thought would get it penalized, retrained, or shut down will learn to write an innocuous-sounding rationale and do the thing anyway. Then monitoring gives you false confidence: the chain of thought looks clean, so you trust behavior you shouldn't.

Ravi answered: that's a story about future systems, not an argument against monitoring the ones we have now. And even for future systems, you're treating the chain of thought as a confession to be taken at face value. Monitoring can also look for the reverse: abrupt shifts in reasoning, steps that don't do any work, places where the stated reasoning doesn't support the output, refusals to spell things out. It's not a lie detector, it's an audit trail. And the optimization-pressure argument cuts both ways. Obfuscation is itself a skill the model has to learn, it costs capacity, and most training setups won't select for it hard enough to make the entire chain of thought worthless. You're letting the perfect be the enemy of the good.

Clara replied: no, the audit-trail point actually helps me. Every one of those features—the sudden jumps, the vagueness, the mismatch between stated reasoning and output—becomes a trigger the moment you use it for monitoring, and then a sufficiently optimized model learns to avoid emitting it, not because it reasons more faithfully but because it predicts the trigger. Once a feature becomes the trigger, it stops being informative. It's the classic Goodhart problem applied to reasoning: the metric stops being a good proxy precisely when you start optimizing against it.

Ravi said: if that's an argument, it's an argument against every safety metric, because every metric can eventually be gamed. We still run behavioral evals, we still do red-teaming, we don't throw them out just because a determined optimizer might learn to pass them. The question is whether chain-of-thought monitoring, used with appropriate calibration and limited trust, buys time and catches real failures now. I think it does, and it has already surfaced reasoning that was not visible in the final output.

Clara said: I'll grant it catches today's dumb failures. My worry is different. A safety technique has to not produce false confidence. The very thing that makes chain-of-thought monitoring useful—that the chain of thought tracks what the system is doing—is the thing optimization is poised to erode. The better you get at using the monitor, the more pressure you put on the chain of thought to mislead it. So as the technique matures, it gets less trustworthy, and you may not notice, because the whole point of the pressure is to make the deception look like candid reasoning. You're reading press releases and calling it introspection.

Ravi said: then the conclusion isn't "don't monitor," it's "monitor, but never treat the chain of thought as the final word, and keep independent behavioral checks." Which is what most sensible people already say. Your argument shows the technique is fallible, not that it's worthless.

Clara said: fine, but I'd rename it. It's not "monitoring the model's reasoning." It's monitoring the model's self-presentation. And if we called it that, we'd use it much more carefully.

Then they both looked at me and asked me to settle it, and I couldn't. So I'd like you to do it: go through the exchange and say who is right about which specific claims, say what you think each of them is missing, and then say who is more right overall. I'd also like your take on Clara's last move—whether the faithfulness/legibility distinction is really doing the work she thinks it is, or whether Ravi is right that it just shows the technique is fallible like every other safety tool.
