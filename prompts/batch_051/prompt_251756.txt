I have recently attempted to work through Benjamin Franklin’s plan for moral perfection in his Autobiography: thirteen virtues, a week of strict attention to each, a small ledger with columns for daily failures, the cycle repeated four times a year, and an order beginning with temperance because, Franklin says in substance, it makes the mind cooler and less easily pulled back by old habits while giving strict attention to the other virtues. I expected the plan to be a straightforward piece of early habit technology. In practice, it sits uneasily among several competing readings, and I want an instrument to sort people in our discussion group by what they actually believe about it.

Please construct a decision tree, or a short questionnaire with a scoring key, that assigns a person to one of the following five positions about what Franklin’s project is, at root:

1. A habit-formation technology: the point is replacing bad automaticities with good ones, and the ledger is essentially a feedback mechanism.
2. A virtue-ethical exercise in second nature: the point is not behavior control but the slow formation of character from which actions flow.
3. A deistic form of self-examination: the ledger’s daily recording of faults is the moral core, and the virtue aspired to is something like a consequent self-knowledge.
4. A program of civic usefulness: the virtues are cultivated because they make Franklin a reliable public actor, partner, and citizen.
5. An ironic or rhetorical self-presentation: the project matters as a story about failure and self-image rather than as a working moral technology.

Do not build the instrument by asking someone which label they prefer. Each branching question should be a concrete scenario or forced choice, answerable without specialist vocabulary, and should be selected because two or more of the positions genuinely require different answers. The tree must use at least three scenarios: someone who keeps the ledger faithfully but shows no change in public conduct; someone who selects one virtue and works only on that virtue for a year instead of cycling through all thirteen; and someone who adds a daily hour of public admission or social accountability for failures. The questions should be built from the logical space, not from the names of views: first the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then the five positions placed as leaves, then any unoccupied combinations noted and briefly diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of positions the tree cannot actually separate, and say why. Keep the instrument to at most seven questions. It will be used by educated adults who have tried the method, not by specialists in Franklin or moral philosophy.
