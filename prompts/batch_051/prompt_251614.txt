I'm taking a philosophy of mind course, and we spent a week on David Chalmers's zombie argument. In The Conscious Mind, Chalmers asks us to imagine a creature who is molecule-for-molecule identical to an ordinary conscious human being, with the same physical processes, but who has no phenomenal consciousness at all: there is nothing it is like to be that creature. He claims that this creature is conceivable in the relevant sense—the idea contains no hidden contradiction—and that from this conceivability we may infer that such a creature is metaphysically possible. Since a world containing such a creature would be physically indistinguishable from our own while lacking some of our mental facts, Chalmers concludes that the mental facts do not logically supervene on the physical facts, and so physicalism is false.

A friend pressed me with the following extension of that argument, and I can't find the fault. (1) If a creature physically identical to a conscious human but lacking phenomenal consciousness is conceivable, then such a creature is possible. (2) Such a creature is conceivable, for the same reasons Chalmers gives. (3) So a physical duplicate of me could exist with all the same physical causes and effects but no experiences. (4) My behavior, including everything I say and write, is part of the physical world and is fully fixed by the physical facts. (5) In the possible world described, my duplicate says and does everything I do, including reporting that he is conscious. (6) Therefore my experiences do not explain why I say and do what I say and do. (7) What goes for me goes for every conscious being. (8) So phenomenal consciousness is causally inert: it makes no difference to behavior. (9) But the zombie argument was supposed to be a discovery about ontology, not a proof that consciousness does nothing; Chalmers himself resists the charge of epiphenomenalism. (10) Therefore the zombie conceivability argument proves too much.

I would most like to keep step (1), because I find the conceivability-to-possibility inference compelling, but I cannot accept step (8). Which step should I reject, and what would rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to give up?
