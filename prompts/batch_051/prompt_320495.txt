I am designing a course activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar, Computing and the Mind, taught jointly by philosophy and computer science. My students reliably describe themselves with one of two slogans — "programming is just formalized thought" or "the language of thought is innate, and code only expresses it" — and I want an instrument that sorts them by their actual judgments about concrete cases rather than by which slogan they endorse.

Please write a ten-question sorting quiz, "Which view of the relationship between thought and code do you actually hold?" Each question should present a concrete vignette and two or three answer options. No option may state a philosophical label or ask the respondent to self-classify; the options should embody the positions anonymously.

Build the instrument from the following distinctions rather than by reverse-engineering from the labels: (i) whether there is a determinate medium of thought at all; (ii) whether a public notation such as a programming language can become that medium, merely express it, or form part of a larger thinking system; and (iii) whether cognition ends at the skull. Use the answer options to register where a respondent falls on these axes, and make sure each question is one a student could answer without knowing the philosophical literature.

The scoring key should map response patterns onto exactly the following five positions:

1. Innate language of thought (Fodor-style). Thought already runs on an innate, compositional, amodal symbol system that is not any public notation; programming languages can at most partially express, externalize, or scaffold it, and learning one does not change the medium of thought.

2. Acquired code-as-language-of-thought. For sufficiently fluent programmers, reasoning in some domains literally proceeds in a programming language, so a public notation can become the vehicle of thought rather than merely a notation for thoughts already present.

3. Extended-cognition scaffolding. The programming environment and the programmer jointly form the thinking system; code is a cognitive artifact, and "where the thinking happens" is a boundary-drawing question rather than a discovery about a hidden inner syntax.

4. Anti-syntactic. The mind is not a formal symbol manipulator in any explanatory sense; programming languages are culturally successful artifacts whose syntactic neatness tells us nothing direct about mental representation.

5. Deflationary. "Language of thought" is a façon de parler; there is no determinate hidden medium to discover, and formal notations are tools whose properties we design rather than find.

Use at least the following six vignettes, and add up to four more in the same register:
(a) a programmer who can only locate a concurrency bug by writing the interleaving traces in code, without first having any articulate picture of them in their head;
(b) a child who, after playing with a block language, solves recursive pattern problems correctly before she can state in ordinary words what recursion is;
(c) a pianist who reports that skilled improvisation is wordless, image-free, and involves no notation;
(d) an AI coding assistant that proposes a refactoring which works, even though no one on the team can state the principle behind it;
(e) a developer who moves from an untyped language to a statically typed one and finds that her algorithmic reasoning now follows the type system;
(f) a student who writes the type of a theorem in a proof assistant and reaches the proof by construction without a prior conceptual "aha."

Present the scoring key as a rubric. Wherever possible, every pair of positions should be separated by at least one question on which they require different answers; if that cannot be achieved for some pair, say so explicitly. Finally, flag any pair of positions that the instrument cannot cleanly separate — I suspect position 2 and position 3 will share several answers — and mark the corresponding patterns as "mixed" rather than forcing an assignment. For each such pair, either propose one further diagnostic question that might split them or explain briefly why the two positions blur into each other at the boundary.
