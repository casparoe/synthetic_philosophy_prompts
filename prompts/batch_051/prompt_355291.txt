I spent most of my professional life in a physiology department, much of it running a sleep laboratory, and since retiring I have been working through the philosophy of mind. A question about the laboratory's most basic practice has begun to bother me, and I would value help thinking it through.

The practice is this. A subject is wired for polysomnography and falls asleep. When the electroencephalogram and eye channels show a clear REM epoch—low-voltage, mixed-frequency cortical activity, rapid eye movements, and near-total muscle atonia—an investigator wakes the subject and asks, approximately, what was going through the subject's mind just before being called. The answer is recorded, transcribed, and later scored for the presence of imagery, narrative structure, emotional content, and bizarreness, and the scored report then enters the literature as a datum about a dream that occurred during the preceding minutes of sleep. Other groups in the field use the same reports as evidence in theories of memory consolidation, apparently treating the report as a recollection of events that unfolded during sleep.

Philosophers I have recently read pull this practice in two directions. Norman Malcolm maintains that the concept of dreaming is not the concept of an experience occurring during sleep; on his view the waking report is not a fallible memory of a hidden inner episode, but rather the criterion by which we say that someone dreamed. Daniel Dennett considers the possibility that the narrative produced upon waking is generated at that moment—a kind of insertion of a memory with the feel of recall—rather than a faithful account of a conscious episode that took place during sleep, and he argues that neither introspection nor laboratory data may be able to decide between these alternatives.

My question is what theory of the dream report ordinary laboratory practice presupposes. Does transcribing, scoring, and citing these reports commit the field to the view that a dream is a conscious experience that takes place in the sleeping brain? Or is the practice, when described carefully, compatible with Malcolm's claim and with Dennett's alternative? Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics as precisely as you can, distinguishing what the experimental design strictly presupposes from what investigators merely find convenient to say. Then tell me what, if anything, would need to change in how a laboratory describes its data if one of the deflationary views were adopted—and whether the practice would still be scientifically coherent.
