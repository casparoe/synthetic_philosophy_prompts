I have been arguing with a colleague who defends a constitutive version of panpsychism: the view that the fundamental constituents of the physical world have some sort of minimal experiential lives, and that the unified consciousness of a person or animal is constituted by these micro-experiences in roughly the way a complex physical object is constituted by its parts. When I press the combination problem — how do many little subjects of experience add up to one big subject? — he replies that I am holding consciousness to an unfair standard. Physics, he says, gives us composition wherever we look: quarks compose protons, protons and neutrons compose nuclei, nuclei and electrons compose atoms, atoms compose molecules, and molecules compose cells and bodies, without anyone calling this an explanatory scandal. So the real scandal, he says, is not that micro-experiences combine but that we treat phenomenal combination as uniquely problematic; the combination problem is mostly an artifact of a lingering intuition that consciousness is more mysterious than matter.

I want a careful audit of this analogy — "phenomenal combination is just like physical combination" — as it is being used here to license the conclusion that the combination problem is overblown. Please spell out the intended mapping (what is supposed to correspond to what), distinguish the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from the ones that don't, identify what the analogy quietly imports from physical composition into the phenomenal case (for example, third-person observability, the straightforward summation of properties like mass, and the absence of a combined point of view in the physical case), and then say whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If some other analogy would do more honest work for panpsychism, describe what it would need to include.
