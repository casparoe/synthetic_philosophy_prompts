I spent most of last night in a long argument with a friend about where a fixed sum of money can do the most good. He was defending organizations that aim at systemic change — governance reform, building institutional capacity, changing the incentives that keep people poor — while I kept defending narrower, marginal interventions: bed nets, deworming treatment, cash transfers that relieve a concrete problem for a concrete person right now. At one point I said that a bed net prevents malaria this season even if it does nothing to change the underlying system, and he answered with the proverb: "Give a man a fish, and you feed him for a day; teach a man to fish, and you feed him for a lifetime." He plainly took this to make direct aid look myopic.

The trouble is that I am not sure exactly which claim I was being asked to accept. Beneath its surface, the proverb seems able to mean several quite different things, and some of them are near-trivial while others are very strong empirical or normative theses. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher who is also comfortable with the structure of altruistic decision-making. Start by brainstorming the distinct precise claims the proverb could be making, from the nearly trivial to the radical, and then present them as a numbered menu. For each reading, state it explicitly and give a short verdict — trivially true, true but not decisive, empirically doubtful, or false — with a sentence or two of justification. Then apply the menu to the argument I actually had: which readings would genuinely support my friend's dismissive attitude toward direct aid, and which would not bear the weight he placed on them? Close with a short overall assessment of how much work the proverb can actually do in that dispute. Please think through the readings carefully and avoid settling for the familiar moral; the interesting question is which of the less obvious readings survive scrutiny.
