I am the grants director of a private land-conservation foundation whose board has just added a new criterion to every funding decision: alongside biodiversity, connectivity, and carbon, each habitat protection or restoration project must be evaluated for its expected effect on the welfare of individual wild animals. This is not merely symbolic, and a project can be declined on welfare grounds alone. My program staff review about forty proposals a year, from wetland restoration and reforestation to purchases of old-growth remnants and grassland easements. The staff are trained conservation biologists, not animal welfare scientists or philosophers. Each proposal must receive a written recommendation of fund, modify, or decline within two weeks, and anything nonstandard goes to a board committee for final approval.

The staff are divided. One group argues that protecting or restoring habitat is usually welfare-positive: it supports long-lived, slow-reproducing animals, and it prevents the injuries and deaths caused by clearing, draining, mowing, and burning. The other group argues that many of our projects create or maintain habitat for very large numbers of small, short-lived, heavily predated or disease-prone animals, and if a typical such life contains on balance more suffering than enjoyment, then more habitat means more net suffering. They point to our standard wetland restorations, which produce amphibians, small fish, and invertebrates whose most common fate is to be eaten shortly after hatching or to die in the first dry season. A third position in the office says the procedure should not count animals whose existence depends on our decision at all, or should count them at a discount. A fourth says that destroying habitat kills present animals in a way we can see, and this is morally different from merely failing to bring future animals into existence. The board has told us it wants one written procedure, not a recurring argument, and it wants the value questions it ultimately owns made explicit.

Please design a decision procedure my staff can actually run on each proposal. It should be a sequence of questions in order. For each question, state what would count as a sufficient answer, and what the staff should do if the answer is simply unknown given available data; where a question cannot be answered, the procedure should say whether the default is to decline, to proceed, or to refer, and on what principle. The procedure must mark the points at which it stops with a clear decision of decline or of welfare-grounds approval, and the points at which it stops being an empirical assessment and requires a value judgment; at those points it should hand the matter to the board committee with the specific value question stated in plain language. The procedure should also say explicitly which quantity it is tracking across the project's effects — total welfare, average welfare, welfare per animal-year, or something else — and whether it counts animals whose existence depends on the decision, and why those choices are being made rather than assumed.

Then run the procedure on two proposals we are currently considering.

Proposal A: Restore a drained seasonal wetland on roughly four hundred acres of retired row-crop land by recontouring the land and re-establishing native hydrology and vegetation. The applicant expects the site to support very large breeding populations of amphibians, small fish, aquatic invertebrates, and waterfowl, with heavy juvenile mortality from predation, disease, and seasonal drying. The site would be permanently protected from development.

Proposal B: Purchase and protect in perpetuity a two-thousand-acre block of mature forest and adjacent native grassland. The parcel already holds a relatively stable population of long-lived mammals and birds and a smaller population of mesopredators. Management would be limited to preventing clearing, motorized access, and poaching; there is no plan for active population control.

For each proposal, show how the procedure runs through its questions and where it reaches its decision. Make sure at least one application shows the procedure returning an answer my staff or board will not like, if that is what the procedure actually does, and explain plainly why it does. Finish with a short account of what the procedure cannot settle, no matter how carefully it is followed.
