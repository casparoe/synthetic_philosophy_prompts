I just reread the part of Franklin's autobiography where he lays out his project for arriving at moral perfection: the list of thirteen virtues (temperance, silence, order, resolution, frugality, industry, sincerity, justice, moderation, cleanliness, tranquility, chastity, humility), the little ruled book with a page for each virtue, one virtue as the week's focus, and a black dot in the chart for each daily offense. I know he conceded the project made him better but not perfect — that he found order nearly impossible, and that working on humility mostly gave him the appearance of it while pride kept sneaking back in. My reading group, there's six of us, wants to actually run a version of it, but one member has proposed making it an institution rather than a private self-help exercise. Here is the scheme. Everyone copies the thirteen virtues into a shared spreadsheet and marks their daily offenses, Franklin-style. At the weekly meeting, one person reads everyone's sheet aloud. Every black dot costs the offender a small fixed amount (we said two dollars, but treat the amount as adjustable) paid into a common pot. At the end of each thirteen-week cycle, whoever has the fewest marks takes half the pot and picks a charity for the other half. To keep people from quietly leaving marks off, the group votes each week to audit one member's sheet: that person has to retell one concrete episode behind each mark that week — what happened, who else was there. If the group judges that the sheet looks under-reported relative to the week, the member pays double into the pot.
