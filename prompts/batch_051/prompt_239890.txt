Design a structured classroom activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on collective responsibility (about 18 students, 90 minutes) that makes participants feel the force of the claim that a corporation can be blamed for an outcome even when no individual member of the corporation is clearly blameworthy for it—and that also makes them feel the cost of accepting that claim. The activity should be a role-play or structured deliberation, not a lecture, and the philosophical positions should be named only in the debrief.

The scenario should center on a company whose internal decision procedure produces a plainly harmful action across several roles—for example, an executive sets a target, a risk committee approves a policy, a middle manager applies it, a legal reviewer signs off on a narrow reading of the rules, and a frontline employee implements it. The rules and private information should be arranged so that each participant can truthfully say that, given what their role allowed them to see and the norms appropriate to that role, their own step looked defensible—while the corporate decision as a whole is clearly harmful. Include a mechanism (votes, sign-offs, information channels, or role-specific constraints) that ensures the harm does not clearly trace back to any single mind.

Then stage an inquest or board-review phase in which some participants must decide where blame should land: on individuals, on the group as such, on the procedure, or on no one. Build the activity around a comparison of two configurations of the same harm—one aggregative, in which the harmful action is chosen by a fully informed majority vote, and one distributed, in which the harm emerges across roles with no participant seeing the whole—so the debrief can compare where participants' blame-attributions actually settle under the two architectures.

Specify the roles and rules, any props or written materials, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and what the facilitator should do during each phase. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the main positions in the corporate-blame debate—roughly: (i) corporate blame as reducible to the blameworthiness of individual members, (ii) blame as an emergent property of a corporate decision procedure that is not identifiable with any member's state of mind, and (iii) skepticism about blaming corporations because they lack the right kind of sentience or subjective experience. It should also distinguish moral blame from legal liability only insofar as the activity itself raises that distinction.

Finally, say what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run—for instance, participants refusing to role-play harmful choices, the inquest turning into an argument about legal fine print, or the case becoming so complicated that no one can track who knew what—and say how the facilitator should handle each. Be explicit about which feature of the philosophical idea the classroom game cannot reproduce: for example, the blame felt in a fictional role-play lacks the real emotional stakes of actual corporate harm, and the exercise can make a real corporation look more mind-like than it is. Participants should leave seeing that the question who do we blame can outrun the question which individual did wrong, without having been told that in advance.
