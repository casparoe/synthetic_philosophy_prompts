Yesterday a friend and I argued late into the evening about an argument he had hit on, which he said shows that no one has ever really followed an instruction. I couldn't find the flaw, but I also refused to accept the conclusion. Reconstructed, the argument runs:

1. Following an instruction requires doing what the instruction says.
2. Doing what an instruction says requires knowing what it says.
3. An instruction is only marks or sounds; what it says is not written on its face but is fixed by how it is to be interpreted.
4. Interpreting an instruction is itself a case of following an instruction — namely, the instruction supplied by the language, or by the instructor, as to how those marks or sounds should be taken.
5. Therefore every act of following an instruction depends on a prior act of following an instruction, and then on another before that, and so on without end.
6. A finite agent cannot have completed infinitely many prior acts.

From this he concluded that no one has ever followed an instruction — not a recipe, not a law, not a spoken order. That seems to prove too much. But I couldn't see which step to reject, and I also couldn't say why the burden should fall on one step rather than another. I am reluctant to give up (2): acting on an instruction without any knowledge of what it says looks more like coincidence than following. Which step should be given up, why did it look so difficult to give up, and what does each main repair commit one to? I would also like to know what principle, if any, separates a vicious regress from a harmless one.
