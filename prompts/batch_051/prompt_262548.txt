I'm a backend engineer and I just read Lewis Carroll's What the Tortoise Said to Achilles, and this part keeps rattling around my head:

"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"

I've been trying to formalize what I think about this in terms of rule engines and inference systems, and I've ended up with a list of beliefs. I'd like someone to check whether they actually hang together, because I honestly can't tell where the problem is.

Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) A valid argument is one where there's a rule that lets you go from premises to conclusion, like modus ponens.

(2) But adding a rule as an extra premise doesn't help; at some point you just have to use a rule, not merely believe it.

(3) So the tortoise isn't missing a premise, it's refusing to play the game.

(4) I also think "playing the game" is just what a formal system is: rules are the system's allowed transformations, and they don't need any outside justification.

(5) Yet I can't shake the feeling that formal systems are arbitrary symbol games, so they can't explain why anyone ought to reason one way rather than another.

(6) Maybe the right answer is that inference is a practice we learn, like knowing how to do something, not a proposition we know.

Do these hang together? In particular I keep going back and forth on whether (4) and (5) are actually a contradiction or just me confusing two different senses of "ought." And is (3) in tension with (6)? Please audit my list and tell me which ones actually conflict, which just look like they conflict, and what the cheapest fix would be.
