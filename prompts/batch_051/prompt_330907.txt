I am a retired natural scientist, recently come to philosophy, and I have been trying to formalise a defence of Malthus that I find implicit in recent writing but rarely stated carefully. My starting point is the opening chapter of An Essay on the Principle of Population (1798), where Malthus sets out his two postulates about the necessity of food and the persistence of the sexual drive, and treats them as fixed laws of human nature. I want to know whether the following argument, which I have reconstructed from that passage, is valid.

(1) Malthus's central claim is that, given these fixed laws, the reproductive tendency of a human population will always, sooner or later, be checked by some limitation on the means of subsistence.
(2) The empirical record since 1798, in which food production has largely kept pace with population growth, does not contradict that claim, because that record was produced under conditions of accelerating technological change that Malthus's model did not incorporate.
(3) A long-run structural prediction cannot be refuted by data generated under conditions that violate the model's ceteris paribus assumptions, in this case the assumption that the means of subsistence can grow only slowly.
(4) Therefore the post-1798 record shows only that Malthus's timing was premature, not that his central claim was false.
(5) Hence a theory can be right but early: it can correctly identify a binding long-run constraint while overestimating the rate at which the constraint will bind.

I have a suspicion that step (3) is doing too much work, because it seems to license the rescue of nearly any failed long-run prediction by expanding the ceteris paribus clause. But I cannot pin down whether the problem is in step (3), step (4), or the move from (1) to (2). Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what would a corrected version of that step say?
