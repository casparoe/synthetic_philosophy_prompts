I am a high school student writing an extended essay for a philosophy and media course, and there is a pattern I keep noticing in how video games depict war that I do not have a word for.

Consider two cases. In a mainstream first-person shooter set during the Second World War, the single-player story treats a famous beach landing as a catastrophe: it shows soldiers drowning and civilians suffering, and the tone is somber. The same game's multiplayer mode uses that beach as a map on which two balanced teams compete to capture control points. A killed player respawns within seconds; a scoreboard records kills and deaths; the match is tuned to be close, fair, and enjoyable. The game never says the real landing was fun. The horror remains visible as scenery — the burned vehicles, the corpses at the waterline — but no system in the game treats that horror as a reason to stop, hesitate, or refuse.

In a strategy game about a historical empire, a player may order the razing of a conquered city. The interface presents this as an ordinary decision: the action damages diplomatic reputation but frees up resources. An in-game encyclopedia entry, in a flat factual tone, records that the real practice involved the slaughter of civilians. The player can read the entry and then click the button. The system scores the act as a slightly risky investment, not as a crime. The entry is not hidden, and the game does not deny its seriousness; the seriousness simply has no purchase on how the game evaluates the action.

What strikes me is the relationship between these two levels. The game's framing materials say, in effect, that the real event was terrible, and then the game hands that event to systems whose only measures are balance, fairness, risk, and reward. The result is not that the player is told war is good. It is that the moral weight of the real event is rendered inert while remaining visible: the player can feel both “this is balanced and fun” and “this was a real massacre,” and the design ensures that only the first feeling matters for what happens next. The second feeling becomes decoration.

I want to know whether this exact configuration has an established name in the ethics of games, media studies, or the broader philosophy of entertainment. If it does, give me the name and define it precisely: which features are essential to it, and what case lies just outside its boundary? If no established term fits precisely, then coin one and define it with clear limits. In either case a bare label will not satisfy me. And I need you to distinguish my phenomenon from three neighbours. It is not exactly the glorification of war, since the games openly say the events were terrible; it is not exactly trivialization, since the atrocity is kept visible and somber rather than laughed off; and it is not exactly the familiar clash between a game's story and its mechanics, since the two levels are not contradicting each other — the mechanics simply decline to take up what the story asserts. I need an account of why the phenomenon is distinct, or of why one of those neighbour terms is the best fit only after being carefully redefined.
