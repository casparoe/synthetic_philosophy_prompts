I don't have any academic background, and if this question has a name in the scholarly literature I don't know it. I've been trying to think it through on my own because it keeps appearing in my life — on the board of a small nonprofit, in a neighborhood association, in a congregation I belong to. People who sincerely disagree about right and wrong sometimes make deals with the structure of a trade: each side agrees to tolerate, or help bring about, something it privately believes is wrong, on the condition that the other side does the same with something it privately believes is wrong, and afterward each side can honestly say that, by its own standards, the world with the deal is better than the world without it. My question is whether such deals are ever legitimate, and if so under what conditions.

I would like you to write a decision tree that sorts a person into a named position on this question. It is for a small discussion group of careful general readers, so keep the language nontechnical, and give a leaf an established label only if you are confident the label is standard; otherwise call it unnamed.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of position names. Start with diagnostic questions a thoughtful person could answer without already knowing the positions, and say which combinations of answers are occupied by actual positions and which are empty, with a one-line diagnosis for each empty combination: internally inconsistent, consistent but unattractive, or merely unexplored. Label every branch point with the question that separates the positions below it, not with a thesis, and make the questions concrete claims or small scenarios rather than invitations to pick a label.

The questions should test at least the following fault lines, though you may refine or replace them if you see a cleaner cut: whether a person can even compare two states of the world when the two sides do not share a single measure of what is good, or whether the judgment that the world with the deal is better by one's own standards is an illusion; whether some class of matters is off-limits to trade no matter what is offered in return; whether acting against one's own moral convictions is a conclusive objection even when the deal prevents more of the same kind of wrong by the other side; whether a deal struck by a few people can bind a larger group that never consented to it; and whether the habit of making such deals gradually wears down each side's conviction that anything is off-limits.

The tree should locate at least these five positions as leaves, phrased in ordinary language: (1) no such deal is ever permissible, because deliberately doing what one believes is wrong is never justified; (2) such deals are permissible and often wise, provided both sides keep the terms; (3) such deals are permissible in minor matters but never in a matter either side regards as sacred; (4) such deals change nothing about what either side is obligated to do, but they can still be the least-bad option available, to be accepted with a clear-eyed sense of having done something wrong; (5) the deals are suspect not because of any single wrong they require, but because over time the practice corrupts everyone involved, so they should be refused even when a particular deal looks beneficial. Aim for between six and nine leaves altogether, adding any other consistent positions the space turns up.

This is not a request for a survey of what anyone has written or for citations; I want the instrument itself. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, and flag any place where two different answers to the same diagnostic question lead to the same leaf.
