I've been reading about inverse reinforcement learning—the idea that you watch someone's behavior and back out the reward function they're acting as if they're maximizing. The thing I keep tripping over is which behaviors actually count as evidence of a real preference and which are just habit, compulsion, or someone following a rule. My gut on cases keeps pulling in different directions. My partner orders black coffee at the same cafe every morning, but says he actually likes lattes and just defaults to black out of habit; I don't think the behavior shows a preference for black coffee, just a default. A friend says she wants to quit gambling but still goes to the casino every weekend; I don't think that reveals a stable preference for gambling, though it does reveal something about what's rewarding her right now. My uncle has been sober for ten years and still walks the long way around to avoid the bar; I don't take that as evidence he prefers not drinking the way I prefer tea over coffee—it looks like a second-order commitment he's enforcing against a first-order pull. A colleague donates blood every eight weeks but says he hates it and only goes because he'd feel guilty otherwise; I think this reveals a norm, or a preference to avoid guilt, not a preference for donating. A different colleague spends hours practicing a hard skill nobody knows he's working on; that one feels clearest, the behavior just is the preference. And I set my alarm for six every night genuinely intending to get up, and hit snooze every morning; I honestly can't say which is my real preference.

Is there a principled way to separate behaviors that reveal a preference from those that don't—or a small set of principles that gets most of these verdicts? What would your account say about a lab rat that presses a lever for food when hungry but ignores it when full? And which of my verdicts do I have to drop if I want the principle to stay simple?
