I have been asked to present an interdisciplinary seminar on moral philosophy's debate over negative utilitarianism and the commensurability of pleasure and pain. My own training is in economics — specifically decision theory and social choice — and I would rather do the work in my own idiom than merely report the philosophers' phrasing. But I am worried that when I restate their claims in my terms, something gets silently changed, so I want the translation checked.

My working vocabulary, roughly at the level of a graduate micro-theorist but with no philosophical training, includes: ordinal and cardinal utility; preference orderings and their representation by real-valued utility functions; lexicographic orderings, in which one dimension dominates another absolutely however much of the second dimension is at stake, and the non-Archimedean (vector- or infinitesimal-valued) utilities used to represent them; the continuity/Archimedean axioms that rule such orderings out; compensating variation and willingness-to-pay; indifference curves; revealed preference; interpersonal comparability and the measurability assumptions familiar from social choice; and the distinction between an agent's choice behaviour and the value function an analyst constructs.

Please restate the following bundle of positions in that vocabulary, one by one:
(a) negative utilitarianism, understood here as the claim that suffering has moral priority over happiness — a systematic asymmetry between the moral weight of avoiding pain and that of producing pleasure of the same magnitude;
(b) the lexical-threshold version of this view, on which suffering at or above some severity threshold cannot be outweighed by any quantity of mild pleasure or other goods, even though below the threshold ordinary pain–pleasure tradeoffs are permissible;
(c) the claim that pleasure and pain are commensurable — measurable on a single scale, so that comparisons across severities and across the positive–negative divide are possible — together with the opposing claim that a lexical threshold renders them incommensurable;
(d) the residual question of whether a threshold whose location is vague can still function as a lexical threshold.

For each item, say which parts translate cleanly, which can be forced into my vocabulary only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in it at all. Then give a residue report: after the translation, is anything left over that the debate needs, and does that leftover mark a gap in my economics vocabulary or a vagueness in the philosophical claims? In particular, I want to know whether lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism is, in my terms, simply a refusal to grant the Archimedean/continuity axiom for the moral ordering — or whether that formalization quietly drops something moral rather than merely technical.
