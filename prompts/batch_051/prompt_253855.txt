I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been reporting a feature about why we divvy up the past into eras the way we do. A historian of science walked me through an argument and now I cant find the seam.

(1) If the Scientific Revolution is a real episode in history, then the work grouped under that label should form one genuine development, not just a heap that later historians found it useful to link together.
(2) It doesn't. Copernicus rearranged the heavens while staying attached to a lot of older assumptions; Paracelsus was an occultist at war with university medicine; Bacon and Descartes disagreed about what method even was; Newton's physics still made room for God and prophecy.
(3) So on any standard strict enough to tell a real development from a mere family resemblance, the Scientific Revolution fails.
(4) So the Scientific Revolution is a later invention — the past had no such thing.
(5) The same standard wipes out the Renaissance (humanism, linear perspective, the new science, and Neoplatonism don't share a project) and the Middle Ages (originally an insult thrown at the centuries between the ancients and their self-appointed reborn admirers).
(6) But the standard also wipes out every period label going — the Cold War, the Great Depression, the Industrial Revolution — because no period is made of events that all share a single property.
(7) And if every period label is an invention, then all history-writing is just later generations imposing stories on a past that had no shape of its own.

That's too much to swallow. So either the chain breaks somewhere or one of those steps is doing too much work. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep saying there really was a Scientific Revolution, but I want to know what I'd have to give up for it.
