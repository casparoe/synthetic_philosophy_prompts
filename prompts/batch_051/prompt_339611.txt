I am a third-year undergraduate enrolled in a seminar on practical rationality, and we have just finished a unit on Parfit's hitchhiker case (the driver helps only on a promise of later payment, and a purely self-interested hitchhiker would then have no reason to pay once the ride is over) and the debate over whether it can be rational to make oneself irrational. I am building a revision exercise for my study group and would like you to construct one examination-style essay question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, that tests whether we have actually worked through the argument rather than merely memorized the slogan "it can be rational to be irrational."

Please take on the role of the course examiner: a professional philosopher who teaches practical rationality and knows both Parfit's discussion and the standard lines of criticism raised against it. Before locking in the final question, think through the specific conceptual moves an excellent student would need to make, and work out what you would count as the strongest answer. Then produce the final structured deliverable described below.

The essay question should be self-contained, should explicitly refer to the hitchhiker case, and should be answerable purely from the ideas, with no need for citations, page references, reading lists, or verbatim quotation. It must not be answerable by reciting the distinction between acts and dispositions: it should force students to use that distinction to resolve a tension. In particular, the question, model answer, and marking scheme should force engagement with at least the following: (a) the difference between evaluating individual acts and evaluating dispositions, policies, or traits of character; (b) the fact that the advantage of being trustworthy depends on the driver's being able to detect the disposition, and what happens to the argument if detection is absent or unreliable; (c) the bootstrapping problem, i.e. whether one can simply decide to acquire a disposition like trustworthiness; and (d) the self-effacing worry, i.e. whether the view recommends cultivating motives or dispositions that it officially classifies as irrational, and whether this is a defect. The model answer should take a definite position on whether the hitchhiker's paying is irrational, rational, or only classifiable once a criterion of rationality is stated, and the marking scheme should identify the specific moves that separate an A answer from a B answer.

Return exactly one JSON object, with no text outside it, using this schema:

- "preliminary_brainstorm": a string containing your working notes in compact bullet-list form, showing the moves you considered before settling on the question.
- "exam_question": a string containing the essay question, at most three sentences, worded for an upper-level undergraduate exam.
- "model_answer": a string of roughly 650 words of continuous prose presenting the strongest answer. Do not use verbatim quotations; paraphrase anything attributed to Parfit.
- "marking_scheme": an array of exactly 5 objects, each with these fields:
  - "criterion": a short name for what is being assessed.
  - "weight_percent": an integer; the five weights must sum to 100.
  - "what_an_A_answer_must_do": the specific claims or moves a top answer has to make to earn these marks.
  - "what_distinguishes_a_B_answer": what a merely adequate answer does instead, so that the failure this criterion catches is named explicitly.
- "common_misunderstandings_tested": an array of strings, each naming one identifiable misconception the question is designed to expose and the move that should reveal it.

Output only the JSON object.
