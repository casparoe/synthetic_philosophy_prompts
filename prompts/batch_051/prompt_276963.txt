After a career in physical chemistry I've gotten into the well-being literature, and the argument I keep not being able to shake is the experience machine. Ive landed provisionally on the anti-hedonist reading: the fact that most of us wouldn't plug in for a lifetime of guaranteed nice experiences shows felt experience is not the only thing that matters for a good life, so hedonism about well-being is at least incomplete and probably false as a account of welfare. I want to know what would count against that conclusion. Not just the easy "I'd plug in" reply, but the strongest defeaters on offer. I've seen something like a reverse experience-machine move, where you ask whether you'd prefer that the life you're now living be real rather than a simulation that feels identical — does the intuition flip? And I've seen the status quo bias complaint, that the setup rigs the choice by starting you outside the machine and asking you to leave your actual life, rather than offering a symmetric choice between two possible lives. There's also the line that refusal just shows we value truth or authenticity as something separate from our own good, or that we're being irrational about loss aversion, so the intuition doesn't actually touch what's good for a person. Which of these should actually move me, and which are just clever? What should an honest hedonist concede counts against their view — people do refuse in surveys, and the view seems committed to saying a maximally pleasant life of hallucination is just as good as a real one — and what do they rightly say doesn't touch the theory? I'd like the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward. And tell me straight whether my position sticks its neck out at all: what would it predict that rival views don't, and has it been qualified into something unfalsifiable? Coming from a field where a hypothesis has to risk being wrong, I want to know where this one risks being wrong.
