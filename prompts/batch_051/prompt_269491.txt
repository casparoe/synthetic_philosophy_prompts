As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am drafting an article on the limits of free speech, and in the course of it I have built an argument that now seems to prove far too much. I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis.

(1) The harm principle says that the only legitimate ground for coercive interference with a person's conduct — including communicative conduct — is the prevention of harm to others.

(2) Speech does not work like a projectile. An utterance changes the world only through a hearer's uptake: she must interpret it, give it some weight, and decide to act on it.

(3) When a competent adult deliberates on what she has heard and then acts, the resulting injury is her own deed, for which she rather than the speaker is answerable. A free agent's choice is not an effect produced by the speaker; it is exactly what interrupts any causal chain from the words to the outcome.

(4) Therefore, strictly, speech never harms others: whatever mischief follows through a hearer's choice is harm done by the hearer, not harm done by the speaker.

(5) But if (1) and (4) hold, the harm principle can support almost no restriction on expression. It cannot support punishing incitement to violence, publishing detailed instructions for manufacturing explosives, knowingly false financial claims that ruin those who rely on them, or denunciations that foreseeably get someone killed.

(6) That conclusion is impossible to accept. Mill himself thought that the same criticism of corn-dealers that is harmless in print may properly be punished when delivered to an excitable crowd gathered outside the dealer's house; and any functioning legal system restricts at least incitement and certain fraudulent misrepresentations.

(7) So the harm principle, applied consistently, seems to force free-speech absolutism upon us; and since free-speech absolutism is almost universally rejected, one of the earlier steps must go.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? If forced to choose, I would rather keep the principle in (1) and the ordinary permissions in (6), but I want to know which step to reject, why that step looked sound, and what the corrected principle says about speech, causation, and responsibility. Please write your answer in British English.
