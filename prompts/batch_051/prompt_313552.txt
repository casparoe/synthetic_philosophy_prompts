I recently spent an evening arguing with two friends about whether the traditional view of hell as eternal conscious torment can be squared with divine justice. I kept pressing a version of the thought that the punishment should fit the crime: a finite creature's sin cannot warrant an unending penalty, so a just God would eventually restore the sinner. One friend, an annihilationist, said the same slogan actually lands one step short: it rules out eternal torment, but it points to the sinner's destruction, not to universal salvation. The other, more traditionalist friend, said he had heard the slogan run in the opposite direction altogether: if the seriousness of a sin is measured by the worth of the one it offends, then a sin against an infinite God is itself infinitely serious, and the fitting punishment is infinite. I now suspect the apparently simple slogan is doing three contradictory jobs at once.

I'd like you to lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise principles 'the punishment should fit the crime' could be expressing when invoked in debates about hell. Distinguish candidate metrics of fit — duration of suffering, intensity of suffering, the harm actually caused, the dignity or infinite worth of the party offended, the offender's knowledge and freedom, and so on — and candidate rationales that might supply the metric, such as retribution, deterrence under the assumption of an afterlife, the expression of divine seriousness, or the restoration of the sinner. For each reading: say whether it is plausible in its own right, whether it remains coherent when the penalty is infinite and the agent merely finite, and, above all, whether that reading actually supports my universalist conclusion, the annihilationist's intermediate conclusion, the traditionalist's rejoinder, or none of them. I want a set of verdicts I could bring back to the argument, not a single settled answer.
