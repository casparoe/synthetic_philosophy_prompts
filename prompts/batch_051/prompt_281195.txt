It has become a running theme in my department, prompted by the scorekeeping picture familiar from Lewis's work, that conversation is a game played with a scoreboard. On this picture, the common ground is the scoreboard, the participants are the players, assertions are moves, and the board is updated not by whatever anyone privately believes but by rules of the game. A colleague has been pressing the consequences of this picture for the theory of presupposition. His claim in particular is that the rule of accommodation is just one more rule of scorekeeping: when an assertion requires a presupposition that is not yet on the board—say, that the speaker has a sister—the missing entry is added in virtue of the rule, much as a run is recorded when the ball crosses the plate whether or not anyone on the field was watching the scoreboard. He concludes that accommodation is a conventional, rule-governed update of the conversational record, and that an adequate account of it need not appeal to what a hearer infers about the speaker's intentions or to how participants reason about one another.

I would like a systematic audit of that analogy. Begin by giving the correspondence table: what in conversation is supposed to play the role of the scoreboard, the players, the score, the rules, the officials, the permitted moves, and the point of the game. Then identify the places where the mapping breaks. As you do so, separate the disanalogies that are merely harmless idealizations of a model from the disanalogies that matter for my colleague's conclusion. In particular, address at least the following: whether accommodation is automatic in the way score updating is; whether a conversation has anything analogous to an official or referee who can correct a misrecorded score; whether the scoreboard can contain entries that no participant believes, or entries that conflict, and what that would mean if the scoreboard is supposed to be the common ground; and which features of real games the analogy quietly imports—publicity, countable discrete updates, a shared aim, authority for settling disputes—that a conversation may not have. Finally, say whether the conclusion that accommodation is a purely conventional and psychologically inert matter of scorekeeping survives the disanalogies, and if the analogy fails at the load-bearing points, offer a better analogy for what accommodation does.
