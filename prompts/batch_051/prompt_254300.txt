So I keep going back and forth with a friend about whether its actually rational to treat an LLM like ChatGPT as a source of everyday factual info—like what happened at last night's city council meeting, or whether two medications interact. I'm reading Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding in class, and this bit from Part 1 of Section 10 ("Of Miracles") feels like it's aimed exactly at the question, even though it obviously isn't. Hume writes: "Were not the memory tenacious to a certain degree; had not men commonly an inclination to truth and a principle of probity; were they not sensible to shame, when detected in a falsehood: Were not these, I say, discovered by experience to be qualities, inherent in human nature, we should never repose the least confidence in human testimony."

For Hume, trust in testimony is supposed to be earned from experience: we learn that human reporters generally have those qualities, and that usually their reports line up with the facts. But with a language model the analogy gets wierd fast—it has no memory in Hume's sense, no shame, no probity, no human nature; yet I've got a lot of experience of it being right-ish, mixed with experience of it being smoothly, confidently wrong in a way I'd rarely get from a careful human. So here's what I want figured out. Treat the quoted passage as a kind of rule for when testimony deserves confidence, and apply it to the case of an LLM reporting facts it never witnessed to a user whose track record with it is mixed. What does the wording itself tell you about whether this counts as the sort of testimony Hume is licensing? What does the underlying purpose of his reductionist account say? And what do you think Hume himself would have said about machine testimony if he could have tried it? Which of those three angles should win here, and does the anti-reductionist view that we're built to default-trust testimony handle the machine case any better or worse?
