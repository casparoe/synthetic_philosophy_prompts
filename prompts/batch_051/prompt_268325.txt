Write a dialogue between a continuity theorist and a patternist about whether a whole-brain emulation would be the same mind as the biological original. The continuity theorist holds that surviving as a person requires the right kind of physical and psychological continuity, so a destructive scan-and-instantiate procedure amounts to death, whereas a gradual, stream-preserving replacement of neurons might allow survival. The patternist holds that survival is a matter of preserving the causally relevant computational structure, so the scan step is no more lethal than the brain's ordinary replacement of its physical constituents. Both characters should be equally competent and inventive. Have them test each other's views against concrete cases rather than slogans: a lossy emulation that reproduces behavior and self-report but omits detail below some threshold (such as glial dynamics or precise spike timing); a fork that produces two divergent emulations; a gradual replacement that briefly interrupts consciousness; and the question of whether their dispute is ultimately about a fact or about what we ought to care about. They should answer each other's actual arguments, adapt under pressure, and be left with at least one point of genuine, precisely located disagreement.
