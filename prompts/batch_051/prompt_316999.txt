I've recently been asked to join a small working group on the discount rates our organisation uses when it evaluates long-term emissions-reduction projects, and a colleague circulated a memo that summarizes the issue this way: 'Economists discount the future because people are impatient and would rather have benefits now than later. Applied to climate change, that means valuing a life saved in 2100 less than a life saved today simply because it is further away in time. Most economists working on climate think that is fine, but Stern showed that this is ethically indefensible and proved that the only defensible approach is to use a near-zero discount rate. Once you accept a near-zero rate, essentially every serious emissions-reduction proposal pays for itself.' I have to respond to this memo, and I suspect it is a mix of accurate and misleading, but I don't have the background to say exactly where the line falls. Could you go through it claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful economist or philosopher would accept, which are subtly wrong or conflated, what someone who defends conventional discounting practice would say in reply, and whether the memo's final sentence follows from anything true in the summary? In doing so, please separate what is settled in this area from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and don't present a contested position as the consensus.
