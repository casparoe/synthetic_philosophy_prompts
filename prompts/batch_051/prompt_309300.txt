I am compiling the standard arguments for the claim that a visitor is morally required to follow the everyday conventions of the place they are in — tipping in American restaurants, queueing rather than jumping the queue in Britain, not jaywalking in a German city — even when the visitor's own culture has no such practice or observes a different one. My list so far has four kinds: (1) fair play, because the visitor benefits from a scheme the locals maintain and should not free-ride on it; (2) reliance, because locals reasonably rely on compliance and may be harmed when that reliance is disappointed; (3) implicit consent, because voluntarily entering a country with knowledge of its conventions amounts to accepting them; and (4) respect, because flouting local norms expresses contempt for, or indifference to, one's hosts. All four locate the obligation in the visitor's relation to particular locals. I believe a further standard kind of argument locates the obligation elsewhere; state it, lay out its structure, explain why it is not just a rewording, special case, or combination of the items listed, and say which kind of case it handles more naturally than they do. Keep the answer under 300 words.
