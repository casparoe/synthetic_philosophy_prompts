A colleague of mine works on the outside view of state failure: she always picks a reference class of earlier polities, applies its base rate, and only then adjusts for case-specific facts. I am trying to reverse-engineer which reference class is actually doing the work from her track record before I ask her about a new case.

Her retrospective calls are:

- Ottoman Empire, as of 1800: high risk of terminal contraction and eventual dissolution.
- Spanish-Habsburg composite monarchy, as of 1600: high risk of losing the possessions that sustained its European position.
- Russian Empire, as of 1900: high risk of revolutionary collapse.
- Austria-Hungary, as of 1900: high risk of fragmentation into national states.
- Soviet Union, as of 1970: high risk of eventual fragmentation.
- French Third Republic, as of 1900: low risk of state collapse.
- United States, as of 1900: low risk of state collapse.

The new case is a wealthy, consolidated parliamentary democracy with two large linguistic communities, no overseas empire, no history of authoritarian rule, and one of its two national communities has just held a narrowly failed independence referendum. What verdict should I expect from her over a comparable long horizon? In particular, give me your best prediction under each candidate rule, state which rule you think best explains the record—or say honestly that the record underdetermines it—identify the closest analog among the precedents, and name a further case, not on the list, whose verdict would cleanly distinguish the live rules.
