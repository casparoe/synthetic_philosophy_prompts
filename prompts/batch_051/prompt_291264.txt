I spent forty-odd years as a geologist, mostly stratigraphy and basin analysis, and I've only recently started reading philosophy of science seriously. Goodman's grue problem has really got its teeth in me. The way I understand it: 'green' and 'grue' both fit all the emeralds we've inspected so far, where grue applies to a thing if it was inspected before some future time t and is green, or was not inspected before t and is blue. So plain induction seems to license predicting that the next emerald is green and also that it is blue. What nags at me isn't the puzzle itself but the feeling that the philosophical discussion may be aimed at a slightly artificial target. Working geologists regularly use predicates that are, in a loose sense, time-indexed or boundary-defined. Stratigraphic units are defined by "golden spike" sections chosen by committee rather than by any single intrinsic property of the rock: the Cambrian is more or less whatever lies above an agreed-upon horizon in a particular section, not a rock type you could identify blind. Yet we make perfectly good projectible generalizations about such units (fossil content, subsidence history, sea-level behavior), and nobody in geology loses sleep over whether "Cambrian" is grue-like. So maybe the real problem of induction has more to do with how the practices and purposes of a science earn a category its projectibility than with blue emeralds.

Among the main approaches to Goodman's new riddle -- entrenchment, natural-kind and similarity accounts, formal learning theory, machine-learning-flavored treatments of overfitting and regularization, or the idea that grue is defective because it can't be specified without a particular time -- which line of work is most worth pursuing if the goal is an account of induction that fits real scientific practice rather than just tidies up a toy example? And which lines do you think are dead ends, or only work if you accept assumptions that make the original problem artificial?
