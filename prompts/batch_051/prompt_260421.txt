I have been trying to sort out what I actually believe about animal suffering before the Fall — the tension between the doctrine that creation was made good and the evolutionary record, in which predation, parasitism, disease, and mass death run for hundreds of millions of years before any human being exists to sin. I am not asking for a survey of theological positions or for references; I want to know whether my own intuitive verdicts on particular cases hide a coherent principle, and if so, what that principle is, stated precisely enough to apply to new cases.

Here are the cases, with my honest reactions. (1) A lioness brings down a wildebeest and the kill takes several minutes: I am not much troubled by this, because the predator must eat and the victim's death feeds other creatures. (2) A parasitoid wasp lays eggs inside a live caterpillar, and the larvae consume the host's nonessential tissues first so that it stays alive as long as possible while being eaten from within: this troubles me far more than the lion, because the prolonged destruction of the host seems positively selected for rather than an unavoidable byproduct of something valuable. (3) A mass extinction wipes out the large majority of species over a geologically brief interval: this does not trouble me in the same way, since it feels like a turnover of forms rather than the designed torment of individual organisms. (4) A young elephant separated from its herd during a drought slowly starves, conscious and alone, with no predator to end it quickly: this troubles me as much as the wasp, though no second agent is involved. (5) Prey animals live with a near-constant baseline of vigilance and stress physiology even when no predator is present: I find this more troubling than the lion kill but less than the elephant, because the cost is distributed across entire lives rather than located in one event. (6) Ecosystems depend on death and decay to cycle nutrients, so that nearly every form of life depends in some way on the death of others: I am inclined to count this as compatible with a good creation, because death functions as a structural support for flourishing. (7) A fawn is born with a painful genetic defect that leads to early death: this bothers me because it seems like pure loss, instrumental to nothing.

My provisional principle is roughly that naturally occurring animal suffering is compatible with a good creation when it is an unavoidable byproduct of processes that produce great goods — ecosystemic flourishing, biodiversity, the fit of organisms to their niches — and incompatible when it is gratuitous. But the principle already strains: the wasp's behavior is as much a product of natural selection as the lion's, yet it feels different in kind, and I do not know how to make the notion of gratuitousness precise enough to exclude the wasp while retaining the mass extinction and the nutrient cycles. I also keep going back and forth on a further case: a parasite that alters a host's behavior so that the host exposes itself to a predator, like an ant that climbs a blade of grass and waits to be eaten so the parasite can complete its life cycle. What principle best fits the seven verdicts, what does it say about that eighth case, and which of my verdicts, if any, would I have to surrender to keep the principle from becoming an ad hoc patchwork?
