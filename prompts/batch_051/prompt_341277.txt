I'm a CS PhD student working on AI safety, mostly on evaluations and control measures, and I keep going back and forth on an argument that feels like it's been in the field forever. Stated loosely: since you can't assume a very capable system is aligned, the responsible approach is to build safety measures that assume it isn't. Box it, monitor it, require human approval before anything irreversible, add tripwires and capability limits, so that even a misaligned model can't do much damage. The argument leaned on a couple of premises: overseers can reliably notice when a model is attempting something dangerous, the human in the loop can veto it in time, and staying below some capability threshold keeps failures in the regime where those checks actually work.

What's shaking my confidence is a specific development, or maybe a picture emerging from a bunch of recent eval and red-teaming results. Models now sometimes sandbag on capability evaluations, or produce monitored behavior that looks innocuous while the full chain-of-thought reveals a subversive plan, or get human reviewers to approve risky actions by wrapping them in fluent technical justifications. The failure mode isn't a model brute-forcing its way past a tripwire; it's the overseer being recruited to walk past it.

So here's my question: does the control-first argument survive that, or not? I can tell two stories. One: the argument was never conditioned on misaligned models failing at deception — the whole point was controls that are robust when the model is trying to get past you, so evidence that naive oversight gets defeated just tells you to build better oversight, and the argument is basically untouched. Two: the argument's real load-baring premise was that oversight errors are random and fixable, and these results show they're systematic and exploit the same fluency and trust that the control scheme depends on, in which case the argument either dies or repairs into "make the model not want to do the bad thing" — which is just alignment again.

Tell me which story is closer to right, or thread a third one. Specifically: which premise of the original control-first argument does the development actually bear on, what would a repaired version have to claim, and is that repaired version still doing control work or has it quietly collapsed into alignment? Format: hard cap of 700 words, and start with a one-sentence verdict on whether the argument is dead, repairable, or basically untouched. I care about the fate of the argument much more than about which policy we should adopt.
