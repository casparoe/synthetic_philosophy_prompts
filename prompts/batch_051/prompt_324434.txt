I cover ideas and the social sciences for a general-interest outlet, and lately I keep running into the same question in different clothes: when, if ever, is a person morally on the hook for something they didn't personally do? The word "guilt" is doing a lot of work in these arguments — original sin, reparations, corporate liability, even who gets blamed for a country's past — and I'd like a working defintion of inherited moral guilt (or answerability, or whatever you think the better term is) that gets these cases right:

1. A newborn baby, in the story where all human beings inherit the guilt of a distant ancestor's first sin.
2. A getaway driver who never planned or wanted a killing, but drove the shooter away after the robbery went bad and saw the body.
3. An adult who knowingly spends money their parent stole.
4. The same adult, if they spent it for years without knowing where it came from, then found out and did nothing.
5. A corporation that is fined today for pollution its now-dead founders caused decades before it changed hands.
6. A person born in 2005 in a country whose government committed a genocide in the 1970s, who had an ordinary childhood and never thinks about it.
7. An adult immigrant who moves to that country, takes citizenship, and buys a home cheap partly because of that history.

I'm confident the baby isn't guilty in any ordinary sense and the getaway driver is, but the middle five I keep flip-flopping on depending on the week. Propose the definition, run it through all seven, and tell me which cases it handles badly or where you had to stretch it. Where it gives a weird answer, either revise it or defend the weird answer. Please write in American English.
