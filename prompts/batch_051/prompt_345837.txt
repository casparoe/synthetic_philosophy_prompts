I chair the program committee of a private foundation that makes multiyear grants aimed at improving long-term human welfare in low-income countries. We are about to decide whether to fund a ten-year, $30 million unconditional cash-transfer program across several rural districts. The proposal's explicit rationale is consequentialist: the near-term evidence from randomized trials suggests the transfers improve child health, schooling, and household investment, and the proposers infer that these gains will compound into better adult outcomes over the next few decades. A minority on the committee objects that this inference is exactly what consequentialist reasoning cannot support. On their view, the outcomes that would actually decide whether the program does good, such as its effects on labor-market participation, migration, local prices, political behavior, fertility, and social norms two or three decades out, are unknowable in advance, so no one is in a position to say the grant would have good consequences overall. They call this the cluelessness objection. The majority replies that every course of action, including declining the grant, has equally unknowable long-run consequences, and that refusing to act on the best available near-term evidence would simply replace one guess with another. Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before our meeting next week. It should recommend approving, rejecting, or approving the program with conditions; it should give reasoning the full board could act on; and it should engage seriously with the strongest form of the cluelessness objection rather than dismissing it as mere risk-aversion.
