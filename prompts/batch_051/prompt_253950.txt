I'm a philosophy of science PhD student, and in three weeks I'm giving a talk in my department's philosophy of science workshop. My thesis is that the circularity worry for Woodward's interventionism is worse than the standard reply lets on. The standard reply is that the circularity is harmless because Woodward never promised a reductive analysis: 'intervention' is a thick causal notion, and the account is just a non-reductive functional characterization of how causal claims work. I want to argue that this handles the metaphysics but not the epistemology. When you actually try to use invariance under interventions to pick out causal structure — e.g., to tell apart a structure where A causes B from one where A and B share a common cause C, or one where B causes A — you can't tell whether you've performed a genuine intervention on A unless you already know enough about which way the causal arrows point. So the account is great for regimenting and testing causal hypotheses you already partly have, but it can't be the non-question-begging bridge from data-plus-manipulations to causal structure that some of its users seem to want.

The audience: a philosophy of science colloquium. Mostly philosophers of science — a few mechanism people who like Woodward but think interventions are just a special case of mechanisms; one or two people who run causal discovery algorithms (Spirtes–Glymour–Scheines style) and think the circularity objection is a philosophers' hangup because the algorithms just test conditional independences; a senior Humean regularist who thinks any account that uses 'cause' in its own definiens is empty until it's been reduced to regularities; and a grad student who has read all of Woodward cover to cover and will call me out if I get the intervention conditions wrong. I'll have the exact wording of the conditions on a slide, but that's the kid to worry about.

For each one: what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, a two-minute reply, and where that reply is softest. Also flag the weakest point in my own argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices, and tell me the one question I'm really hoping nobody asks. Write the whole prep sheet in American English.
