I direct the ethics-and-compliance training unit at a large teaching hospital. Each quarter I have to sign off, for roughly two hundred nurses, residents, technicians, and rotating contractors, that each of them genuinely understands our standards for patient safety, handoff communication, and informed consent before they may work unsupervised. The decision is high-stakes in both directions: falsely certifying someone can lead to patient harm, while refusing to certify can leave wards dangerously understaffed. Our assessment tools are constrained—written scenario tests, one or two structured simulations, and supervisors' recent reports—and we cannot observe most trainees in sustained real clinical work before the sign-off date. The current process certifies people who can recite the standards and choose the correct multiple-choice answer; when those same people later fail to act correctly under pressure, we console ourselves by calling it a knowledge-action gap.

I have been reading Wang Yangming's doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action, and I want our sign-off process rebuilt around it. As I understand the doctrine, it holds that someone does not genuinely know a norm or virtue unless they act on it; action is not a later consequence of a finished private item called knowledge but its completion; and a person who says the right things and then fails to act has not yet truly known, however fluent the recitation. Please write me a reusable decision procedure, grounded in that idea, that my instructors—who are not philosophers and have limited time per trainee—can actually run. It should specify the questions to ask in order, what counts as sufficient evidence at each step, where the procedure stops with knowledge not demonstrated, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than evidence. Please also be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: whether it can certify someone who has not been observed under real conditions, and how it should treat someone who acts correctly by habit but cannot articulate the standard.

Then run the procedure on two cases: (a) a resident who gives a polished explanation of the informed-consent standard but was observed obtaining a signature from a sedated, confused patient; and (b) a nursing assistant who cannot state the formal handoff protocol in its official language but whose shift reports consistently contain exactly what the protocol requires and who corrects others when they omit items. I expect at least one answer I will not like. Please say which feature of Wang Yangming's doctrine forces that answer, and how someone who believes in a knowledge-action gap would likely attack the procedure.
