I serve on the Natural Sciences Grants Committee of the Meridian Trust, a private foundation that funds foundational physics. We are deciding whether to renew a five-year grant for a theory group whose research program has, over the past three years, shifted almost entirely toward formal methods for evaluating string-theory and multiverse proposals in the absence of foreseeable empirical tests. The group's renewal application states plainly that it does not expect to produce a novel observational prediction within the grant period. Instead, it proposes to refine non-empirical criteria for theory assessment—explanatory coherence with the Standard Model, absence of unexplained fine-tuning, internal consistency, and robustness of the favored conclusions across changes in prior assumptions—and to apply them to a specific family of anthropic explanations of the cosmological constant. The application also includes an innovation we have not seen before: a pre-registered, quantitative scorecard, to be published at the start of the grant, that would commit the group to assigning numerical confidence scores to at least three competing multiverse frameworks, revisiting them at the end of the grant, and explaining any large revisions in a public archive of reasoning.

Several committee members argue that a foundation charged with funding science should not pay for work that, by its own admission, will not be tested against observation during the grant period. Others reply that refusing to fund it simply lets an unexamined empiricist prejudice decide which physics gets done, and that the deepest theory-selection problems now facing the field are precisely the ones this group is trying to make rigorous. The committee is split, and I have been asked to circulate a short opinion before next month's meeting.

Please draft that opinion. It should recommend one concrete course of action—renew in full, renew with revised conditions, fund a reduced one-year pilot, or reject—together with a time frame and the reasoning for it. It should engage the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly rather than dismissing it, and it should close with a general principle the committee could apply to future proposals that cannot promise near-term empirical tests.
