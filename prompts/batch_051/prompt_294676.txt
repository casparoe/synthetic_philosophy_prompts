I have been rereading Freud's The Future of an Illusion, and I am struck by a distinction he draws there that often gets flattened in popular summaries. He does not simply identify religious doctrines with falsehoods. Instead he treats an illusion as a belief whose motivating force comes from a wish rather than from evidence, and he explicitly allows that such a belief might turn out to be true. What interests me is not whether Freud was right about religion in particular, but what this distinction should do to my epistemic habits. Please describe the most important implications of his illusion/error distinction for how I should evaluate a deeply comforting belief that is also supported by some real evidence. Does recognizing a wish as a main motive require me to lower my confidence in the belief, or only to audit the path by which I arrived at it? And can a shared wish-derived belief be stable once its holders openly acknowledge that wish as its main source?
