I keep coming back to the opening of John Stuart Mill's On Liberty, where he sets up the problem this way: "The subject of this Essay is not the so-called Liberty of the Will, so unfortunately opposed to the misnamed doctrine of Philosophical Necessity; but Civil, or Social Liberty: the nature and limits of the power which can be legitimately exercised by society over the individual." I want to apply that sentence to a recurring confusion I have about moral rules. When someone says a practice is wrong, they often seem to be claiming that society has a legitimate say over that part of a person's conduct. But when I try to locate that legitimacy, I find only the preferences, aversions, and biases of whatever groups happen to have enough social power to make their view stick. The claim I cannot get out of my head is this: the difference between "a moral rule" and "a group preference enforced by social power" is not something we detect; it is something we add afterward. Design the strongest test you can for whether that distinction does real work. What observation, experiment, or comparison could show that a society's moral rules are something over and above enforced preferences—for example, what happens when the factual beliefs feeding a rule are corrected, when formerly weak groups gain power and keep the rule anyway, or when independent traditions converge on the same rule without contact? Be concrete about what result would support the distinction and what result would undermine it. Then say what even your strongest positive result would still leave unsettled about the original worry.
