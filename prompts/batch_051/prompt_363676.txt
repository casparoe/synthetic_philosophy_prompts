I was talking the other evening with a friend in the maths department, and somehow we got onto the old business of computer-assisted proofs. He said the four-colour theorem has now been checked end to end, and the Kepler conjecture too, and yet mathematicians still sort of shrug at them: the proof is right, sure, but it isn't surveyable, it doesn't give you the reason why, the code might have a bug, and anyway you'd need to verify the verifier, and then verify the verification, and so on. But here's the thing that stuck with me. The same people who say all this will happily treat a proof as settled once two or three overworked human referees have signed off on it, even though referees miss gaps the whole time and nobody can hold a two-hundred-page proof in their head either.

So there seems to be an asymmetry in ordinary mathematical practice: human checking is treated as the gold standard however rushed and fallible it is, while checking by a proof assistant or a programme is treated as second-class however exhaustive it is. Is there a defensible principle that generates both attitudes, or is the difference really just about prestige, intuition, and wanting proofs to be stories we can enjoy? Give me the strongest justification you can find for the asymmetry, test it against nearby cases, and say honestly wether we should keep it, reverse it, or give it up.
