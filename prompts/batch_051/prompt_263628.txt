You are a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate course in the history of political thought. The midterm included the following short-answer question, worth 15 points:

"Explain the relationship between fortuna and virtù in Machiavelli's The Prince. Does Machiavelli think that a prince of sufficient virtù can reliably master fortune? Answer with reference to the main images or arguments Machiavelli uses."

Grade the student answer below out of 15 points, and then provide the point-by-point comments that justify the grade. In your comments, identify which claims in the answer earn credit and which are mistaken, overstated, or in tension, and explain what the student would need to revise to reach full credit. Do not give only an overall impression; your comments should show exactly where points were awarded and withheld.

Student answer:

Machiavelli's virtù is not Christian moral virtue; it is a capacity for decisive, adaptable agency: knowing when to be bold and when to be cautious, and being willing to depart from ordinary moral standards when necessity demands it. The fox-and-lion example illustrates this, since the prince needs the fox's cunning to recognize traps and the lion's force to frighten wolves. Fortune, by contrast, is the realm of contingency, what is beyond the prince's control. Machiavelli's main image for fortune is a river: when the river is quiet, a ruler can build dikes and embankments so that the next flood will do less damage. He also describes fortune as a woman who yields to those who act with boldness rather than cold calculation. This shows, he thinks, that a prepared and spirited prince is not at fortune's mercy. Indeed, Machiavelli says explicitly that fortune is the arbiter of only about half of what we do, leaving us the other half—or a little less—to govern. He therefore concludes that virtù can make fortune largely or even entirely predictable: the prince who studies the times and adjusts his conduct will be able to master circumstance. His late advice to be impetuous rather than cautious confirms this, because bold action will always prevail over luck.
