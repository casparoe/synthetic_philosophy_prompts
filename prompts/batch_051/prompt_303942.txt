In my philosophy of mind course we have been working through the rationalist–empiricist debate over whether the mind has any innate knowledge. The rationalist's strongest move, as we have reconstructed it, is that a blank-slate learner could never get started: any learning rule must already treat some inputs as more salient than others, some patterns as more projectible, and some candidate hypotheses as more plausible, so the learner must bring structure to the input. I keep noticing that machine learning appears to make the same point in its own vocabulary: every learning algorithm has an inductive bias, and without some bias there is no generalization, because infinitely many hypotheses are compatible with any finite set of observations. I cannot tell whether this is one point stated twice or a loose analogy that dissolves once the terms are made precise. Spell out the mapping between inductive biases and innate knowledge; say what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer between them; and identify at least one case where the parallel predicts something false, such as an inductive bias no rationalist would count as knowledge, or an innate constraint that would not appear as an inductive bias. Finally, give a verdict on whether the machine-learning thesis that generalization presupposes an inductive bias actually supports rationalism or is neutral between rationalism and empiricism once the terms are made precise.
