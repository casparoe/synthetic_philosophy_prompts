For an upper-level seminar in applied population ethics and conservation policy, I am preparing a case-study exercise designed to break students of the assumption that wild-animal welfare and habitat protection always point in the same direction. Please construct a thought experiment to the following specification.

The scenario must be a single, realistic paragraph: a conservation authority of some ordinary kind, such as a land trust, wetland manager, or parks agency, must decide whether to carry out a deliberate habitat-degrading intervention on a parcel that currently supports a dense population of sentient wild animals. No science fiction, no exotic technology, and no painless welfare engineering; the intervention must be a mundane management action, such as draining, cutting off a water supply, or removing cover.

The case must satisfy these four specifications:

1. Total-welfare consequentialism, which counts the welfare of all sentient wild animals, including present ones and those that would come to exist under each option, and which treats ecosystems as having only instrumental value, must favor the intervention.
2. An ecocentric environmental ethic, which locates intrinsic value in ecological systems and processes independently of the welfare of individual organisms, must oppose the same intervention.
3. A prior-existence, harm-based animal ethic, which forbids harming presently existing sentient animals but recognizes no positive reason to bring future sentient lives into existence or to prevent them from coming into existence, must also oppose the intervention, but for a different reason.
4. The case must isolate one structural feature: the intervention harms existing individuals in order to prevent a much larger number of future net-negative lives. If the case were altered so that the very same habitat were merely kept from coming into existence, with no presently existing animals harmed, the prior-existence view would no longer oppose the decision, even though the total-welfare view would still favor it.

Important constraint: the total-welfare verdict must be generated by an explicit, empirically plausible welfare calculus. Specify the population dynamics, the main sources of mortality and suffering, and the overall sign and distribution of the lives at stake, for example a large annual pulse of juveniles that die early from predation, desiccation, or disease. Do not settle the case by simply stipulating that wild animal lives are net negative.

After the scenario, provide a walkthrough showing, for each of the three positions, why it reaches the specified verdict. Then answer these three questions: Which feature of the case is doing the main work? What happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed? And how would a defender of the ecocentric view and a defender of the prior-existence view each attack the total-welfare verdict? End with a short note on any contestable empirical assumption the case depends on and how a student could use that assumption to resist the intended verdict.
