I'm presenting a paper to a constitutional theory workshop defending Frederick Douglass's 1860 Glasgow speech, "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?", as a serious account of constitutional interpretation rather than merely abolitionist polemic. The centerpiece of the paper is this passage:

"It would be the wildest of absurdities, and lead to endless confusion and mischiefs, if, instead of looking to the written paper itself, for its meaning, it were attempted to make us search it out, in the secret motives, and dishonest intentions, of some of the men who took part in writing it. It was what they said that was adopted by the people, not what they were ashamed or afraid to say, and really omitted to say."

My reading is that Douglass is not just pressing for the right substantive result: he is advancing a methodological claim — that the Constitution's meaning is set by the text the people ratified and by what its language actually says, not by the framers' unexpressed intentions — and that on this method the Constitution grants slavery no guarantee. The audience will include public-meaning originalists, a few living constitutionalists, and two historians of abolition sympathetic to Garrison's view that the Constitution was a pro-slavery compact. Prepare a Q&A briefing for me: what objections will each of these groups actually raise against using Douglass this way, phrased in their own idiom, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are? For each objection, give a short reply, note where the reply is soft, and say what, if anything, I should concede. End with the question I should most hope nobody asks.
