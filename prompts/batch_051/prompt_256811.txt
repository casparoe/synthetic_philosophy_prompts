I've been trying to figure out whether an analogy I keep noticing is actually doing work or is just a loose metaphor. The problem of the criterion in epistemology says, roughly, that you can't justify a criterion for telling good beliefs from bad ones without already relying on some beliefs, and you can't justify those beliefs without already relying on a criterion, so the whole thing either goes in a circle or bottoms out in something arbitrary. I was reading about how large language models are evaluated and the same shape seemed to be there. To know whether a model's answers are reliable you need a benchmark or a set of trusted reference answers. To know whether the benchmark is good you need some independent way of knowing what the right answers are. But the only independent ways available are either another model, which is circular, or human judgment, which is exactly the kind of fallible procedure you were trying to check in the first place. Even the fancier methods, like using one model to grade another model, look like trying to pull yourself up by your own bootstraps.

I can't decide whether this is a genuine structural identity or just a superficial resemblance. On the one hand, the ML case seems to have an escape hatch the epistemological case lacks: you can sometimes go out and get fresh ground truth from the world, run experiments, or wait for outcomes. On the other hand, that only works for tasks with observable results, and for open-ended reasoning or subtle value judgments there is no ground truth to fetch. So: is this the same problem or not? If it is deep, does anything from the old debate about the criterion—like the split between starting from particular cases and starting from a method, or the skeptical conclusion that no non-arbitrary starting point exists—transfer to how we should interpret benchmark results? If it's superficial, where exactly does the mapping break, and what would a real ML analogue of the criterion problem have to include that this one lacks?
