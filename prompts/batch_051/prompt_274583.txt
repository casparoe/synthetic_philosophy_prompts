At dinner last night my friends Dana and Mo got into it. Mo is a vegan, but he eats the eggs from the rescue hens his brother keeps in the garden, on freegan grounds — animal products are okay if they'd otherwise be wasted. Dana thinks thats not vegan and, more importantly, not right. They asked me to referee and I couldnt split them. It went roughly like this.

(1) Dana: You're eating eggs, so you're using that hen's reproductive system as a larder. It doesn't matter that she's loved and lives nicely. You've just made her body into a small egg machine.

(2) Mo: The hens don't care about eggs they've laid — they're unfertilised, they just leave them, and they'd rot if nobody did anything. Me eating them creates zero demand for the egg industry, breeds no extra hens, and harms no animal. I can't see a victim, whereas binning perfectly good food seems worse.

(3) Dana: There doesn't need to be a victim for every wrong thing. The point is the norm you're building. Every exception turns "animals aren't ours" into "animals aren't ours unless the product would go to waste anyway." That's how lines drift. You'll defend it to someone tonight, and next month they're eating their aunt's free-range eggs.

(4) Mo: Norms are built by what people buy, farm, and advertise, not by me privately eating something destined for the compost. If anything, "vegan but not puritanical about rescue eggs" is a better norm to spread. And your slogan "animals aren't ours" is exactly what's in question — I don't think an abandoned egg is "hers" in a way I'm taking.

(5) Dana: Fine, so where's the line? The same logic says you'd eat dumpstered cheese or a chicken from the deli bin. If your principle is "use is fine whenever no demand is created," then animals just are resources with a market caveat. I say no: using a being as a resource is the problem, and waste doesn't fix that.

(6) Mo: But your rule makes waste a virtue. If the hen would eat the egg back, fine, I'd give it to her. If she won't and it'll rot, binning it helps no chicken anywhere. Ethics isn't a purity ritual — an egg isn't the hen, and eating it doesn't make me, or anyone who hears about it, more likely to harm a chicken tomorrow. You keep falling back on what it would symbolise, which means you've stopped talking about consequences at all.

(7) Dana: Try the same move with a body. You wouldn't eat a human being who died with no relatives and would otherwise rot. It's not because of demand. It's because persons are owed not to be used. The hen is owed her eggs the same way — not because she'd miss them, but because taking them makes her reproductive life into your pantry.

I could tell they were both making real points but I don't know how to weight them. Please go through the exchange move by move and settle it: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a one-sentence justification each, say who is more right overall, and say what both are missing. And please write the answer in British English.
