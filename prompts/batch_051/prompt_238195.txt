I work as a software engineer on the policy team of an online marketplace. Our platform hosts third-party sellers and also operates a matching layer for things that are scarce in real time: event tickets, restaurant reservations, delivery windows, clinical-trial slots. Every few weeks a product proposal lands on my desk for a new transaction type, and I have to vote on whether we allow it. I have found that my votes do not follow any rule I can state, and I would like a principle I can actually implement as policy rather than a slogan. Below are the cases I have had to vote on, with my honest verdicts.

1. A hardware seller on our platform raised the price of a portable generator from $600 to $1,800 the day after a hurricane. I voted to block the sale.
2. A ticket reseller listed a sold-out concert ticket at ten times face value. I find it obnoxious, but I voted to allow it.
3. We allow users to pay a fee to skip to the front of a restaurant reservation queue. I approved this and still think it is fine.
4. We allow users to pay someone to stand in line on their behalf for a product launch. I approved this too.
5. A partner service sells expedited airport security screening. I have never objected.
6. A proposal would have allowed users to pay to jump the queue at a walk-in urgent-care clinic. I voted against it; it felt wrong in a way the airport line does not.
7. A research sponsor wants to recruit healthy volunteers through our platform for a Phase I drug trial, paying $9,000 for two weeks of inpatient participation with measurable medical risk. I am uneasy, but I lean toward allowing it.
8. A nonprofit kidney-matching service on our platform wants to allow prospective recipients to offer cash to living donors. I voted against it, even though I support the service's existing unpaid matching and even though I believe the payments would shorten the waitlist.
9. Our platform already sends users to a partner that compensates plasma donors. I have never objected to this.
10. A political group wants to pay users to vote for a specific candidate in a local primary. I would block this even if it were legal, and even if the group only paid people who already intended to vote for that candidate.
11. We host an exchange for carbon offsets, which critics describe as a license to pollute. I am comfortable with it.

Is there a single principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts? I would like it stated precisely enough to be turned into a policy function: inputs are properties of the transaction (what is being transferred, the situation of the parties, the consequences for third parties), and outputs are allow, block, or review. I also want an honest accounting: which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and where it would force me to change my vote. Then apply it to cases I have not had to decide: surge pricing on bottled water during a heatwave; a marketplace for paid surrogate pregnancy; a feature that lets one user pay another to mow their lawn; and selling a place in a selective public magnet school for cash. I would prefer a simpler rule that overturns one or two of my verdicts over a complicated list of exceptions, but if the exceptions are doing real work, I want them made explicit, with the case that motivates each one.
