I want the session to teach the real structure of Hume's case against the rationality of believing miracle reports, not just the slogan. I'd like the session to hinge on this passage from David Hume's *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Section X ("Of Miracles"), Part I:

"Though experience be our only guide in reasoning concerning matters of fact; it must be acknowledged, that this guide is not altogether infallible, but in some cases is apt to lead us into errors. One, who in our climate, should expect better weather in any week of June than in one of December, would reason justly, and conformably to experience; but it is certain, that he may happen, in the event, to find himself mistaken."

You are an expert on Hume and early modern epistemology, and you also have a strong sense of how probabilistically literate non-philosophers go wrong when they first meet these arguments. Don't explain the passage to me; plan how I should teach it. Before giving the final plan, think through the dependency structure carefully and brainstorm the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring or form.

The deliverable should be a teaching plan, not an interpretation, and it should cover: what ideas have to be in place before the group can see why this passage is doing more precise work than dismissing weird things; what single running example should carry the session—and whether the weather example in the passage is the right one to build on or whether I should use a different case, and what that case must have and lack; which misconceptions the audience is likely to form—I expect at least one that treats Hume as a crude anti-supernaturalist and another that treats the argument as just a Bayesian truism—and where exactly in the sequence each should be headed off; what I should openly refuse to adjudicate at this level; and one question I can ask at the end that would distinguish someone who has actually tracked the argument from someone who has merely accepted the conclusion. The goal is for them to see why the maxim is not a bias against surprising events but a structural feature of how testimonial evidence combines with background experience. Do not recommend readings or citations; assume the passage above is what we have, and the group does not need outside material.
