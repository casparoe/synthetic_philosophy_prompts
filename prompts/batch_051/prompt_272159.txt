I run a philosophy club at my high school, and a member recently presented an argument that seems to prove too much, but I cannot locate the mistake. He wants to defend the claim that a society with greater material wealth is morally better than an otherwise identical society with less, even when the extra wealth buys no identifiable good at present. His argument runs:

(1) If material prosperity has only instrumental value, then its value is exhausted by the goods it delivers: health, education, comfort, leisure, security, and the like.
(2) Therefore, if two societies are exactly equal in all of those delivered goods, any further difference in material wealth between them is morally irrelevant.
(3) Compare two societies equal in health, education, comfort, leisure, and security, but Society A has large additional reserves of material wealth while Society B has none.
(4) By (2), the two societies are morally equivalent; B is just as good a state of affairs as A.
(5) But that seems wrong: A's reserves make it more resilient to shocks, give its members more options, and let it meet needs that are not currently on anyone's list. A therefore seems like a better society, not merely an equally good one.
(6) So the extra wealth matters morally even though it buys no identifiable good at present; hence prosperity has some intrinsic worth, and (1) is false.

I would like to keep something like (1), because I find it strange to say that wealth matters independently of any difference it makes to anyone. But I also do not want to undervalue what is described in (5). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Is there a way to do justice to (5) without concluding that wealth itself has intrinsic moral worth?
