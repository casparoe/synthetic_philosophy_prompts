I am a novelist finishing a novel set in a secondary world governed by a temple-state, and I need help making a legal-religious institution in the novel behave with real consistency. The Court of the Veil is a tribunal that hears three kinds of cases: blasphemy, sacrilege, and offense against the sacred. In my world, blasphemy concerns contemptuous words or images, sacrilege concerns physical violation of consecrated persons, places, or things, and offense against the sacred concerns wounding the faithful by what one does or says. In an upcoming chapter, the Court must rule on a new case, and I want its ruling to follow from the Court's own earlier decisions rather than from what the plot requires. The Court's prior verdicts, all already fixed in the novel, are as follows.

1. The Shoe-Scribe. A calligrapher who was angry after a dispute with a temple official wrote the god's name in ink on the sole of his sandal and walked through the crowded noon market until the name had been scuffed into the dirt. The Court found him guilty of sacrilege and exiled him.

2. The Shipwrecked Sailor. A foreign sailor, the sole survivor of a wreck, spent a freezing night in a coastal shrine shed wrapped in a consecrated altar cloth, not knowing what the cloth was. He was alone; after learning the cloth's use, he reported himself. The Court imposed no penalty and required only a brief purification.

3. The Festival Comedian. A comic actor at the licensed midwinter festival, before a large crowd, performed a tale in which the god was a lecherous fool bested by a donkey. No consecrated object or place was touched, but devout families complained. The Court found him guilty of blasphemy, fined him heavily, and barred him from performing for a year.

4. The Theologian's Treatise. A respected elderly theologian, writing for the seminary, published a sober and devotional treatise arguing that the god had never walked as a man and that the body was only a garment worn by a force beyond all names. Many priests demanded prosecution. The Court imposed no penalty and issued a private warning against having the thesis preached to laypeople.

The new case is this. A celebrated sculptor has spent years carving a statue of the goddess as an old, weather-beaten woman kneeling to wash a child's feet. The work is sincerely devotional — witnesses say the sculptor prayed and fasted while carving — but it abandons the customary iconography of the enthroned, radiant goddess. He unveils the statue in the festival square before a large crowd. Some onlookers weep with devotion; a much larger group is scandalized; a shoving match breaks out. A group of priests now asks the Court to rule on whether the sculptor has committed blasphemy, sacrilege, or a punishable offense against the sacred.

Approach this as a philosopher of law and religion consulting on the manuscript. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the general rules that fit all four prior verdicts; do not settle on the first rule that occurs to you. Then set out: (a) the strongest candidate rules and what each one predicts for the statue case; (b) your recommended ruling if the Court is to stay consistent with its own decisions, including which written precedent is the closest analog and what principle the Court would be adopting with each possible ruling; and (c) one further case that would settle which candidate rule the Court is really following. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so plainly rather than pretending one ruling is forced. Please write your answer in American English.
