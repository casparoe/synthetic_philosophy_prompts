I'm working on a feature about a city council fight over a proposed package of automated public-safety technologies: acoustic gunshot detection, automatic license-plate readers, and a predictive dispatch system that shifts patrols among neighborhoods. The mayor's office and the vendors keep returning to a single comparison: these systems are just tools, like a hammer or a camera, and a tool has no politics of its own; responsibility lies entirely with the humans who decide how to use it. The coalition opposing the plan answers that some tools are not like hammers, because their design already settles moral and political questions—where the sensors go, what counts as a signal rather than noise, who is treated as a subject of suspicion—before anyone picks them up. My editor wants the piece to go beyond he-said-she-said, and I suspect the older philosophical question whether artifacts can have politics, and whether technology can determine outcomes rather than merely serve them, is doing the real work underneath the dispute.

Could you audit the tool analogy for me the way a philosopher would? I want to know what corresponds to what when we call a surveillance system a tool, where the mapping breaks down, and which features of an ordinary hand tool the analogy quietly imports into technology policy even when they do not belong there. Then I want the bottom line: does the neutrality conclusion actually survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is the analogy doing more rhetorical work than argumentative work? If some version of it still holds, tell me where; if it should be retired, tell me what comparison would do better.
