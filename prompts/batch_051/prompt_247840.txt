I have spent the past month thinking about a family dispute, of all things, and it has pulled me deep into the philosophy of speech acts. My brother left a voice memo intended only for himself in which he rehearsed, in the second person, the sentence "I promise I'll pay you back by Friday"; a transcription app misfiled it, surfaced it in our family group chat, and now my mother treats those words as a promise to her. He insists no speech act occurred because he was not addressing anyone; she insists the conventional words were produced and that settles the matter. Reading around in Austin and Searle has convinced me that this is one instance of a more general fault line: to what extent does a promise depend on the speaker's communicative intention, and to what extent on a standing social practice that makes certain forms count as undertakings regardless of what else the speaker has in mind?

I would like you to construct a thought experiment to a precise specification, for use in a term paper on promising that I am drafting. Work with two accounts, characterized stipulatively in the general spirit of Austin and Grice rather than as exegesis of either. On the convention-first account, a promise is made when a speaker in an appropriate context produces an utterance of a form that a standing social practice designates as sufficient for undertaking an obligation to the addressee, provided the practice's conditions on parties, form, and uptake are met; the speaker's further purposes matter only insofar as the practice incorporates them. On the intention-first account, no promise is made unless the speaker produces the utterance intending the addressee to recognize, at least partly through the utterance, that the speaker is thereby undertaking an obligation to them, and the speaker intends that recognition to be part of what constitutes the undertaking. I want a case in which the convention-first account yields "a promise was made" while the intention-first account yields "no promise was made" — the direction that is usually harder to construct, since the standard counterexample to conventionalism runs the other way, with a speaker and hearer who try to promise without any standing practice. Constraints: an ordinary non-legal setting (a workplace, an association, or a family), one paragraph, no theatrical, quoted, or other use of the kind Austin calls parasitic, since both accounts may reasonably suspend force there; the hearer must actually perceive the utterance and take it as an undertaking; the speaker must deliberately produce the words, so the divergence cannot be dissolved by blaming a misrouted file or some other accident; and the promised content should be something a normal person could be obligated to do. The scenario should be new rather than a tidied version of my voicemail anecdote.

After the scenario, walk through what each account says about it and why, identify the single feature of the case that is carrying the divergence, and say what changes if that feature is removed — does the case collapse back into the familiar no-practice case, or does it become one on which the two accounts agree? Then give the strongest reply available to a defender of the account that comes out looking counterintuitive, and close by separating what is settled about these two accounts from what is genuinely contested among people who work on speech acts; please do not present a contested exegetical claim about Austin or Grice as if it were the consensus view.
