I am a retired materials scientist, now reading moral philosophy and assisting a small fair-labour certification body with the occasional compliance review. A case has come to me that turns on the interpretation of a clause in that body's supplier code of conduct, and I would like your analysis as a philosopher of rules. The clause reads:

"Clause 4.2 (Exploitation). A supplier shall not exploit workers' vulnerability. Workers are exploited for the purposes of this clause where the supplier uses a worker's poverty, lack of alternative employment, or restricted freedom of movement to obtain labour on terms that undermine the worker's health, safety, or dignity. The fact that a worker consents, or that the worker would be worse off without the work, does not by itself cure a violation of this clause."

The clause was adopted in 2011, after a series of brick-and-mortar factory scandals, and its drafters had in mind supplier-controlled workplaces with fixed rosters and direct employment.

The case is as follows. A garment-finishing supplier in Da Nang operates a voluntary after-hours microtask platform. Workers receive notifications of small finishing jobs, accept however many they wish, are paid by the piece through mobile money, and are not rostered. The platform's logs show that over the past quarter, twelve workers accepted tasks in every rolling seven-day period for five consecutive weeks; each logged more than sixty-two hours of active task time per week, much of it between 23:00 and 05:00, when piece rates are doubled. The supplier can show that the workers were free to ignore the notifications, that several did, and that all twelve earned roughly twice what they could earn in their next-best alternative, full-time finishing at another local workshop. Independent interviews found no complaints; several workers described the platform as the best opportunity they had ever had. A workplace physician retained by the certification body reports that the sleep disruption alone materially undermines health.

The supplier maintains that Clause 4.2 cannot apply, because the clause targets supplier-controlled schedules and this arrangement is worker-directed and mutually beneficial. Our junior auditor maintains that it does apply, because consent and being better off are expressly not curative, and the health evidence is decisive.

I want you to answer two questions. First, does Clause 4.2 apply to this arrangement, and what should the certification body require of the supplier? Second, in reaching that conclusion, work through four approaches: (1) the plain words; (2) the clause's purpose; (3) what its 2011 drafters would most likely have said if asked about app-mediated piecework; and (4) what a reasonable compliance officer responsible for health outcomes would take the clause to mean. Then tell me which of these approaches should control, and why.

Return only a JSON object, with no prose before or after it, using this schema:

- "clause_applies": a boolean giving your overall determination.
- "readings": an array of four objects, each with:
  - "approach": one of "plain_words", "purpose", "drafter_intent", "reasonable_reader";
  - "decision": one of "applies", "does_not_apply", "indeterminate";
  - "reasoning": two to four sentences for that approach.
- "controlling_approach": the name of the approach that should control, followed by a one- or two-sentence justification.
- "required_remedy": a one- to three-sentence statement of what the certification body should require of the supplier if the clause applies.
- "residual_uncertainty": the single factual or definitional matter that should be clarified first if the supplier appeals.
