I'm an economist who works on welfare measurement and health-state valuation. My working toolkit treats wellbeing as something that can, at least in principle, be placed on a single cardinal scale: utility functions, willingness-to-pay and compensating variation, social welfare functionals, separable aggregation across persons. I keep running into philosophical claims about pleasure and pain that seem to reject exactly that assumption, and I want to know how much of the disagreement is real and how much is a difference in vocabulary.

Here are three positions about the relation between pleasure and pain:

(a) Single-scale commensurability (the classical utilitarian picture): pleasure and pain are quantities of one underlying magnitude, net welfare, so any finite pain can in principle be compensated by a sufficiently large pleasure.

(b) Lexical threshold negative utilitarianism: suffering at or above a threshold of intensity has lexical priority over pleasure — no amount of pleasure, of any kind or for any number of people, can justify or compensate for one instance of suffering at or above the threshold — while below the threshold, ordinary finite trade-offs between smaller pains and pleasures still hold.

(c) Strong incommensurability: pleasure and pain are not quantities of one magnitude at all, and cannot properly be traded against each other at any finite ratio, even below any threshold; they are evaluated on separate scales.

Please restate each position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of economic theory and decision theory — preference orderings, lexicographic orders, the Archimedean and continuity axioms, representability by real-valued utility functions, separability and interpersonal aggregation, indifference curves and marginal rates of substitution, incomplete preferences. Use the technical vocabulary straight, not a watered-down version.

Then give me a residue report. After the translation, what is left over in each position that my vocabulary cannot say, or can only say by distorting it? Be specific about (i) whether treating the lexical threshold as a lexicographic preference captures the normative claim that the trade-off may not be made, or only captures the choice behavior; (ii) how the philosopher's claim about commensurability of value maps onto the economist's operationalization of commensurability through compensation and choice, and where those operationalizations may beg the question; (iii) why a moral threshold defined by intensity of suffering sits awkwardly in a framework built from marginal rates of substitution, and whether my aggregation tools can even state the difference between a threshold that binds per experience, per person, or per population; and (iv) whether the strong incommensurability position becomes an incomplete preference ordering in translation, and what is lost in that move. End with a verdict: is the residue evidence that my framework is missing something it ought to be able to represent, or that the philosophical positions are less precise than they look?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating the question.
