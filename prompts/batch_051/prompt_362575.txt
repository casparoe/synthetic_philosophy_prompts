I'm in a phil of language course and we've been doing speech acts for a couple weeks, and I keep getting tangled up on one thing. The standard line we got is that performatives like "I promise to pay you back" or "I now pronounce you married" aren't true or false; they do something, provided the felicity conditions are in place. Fine. But then we also talk about a promise being sincere or insincere, an apology being genuine or hollow, a bet being serious or just theater. Doesn't calling an utterance sincere already treat it as a kind of report about the speaker's inner state, which would make it true or false after all? If I say "I promise" while fully intending not to keep it, is the utterance false, or is it a perfectly good promise that happens to be dishonest?

And what about cases where the act doesn't come off—betting on a race that already finished, or a judge pronouncing sentence in the wrong courtroom? We don't say those were false; we say they were void, or that nothing was done. So maybe truth and falsity are just the wrong axis for the whole category, and I'm smuggling them back in every time I use the word "sincere." I'd like someone to untangle what exactly is being evaluated when we call a performative sincere, successful, appropriate, or void, and whether the original constative/performative distinction is doing more work than I'm giving it credit for.
