I accept the lesson usually drawn from Bertrand's paradox: the principle of indifference cannot serve as a general rule for assigning equal prior probabilities, because the same question — how likely a randomly chosen chord of a circle is to be longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle — receives 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 depending on whether one uniformizes over endpoints on the circumference, a point along a radius, or the chord's midpoint in the disc. Tell me what would have to be true for this lesson to be wrong or overstated. I want the candidate defeaters sorted from decisive to merely awkward: that indifference survives once restricted to a well-behaved algebra of genuinely symmetric alternatives; that a symmetry or transformation-invariance constraint singles out one correct parametrization and thereby dissolves the paradox; that the paradox shows only that indifference needs an explicit background model, not that no principle of its kind can be stated; and any other live reply you take seriously. For each, say whether it is a live position rather than a merely logical possibility, and what it would restore for the project of objective prior probabilities. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
