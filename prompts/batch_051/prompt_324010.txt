Last night I had a long argument with a friend about total utilitarianism, and I still cannot settle which of us gave the better account. I have long been attracted to the view because it offers a definite, completely general procedure: count every person’s utility equally, sum across all affected, and choose the action that maximizes the total. The impartiality, the absence of an arbitrary baseline, and the way it extends cleanly to decisions about future generations all strike me as genuine theoretical virtues. But my friend kept pressing a version of Robert Nozick’s utility monster from *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*. As he presented it, imagine a being who derives vastly more utility from any given resource than ordinary humans do. Every unit of food, shelter, attention, or freedom given to it produces an enormous surplus of well-being compared with what the same unit would produce for anyone else. If total utilitarianism is correct, he argued, then the view seems to require us to direct more and more resources toward the monster, perhaps eventually requiring ordinary people to sacrifice nearly everything for it, because each sacrifice yields a greater total utility than the cost to the one making it. I found that conclusion as repellent as he did, but I could not decide whether it is a genuine problem for the theory or merely a vivid case designed to trigger intuitions that do not track anything morally relevant. I said the monster is wildly unlikely; he replied that a theory of value should not need to hope such a being never exists. I said a correct theory may have astonishing implications in distant possible worlds; he replied that the issue is not the distant possibility but what the theory says about the structure of moral claims here: a being’s greater capacity for satisfaction should not amount to a license to consume others. I do not want a verdict that simply picks a side. I want to know what it would cost to treat the firm intuition that we may not sacrifice everyone to the monster as a debiasing target, what it would cost to amend or abandon total utilitarianism, and whether there is a reading of equal consideration and impartiality on which the aggregative theory and that intuition are compatible. In particular, if the right response is to constrain the maximizing requirement with a deontic limit or a distributive principle, explain whether that is a revision of the theory or a clarification of what its impartiality was always committed to. Also, say how confident you are in the claims you make and single out the point you are least sure of.
