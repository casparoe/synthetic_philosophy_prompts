Me and my friend spent way too long last night arguing about whether one of us should post a public warning about a guy in our extended social circle. A few of us have heard sketchy stories about him, but the evidence is messy and mostly secondhand. If the stories are true, warning people would protect a lot of folks; if they're wrong or exagerated, we'd be ruining an innocent person's reputation and there'd be no way to undo it. Any one of us could post the warning on our own, and our group chat is seperate enough that nobody's really in charge of the decision.

My friend's whole position was: each of us should look at the evidence and just do what we honestly think is right. If that means one person posts it, so be it — that's just conscience. He also kept saying that if even one of us has good enough evidence, the fact that the rest of us are unsure doesn't make the action wrong. My worry, which I couldn't articulate, is that if we all do that, the person with the most panicked or credulous read of the evidence ends up making the decision for everyone, and the cautious people's judgment counts for nothing.

Afterward someone told me this has a name — the unilateralist's curse — and that it's a known pattern in decision theory, not just me being paranoid. So: please describe the most important implications of the unilateralist's curse for how I should handle situations like this, where any one person in a group can act on their own and the rest of us can't undo it. I'm especially interested in what it implies about my friend's "just follow your conscience" line, and whether there's a better rule we could agree on as a group. Please write in American English.
