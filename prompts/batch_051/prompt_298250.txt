Write me a two-voice poem about a split-brain patient doing that classic chicken-claw / snow-scene task. In case you don't know it: the right hemisphere sees a snow scene and the left hand points at a shovel; the left hemisphere sees a chicken claw and the right hand points at a chicken. When the patient is asked why the left hand went for the shovel, the speaking left hemisphere, which never saw the snow, invents a tidy story about the shovel being for cleaning out the chicken shed.

The form: fifteen lines, no two-column trick. Twelve alternating lines, six in each voice, then a closing three-line stanza in the left hemisphere's voice alone. Left-hemisphere voice: grammatical, breezy, completely sure of itself, fond of because. Right-hemisphere voice: no syntax to speak of, just nouns and small images, clipped. The two voices should share a rhyme scheme across the alternation so they sound like they are finishing each other's sentences, but the closing stanza should force a rhyme that does a bit of violence to the right voice's final image. The poem's turn should be the moment the reader realises the two voices have never once reffered to the same scene. Get the lateralisation and the confabulaton right enough that a philosopher of mind wouldn't wince, and make sure the left hemisphere's final story is a genuine misreading of what the right hemisphere pointed at, not just a vague gloss. Oh, and don't be too tidy at the end: leave open whether there's one person in that skull or two.
