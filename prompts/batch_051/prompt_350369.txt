Over the past few months I have been helping a small group write safety documentation for autonomous systems — a hospital delivery cart, a floor-cleaning robot, that kind of thing — and we keep running into the same wall. Everyone agrees the machine should not cause side effects, but when we try to state that as a checkable requirement, the only formulations anyone can offer are variations on: after the task, leave the environment as close as possible to how it would have been without the machine, or minimize any changes that are not part of the explicit goal. That sounds neutral, but the more I look at it, the more it seems to be doing moral work behind the scenes, because words like close and part of the goal quietly assume a standard for which changes matter. I would like a single worked example that makes the problem hard to ignore, so I can bring it to the next meeting and show why our current wording is not yet doing what we think it is doing.

Construct a thought experiment in which a measure of that leave-the-world-as-close-to-baseline kind rates an AI as having been low-impact, even well-behaved, while an ordinary onlooker would say the AI caused a serious side effect that any reasonable definition of avoid side effects should have caught. Constraints: a near-term, mundane setting, such as a home, an office, a hospital floor, or a warehouse; a narrow AI with no general intelligence and no perfect world model; and the measure must be the kind of thoughtful version an engineer would actually propose, not a straw man. Its failure should come from a structural feature of trying to define side effects without any account of value, not from a careless implementation.
