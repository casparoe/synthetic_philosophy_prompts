For the philosophy-and-technology club I help run at my high school — around fourteen students, meeting for a single seventy-five-minute block once a month — I would like a runnable simulation on the question of whether the profit motive is the root cause of AI risk. I have read one side arguing that AI labs cut safety corners because they are answerable to shareholders and must maximize returns, and the other side arguing that labs would race just the same under state planning, worker cooperatives, or any arrangement with multiple teams competing to deploy first, because the decisive pressure comes from competition rather than from profit specifically; in other words, whether capitalism is the root cause or just one instance of a more general competitive dynamic. I do not want to settle this with a lecture, and I do not want the club to rehearse positions we have already heard. Could you design a game that makes the members run into the problem through their own choices before the debrief names the competing explanations? The main thing I want members to come away seeing is why even well-intentioned groups might cut safety corners, and whether that pressure weakens when profit is removed.

The group should be split into small teams acting as AI labs, with one or two additional roles such as a safety auditor, a customer or procurement office, and a central planning board. Please use a point system rather than real money. Where possible, build in two versions of the same round: one in which labs earn points through a market, and one in which a central planner awards points to whichever lab first meets performance milestones, so that we can compare how much safety testing gets skipped when profit is present and when it is absent. If you think a different structure tests the question more fairly, describe that structure and why you would use it. Keep the two conditions as close as possible except for the source of the reward.

The entire meeting lasts seventy-five minutes, so please include a concrete timeline covering setup, rules, the rounds, and the debrief. In the debrief, map specific decisions or failures in the game onto the underlying ideas — for instance, the cost of delaying deployment for extra safety testing, the advantage that accrues to whichever lab cuts corners first, and the way that pattern can continue even when every lab would prefer a world in which nobody cuts them. The debrief should also say what will predictably go wrong when I run this, and how I should handle it. Finally, please be explicit about what the simulation cannot reproduce about real AI risk, and which part of the argument I will have to supply afterward in remarks.
