I've been arguing with a friend about population ethics, and I've found that my gut reactions to particular comparisons are firmer than any principle I can state. I'd like you to reverse-engineer the principle from my verdicts: tell me what view of population axiology, if anything, captures all of them, or else tell me which of my verdicts I should overturn. Assume a welfare scale on which +10 is an excellent life, 0 is a life neither worth living nor not worth living, +0.1 is a life only barely worth living, and negative numbers are lives not worth living. Ignore nonhuman animals, extinction risk, and the identities of the people involved unless a case forces the issue. I know the stakes: this is the neighborhood of the repugnant conclusion, and my instincts are anti-repugnant, yet several of my own verdicts seem to pull me toward it.

Here are the verdicts. (1) I prefer a future with 10 million people at +10 to a future with 50 billion people at +0.1; I would not trade the first for the second, even though the second contains vastly more total welfare. (2) Between a future with 10 million at +10 and a future with those same 10 million at +10 plus an additional 5 million at +5, I judge the second at least as good, probably better. (3) Between a future with 10 million at +10 and one with those same 10 million at +10 plus an additional 5 billion at +0.1, I also judge the second no worse; I cannot see how adding lives worth living makes a future worse when no one is harmed. (4) Adding 100,000 lives at -1 to the 10 million at +10 clearly makes the future worse. (5) Between 10 million at +10 and 20 million at +6, I judge the second better, though I am less confident than in the other cases.

I can already feel the strain: verdicts 2 and 3 seem to commit me to harmless additions, and a chain of such additions appears to lead from the small flourishing future to a future I rejected in verdict 1. So I want an honest diagnosis. Is there one principle — some form of totalism, averagism, a critical-level view, a person-affecting view, or something else — that delivers verdicts 1 through 5? If there is, state it precisely and show how it handles each case, including which feature of each case does the work. If there is not, tell me which verdict or verdicts the simplest acceptable principle forces me to give up, and why. Finally, tell me what your preferred principle says about a case I cannot settle: comparing a future with 10 million at +10 and 5 billion at +0.1 against a future with the same number of people but the first group at +7 and the larger group at +1. Is the second better than the first?
