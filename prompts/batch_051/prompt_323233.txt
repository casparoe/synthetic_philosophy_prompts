I keep flip-flopping on how to define life. My working criteria: it's a self-sustaining chemical system that keeps itself far from equilibrium, has a boundary, reproduces, and evolves by natural selection (heritable variation plus differential survival). Now consider a lytic virus mid-infection: the free virion outside a cell looks dead on all four counts — no metabolism, no self-maintenance, just nucleic acid in a protein shell — but once it infects a host cell it turns that cell into a virocell, rerouting the host's metabolsim to churn out new virions while the viral genome mutates and gets selected across rounds of infection. So the virocell metabolizes, is bounded by the host membrane, reproduces by making virions, and evolves. Is the virus alive only while it has a virocell? Is the virocell the actually living thing, with the virion as a kind of spore? Go through my four criteria for the virion, the virocell, and the whole infection cycle, and flag every point where the verdict turns on a reading of "self-sustaining" or "reproduces" I haven't pinned down. If the problem is my whole frame — that "life" attaches to objects instead of to processes, or that my criteria already smuggle in an answer — say that plainly rather than forcing a verdict.
