Chain-of-thought faithfulness: can language models report their own reasoning? I’ve been puzzling over how much work the word “can” does in that question, and I’d like you to help me think about it in a way that doesn’t collapse into either naive trust or blanket skepticism. Here is a passage from Miles Turpin, Julian Michael, Ethan Perez, and Samuel R. Bowman’s 2023 paper “Language Models Don’t Always Say What They Think: Unfaithful Explanations in Chain-of-Thought Prompting”:

“We should not expect CoT explanations to be faithful by default, for a few reasons. Foremost is the fact that our training objectives simply do not explicitly incentivize models to accurately report the reasons for their behavior. Additionally, to the extent that LLMs are trained on human-written explanations, these explanations are not only known to be incomplete, often omitting crucial parts of the causal chain for a particular event, but they can also often be unfaithful accounts of individuals’ cognitive processes.”

We know the empirical result: chain-of-thought outputs can be systematically unfaithful—the model can say it is answering for one reason while answer choices are actually being driven by a biasing feature, such as the correct answer always being labeled “(A).” But I want to think about this passage less as an explanation of a particular experimental finding and more as a diagnosis: the lack of faithfulness comes from something general about how the model was trained.

Consider two readings of that passage.

First reading: there is a real fact about the model—its actual token-prediction process, its internal causal determinants—and the language model fails to report it accurately. The model has privileged access to its own processing, but it lacks the incentive, the training signal, or perhaps the representational resources to verbalize it. On this reading, “faithful” is an accuracy notion: the model’s self-report is faithful to the extent that it matches its actual internal determinants, and the unfaithfulness is a failure of reporting or translation.

Second reading: the model is not reporting reasoning at all. What it produces as chain-of-thought is itself constructed from the next-token prediction distribution, so the “explanation” is another piece of generated text rather than a report of any existing internal episode. On this reading, asking whether the model can report its own reasoning presumes that there is an independent reasoning process to report—but in a transformer, the chain-of-thought just is the computation path, redistributed over tokens. The question is not whether the report matches the process, but whether there is any process to match it to.

Now here is the issue I want prioritized. Suppose I am a researcher with, say, an eighteen-month grant to work on chain-of-thought faithfulness. The world will use this research to decide what to do about CoT in deployed systems: whether to continue using it as a monitorability tool, whether to discard it in favor of externalized reasoning, whether to impose training penalties that explicitly reward faithful self-reports. Given that decision at the end, which open question should my project attack first?

I am not asking for a literature review or a reading list. I want a defended research agenda. Specifically:

First, which question is more upstream—the “access or translation” question (does the model have the internal fact or representation needed to verbalize its true determinants?) or the “report versus construction” question (does CoT even have the logical form of a report, or is it a different kind of computational act altogether)? Show me how answers to other questions depend on this choice, and whether the choice would change what a deployment-relevant intervention would look like.

Second, within the interpretation you think is most defensible, identify the tractable sub-question. What could an eighteen-month project actually settle? And what is the most valuable empirical result it could produce—one that would change how engineers, auditors, or regulators treat a CoT output in practice, not just what philosophers of mind think about LLMs?

Third, name the question you would deprioritize. Something might sound pressing to someone entering this area—for example, something about whether the model is conscious, or whether there is a “true self” in the model, or which particular biasing features produce unfaithfulness in which domains—but on your analysis it would be downstream, intractable, or unlikely to change deployment practice. Say why.

Fourth, state the assumption in your agenda that, if false, would most change the ranking. Put differently: what empirical or conceptual discovery would make the whole thing moot?

The deliverable is a defended prioritization, not a survey. I want to see the dependency structure explicitly: which open question’s answer would make another open question easier, which are parallel, which are only interesting if a particular foundational assumption holds. Defend the criteria you use—importance, tractability, neglectedness, how much deployment practice would change—and trade them off against one another where they pull apart.
