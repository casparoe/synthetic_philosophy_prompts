Before I call a deposit "ritual," I require that it display several of the following five features, which I treat as signs rather than individually necessary conditions: (1) prodigal expenditure — material, labor, or skill beyond any obvious practical return; (2) invariant repetition — the same sequence of operations repeated with little variation across generations; (3) spatial separation — the activity occurs away from the places where the objects were made and used; (4) deliberate arrangement — objects placed or sorted so that the pattern seems to refer beyond itself; and (5) renunciation of utility — usable or valuable objects broken, buried, or left unrecycled.

Now consider a hypothetical deposit excavated at a small settlement devoted to pottery production. Behind a bank of kilns, excavators find a masonry room containing thirty shallow basins, one built above another over roughly two hundred years. Each basin holds hundreds of cups from the same workshop, all of them smashed. The cups are unusually thin-walled and carefully burnished, but none shows scratching, staining, or other traces of use. Every cup was pierced through its base with a smooth-edged hole before firing, and the breakage is deliberate rather than accidental. The sherds in each basin are graded by size into concentric rings, coarsest at the edge and finest at the center, and at the exact center of every basin sits a single intact miniature cup, one to two centimeters high, too small to hold more than a sip. The settlement's domestic quarter contains intact, heavily used cups of the same type, but no basins, no size-sorting, and no miniatures.

Is this basin deposit evidence of ritual, on my criteria? If you conclude the criteria cannot settle the case, say what additional evidence would settle it and whether "ritual" is carrying its weight as an inference from objects.
