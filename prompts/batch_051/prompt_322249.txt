I'm in a small reading group on classical Indian epistemology, and after our session on Mīmāṃsā two of the members got into an argument that I'd love to have someone actually referee. For background: Mīmāṃsā holds that the Veda is authorless (apauruṣeya) and that cognitions are intrinsically valid (svataḥ prāmāṇya)—you're entitled to treat what a source gives you as true unless you identify a specific defect in that source, rather than having to wait for outside confirmation. That's what lets them say the Veda needs no author to vouch for it, while a human report can be defeated if you learn the speaker is deceiving you or confused. After the session, Anya said this principle has a modern payoff: if an AI assistant gives you a factual answer, you should by default accept it unless you can point to a specific reason to doubt this particular answer or this model on this topic. Rahul said that's the exact opposite of the Mīmāṃsā view of testimony: the whole point of the authorless Veda is that no human intention can contaminate it, whereas an AI system has designers, a profit-seeking company, and training data produced by millions of flawed people—so there's already a standing defect, and the default should be skepticism. Anya replied that "has a maker somewhere in its causal history" is too blunt a criterion; by that standard even the Veda would be compromised, since it's transmitted through human teachers. The relevant question, she said, is whether the output is someone's assertion made with a purpose about which we can ask "whose interest does this serve?" A particular chatbot answer isn't anyone's assertion in that sense—it's emitted statistically—so it may be closer to Veda-like speech than to ordinary testimony: the only "defect" is stochastic error, not a speaker trying to mislead. Rahul answered that not being a lie is not the same as being authorless. Training data gets curated, reward models get tuned, and the outputs are shaped by a company that wants you to keep using the product. He said calling an LLM authorless because no single human chose the exact words is like calling a committee report anonymous because no one member wrote every clause—and Mīmāṃsā never made mere anonymity a route to authority; it made uncreatedness the criterion.
