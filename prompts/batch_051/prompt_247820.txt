I've been working through the no-trade theorems in the economics of information, and I've ended up defending a stronger epistemological claim than I expected to. The core result, as I understand it, is that two rational agents with a common prior should not be willing to make a purely speculative bet against each other merely because they hold different private information: each should pause and ask what the other's willingness to trade reveals. From this I've drawn a broader view that I now want stress-tested. The view is this: in a well-functioning market, the volume of purely speculative trade is best read as a measure of epistemic failure — of non-common priors, of bounded rationality, or of traders overtrusting their own information — and persistent speculative volume is therefore a reason to distrust the cheerful story that market prices simply aggregate dispersed information. On my view, the price is only "what the market knows" under assumptions strong enough to make most speculative trade disappear.

I don't want the usual objections; I want a map of my exposure. Tell me what would count against this position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Are there actual findings about trading volume around earnings announcements, about prediction markets, or about speculative bubbles that should embarrass the view?
