I've come to hold a fairly strong position about naked statistical evidence: that it is rightly treated as insufficient to prove a particular fact in legal proceedings, even when, taken alone, it would clear the relevant standard of proof — including proof beyond reasonable doubt in a criminal case. By naked I mean evidence whose probative force runs entirely through the frequency of some feature in a reference class, and that says nothing further about the party it is offered against. The familiar hypotheticals will do: the blue bus case, in which the defendant operates 80 percent of the buses on the route where a hit-and-run bus injured the plaintiff; and the gatecrasher case, in which the only evidence against one attendee of a rodeo of a thousand is that 501 entered without paying. I take the gatecrasher's just-over-half probability to be inessential: a 95-percent version would be defective in exactly the same way, just with a higher base rate. I think the law's refusal to find for the plaintiff on the bare statistics is not a pragmatic compromise or a jury-management heuristic, but tracks something about the concept of proof itself — that a verdict needs a basis connecting the evidence to the particular defendant in the way knowledge, or at least knowledge-grade justification, requires, and that a base rate alone never provides one.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this position, not a defense of it. The view leans on a cluster of distinctions — individualizing versus merely statistical evidence, evidence that tracks the defendant versus evidence that merely classifies, causal or explanatory connections, sensitivity and safety, and the anti-lottery thought that the odds alone never warrant believing that this ticket lost. I want to know which of these distinctions is doing the real work, because if the load-bearing one fails, the whole position fails with it. Are there actual or hypothetical cases that should force a defender of the strong view to give ground — for example, market-share liability, DNA evidence offered through a random-match probability, a reference class so tightly fitted to the individual defendant that the category of statistical evidence starts to lapse, or a reformed system that openly convicts on naked statistics and is not obviously an epistemic disaster? Which of these are merely awkward and which would be fatal? And, separately, which of them would a committed defender of the position have to concede counts against it, as opposed to points only a critic would press?

I'd also like your own verdict on whether the position risks being insulated past the point of testability. The move of re-describing every recalcitrant case as not naked after all, or as really individualizing once properly described, is always available, and I want to know whether deploying it makes the view safer or merely emptier. If the view turns out to be well insulated, tell me whether that is a problem for a view of this kind — a view about the norms of legal fact-finding, which may be prescriptive through and through. Be committed throughout: take definite positions on which distinctions carry the weight, which objections are live, and whether the strong reading ultimately survives honest scrutiny, rather than laying out a menu of possibilities for me to choose from.
