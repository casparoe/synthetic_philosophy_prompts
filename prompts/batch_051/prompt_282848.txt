I have a working rule for when I will call an action courageous, and I have noticed that it shapes who I admire and how I interpret everything from news stories to history. The rule has three conditions: the person must actually feel fear; the risk must be to physical safety — life, limb, or serious bodily harm — rather than to reputation, livelihood, or social standing; and the act must be for someone else's sake rather than for status or self-image. By this standard, a firefighter entering a burning building is courageous, and a soldier holding a position under fire is courageous, but a student who reports a violent hazing incident at the cost of being socially frozen out is not quite courageous — I would call him honest, principled, or brave, but not courageous. I apply the same test to a person who endures a long illness without complaint: they may be resilient, but there is no chosen risk of physical harm, so I do not call it courage.

I know this rule sounds like a leftover from a culture that treated courage as a martial virtue, demonstrated mainly in battle, and that in a peaceful society most serious threats are social, professional, or institutional. Is my policy defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as a definition? What, if anything, does the physical-risk condition actually track — perhaps that courage requires a cost the agent cannot afterward recover by apologizing, resigning, or changing jobs? Could the rule be restated to keep that insight while admitting civilian instances such as the hazing reporter, without making courage a synonym for ordinary risk-taking? Under what circumstances would my strict rule be a reasonable shortcut, and at what point does it stop protecting a real distinction and start protecting a romantic picture of physical bravery?
