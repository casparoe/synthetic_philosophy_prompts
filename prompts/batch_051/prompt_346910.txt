I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of mathematics, and I've been tying myself in knots over what should count as a proof. For a chapter I'm writing I've been working with four criteria: a proof (a) starts from explicitly stated axioms, definitions, or previously established results; (b) each step is licensed by an explicitly stated rule of inference; (c) the diagram never does justificatory work; and (d) the conclusion follows necessarily. Now take the first proof in Euclid's Elements—the construction of an equilateral triangle on a given segment AB. Draw a circle with center A and radius AB, and another with center B and radius BA, then just take a point C where the two circles intersect. Then AC and BC each equal AB, so the triangle is equilateral. The last bit is fine. But the move where you take C to exist is not something Euclid's stated postulates and common notions by themselves actually guarantee; he's in effect letting the picture vouch for the existence of C. So by my four criteria, the founding proof of the Western mathematical tradition isn't a proof. That conclusion feels like an anachronism—Euclid would be puzzled by the objection, and nobody thinks Book I Proposition 1 is a mistake that needed fixing. Go through the criteria one by one and say whether the construction meets each of them. Where it fails, tell me whether that's a strike against the proof or against my criteria, and whether the right response is to say that proofhood is context-relative, or that Euclid's proof counts as a proof only relative to a later patched-up axiomitization. And is there a difference I should be drawing between "the proof has a gap" and "it's not a proof at all"?
