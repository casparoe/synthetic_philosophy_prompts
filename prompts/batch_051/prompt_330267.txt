A weird one for you. My state's inheritance tax law has this clause:

"There is levied a tax on every transfer of property located in this State, whether by will, intestate succession, or otherwise, from a decedent to any person other than the decedent's spouse or a recognized charity. The tax attaches when the right to receive the property becomes fixed and indefeasible."

The statute's preamble says the point is to keep enormous fortunes from compounding across generations and to stop children's starting positions from being fixed by their parents' money—dynastic wealth and equal opportunity, basically.

Here's the case. A tech founder died (legally dead) and had his body cryonically preserved. His will put basically everything—stock, crypto, the house—into an irrevocable trust. The trustee has to pay the preservation bills indefinitely, invest the rest as aggressively as the rules allow, and, if revival ever becomes possible, transfer the whole remaining corpus back to him once he's alive again. If two doctors ever declare revival impossible, the money goes to a private foundation controlled by his children. The family's lawyer says no tax is due, because no "person" ever got a fixed and indefeasible right: the trustee is only a manager, the revived decedent does not yet exist and may never exist, and the foundation's interest is contingent. The state tax people say death is death, a trust counts as a person, and this is just laundering a dynasty through a freezer.

I don't much care who wins as a practical matter. I want to know how a clause written in the seventies is even supposed to decide a case its drafters couldn't have imagined. Walk it through the ways of reading a rule—the words, the purpose, what the drafters would say if they'd been asked, what a reasonable reader would understand the law to mean—and tell me which approach should settle it, and whether any of them yields a principled answer rather than just blessing whatever conclusion someone started with.
