I teach a seminar on philosophical method, and my students tend to assume the evidential hierarchy is obvious: a vivid intuition about a particular case always beats an abstract principle, so if a theory gives the right verdicts case by case but violates transitivity or offends parsimony, the principle is what must be revised. I want to hand them a written dialogue that shows this assumption is itself a contested position about what counts as evidence in philosophy.

Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers who disagree on exactly this point. One maintains that considered judgments about concrete cases are the basic evidence, and that principles such as transitivity or restrictions on ad hoc hypotheses are only useful summaries of those judgments, to be abandoned whenever they misclassify a case. The other maintains that such principles carry independent evidential weight, that a theory's failure to respect transitivity or its resort to baroque complications is itself a datum against the theory, and that under sufficient pressure from a strong principle we should sometimes revise or set aside the case-intuition instead. The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth rather than talking past: each should address the other's strongest version of the position, neither should be a strawman, and each should have to refine or concede something under pressure. Anchor the disagreement in a concrete running example of your own devising — perhaps three options whose pairwise betterness judgments are each compelling in isolation but jointly form a cycle, or a theory that fits every case only by piling up independent epicycles — and let the two of them work that case until it becomes clear where their deeper disagreement about evidence actually lies. It is fine if no one wins; the point is to show the dispute is real and not settled by the obvious facts.
