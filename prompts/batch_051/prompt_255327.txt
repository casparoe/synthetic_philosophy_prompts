I have no formal training in philosophy, but for a few years I have been thinking on my own about how to assess envy and resentment, especially when they show up in political life. I have written down the position I currently hold, and I would like someone to check whether it hangs together. I am not asking for an introduction to the topic; I am asking for an audit of these commitments.

My current commitments are these:

(1) Envy, understood strictly as being pained by another person's good because it is theirs, is a vice. It is distinct from wanting the same good for myself, or from preferring to be better off, neither of which is necessarily vicious.

(2) Resentment is a different kind of thing: it is a moral emotion, a response to a wrong done to me. A person who could not feel resentment when genuinely wronged would be lacking in self-respect, not admirably free of bitterness.

(3) What decides whether a feeling is envy or resentment is not its inward character but whether the advantage it responds to is actually unjust. If someone burns at a neighbor's success and there is no injustice behind that success, the feeling is envy, even if the person sincerely describes it as resentment at unfairness.

(4) I also accept something like Rawls's idea of excusable envy: when the gap between the worse-off and the better-off is so wide that it damages the self-respect of those at the bottom, their envy can be excusable — we should not blame them for it — even if the arrangements that produced the gap are not unjust.

(5) But excusable is not justified. Excusable envy is still a vice and still a defect of character, even where the person deserves sympathy rather than blame.

(6) Given (4), a decent society should be arranged so that people rarely have occasion for excusable envy. Reducing the conditions that predictably produce it is a legitimate aim of institutions, alongside the aim of reducing injustice, though I regard the second aim as more fundamental.

I cannot tell whether this is a stable package or whether I am quietly contradicting myself. Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look that way, how severe each real tension is and what would have to give to resolve it, and which single revision would do the most to make the set coherent. Where you draw on the scholarly debate, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
