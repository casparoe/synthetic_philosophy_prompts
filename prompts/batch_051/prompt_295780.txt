I am trying to settle what I actually think about what it is to be rationally committed by a deductive inference, and I want to know what principle—or what small set of candidate principles—captures my verdicts on the following cases, even if the principle forces me to give one of them up.

Here are the cases, with the verdicts I am currently stuck with.

1. A colleague believes it will rain this afternoon, and she believes that if it rains this afternoon, the match will be called off. When I ask her whether the match will be called off, she says she has no view. I find this a genuine rational failing on her part, not a mere gap in her information: the inference is a single step, and she holds beliefs that already entail the conclusion.

2. A student accepts the usual axioms of arithmetic and is shown a correct two-hundred-line proof of some theorem. She declines to accept the theorem because she cannot hold the whole proof in view at once. I do not think she is irrational in the way the colleague is; I would call her cautious, or out of her depth, but not incoherent.

3. In the familiar tortoise exchange, the tortoise accepts P, and accepts if P then Q. He also accepts that if P and if P then Q, then Q. Still he declines Q, and asks why this new conditional does not count as a further premise he must accept before reaching Q. My reaction is that no number of conditionals of this kind would change the situation: the missing ingredient was never a further premise.

4. Someone believes P, believes if P then Q, and also believes not-Q. I want to say logic requires him not to hold all three together, but that it does not by itself single out which of the three to abandon. The "must" here is a demand for coherence, not an instruction to revise a specific belief.

5. Another person accepts P, accepts if P then Q, and even endorses the rule: whenever I believe P and believe if P then Q, I shall accept Q. I still cannot shake the thought that the rule, like any sentence he accepts, can be accepted without being applied, and that applying it is something he does rather than something the rule itself brings about. But if I say that too quickly, I seem to be denying that anyone can ever be forced to accept a conclusion—which is exactly what I affirmed in the first case.

A tentative principle I keep reaching for is this: a rule of inference is not another premise; it sits in a different register—it is what one does with premises, not an item among them. That handles the tortoise, but it seems to make following a rule a bare act that can be withheld at will, which brings me straight back to case 1.

I would like you to do three things. First, state precisely the account—or the small set of candidate accounts—that best captures these verdicts, and say which case each component of the account is doing work for. Second, apply the account to a case I have not listed: someone who accepts the premises of an inference in a logic they have never studied, and declines the conclusion because they cannot see that the rule is valid; does your account say they are rationally at fault, and in which sense of "must" is the conclusion required of them? Third, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the account cannot accommodate, and whether that should make me drop the verdict or complicate the account.
