I'm writing a term paper on whether physics could have a final theory, and whether we'd be in a position to know we'd found one if we had. Before I draft, I want a consistency check on my own view, because I keep flip-flopping in the literature review and I suspect my commitments don't all cohere, but I can't locate where the friction is. You're a professional philosopher of physics, so please think carefully and start by actually mapping the pairwise tensions rather than giving me a vibe check.

Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I'm a physicalist: there's one fundamental stuff and one fundamental dynamics, so a "final theory" in the sense of a true, exceptionless, complete fundamental physics is at least possible — not guaranteed, but possible.
(2) I also find the pessimistic meta-induction convincing: every past theory in physics has turned out to be false or limited outside some domain, so the smart money says no theory anyone can currently articulate is final.
(3) But I don't think that rules out our ever knowing a final theory. I'd count a theory as final if it has no free parameters, generates the Standard Model parameters from first principles, and can be shown to be the unique mathematically consistent quantum theory of gravity. Under those conditions I think we'd be justified in calling it final, even before testing all its predictions.
(4) At the same time, I think empirical success is doing the real epistemic work. If a theory nails every measurement we can make, across every energy scale accessible in principle, then I think we'd know — we'd have enough evidence to be certain in the ordinary scientific sense.
(5) Yet I also think underdetermination bites here: for any theory with an empirical signature, a rival with the same predictions but a different ontology can be constructed. So empirically equivelent rivals are always available, which makes me doubt that empirical success alone could single out a final theory.
(6) I reject the idea that a final theory has to be "beautiful" or elegant in an aesthetic sense; but I do think theoretical virtues like algorithmic simplicity and absence of ad hoc parameters are truth-conducive, not just pragmatic.
(7) Finally, I don't think a final fundamental physics would make chemistry, biology, or psychology obsolete; those higher-level descriptions would remain real and explanatory, even though they're grounded in the fundamental dynamics.

I'd like an audit: which pairs are in genuine tension, which pairs only look like they are, and how severe each tension is. Rank them rather than listing everything as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix is. Also tell me where your own verdict is least secure — what argument or consideration would change your mind about which of my commitments has to go.
