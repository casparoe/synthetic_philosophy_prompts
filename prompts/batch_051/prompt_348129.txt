A friend and I have been arguing about the Fermi paradox for years, and I've started to suspect the reason nobody moves is that the three standard positions don't actually share a question. The first position points out that life got going on Earth almost as soon as the surface could support it, so the origin of life can't be the hard part; the real bottleneck has to be later — the enormous gap before complex cells, or the fluke of intelligence, or the brevity of technological civilizations. The second retorts that you can't infer any of that from a single planet, because the planet that produced the beings asking the question will always look, from the inside, like it got life early. The third treats the silence itself as the evidence and concludes the bottleneck is ahead of us. Every few years the exoplanet people announce a new estimate for how common Earth-sized planets are, and each camp just absorbs the number into its own story; the same goes for the admittedly patchy null results from SETI. I keep coming back to the thought that this is really a fight about anthropic reasoning and about how much you may update from absence, not a fight about astrobiology. But I might be wrong, and I don't want to just assume the people I disagree with are confused. What kind of disagreement is this, exactly — about priors, about selection effects, about standards of evidence, about what counts as an explanation for silence? And if you can diagnose it, what would actually count as progress: a new observation, a formal result, a cleaner distinction, or should we expect the stalemate to persist?
