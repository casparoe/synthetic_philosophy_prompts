I keep having this argument with my housemate, who's doing a decision theory unit and thinks I'm being incoherent. i basically maximise expected value in the rest of my life — careers, donations, even medical decisions get decided by rough expected-good-per-unit-of-effort — so I'm not hostile to the idea that small probabilities matter. But I have a firm personal rule that I never let a probability below one in a billion move me, no matter how large the payoff is meant to be. The classic Pascal's mugging move, a one-in-a-trillion chance of infinite (or astronomically large) utility, leaves me cold, and I think that's the correct response, because any number that small is basically a made-up number. Nobody is actually calibrated at one in a trillion; it's just a story someone is telling, and the 'infinity' does all the work. My housemate says I can't have it both ways: if I'm an expected-value maximiser, then a tiny probability times a big enough payoff can dwarf ordinary options, and my cutoff is just fanaticism in reverse, and anyway it's arbitrary — why one in a billion and not one in ten billion?

The thing that confuses me is that I don't apply the rule consistently. The other week a charity claimed a one-in-ten-billion chance of preventing a catastrophe that would wipe out a huge number of future lives, and I dismissed it out of hand, exactly as my rule says. But I do take seriously a medication with a one-in-a-hundred-thousand chance of a serious side effect, and I'd think carefully about a one-in-a-thousand risk on a long drive. So I'm not opposed to small numbers as such; I'm opposed to the ones that smell like thought experiments. I want to know whether this habit is defensible or just a bias dressed up as hard-headedness. Is there a principled version of it — some way to think about how small is too small, or a sensible way of discounting reports of tiny probabilities — rather than either treating every near-zero number as real or zeroing them all out? And how would I tell from the inside when the rule is protecting me from bad forecasts rather than protecting me from uncomfortable conclusions?
