In a running debate with a relative of mine — a thoughtful lay Catholic who reads the Sermon on the Mount as a program rather than an ideal — he has repeatedly compressed the whole ethics of forgiveness into a single analogy: being wronged is like having someone contract a debt to you, and forgiving is like cancelling that debt. On his account the wrongdoer owes the victim, the victim is sole creditor, and a creditor may release a debt gratuitously, without waiting for the debtor to become contrite or even to acknowledge the debt; and when the debt is simply unpayable — he has in mind someone who cannot undo the damage or make restitution, and perhaps cannot even manage a coherent apology — continuing to insist on collection begins to look like extracting from the destitute. So he draws two conclusions: forgiveness can be perfectly unconditional, and sometimes it is actually obligatory, not merely supererogatory.

I want an audit of this analogy rather than a verdict on forgiveness in general. Lay out the correspondence table first: what maps to the creditor, the debtor, the principal, the interest, the repayment, the books, the act of release. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and be ruthless about it: which features of debt are quietly being imported into forgiveness — e.g., that what is owed is fungible or quantifiable, that a third party could pay it, that the claim can be transferred, that the victim has standing to release it unilaterally — and which of those imported features are actually carrying the two conclusions above. For each conclusion, say whether it survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether the analogy was doing the work or merely illustrating a position that has to be argued for on other grounds. If the debt picture ultimately misleads, say what a better analogy would be, and what it would and would not license about unconditional and obligatory forgiveness.
