You are an expert on Dostoevsky and on designing experiential philosophy activities. I run a college reading group, 14 students, one 75-minute session, that has just finished the Grand Inquisitor chapter of The Brothers Karamazov. Most of them understood the argument but didn't find it compelling. Design a game or structured deliberation that makes them feel the pull of the Inquisitor's claim that ordinary people cannot bear the burden of free choice and would rather hand over there own decisions to a competent authority in exchange for security, predictability, and someone to take responsibility. The activity shouldn't be a debate about the text, and you shouldn't mention the Inquisitor until the debrief. Start by brainstorming two or three possible mechanics, then commit to one and give the rules, roles, and debrief for a 75-minute session. In the debrief, map specific moments in the activity to the three temptations—miracle, mystery, and authority—and make clear what resistance at each point costs. Also be honest about which part of the Inquisitor's position the activity can't reproduce and what the facilitator has to supply. I want a committed design, not a menu of options: choose the mechanic you think actually works and defend it against the obvious alternatives.
