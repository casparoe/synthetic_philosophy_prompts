My lecturer in a philosophy of mind module summarised the argument from illusion and the disjunctivist response like this. A stick half in water looks bent, and a hallucination of a dagger can be subjectively indistinguishable from seeing a real one. In both sorts of case what you're directly aware of isn't a mind-independent object, and since an experience could be just like that without the world being that way, what we're directly aware of in every perceptual experience must be a mental sense-datum rather than the world itself. Disjunctivists reject this by saying veridical perception and hallucination are entirely different kinds of state that merely happen to feel the same, so illusions and hallucinations tell us nothing about what happens in genuine perception. But then, the usual objections go, disjunctivism makes veridical perception infallible — when you really are perceiving, you could never be mistaken about what's in front of you — and it leaves you with no way to tell perception from illusion, so it collapses into scepticism. I suspect this is a bit of a caricature, especially the infallibility claim, but I can't work out exactly where it goes wrong. Which parts accurately capture the argument from illusion and what disjunctivists actually claim, which parts are misrepresentations, and do the two closing objections survive once the view is stated properly?
