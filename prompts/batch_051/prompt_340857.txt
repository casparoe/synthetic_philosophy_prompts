After a long career in condensed-matter physics, much of which involved studying how systems negotiate energy landscapes and settle into local minima, I have turned in retirement to philosophy of biology. The position I have settled on, and would like you to test, is this: the comparison between natural selection and gradient descent is not a loose metaphor but a structural claim. In what I take to be its core regime—large populations, small-effect heritable variation, a reasonably stable environment, and fitnesses that depend primarily on the organism rather than on the composition of the population—natural selection is a myopic hill-climber on a high-dimensional fitness landscape. If that is right, the familiar failure modes of gradient-based optimization (local minima, saddle points, sensitivity to step size and initialization, absence of foresight, inability to take in the whole landscape at once) are the correct first-pass explanation of why evolution produces kludges, frozen accidents, and path-dependent dead ends, and of why it resembles a stupid optimizer rather than an engineer. Conversely, I regard the features that plausibly let evolution escape some of these failure modes—recombination, neutral drift, changing environments, population structure—as corrections to the gradient picture rather than reasons to abandon it, and I suspect they have under-exploited counterparts in the design of optimization algorithms.

I am aware of the standard disanalogies. Allele-frequency change need not ascend the gradient of any scalar quantity once fitness depends on the composition of the population; recombination and large-effect mutations are not locally infinitesimal moves; development and plasticity alter the mapping between genotype and phenotype; and drift can push a population downhill. I have tended to treat these as qualifications that delimit the core regime rather than as refutations, and I take the serious rival to be the view that natural selection is not usefully described as optimizing anything at all, even approximately. What I want is an honest map of how exposed my position actually is. What would have to be true for the gradient picture to be wrong, not merely incomplete? Please sort the candidate defeaters—formal results, empirical cases, conceptual arguments—from fatal to merely awkward, and say which of them a committed defender should concede count against the view. Tell me also what the gradient picture predicts that the no-optimization rival does not. The evidence that would move me most would be either a demonstration that a biologically realistic evolutionary process reliably finds solutions that a gradient-based search with comparable resources cannot, where the difference traces to a mechanism with no gradient-descent counterpart, or a formal result showing that even in the core regime the dynamics is not gradient-like in any defensible sense. Finally, I want an explicit verdict on whether, once all the standard caveats are granted, the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability—and, if so, whether that is a defect in a claim of this structural kind or a tolerable feature. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
