I cover technology and the ideas underneath it for a living, and over the years I've accreted a set of opinions about what you're allowed to do with a creative or technical work after the person or company behind it has vanished. I'm trying to figure out whether there's one principle underneath all of them, or whether I've just got vibes. Here are my verdicts.

1. A lone developer made a niche text expansion app ten years ago, then died; no company, no heirs, no contact. Someone on a forum posts an updated build that keeps it running on a modern OS. I say fine. Actually, thank god for the forum person.

2. A novelist died in the 90s and nobody can locate an estate. A fan scans the out-of-print second novel and uploads it. I also say fine, though I'm a little less comfortable than in the app case and I can't fully say why.

3. A 70s band broke up, the label dissolved, and nobody knows who holds the rights to the master. A fan presses 300 vinyl copies of a live tape and sells them for roughly cost. I say fine.

4. An ex-employee of a defunct game studio recreates the studio's unreleased game from its design documents and his own memory, polishes it up, and sells it for a profit. I say not fine, even though the underlying rights seem just as abandoned as the app in case 1. That pair bugs me.

5. A glossy magazine publishes an old photo it found on a stock forum with no credit, after about five minutes of trying to find the photographer. I say not fine. The photographer might be alive and merely bad at search results. But I have no evidence they are.

6. A university library holds the only copy of an unpublished manuscript by a poet who died in the 1960s and whose heirs can't be located. A young scholar wants to publish it with annotations. I say fine, but only if royalties go into escrow in case someone surfaces later with a claim.

7. An AI company scrapes tens of thousands of abandoned personal blogs (people who died, moved on, or just stopped responding) and sells subscriptions to a chatbot trained on them. I find this much worse then the forum app case, even though I can't point to a single owner who is more wronged than the dead developer.

My working principle has been something like: when the owner is genuinely out of reach, the work belongs to the people who care about it, as long as they aren't strip-mining it. But that's a metaphor, not a rule. Is there a coherent account of when "nobody owns it anymore, use it" is and isn't acceptable that captures my verdicts? Which of my verdicts would that account force me to drop? And what would it say about a local museum that digitizes and sells prints of an orphaned photo archive it houses but does not own?
