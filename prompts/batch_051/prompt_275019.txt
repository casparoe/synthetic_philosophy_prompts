In arguments I keep having about contested terms — "woman," "racism," "health," "addiction" — someone will eventually say "words have meanings" as though that settles whether a word is being used correctly or stretched beyond what it can mean. I want to know what that sentence is actually doing. Write me a memo that lays out the distinct precise theses "words have meanings" could express, ordered from the least to the most demanding. For each thesis, state it as a principle (not just a paraphrase of the slogan), and say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or a false or misleading one, with the consideration or example that shows which it is. The readings should include at least: (a) that words are not meaningless, since uses of a word exhibit regular communicative effects; (b) that a word can be used incorrectly relative to a linguistic community's practice; (c) that each word has a determinate standing meaning shared by competent speakers; (d) that a word's standing meaning is fixed by something prior to or independent of its actual use — a definition, an essence, an original etymology; (e) that a word has a single legitimate sense, so extended, figurative, or deviant uses are misuses; and (f) that semantic change is a deviation from how the word really means. Then answer the question I actually need: which of these readings is the slogan doing work as in the arguments where it gets used to shut down a contested or extended use, and does that reading survive? Along the way, distinguish the things the slogan runs together: a word's semantic range versus a supposed single core; polysemy versus ambiguity versus vagueness; standing meaning versus speaker meaning on an occasion; and a dispute over what a word means versus a dispute over what to do with the term. Write the answer in American English.
