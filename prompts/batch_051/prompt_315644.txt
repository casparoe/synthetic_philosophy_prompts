Next month I defend a version of the dark forest hypothesis at a physics and astronomy department colloquium, and I want to be prepared for the questions this particular audience will actually ask. My thesis is that the absence of detected extraterrestrial intelligence is best explained not by the rarity of life or of technological civilization, but by a selection effect: under deep uncertainty about other civilizations' capabilities and intentions, technological civilizations either adopt radio silence and keep their thermal emissions low or are destroyed, because broadcasting invites a risk of preemptive attack that swamps the expected benefit of contact. My paper has two moving parts: a game-theoretic model in which silence is an equilibrium across a wide range of priors about hostility, and the claim that this model accounts for our null search results better than rare-life or Great Filter explanations do.

The room will be mostly exoplanet observers and stellar astrophysicists, plus two theorists who do Bayesian model comparison, a planetary scientist working on biosignature detection, and a philosopher of science who studies theory choice and epistemic virtues. They are not hostile to Fermi-paradox work, but they are wary of just-so stories: they will want to know what the hypothesis rules out, which parameters do the work, and whether it makes any prediction that could actually surprise them.

Put together a Q&A prep sheet for that room: the eight or so objections I should expect, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is — not the objections I would meet in an online rationalist forum or among science-fiction fans of the idea. For each, say who in the room is most likely to raise it, put the objection in the words that person would actually use, give me a two-minute reply, mark where that reply is soft, and tell me what to concede and what to hold. End with the question I most hope nobody asks.
