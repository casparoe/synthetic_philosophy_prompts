I'm putting together a small reading group on the moral psychology of self-conscious emotions, and the session everyone keeps postponing is the one on guilt versus shame. The two organizers disagree so productively that I want to render their exchange as a text. Please write a dialogue between them.

One organizer is a moral psychologist with cross-cultural training who defends shame as the better moral emotion; the other is a philosopher of a broadly Kantian temper who holds that guilt is superior. The shame-defender should begin from the thought that shame tracks how one actually stands in the eyes of others, so it keeps moral motivation tied to the communities and relationships that make demands concrete, whereas guilt pretends to be a private tribunal but is really internalized social control with the audience hidden. They should be willing to concede that shame is audience-dependent and can be enlisted to enforce hierarchy, and should try to turn even that into an argument rather than a mere concession. The guilt-defender should begin from the thought that shame is unreliable because it responds to who is watching rather than to what was done, and that it drives concealment and self-abasement rather than repair; guilt, by contrast, holds the agent answerable to standards the agent can endorse, which is a form of respect. But the guilt-defender must not treat shame as mere concern with others' opinions; they should engage the claim that selves are socially constituted. Both should treat the familiar empirical formula that shame targets the whole self while guilt targets a particular act as a datum to be interrogated, not as the end of the discussion.

The dialogue must be a real back-and-forth in which each responds to the other's strongest point, concedes what needs conceding, and is forced into sharper formulations rather than repeating a position. Both must remain equally competent throughout; neither is a strawman. Around a third of the exchange should turn to the second question: even granting that one emotion is better, can a culture choose to cultivate it—through child-rearing, legal institutions, and patterns of mutual accountability—or are emotional regimes too deep, too slowly shaped, and too entangled with material institutions to be chosen at will? Do not let either side collapse; the exchange should end with the disagreement in a cleaner and more precise form than it began, not with a winner. The deliverable should be the dialogue itself, not a preface or a critical commentary on it.
