I was reading Locke on the imperfection of words, the bit in Book III where he says that the same word can stand for one idea here and a different one there, and that even in a conversation the sound might be the same while the thought underneath is doing something else. It left me with an argument I can't shake, and I need someone to tell me whether I'm fooling myself. It goes like this: (1) Nearly every word in ordinary speech can be used to pick out more than one thing, so the same expression can carry different senses depending on pitch, tone, and the surrounding situation. (2) Because of that, a sentence doesn't have one determinate meaning on its own; it only gets fixed once we somehow already know the speaker's intended sense. (3) But what reaches me is the string of words, not the speaker's insides. (4) So the hearer is never actually given anything with a determinate meaning. (5) So language shouldn't be able to give us public, shared meanings at all. And yet obviously it does. Obviously there's a gap, and my hunch is that the move from (1) to (2) treats "possible" like "actual" in a way a proper logician wouldn't allow. But I'm not sure that's right, and I'm also not sure the real problem isn't in (4). Walk me through it: is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and what's a corrected version of that step that still takes Locke's observation seriously? I'd rather not get a survey of the history of the ambiguity debate, just a close diagnosis. And please just write this as continuous prose, the way you'd explain it in an email.
