I spent my working life as a physical chemist building models where the line between a computation you can actually finish and one you can't was mostly a matter of whether you'd still be alive when it terminated. Since I retired I've been reading around in philosophy of computation, and I keep noticing that people use "easy" and "hard" to mean completely different things, then talk past one another for entire evenings. My local reading group—mostly retired engineers and scientists—has asked me to put together a short quiz for a session on the philosophical upshot of computational complexity. I don't want a quiz about which labels people like; I want one that sorts people by what they say about concrete cases, the way a personality test does.

Here's what I'd like. You're an expert on the philosophy of computational complexity, and I'd like you to write me a "which position do you actually hold?" quiz, eight to ten questions. Each question should be a described case, not a request for a definition, and definately not a question about whether someone likes the phrase "complexity class." Please build the instrument from the logical space rather than from the list of names: before you write a single question, brainstorm the axes on which the positions genuinely come apart, then design questions that diagnose those axes, then map answer patterns onto leaves. The leaves I want included are roughly: (a) complexity realism, according to which complexity classes carve computational reality at the joints and P vs NP is a discovery about the world; (b) an epistemic-limits view, on which easy-to-check-but-hard-to-find marks a real boundary on what agents can know or understand; (c) pragmatism about tractability, on which hard/easy is relative to resources, heuristics, and practice, so worst-case complexity is only a rough idealization; and (d) a deflationary view that P vs NP has no deep philosophical import at all. Please flag any pair of positions your questions can't cleanly separate, and note unoccupied combinations of the axes if any turn up—is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just underexplored?

Some cases I specifically want used, because they're the ones that split the group last time: (i) a problem that's O(n^100) in theory but hopeless for n > 10, versus one that's exponential with a tiny constant and therefore fine for every input we'd ever actually meet—which of them is "really" easier; (ii) the fact that modern SAT solvers chew through industrial instances with millions of variables even though SAT is NP-complete in the worst case; (iii) a valid proof that is short to check but whose discovery would require a search no currently known algorithm can do in subexponential time—does the person who finds it know something the rest of us can at best verify; (iv) two problems that are polynomial-time reducible to each other yet feel completely different, like a scheduling problem and a graph-coloring problem, where the class boundary seems not to care which representation we find natural. Give each question two or three answer options, and end with a scoring key that maps the patterns onto the leaves. Since the group is scientists rather than philosophers, write in plain language and don't ask the reader for citations or reading lists; everything should be answerable from the case itself. And think carefully before you commit to the scoring key—the whole point is that some of us will hold a mix that no single leaf captures, and the quiz should say so honestly rather then force the fit.
