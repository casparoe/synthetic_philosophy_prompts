I'm putting together a one-page statement of my views on distributive justice for a seminar, and I'd like a consistency check before I send it around. Here's where I've landed:

1. Equality is fundamentally about what people can actually do and be — capabilities in Sen and Nussbaum's sense — not about bundles of resources or about how satisfied they feel. The standard line that a wheelchair user needs more resources than I do to have the same real options seems obviously right to me, so resource egalitarianism is out.

2. I also think people should bear the costs of their own choices. If someone has deliberately cultivated expensive tastes — trained themselves to only enjoy things that cost a fortune — society doesn't owe them extra when that makes them miserable or poor. Unchosen circumstances are a different matter entirely.

3. I think there's a deep moral difference between disadvantages traceable to unchosen facts (a disability you're born with, lousy parents, the country you happened to be born in) and disadvantages traceable to option luck, like gambling away your savings or starting a business that flops. The first demand redress. The second I'm inclined to leave alone.

4. When we measure how well a society is doing, I think we should look at whether people have the genuine freedom to achieve a list of basic functionings — being adequately nourished, moving around, appearing in public without shame, participating in community life — not at total preference-satisfaction. A society where everyone can do these basic things but some people are vastly richer doesn't strike me as the problem.

5. Methodologically, I trust case verdicts more than I trust big principles. If a principle gives me an absurd result in a concrete case, I'll tweak the principle, not the case.

Which of these are actually in tension with each other, and which just look like that? Rank the tensions by severity and say what the cheapest fix is for each one. I honestly can't see where the weak spot is, so be specific.
