In a seminar on value theory, a colleague of mine defended a view he called tranquilism: the only thing that is intrinsically valuable—valuable for its own sake rather than merely as a means—is the absence of craving and suffering. On this view, achievement, knowledge, relationships, freedom, and even positive pleasures matter only derivatively, insofar as they produce or obstruct a state in which one is neither pained nor driven by wanting. That state, and only that state, is good in itself. The position struck me as having a strange interaction with a familiar thought experiment.

Consider a modified version of Nozick's experience machine. Suppose the machine does not offer a simulated life of pleasure or accomplishment. Instead, it directly induces and reliably sustains a genuine, lucid, and wakeful state of complete equanimity. The person connected to it experiences no pain, fear, boredom, grief, or longing, ceases to feel the pull of prior attachments, and forms no new projects or curiosities. This state is not produced by insight, effort, or any transformation of character; it is a reversible effect of the machine, fully documented and safe. If someone is unplugged after decades, their earlier desires, anxieties, and sorrows return undiminished, along with the ordinary pains of ageing. While connected, the person really is tranquil and has no desire to leave.

Please work through what a strict tranquilist should conclude about whether to plug in, and compare that with what would be said by a negative hedonist (who treats the absence of pain as the sole intrinsic good) and by someone moved by the familiar objections Nozick raised against the original machine: that we want actually to do things rather than merely have the experience of doing them, that we want to be a certain kind of person, and that we do not want to live in a world of human construction cut off from deeper reality. Is this a case in which tranquilism straightforwardly recommends plugging in, and do Nozick's objections lose their force once the machine offers, not pleasant experiences, but the very condition the view takes to be supremely valuable? Does it matter that the calm is causally maintained and reversible rather than the result of genuinely extirpating the sources of craving? Does it matter that the decision to plug in must be made while craving is still present? Please also separate what is broadly settled in the relevant philosophical discussion from what remains genuinely contested, and avoid presenting a reconstructed or minority interpretation as the established consensus.
