Some decisions — call them transformative — are both hard to reverse and likely to change the person making them so that the later self judges the choice differently from how the earlier self could have anticipated: permanent emigration, marriage, taking religious vows, abandoning a settled career to found a company, or undergoing a medical transition. Suppose a national government, prompted by this difficulty, adopts the following mechanism. Any adult may buy from a state-chartered clearinghouse a conditional reversal option for one specified major commitment. The option is priced from a national risk pool rather than individually underwritten, and it guarantees a predefined reversal package if the holder returns between eighteen months and five years after the commitment and signs a declaration that they now judge the original decision to have been wrong. No evidence of mistake, coercion, or changed circumstances is required. For emigration, the package covers return travel, temporary housing, and a resettlement grant; for marriage, it pays the legal costs of divorce and two years of limited spousal support; for religious vows, it covers exit costs and vocational retraining; for a business, it funds an orderly wind-down and a year of partial income support; for medical transition, it funds reversal procedures where those exist. No one is required to buy an option, but anyone who makes one of the specified commitments without having bought an option at least six months earlier becomes ineligible for certain public assistance if that commitment collapses within the five-year window.

Treat this as a mechanism to be analyzed, not as a moral position. How would it actually behave? In particular: what does it incentivize among people deciding whether to make a major commitment, and among people who have already made one; who would select into buying options and who would be unable to; how would it be gamed by people who buy options for commitments they never intend to keep, or by partners, employers, creditors, or communities that learn of the option; what secondary markets or intermediaries would appear; and how would the mere existence of a priced exit option change the character of the commitment itself? End by identifying the two or three failure modes most likely to dominate in practice, and explain whether each could be fixed by changing eligibility, timing, pricing, or the contents of the reversal package.
