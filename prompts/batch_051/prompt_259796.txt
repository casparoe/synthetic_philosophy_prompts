I have been following the dispute over whether signing up for cryonic preservation is a rational bet, and after several years of reading exchanges I am struck less by the individual arguments than by the fact that neither side appears to have moved. The two camps agree on a surprising amount. Both grant that current technology cannot revive anyone; both grant that no demonstration has established that revival will ever be possible; both grant that the financial cost of membership and insurance is real and could be spent elsewhere; and both grant that, if revival did succeed, the payoff would be enormous in the form of additional conscious life. From this shared starting point, one side routinely concludes that signing up is a reasonable expected-value gamble, while the other routinely concludes that it is an expensive fantasy.

The proponent's argument is roughly this. A probability of success need not be high to justify the bet; it need only be high enough that, multiplied by the enormous value of the payoff, it yields an expected value exceeding the certain cost. Calling the odds long does not settle the question, because the rational question is not whether revival is likely but whether the gamble's expected value is positive. On this view, refusing to sign up resembles declining a cheap ticket in a lottery that pays a fortune, merely because the odds are poor; the asymmetry between a modest certain loss and a vast possible gain does the real work.

The critic's argument is that the probability here is not the kind of small but well-behaved quantity that expected-value reasoning can use. To estimate a chance of success one must chain together a long series of conditions: that the preservation process does not destroy whatever information memory and personal identity depend on; that a revival technology is eventually developed; that the institution holding the bodies survives for decades or centuries; that future people will regard revival as worth attempting rather than as a questionable use of resources; and indefinitely many further conditions of the same kind. Each link is uncertain, and the number of links is not fixed by anything in the evidence. The critic concludes that any numerical probability one assigns is largely an artifact of where one stopped listing failure modes, and that the wager reduces to the pattern sometimes called Pascal's mugging: a tiny and arbitrarily adjustable chance of an enormous payoff is allowed to dominate the decision because the decision rule places no limit on how far the product of probability and value may run.

My working hypothesis is that this is not, at bottom, an empirical disagreement about preservation quality or institutional reliability, and not a disagreement about the value of extra life. It is a disagreement about the scope of expected-value reasoning itself. Both camps seem to accept expected-value maximization in ordinary, repeated cases where frequencies or stable base rates exist. What they divide over is whether a decision rule that is mandatory in those cases is also permissible, or even meaningful, in a one-shot case where there is no reference class from which to read off a probability, where the probabilities are dominated by modeling choices rather than by observed frequencies, and where the conclusion flips under small changes in an arbitrary stopping point. On this diagnosis, the real question is whether low-probability, high-payoff bets with no base rates fall inside or outside the domain in which maximizing expected value is the correct standard of rationality.

Is this diagnosis correct? If it is not, what kind of disagreement is this actually: a hidden empirical question, a difference about how much to discount the far future, conflicting starting intuitions about what counts as a rational bet, or something else? And given the diagnosis you defend, is there anything that would count as progress here—a decision-theoretic theorem, a sharper account of admissible priors, a distinction between kinds of uncertainty, or a reason to conclude that the impasse is expected and that neither side can rationally require the other to move?
