I've been working through the Humean and anti-Humean literature on laws of nature, and I keep finding that the debate already presupposes a working notion of what a law of nature is—one I don't have and couldn't apply to an arbitrary candidate generalization. What I want from you is a working definition of law of nature, precise enough to put any given generalization in the law column or the non-law column, even if it then needs repair.

Here are the cases it should sort. I take the clear cases to be settled: Newton's law of universal gravitation and the conservation of energy are laws; the claim that all the coins currently in my pocket are copper and the claim that no U.S. president has ever been a woman (so far) are not laws. The hard cases are these:

- The claim that no sphere of gold anywhere has a diameter greater than one kilometer. Presumably true, but it seems to reflect how much gold happens to be distributed through the universe rather than how the universe is constrained.
- Kepler's laws of planetary motion. Mathematically precise, long called laws, but they describe a restricted class of gravitational systems and arguably follow from Newtonian gravitation together with contingent features of our solar system.
- The claim that all electrons have the same charge, approximately 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs in magnitude. This sounds like a law, but it also sounds like part of what it is to be an electron, in which case it may be a definition rather than a law.
- The ideal gas law, PV = nRT. Universally taught as a law, yet strictly false for real gases and correct only in a limiting regime.
- The second law of thermodynamics. It has a statistical force that Newton's laws seem to lack, and small violations are in principle possible.
- The claim that mammals, as a biological kind, have hearts. If true, it seems to record a contingent evolutionary lineage rather than a fundamental constraint on the world.
- The claim that no signal can travel faster than light. This is often treated as a prohibition rather than a description, and that may make it a different kind of law than the others.
- The claim that all emeralds are green, set against the Goodman-style rival that all emeralds are green before some future date and blue afterwards. The rival is consistent with the same observations, but only one of the two feels lawlike.

I suspect the term is doing at least two jobs: one descriptive, marking compendious regularities, and one explanatory or governing, marking what constrains how things can unfold. If you agree, give me the two definitions and say which of the hard cases move between them. If you disagree, give me one definition and defend the surprising verdicts it forces. Either way, run your definition or definitions against every case, flag any place where they give the wrong answer or no answer, and revise or defend accordingly.
