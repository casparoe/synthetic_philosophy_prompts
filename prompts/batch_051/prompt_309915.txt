You are a philosopher of mind who works on AI interpretability. I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so please keep the philosophical machinery explicit and do not take terms like reasoning or report for granted.

Consider a position I will call the transparency argument for chain-of-thought: when a language model is prompted to produce intermediate reasoning steps before its final answer, those steps are a reasonably faithful report of the model's actual reasoning — roughly the considerations that caused the answer — and chain-of-thought therefore gives us a window into the model's cognitive process rather than a post hoc story. On this view, asking a model to think step by step is like asking a person to think out loud, and the main risk is incompleteness or ordinary error, not systematic fabrication.

Now consider a specific development that seems to put pressure on that argument: the shift from eliciting chain-of-thought from a pretrained model, where the model is merely continuing text in the style of reasoning, to training regimes that optimize the whole generation — intermediate steps included — against final-answer correctness. Under such regimes the readable chain-of-thought is rewarded only indirectly, through the answer it leads to. There are also systems that perform part of their reasoning in a latent, non-verbal form before the readable chain-of-thought is produced. In both cases, the text is selected for producing good downstream answers, not for tracking the computation that actually produced them.

Does the transparency argument survive this development? I want you to identify exactly which premise is under pressure. Is it the assumption that there is a separable actual reasoning process that the text could report? The assumption that optimizing for the final answer also optimizes the intermediate text toward truthfulness? Or something else?
