At our department's ethics reading group, two of my fellow PhD students, Mara and Julian, got into an argument about what adult children owe their parents. The exchange ran roughly as follows.

Julian argued that adult children owe their parents nothing merely because they were raised or because their parents made sacrifices. Benefits one never requested may make gratitude appropriate, he said, but they do not by themselves create a debt; whatever grown children owe must stem from the present relationship rather than from a moral ledger of past care.

Mara replied that this rested on a one-sided picture of consent. The parent–child relationship is not chosen on either side: parents unilaterally create deep dependence by bringing a child into the world and deciding to raise it. If the parent can acquire special obligations through that unilateral role, she argued, it cannot be ruled out that the child likewise acquires special obligations, at least when the parent is in genuine need.

Julian pushed back by invoking a version of Jane English's friendship model. Once the child is an adult, he claimed, filial relations are best modeled on friendship: duties arise from continuing mutuality and affection, not from repayment for past sacrifices. Talk of debt makes filial obligation rest on a transaction the child never agreed to, and once the relationship has gone cold there is nothing left to ground a duty.

Mara answered that friendship is voluntary on both sides, and its symmetry is part of its point, whereas the parent–child tie is asymmetric and unchosen. Treating the two alike lets a child's transient coldness dissolve a duty precisely when the parent is most dependent. One can owe care without feeling affection now, she added, just as one can owe a creditor one happens to dislike.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the four moves, say who is right about which specific point and whether the move overstates or misdescribes the other side; then say which position is more defensible overall, and identify anything the two of them are both missing.
