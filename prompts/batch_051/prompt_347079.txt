I'm a novelist, so I spend an unreasonable amount of time deciding what my characters believe and when they're wrong. A few months back I read a bit about deflationary theories of truth, and since then I've been following a rule in my own life: I don't let the phrase "is true" do any real work. If someone says, "It's true that the money was wired on Tuesday," I mentally translate that into "the money was wired on Tuesday," and I respond to that. If someone says a whole story is true, I ask them to give me the sentences, and then I check those one by one. My working idea has been that "true" is just a convenient way of endorsing a sentence without repeating it, and that treating it as a real property makes people imagine some deep fact out there doing the representing. So far this rule has felt like an antidote to vagueness.

But I've started noticing it collapses in certain cases. Last week my agent told me, "Whatever the accountant said about the advance is true," and I couldn't get past the word "true." I couldn't translate it into the accountant's sentence because I wasn't there and didn't know what had been said. I also thought of sentences like "The first thing you say at the trial will be true," which I can't reduce to any sentence in front of me. So now I wonder whether I've just adopted a shortcut that only works when the original sentence is already sitting in view, and dressed that up as a philosophical position. Is there a version of this deflationary habit that handles "whatever she said is true" or "the thing you're about to say is true" without quietly admitting that truth is a property after all? And from the inside, how would I know whether I've found a sensible policy about a word or just a stylistic tic that flatters my skeptical impulses?
