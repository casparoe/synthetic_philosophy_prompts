Having recently retired from a career in experimental physics, I have taken up philosophy in a serious but amateur way, and I have been helping a colleague mark some undergraduate essays in moral philosophy. The next question on the paper concerns the paradox of blackmail. I would be glad of a second opinion, because I want the mark and the comments I give to be defensible under the department's marking criteria. Those criteria award roughly 40% for accurate exposition, 30% for independent argument, and 30% for recognising and engaging with what is philosophically difficult in the question.

The question set for the student was: "Set out what is sometimes called the paradox of blackmail. Then consider whether the wrongness of blackmail is better explained by features of the threatened disclosure (such as breach of confidence or invasion of privacy) or by features of the conditional demand for payment (such as coercion or exploitation), and say whether either approach succeeds in dissolving the paradox."

The student wrote:

"Blackmail is a conditional threat: X demands a benefit from Y, and undertakes to reveal damaging but truthful information about Y if the demand is not met. The standard puzzle is that each ingredient seems permissible by itself. Revealing true information about someone is generally lawful, and requesting money from someone is generally lawful; yet the combination is a criminal offence and is widely regarded as seriously wrong. One can put the difficulty as a question about composition: why should two individually inoffensive acts produce an offence when joined by a conditional?

A tempting answer is that the threatened disclosure is usually not permissible at all, because it would breach confidence or invade privacy. On this parasitic account, blackmail is wrong because the act threatened is itself wrong. The problem is that blackmail can arise even where no duty of confidence is owed: threatening to publish a photograph of someone entering a cinema, or threatening to report a minor breach of food-hygiene regulations, can still amount to blackmail, although the disclosure itself would not be unlawful. So the parasitic account is too narrow.

The better view, I think, is that blackmail is wrong because it turns another person's vulnerability into a private asset. The blackmailer holds no entitlement over the flow of information about the victim; by charging the victim for silence, the blackmailer assumes a right to decide whether and how the victim will be harmed, and it is this assumption that is wrongful. Once we see that, the composition problem disappears: the law does not add two permissions and get a wrong; rather, the act must be redescribed at the level of the whole transaction, and at that level the blackmailer is extorting payment for not harming the victim, which is wrongful in just the way coercion is wrongful."
