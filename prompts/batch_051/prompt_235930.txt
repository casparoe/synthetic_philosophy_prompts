You teach a 12-person advanced undergraduate seminar on distributive fairness and cooperative game theory, and you have one 75-minute session that must be run before any lecture on bargaining solutions. Design a two-round bargaining game, with real but small stakes, that makes students feel why Kalai and Smorodinsky replaced Nash's independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) with a monotonicity requirement. Build it around these two feasible sets, presented to students as ways of dividing a $4 pool (disagreement point (0,0)): Round 1 uses S = {(x,y) : x + y ≤ 4, y ≤ 3}; Round 2 uses T = {(x,y) : x + y ≤ 4, y ≤ 2}. The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution moves from (16/7, 12/7) in S to (8/3, 4/3) in T, while Nash's solution stays at (2, 2) in both — that shift is exactly what the game must make students feel before either solution is named. The key constraint: participants must not be told that bargaining solutions are the topic, or that the two sets were chosen to isolate IIA from monotonicity; the activity should make the problem show up in what they do. Specify (a) the exact rules and on-screen or printed instructions for each round, including a natural cover story for the fact that the ceiling on the second player's payoff falls between rounds; (b) role assignment and whether players switch roles between rounds, with a reason; (c) the payoff conversion to course points or equivalent that keeps the stakes real but small; (d) a minute-by-minute timeline; and (e) the debrief. The debrief must first reconstruct from the round 1-to-round 2 behavior what rule students were implicitly applying (including the possibility that they anchored on 50–50 and did not move), and only then introduce the two solution concepts: the product-maximizing rule that stays at (2, 2) in both sets, and the ray-to-the-utopia-point rule that moves, then connect these to the shared axioms (Pareto efficiency, symmetry, scale invariance), and finally stage the central conflict: IIA treats the options that became unavailable as irrelevant and leaves a still-feasible agreement frozen, whereas monotonicity says that when a player's best simultaneously attainable payoff falls (or rises) for every level the other side can secure, that player's assigned payoff should weakly fall (or rise) with it. Identify the most likely things to go wrong during the run — for example, pairs anchoring on an equal split in both rounds, treating the ceiling as a soft guideline, colluding rather than bargaining, or concluding that the point is that people are inconsistent — and for each, give the debrief move that turns it into evidence rather than giving the game away. Close with an honest paragraph on what no two-instance activity can reproduce: that the actual result is a characterization theorem, namely that the ray-to-the-utopia-point rule is the unique rule satisfying the three shared axioms plus monotonicity (just as the product rule is unique under the three shared axioms plus IIA), and that the cross-set consistency demanded of a solution is something only the facilitator's final five minutes can supply, not something the game itself can make participants feel.
