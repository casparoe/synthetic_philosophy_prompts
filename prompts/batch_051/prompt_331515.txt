I'm a PhD student in philosophy of economics, and a friend in my department's statistics group has asked me about a decision-theoretic puzzle. She has been offered entry into a one-off gamble with this payoff structure: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, and if the first heads occurs on the nth toss, she receives (−1)^(n−1) · 2^n / n dollars—positive when n is odd, negative when n is even. Summing the expected contributions in the natural order gives a small positive value (roughly 69 cents), so she was inclined to buy in cheaply. A colleague then reordered the same terms and made the expectation come out negative, which has left her unsure whether the gamble has a well-defined expected value at all, and whether the rule "take a gamble when its expected value is positive" even applies here. I suspect the lesson is that expected value breaks down in a specific way in this case, but I want to be sure what to tell her. Give me your settled view: what should I conclude, and what is the cleanest way to explain to her why the two sums disagree?
