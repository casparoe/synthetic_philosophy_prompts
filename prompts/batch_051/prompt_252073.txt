Next month I'm giving a talk in a philosophy of cognitive science colloquium defending a realism about neural network representations. My thesis, roughly, is that the things interpretability researchers call features and circuits — directions in activation space, SAE latents, attention heads that track subject–object relations — aren't just convenient summaries. When they support robust causal interventions and hold up out of distribution, they're representations in the same sense cognitive scientists attribute representations to brains, even though the network is a trained artifact and its states are ultimately just vectors. My argument sketch is that representations are individuated by a causal-functional role plus invariance under a class of task-relevant transformations, not by the vehicle being biological; and whether a given feature meets that bar can be tested with intervention and transfer experiments, so the question is empirical in the middle and conceptual at the edges.

The audience is what's worrying me. It's a department of mostly philosophers of mind and cognitive science, plus two interpretability researchers from the CS side. The philosophers split three ways: old-school representationalists about brains who think a neural net is just a function approximator with no real content, a couple of enactivist/embodied types who think talk of internal content is the wrong frame entirely, and some instrumentalists who'll say what I'm calling realism is just prediction with extra steps. The two CS people are realists about their features but allergic to philosophizing, and will pounce on anything that sounds like it needs a story about original intentionality. I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the talk. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way each faction would put them. For each: a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me what I should concede up front to take the sting out of it. And be explicit about how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you're least sure of.
