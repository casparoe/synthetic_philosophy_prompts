I'm a PhD student in comparative politics, so I spend half my time on trust-in-government stuff and half rereading old democratic theory. Lately I've been stuck on a passage in Tocqueville's Democracy in America—the place early in volume 1 where he says, roughly, that local institutions are what teach citizens the practice of liberty, and that without them a country can have a democratic constitution while never really having the spirit of self-government. (I don't have a translation in front of me, so don't take that as a verbatim quote.) The parallel I can't stop thinking about is this: in the Scandinavian trust literature the standard story is that high social trust explains why Denmark, Norway, and Sweden can sustain big universal welfare states without excessive monitoring. But maybe the causal arrow is at least partly the other way round. Those countries have unusually strong municipalities, local control over things like schools and elder care, and high local taxes that make local government salient. Tocqueville's claim seems to predict that this is exactly the kind of arrangement that manufactures trust, rather than merely being made possible by it. American suspicion then starts to look less like a cultural deficiency and more like the reasonable attitude of citizens whose local government has been hollowed out and whose national government is opaque and fragmented. Is this a deep structural parallel or am I just pattern-matching on the word local? What would have to be true at the municipal level for the Tocqueville mechanism to actually show up in the Scandinavian data, and where does the comparison break? I'm especially interested in whether the parallel makes any predictions that turn out false, since that would tell me whether this is real or just a story.
