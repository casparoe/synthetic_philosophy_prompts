I work as a staff attorney in a state legislature's nonpartisan bill drafting office. I review statutory language before it is introduced and flag provisions where a court might refuse to apply the words as written because doing so would produce an absurd result — what courts call the absurdity doctrine. We face hundreds of bills each session; a single provision gets roughly an hour of my time, and my supervisor signs off before anything goes out. If I misjudge, either a bad law gets applied literally or language we wrote gets nullified by a court. I want a reusable decision procedure, not a memo about one bill and not a literature review.

Please give me a checklist a staff attorney without judicial experience can actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure stops, either with a prediction that the text will be applied as written or with a flag that a court is likely to depart from it. Along the way, separate the kinds of case the doctrine might cover — an internal contradiction in the text, an impossibility in application, an obvious drafting or cross-reference slip, and a result that merely seems unwise, costly, or cruel — and make the procedure say which of these count and why. I would like two or three worked applications, at least one of which is a case where the procedure tells me a court will not depart from the text even though I personally find the outcome indefensible. Mark the points at which a human has to make a judgment call that cannot be reduced to a rule, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot do — for instance, whether it can predict a self-described textualist judge. Finally, separate what is settled about the absurdity doctrine from what is genuinely contested among judges and scholars, and do not present a contested view as the consensus; I do not want a checklist that quietly assumes one side of the textualism debate.
