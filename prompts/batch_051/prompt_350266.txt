My study group has an argument that seems to prove too much, and I can't see where it goes wrong. (1) A future is good to live in only if its ordinary days are good, not just its festivals or its big achievements. (2) The features that make a life good from the inside — routines, familiar people, work that matters without being dramatic — survive because they repeat. (3) But from the outside, before you're in it, nothing you can vividly want is ordinary or repetitive. (4) So the very features that would make a future good to live in are features we cant aim at from our current standpoint. (5) If we can't aim at them, we can't intentionally bring them about; any future we actually aim at will be made of exceptions, and once those become ordinary they'll either flatten out or get replaced, so the aim undermines itself. (6) That seems to imply that wanting a good future is either powerless or self-defeating for everyone, not just utopians. That can't be right. Which premise should I reject, and what do I have to pay? I'd rather keep (3), because it feels like plain hedonic adaptation, but tell me if that's the expensive one.
