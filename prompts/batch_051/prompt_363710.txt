I run a nine-person backend team at a midsized company, and I'm the one who signs off on our engineering standards. The team has gotten really into AI coding assistants over the last year, and I've watched our standards around programming languages quietly dissolve. Nobody writes a memo saying we're a Go shop but now were actually a Go/Python/TypeScript shop depending on what the tool autocompleted. It just happens review by review. I need a reusable procedure for deciding when our language standards should still bite.

Realistically I have ten or fifteen minutes per decision and these come up a few times a week. I have to defend the decision up, to a CTO who mostly cares about delivery speed and not getting paged, and down, to engineers who think caring about languages is just gatekeeping. The procedure has to be runnable by someone without a background in programming language theory, but it shouldn't be vibes either. I want it to keep whatever distinctions actually do the work — the language as syntax, the language as a type system, the language as an ecosystem of libraries and people you can hire — rather than collapsing everything into use whatever gets the ticket done.

Cases the procedure needs to handle:

- A backend service is entirely in Go, and the assistant starts generating a small data-processing script in Python because it's shorter. The script gets committed and now it's 200 lines of critical data munging, still Python.
- The assistant generates a regex-heavy validation module that nobody can read a week later even though it passes review. It's in our standard language, so it's technically compliant.
- A new hire writes a prototype for an internal tool in Rust, and it's clean and fast, but only two people on the team can meaningfully review it. The assistant is happy to keep adding to it.
- The assistant starts generating raw SQL directly inside application code instead of going through our existing query layer. Everyone finds it convenient and nobody's quite sure whether that's a language problem at all.

What I want from you: a decision procedure — a checklist or a flowchart in words — that I can actually run in the time I have, telling me when to enforce the standard language, when to bless an exception, and when to rewrite generated code. Mark clearly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then apply it to three cases, including at least one where the procedure gives me an answer I'm not going to like, and tell me honestly what the procedure can't do for me.
