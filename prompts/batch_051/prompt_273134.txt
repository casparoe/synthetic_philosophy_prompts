I'm teaching Aldous Huxley's Brave New World in a philosophy-adjacent seminar next term, and i want to use it to frame the debate about apatheia and ataraxia -- whether freedom from disturbance is actually good, and what it costs. My half-formed reading is that the World State basically achieves a chemically guaranteed version of the Stoic/Epicurean ideal: nobody grieves, nobody rages, soma smooths every edge, and the whole social order is built to keep it that way. John the Savage is the one insisting he wants poetry and danger and the possibility of real unhappiness, and the book seems to treat that as something nobler than the drugged calm. But i cant tell whether the novel is actually landing a blow on the ideal, or just caricaturing it by treating apatheia as anesthesia -- a flat, hedonic numbness -- when the Stoic point is (i think?) that the sage still has the beginnings of emotion: he flinches at a loud noise, pales at danger, wishes people well, and he just doesnt assent to the judgment that the external thing is truly bad or good. If so, the book and the ideal might not even be touching each other. So i'd like a reading of the novel through that debate, one that does justice to both: where does Brave New World genuinely show that a life engineered for zero disturbance loses something essential; where does it trade on the conflation between apatheia and being drugged into comfort; and is there a version of the ideal the World State never even tests? Also, is Mustapha Mond's comfort-over-truth defense actually closer to Epicurean ataraxia than to the Stoic version, and does the novel stack the deck by making the only alternative John's self-lacerating romantic misery? I'm using it in class and dont want to mislead anyone, so the reading should say what the book gets right and what it rigs.
