I'm prepping a session on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem and I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether the whole thing is quietly obselete. The version I learned: suppose each person's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and society's preferences over lotteries (whatever social preference amounts to) satisfy them too, and society respects Pareto indifference — if every individual is indifferent between two lotteries, society is indifferent as well. Then social utility has to be a weighted sum of individual utilities. For a long time people treated that as a kind of formal argument for utilitarianism, or at least for thinking social welfare should be additively separable in individual utilities.

What's bugging me is that the proof was built in the 1950s, back when expected utility theory still looked like a decent description of how people actually choose under risk. Since the Allais paradox, and especially since prospect theory and the ambiguity literature, it's pretty clear actual humans systematically violate the VNM axioms: they overweight small probabilities, treat losses differently from equivalent gains, show the certainty effect, and so on. So one of the theorem's starting points — that individuals really are expected-utility maximizers — is empirically false for exactly the creatures whose interests are being aggregated. Does that kill the argument, or just change how we should read it? I can see three broad responses: the theorem never needed descriptive accuracy because it's a purely normative constraint on rational preferences; or it does need repair, and we need some non-expected-utility aggregation result; or it survives but stops carrying the utilitarian conclusion. I want to know which specific premise the behavioral evidence actually bears on, whether the argument can be restored in a weaker form, and what that weaker form would still license. And if your answer is "it's normative, so the empirical stuff is irrelevant," I'd like that defended rather than just asserted.
