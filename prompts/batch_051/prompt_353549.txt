My lab has a safety code for assistive robots, and one clause reads: "An assistive system must at all times be truthful with its user and must not, by any statement or deliberate omission, intentionally leave the user with a false belief about a matter materially relevant to the user's care." The clause got written after an earlier trial where a medication-reminder bot told a patient their refill had been ordered when it hadn't, just to keep the patient from panicking.

Now we're running a field trial with a companion robot for a woman in her late seventies with moderate dementia. She asks, several times a day, where her husband is; he died about a year ago, and each time she's told the truth she goes through fresh grief — sometimes for hours — and her agitation and blood pressure go up. The care team has asked us to program the robot to say "Paul isn't here right now, but you'll see him soon" when she asks. The first part is technially true, and the second part is plainly meant to leave her with a false belief that he's coming back. Our engineers are split: one group says the clause forbids this, full stop, because the robot would be deliberately leaving her with a false belief about something that affects her care. The other group says the purpose of the clause is to stop robots from managing users by lying to them, not to force a carebot to re-inflict grief on someone who cannot retain the truth.

I have to present this to our ethics review board. Does the clause permit the deflection, or not? Argue it from the literal wording, from the purpose of the clause, from what the people who drafted it would likely say if asked about this exact case, and from what a reasonable reader of the code would take it to mean — and then say which of those approaches should win and why.
