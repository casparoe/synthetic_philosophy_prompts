I am a novelist writing a story set among Mīmāṃsā ritual philosophers, and I want the philosophical commitments of their practice to be exactly right. The institution I am puzzling over combines two features. First, the Veda is treated as authoritative precisely because it has no author at all—no human poet and no divine composer—so that no will or interest could have distorted it; its authorlessness (apauruṣeya) is counted as a strength rather than a defect. Second, the same tradition treats ordinary human testimony as intrinsically valid (svataḥ prāmāṇya): a clear report is accepted as knowledge unless a positive defect—bias, defective senses, a later contradiction—is shown, with no demand for external corroboration. A rival school, by contrast, holds that testimony becomes knowledge only after the reliability of its source has been independently certified. Please reconstruct the implicit theory of knowledge, language, and testimony that the first practice presupposes, distinguishing what is strictly required for its coherence from what is merely historically packaged with it, and marking where the practice leaves the theory underdetermined. Conclude by saying what these practitioners would have to give up or alter if they adopted the rival school's demand that validity always be established from outside.
