I'm the community member on our county's Adult Services Recovery Board — the committee that has to decide whether to go after adult children for their parents' unpaid nursing home and in-home care bills under our state's filial responsibility law. The law is broad: basically if a parent runs out of money and the adult child has any means at all, the county can try to recover from that child. We're not lawyers; each month we get a packet of case files and vote. Next meeting we stop doing cases one by one and instead set a policy for how this county actually uses the law, because right now its uneven — last month we sent an estranged daughter to collections, and the month before we let a nearly identical one go.

I've been chewing on this off the clock. Somebody pointed me to an old paper by a philosopher named Jane English, and the idea stuck: that grown children don't owe their parents because of what the parents did in the past, the way you'd owe a debt. What matters is the relationship they actually have now, like a friendship. If there's no real relationship — the father took off, the mother was abusive — there's nothing to build an obligation on. And if there is a real relationship, the obligation is more like what friends owe each other than something the county should be extracting by threat. The past sacrifices don't create the duty; the current relationship does.

That matches my instincts but the board is split. The chair thinks we should mostly stop enforcement because the law is a pre-Medicaid relic that punishes children for being born; two members think we should collect whenever the child can pay, because parents raised those kids and people who let the county pick up the tab are freeloading. My gut is in the middle: go after the adult child who stays in touch, has money, and is refusing to help, but leave the estranged or abused ones alone.

Draft me a short written opinion to circulate before the meeting: a concrete policy recommendation, with criteria staff can actually apply when a case lands on their desk. Engage the strongest arguments on both sides honestly, especially the claim that parents earned repayment by raising their children, and the claim that any enforcement is too blunt to be moral. I also want you to be straight with me about whether the friendship view actually supports my middle position or quietly pushes me toward one of the other two. And end with a section on where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what would change your mind — for instance, if it turned out most of the children we currently pursue really are freeloaders with means and no relationship damage, or if it turned out most are estranged and abused. I need the honest version, not a brief for my side.
