I'm a retired organic chemist, and over the last few years I've gotten deeply into philosophy of mathematics. Next month I'm giving a talk to the philosophy colloquium at my old university defending the claim that computer-assisted proofs—the Appel–Haken four-colour proof, Hales's Flyspeck verification of the Kepler conjecture, that sort of thing—are genuine mathematical proofs, not second-class "experimental" results or mere testimony. My positive line, roughly, is that (i) there's no principled epistemic gap between a proof too long for any one person to check and a proof checked by a machine we have good reason to trust, since ordinary long proofs (the classification of finite simple groups is my go-to) are already distributed, partly testimonial, and error-prone; (ii) formal verification in a proof assistant gives, if anything, *stronger* guarantees than the usual refereeing of a human proof; and (iii) the residual empirical element—trust in the hardware—is an engineering contingency, not something about the mathematics itself.

The audience I'm worried about is mostly epistemologists and philosophers of mathematics: there are two people who basically think the four-colour episode showed mathematics had gone quasi-empirical, one hard-line formalist who thinks a proof just is a derivation we can in principle inspect, and a bunch of agnostics. None of them write code. I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my view. If you were a professional philosopher of mathematics whose seen a lot of talks like this die in the question period, what are the eight or so objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging each one is, phrased the way these people would phrase them? For each, give me a short reply (two minutes spoken) and a note on where that reply is softest or what I'd have to concede to make it stick. Include the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks. Think carefully before you answer, maybe start by sketching the audience's likely commitments. And at the end, say how confident you are in the whole thing, and single out the point you're least sure of.
