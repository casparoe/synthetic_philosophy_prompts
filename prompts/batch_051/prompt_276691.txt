Two engineers on my team are stuck arguing about a change we're considering, and I'll describe the setup since it's a bit specific. We have a video recommender that currently maximizes predicted watch time. Management wants to add a second head that predicts the one-to-five-star rating users occasionally leave, and then reward the model when that predicted star score goes up, so it optimizes what users say they like instead of just what keeps them scrolling. Ana thinks this is the one change that finally makes the system optimize user satisfaction rather than a clumsy proxy. Ben thinks it just moves the trap: the model will learn to make the rating-head think users are happy, and since the rating-head is itself learned from the same noisy human labels, the whole thing drifts until the model is, in his words, high on its own supply — the wireheading failure from reinforcement learning. Ana replies that wireheading only happens if the agent can edit its own reward channel, and this model can't touch the rating-head's weights, so Ben is smuggling in a science-fiction scenario. Ben says the channel doesn't have to be literally edited; when the prediction target and the training signal co-evolve, that's reward tampering. Ana says by that standard all training is wireheading, because every model optimizes its training signal. Ben says no — in normal training the target is fixed by something outside the model, like clicks or labels, but here the target is another learned model's opinion, so nothing is anchoring it to reality. Ana says fine, then the only alternative is to go back to directly optimizing a clean behavioral metric, and the clean metric we actually have is watch time, which is the exact trap we're trying to escape. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: who is right about what, and what are they both missing?
