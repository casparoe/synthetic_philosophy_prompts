A friend and I spent a long argument on why promises bind. I kept noticing a pattern I don't have a name for. We would agree that the words "I promise" do not report an inner resolution, desire, or act of will; and yet we both treated the utterance as adding something above and beyond any such mental act. He gave examples: someone promises to repay a debt while privately dreading it; someone promises to attend a meeting with no prior resolution; someone says "I promise" while admitting the promise itself is what motivates him. In each case, there is no distinct mental state available to ground the obligation, but ordinary people still take the obligation to be created by the words. My friend said this just shows that promises are social conventions; I said that doesn't yet mark the exact boundary between this and the idea that the words themselves do something. We ended up reading the following passage from David Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature, Book 3, Part 2, Section 5, "Of the obligation of promises":

"The act of the mind, exprest by a promise, is not a resolution to perform any thing: For that alone never imposes any obligation. Nor is it a desire of such a performance: For we may bind ourselves without such a desire, or even with an aversion, declar'd and avow'd. Neither is it the willing of that action, which we promise to perform: For a promise always regards some future time, and the will has an influence only on present actions."

I want to know whether the configuration this passage isolates has an established name—the phenomenon where a promise's bindingness is located in the public act and not in any accompanying private mental act. Please act as a professional philosopher in moral philosophy and speech act theory. Think carefully, start by brainstorming the nearest candidate concepts, and then say exactly what the phenomenon is, whether it has a name, how it differs from nearby views (for example, social convention theories, performative utterance theories, or reductive accounts of promising), and if no established name fits, coin one with a definition and edge cases. I am not satisfied with a bare label.
