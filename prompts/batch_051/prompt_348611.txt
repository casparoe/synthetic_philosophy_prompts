I'm a PhD student in philosophy working in metaethics, and in a reading group I keep finding myself defending a view by leaning on an analogy that I suspect is doing more work than I have managed to make explicit. The analogy compares moral judgement to cheering and booing. Just as a spectator's boo at a foul does not describe the foul — it vents disapproval and encourages those nearby to share it — saying that an act is wrong is held not to report a moral fact but to express a con-attitude toward the act; and similarly, on the other side, for hurrahs and moral approval. The conclusion the analogy is normally used to support is that moral language need not answer to a special realm of moral properties: the practical force of moral judgement, its power to move us, is explained by the attitude-expression on the model of a cheer or a boo rather than by the detection of a queer kind of fact.

What I want is an audit of how far this analogy can carry that conclusion. Please lay out explicitly what maps onto what between the source (cheering and booing at a match) and the target (moral judgement): what is supposed to correspond to the boo itself, the spectator, the uptake by the surrounding crowd, the absence of a described action-property, and the push toward conduct. Then identify where the mapping strains or fails. I am aware that the Frege–Geach problem presses on one such strain: moral sentences embed in conditionals, negations, questions, and arguments in ways that cheers and boos do not — a speaker who says "if that act is wrong, she should apologise" need not be expressing disapproval of the act, and the conditional can participate in valid inference in a way a sequence of boos cannot. Engage with that problem in detail, but do not stop at it. Which disanalogies are merely incidental to the vividness of the picture, and which threaten the explanatory conclusion it is used to support? Once the disanalogies are on the table, does any version of that conclusion survive — perhaps by replacing the cheer/boo picture with a better analogy, or by narrowing the scope of the original — and what would the replacement or restriction need to look like? I would like the answer pitched at someone who has not yet settled whether to be an expressivist, and who wants to know which parts of the analogy are doing genuine theoretical work and which parts are only vivid.
