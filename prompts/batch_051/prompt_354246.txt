I have been trying to sort out whether novelty and variety are components of a good life over and above the pleasure, desire-satisfaction, or other goods they produce. Here are my settled or nearly settled judgments about particular cases, and I cannot tell whether any one principle lies behind them.

1. **Ravi's settled life.** Ravi has a loving marriage, an absorbing craft, close friends, and good health. From around age thirty-five onward, though, his life is nearly changeless: same work, same rituals, same holidays, same circle. He reports high satisfaction and does not want change. I judge that the sameness is a real but mild defect. If I could add variety to his later decades without subtracting any of his other goods and without changing his felt satisfaction, his life would be better in one respect.

2. **Mila's freshness device.** Mila's life is as objectively repetitive as Ravi's, but she wears a harmless device that makes her familiar routines present themselves with a fresh, surprising quality, the way a familiar room looks new when the light changes. She does not form false beliefs and her memory is intact; she knows her days are repetitive. The device clearly raises her enjoyment. I find myself judging that she is better off with it than without it, at least somewhat. Yet I also want to say that the benefit is not the same kind of good as real variety, and that Ravi would still be missing something that Mila has not gained.

3. **Jonas the collector.** Jonas has enormous objective variety: he has lived on four continents, changed careers five times, and dropped every hobby once its initial shine faded. He consumes novelty the way a collector acquires items. He is chronically unsatisfied and describes his life as a series of shallow episodes. I judge that the variety has not made his life go better, and has probably contributed to its shallowness.

4. **Priya's flight from boredom.** Priya also leads a varied life of projects, travel, and calculated risk, but her variety is driven by a dread of boredom; she cannot tolerate an ordinary evening, and her choices feel like flight rather than attraction. I judge that her variety is less valuable than the same activities chosen from wholehearted interest would be, and that at the extreme the pursuit of novelty can be a symptom of ill-being rather than an ingredient of it.

5. **Two otherwise equal lives.** Consider two possible lives for the same person, stipulated to contain exactly the same total enjoyment and the same number of satisfied desires. In the monotonous life, the enjoyment and desire-satisfaction come from a single stable routine. In the varied life, they come from a continually changing range of activities and relationships. I judge that the varied life is the better life. Variety seems to add something over and above the sum of satisfactions.

6. **Tom's repeated suite.** Tom has listened to a single recording of the same cello suite hundreds of times. The recording never changes, but for him each hearing discloses new structural relations; he experiences the piece as inexhaustible. I judge that his listening life is not monotonous in any way that matters. It contains genuine novelty even though his environment is factually unchanged.
