I work in grants administration at a small foundation, and over the past few years I have been trying, on my own time, to get clear about what it would mean to say that transformative AI has already happened. I have no formal training in economics or philosophy, so my thinking has been shaped less by the academic literature than by watching how well-intentioned funding rules actually behave once people begin to game them. It seems to me that the phrase transformative AI is used in two quite different ways: sometimes as a prediction about a future transition, and sometimes as a claim about the present — that the thing has already arrived, or that we are already inside the transition. I would like to think through an institutional design that forces that second kind of claim to become precise enough to be adjudicated.

Suppose the foundation where I work, together with several much larger partners, establishes a Transformative AI Registry. In any year, any person or organization may file a claim that a particular deployed AI system — or a cluster of systems shipped and used as a single recognizable product, such as a search engine, a machine-translation service, or a large language model — has already been transformative. Filing requires a bond of half a percent of the announced prize, forfeited if the claim is rejected and returned with interest if it is accepted. A standing panel then applies a fixed rule. The rule says that a system is transformative just in case, within fifteen years of its first public deployment, it can be shown to have raised the long-run growth rate of output per hour worked, measured across the global economy, by at least half a percentage point per year over a full decade, and that the gain is best explained by that system rather than by some other single cause. Once a particular increment of productivity growth has been awarded to one system, it cannot be awarded to another. The first accepted claim receives a very large prize, four-fifths to the system’s developer and one-fifth to whoever filed the claim. After fifteen years from a system’s deployment date, claims about it become permanently inadmissible, and the panel’s decision can never be reopened.

What I would like is a step-by-step analysis of how this scheme would actually behave: who would file early and why, how developers would respond to the possibility of strangers claiming credit for their systems, how the panel’s productivity rule would interact with the ordinary meaning of transformative, and where the mechanism would be gamed or would break down first. I am especially interested in whether it could produce a stable public answer to the question of whether transformative AI has already happened, or whether it would simply convert the definitional dispute into an adversarial and expensive contest over baselines and causation. Could any of the failures be repaired without abandoning the basic design?
