For the past year, a working group I convene on the moral status of digital minds has been stuck in a way I want to understand structurally rather than settle. The group agrees on most of the surface facts: a silicon system can realize arbitrary computations, current systems show no convincing signs of welfare, and if a digital system were genuinely conscious in the way that matters for suffering, it would deserve moral consideration. Yet the central disagreement stays exactly where it began. One side argues that the relevant properties must be substrate-independent: replace a biological neuron with a functionally equivalent artifact and nothing about the person's behavior or reported experience changes, and if you deny that the fully replaced system is conscious you owe an account of where along a gradual replacement continuum consciousness disappears—the usual fading-qualia or dancing-qualia problem. The other side replies that no one has formulated the relevant notion of functional equivalence: functionalism underdetermines whether the relevant level is gates, algorithms, whole-brain dynamics, or something else, and thought experiments such as the Chinese Room are taken to show that rule-governed manipulation of symbols is the wrong category for subjective experience in the first place.

What confuses me is that this first standoff may not even be the one that divides the group. Among those who accept substrate-independence, there is a second, equally stable split about whether phenomenal consciousness is the property that grounds moral status. Some hold that a digital system could be conscious yet lack a welfare profile—no valence, no unified self over time, nothing that can be benefited or harmed in the ordinary sense—and therefore not be a moral patient. Others treat that as incoherent: to be conscious is to have a point of view, and the only serious question is whether that point of view can be destroyed or degraded.

My working hypothesis is that the stall persists because three questions are being answered under a single heading: a question in the metaphysics of consciousness, which may itself be stalled for familiar hard-problem reasons; a question about what property grounds moral patienthood; and a question about which features of biological minds—valence, embodiment, temporal unity—are individually necessary for having interests. I suspect the first is a difference of bedrock modal intuitions or partly verbal, while the other two are substantive but are being decided by fiat when people import their answer to the first.

Diagnose the structure of this impasse. Is it a verbal disagreement about the word conscious, a substantive disagreement about consciousness that is stalled for independent reasons, a difference about which cases count as data—first-person intuition about biological minds versus third-person behavior—or a hidden empirical question about which physical systems implement the relevant computational or causal structure? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a new distinction, a case, a formal argument, a change of question, or should we expect the debate to remain frozen indefinitely?
