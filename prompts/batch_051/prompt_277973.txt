In my philosophy club we read part of John Rawls's A Theory of Justice in which he draws a distinction between envy and resentment. As our teacher summarized it, Rawls thinks resentment is a moral feeling: when you resent someone, you are upset because they have gained something through a wrong or an injustice, and you want the wrong repaired. Envy, by contrast, is not a moral feeling for Rawls: it is the wish to bring someone down simply because they have something you lack, even when their having it is perfectly legitimate. He also has a category of what he calls excusable envy, but I am not sure how it applies. I have been testing this distinction against my own gut reactions, and I cannot get them to fit a single principle.

Here are the cases, with my honest verdicts. First, a classmate won the science fair with a project I know he mostly copied from his older brother; I was angry, and I still think my anger was justified. Second, a different classmate won the same fair two years earlier with a project she built entirely herself, and I caught myself wishing she had lost; I think that wish was ugly, and I would not defend it. Third, a boy a year ahead of me got a summer internship at a research lab because his father is on the board there; I felt something that initially looked like envy, but I am inclined to say it was really an objection to how the opportunity was distributed, and that the feeling was justified. Fourth, a friend inherited a house from a great-aunt and said she felt lucky; I felt a brief impulse to want her not to have it, and I immediately felt ashamed. Fifth, a girl in my class received the highest grade on a paper, but I know she paid a tutor to edit it heavily while the rest of us worked alone; my feeling there felt different from the inherited-house case, and I am not sure whether to call it envy or resentment, or whether it was justified.

My tentative principle is that a negative feeling about another person's good is justified when the good is the product of an unfair procedure, and not justified when it is the product of luck or fair effort. But that principle seems to fail: the inherited house is luck, yet it produced only shameful envy, while the internship through family connections is an unfair procedure, and I want to excuse that feeling. Meanwhile the copied science-fair project and the paid tutor are both clearly unfair, but the first feels more like resentment than the second does. Does Rawls's distinction between envy and resentment actually explain these verdicts, or do I need a more complicated principle? What principle best captures the pattern, and which of my verdicts would it force me to revise?
