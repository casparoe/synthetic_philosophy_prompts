You're an expert on paternalism and the ethics of behaviour change, and you've been working for years on the question of when nudging shades into manipulation. I want you to act as my Q&A coach for an upcoming seminar, and to think carefully — start by brainstorming a long list of possible objections in note form, then narrow it down to the ones that actually matter.

Next month I'm presenting at a university policy school. The room is mostly applied behavioural scientists and policy people who run nudge-style interventions for a living: defaults, social-norm messages, pre-commitment devices, the usual toolkit. There's also one health ethicist who's read the autonomy literature, and one economist who treats revealed preference as basically sacred. They're not anti-nudge; they're practioners who think the live questions are about effect sizes, equity audits, and wether an intervention is "transparent enough".

My thesis is roughly this. For a sizeable class of nudges, the fact that the mechanism works best when people don't consciously notice it isn't a PR problem a transparency disclaimer solves — it's the problem itself. If a nudge harnesses a bias the target would disown on reflection (inertia, present bias, loss aversion, whatever), then pointing at the mechanism doesn't legitimise it: it either destroys the effect or reveals that the effect depended on bypassing the very kind of agency that defenders of nudging claim to respect. So the standard line — "it leaves all options open, unlike a mandate" — does less moral work than people think, and for these cases a nudge can be more objectionable than an overt mandate, because at least a mandate addresses you as an agent rather than as a bug in your own psychology.

The rough argument I'll be defending runs: (1) many effective nudges work through automatic, non-deliberative processes; (2) if the target wholeheartedly endorsed the reasoning behind the nudge, you mostly wouldn't need the nudge, you could just give them the information or ask; (3) the troubling cases are where the target would not endorse the mechanism if it were made explicit; (4) in those cases "freedom of choice" is doing very little work, because the choice architecture is itself a choice they'd refuse. I'll also concede early on that I'm not talking about cases where the effect survives transparency and the target is fine with it.

What I need from you is a prep sheet, not a defence. Give me the eight or nine objections I'll actually get from this audience, not the ones I'd get from a philosophy seminar on manipulation. Order them by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do. Phrase each one the way these people would actually phrase it — the economist's will be different from the health ethicist's. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply, a note on what to concede on the spot, and a note on where my reply is softest. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, probably from the ethicist, with a line on how I should handle it if it comes up.
