Next month I'm giving a seminar paper called "Why Nobody Deserves Anything," and I want you to help me prep for the Q&A like you're a philosopher of action and moral psychology who has watched a hundred of these go badly. The position I'm defending is Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, more or less: you always act from the way you are—your character, dispositions, motivational set—so to be truly morally responsible for what you do you'd have to be responsible for the way you are; but you can't be, because that would require you to have deliberately brought about the way you are, and that act of self-creation would itself have to issue from a prior self, and so on back. So nobody is ever responsible in the deep, desert sense that would make it fair for someone to suffer or be diminished because of what they did. I'll argue the argument is sound, and then—this is the part I'm least sure about and honestly its the section I keep rewriting—I'll say we should keep most of our blaming and praising practices for their forward-looking social functions, but stop telling ourselves anyone fundamentally deserves it.

The audience is a small philosophy department, mostly compatibilists. Two people work on reasons-responsive accounts of responsibility and will say I've only shown we lack some strange "ultimate self-creation" control that nobody ever meant by responsibility. The chair is a devotee of P. F. Strawson's Freedom and Resentment line and will treat the whole framing as a category mistake about what blame actually is. There's one hard incompatibilist who agrees with my conclusion but thinks the Basic Argument is the weakest route to it—they prefer a moral-luck-style argument—and loves to pounce on sloppy regress reasoning. And there's one libertarian agent-causalist who will say the self-causation premise just denies agent causation by fiat. I already know the stock undergraduate free-will objections; I need you to anticipate what this audience, with these commitments, will actually say, in something close to their words.

Act as the expert. Start by brainstorming the obvious objections, then the ones only these four would press, then order everything by how likely and how damaging it is. For each one: phrase it the way they'd phrase it, give me a tight reply (I've got about twenty minutes of questions), and flag the part of my own position the reply quietly leans on or where it's soft. Tell me what to concede up front and what to refuse to budge on, and include the question I'm probably hoping nobody asks. Be honest about the weakest spot—I'd rather hear it from you than in the room.
