In my course on AI safety, we have been discussing the low-impact approach to avoiding side effects: the idea that an agent can be kept from causing harmful interference without a full specification of human values by penalizing it for making the world diverge from a baseline trajectory—roughly, what would have happened had the agent done nothing. The original arguments for this approach seem to presuppose a fairly well-behaved state space, such as a reinforcement-learning agent in a simulated gridworld or game, where states can be enumerated or embedded, side effects are mostly near-term physical changes, and a neutral baseline is at least approximately definable. I am trying to think through whether the argument survives the shift to large language model agents that act in open-ended digital environments by writing text, running code, and influencing other agents. In these settings the baseline trajectory is less obvious, many effects are informational or social rather than physical, and very small actions can have long-range consequences. Does the low-impact argument still go through under these changed assumptions? Which premise is actually doing the work, and is there a repaired version that preserves the original motivation—avoiding the need for a full account of value—without silently smuggling one in?
