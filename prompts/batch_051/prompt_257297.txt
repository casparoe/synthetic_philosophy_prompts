I have no academic training, but I have been studying the philosophy of probability on my own for several years and lead a small, informal reading group through our public library. I wrote the following exercise for the group, and one of the regular members submitted the answer below. I would like you to act as an independent grader: assign a grade, and give me point-by-point comments that explain the grade.

Exercise:
Lewis's Principal Principle is often glossed by saying that a rational agent should defer to objective chance. Give a more precise statement of the principle and explain the role of the admissibility condition. Then consider three agents. Anne has no evidence about the chance that her coin will land heads and sets her credence at 0.7. Bilal knows that the objective chance of heads is 0.7 and has no further relevant information. Claire knows that the objective chance of heads is 0.7, but she has also received a reliable forecast of the outcome of her toss, from a source that does not report on chance, which predicts tails. For each agent, say what the Principal Principle does or does not require with respect to their credence in heads, and why.

Student's answer:
The Principal Principle says that for any rational initial credence function C and any proposition A, C(A | X and E) = x, where X is the proposition that the objective chance of A is x and E is any admissible evidence. The admissibility condition is required because without it the principle would be too strong. If an agent knows the chance of heads is 0.7 but also has a crystal ball that reliably shows the coin landing tails, the principle should not force that agent to set her credence to 0.7. Direct information and forecasts about the outcome are not admissible, and the principle falls silent about them.

Anne's case is straightforward once one sees the point of the principle. Whatever the actual objective chance of heads is, that is the value a rational credence should track. Suppose the actual chance is 0.7. Then the Principal Principle requires Anne to assign credence 0.7, even though she has no evidence about the chance, because credence should reflect objective chance and not merely subjective guesses. If rational credence were allowed to drift away from the actual chance merely because the agent did not know it, the principle would have no real force.

Bilal's case is the basic application: he knows the chance is 0.7 and has no inadmissible information, so the principle requires him to assign credence 0.7.

Claire's case is different. She knows the chance is 0.7, but the reliable forecast of tails is inadmissible evidence because it bears directly on the outcome. The principle therefore does not require Claire to assign credence 0.7. It is silent about the exact credence she should adopt once inadmissible evidence is in play, though other norms of rationality may require her to move toward tails.
