I'm in a philosophy of law seminar and right now we're on desuetude—statutes that just sit on the books without ever being enforced, like old adultery laws, Sunday trading bans, loitering statutes. I'm making a handout for my study group and I want a decision tree that sorts someone into a view about what decades of total non-enforcement actually does to a criminal statute, and what a court should do when a prosecutor finally dusts one off. Build the tree from the questions up, not from the theory names, so each branch node is a concrete question a student can answer without already knowing wether they'll land in the positivist box or the natural law box. Six to eight branch questions, tops. The leaves you have to include are at least: (1) strong desuetude, where long non-enforcement just kills the law; (2) a discretion view, where the law stays valid but a court may reasonably decline to enforce it; (3) a plain positivist view, where valid law stays valid and judges should enforce it unless there's some separate constitutional defect; and (4) an interpretivist view, where decades of everyone treating the statute as dead change what its words mean in the community's practice. The point is to sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they walked in liking. Flag any two leaves your tree can't cleanly separate. For any combination of answers nobody occupies, say in one sentence whether the spot is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive to hold, or just unexplored. The angle I care about: one node has to ask whether an unenforced law is better thought of as a standing threat the state keeps in reserve or as a promise it's stopped making; another node should push the rule-of-law worry, like whether reviving a dead law after decades hurts people's ability to plan more than quietly leaving it unenforced does. Keep it all under 850 words, tree indented, short key after.
