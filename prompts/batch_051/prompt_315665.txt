Write the desiderata that a theory of biological function must meet in order to count as an adequate account of what it is for a biological item to have a function, addressed to an upper-level philosophy of biology seminar that already knows the basic contrast between selected-effects/etiological accounts and causal-role/system accounts. Present each requirement together with the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark each as either non-negotiable for any adequate account or a preference that one of the two camps is entitled to insist on. Cover at least these cases: the difference between a function and an incidental effect (e.g., the nose supporting spectacles, the heart making sounds); the possibility of malfunction and the normativity that comes with it (a defective heart still has a function it fails to perform); vestigial and functionless traits (e.g., the appendix, pseudogenes); the first occurrence of a novel beneficial mutation, before selection has operated on it; exaptation and change of function (e.g., feathers from thermoregulation to flight); the hierarchical nesting of system-relative functions (a heart's function relative to the circulatory system, which is relative to the organism); and the overattribution problem of privileging one among the many effects a part contributes to a larger system. Then identify any subsets of these requirements that no single account is likely to satisfy at once, and explain what each contender family must give up in order to meet the rest. End with a brief score of the two families against the list, and state which single requirement does the most work to exclude each. The part I most want is the joint-satisfiability diagnosis, not the scorecard. Do not supply citations, a bibliography, or verbatim quotations; the deliverable is the structure of the demands and the trade-offs, not the literature.
