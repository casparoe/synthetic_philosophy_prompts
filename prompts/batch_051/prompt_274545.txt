So I've ended up leading a discussion on whether the brain is literally a computer at a little interdiscplinary reading group—mostly cog-sci grad students plus one guy from robotics who keeps saying "it's all just symbolic processing." I want to anchor the whole session on a passage from Alan Turing's Computing Machinery and Intelligence, but not the famous imitation game bit everyone already knows. The passage I keep coming back to is:

"We do not wish to penalise the machine for its inability to shine in beauty competitions, nor to penalise a man for losing in a race against an aeroplane."

It seems to me that this sentence does a lot of quiet work: it says what kind of question Turing thinks he's asking, and it seems to assume that what matters is cognitive performance, not having a particular kind of body. But I'm honestly not sure I have the concept clean myself—I keep slipping between "the brain is a computer" and "the brain can be described computationally without being one," and I don't want to paper over that in front of the group. I'm not looking for an essay; I need a teaching plan I can actually follow in about ninety minutes. Give me exactly five numbered items, no intro or conclusion, each a sentence or two. Four should cover: the order in which the pieces need to go so the distinction between a machine simulating a mind and a machine having a mind lands; the single concrete example or scenario to build the session around and what it needs and lacks; the misconceptions I should expect and where in the order to defuse each; and the check for understanding that would show people have actually got the distinction. Make the fifth item a note on how confident you are in the plan, and which point in it you're least sure of.
