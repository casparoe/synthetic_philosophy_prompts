I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about the species problem by sorting through a bunch of cases, and I'd like to know what principle my verdicts add up to. Here's where I've landed.

Dandelions. A lot of them just clone themselves — no sex, no pairing up, no gene flow. Still, obviously they come in species: a common dandelion isn't the same thing as a hawkweed. So whatever a species is, it can't be defined by interbreeding.

Dogs and wolves. They interbreed fine and the pups are fertile. But I'd never call a husky and a wolf the same species. So interbreeding isn't sufficient either.

Gulls around the Arctic. Herring gulls and lesser black-backed gulls grade into each other around the pole: each neighboring population interbreeds with the next one all the way around, but where the two ends meet in Europe they don't interbreed. I want to say it's one species the whole way around, but then I'm saying the two end populations are the same species even though they treat each other as not the same right where they overlap. That makes me uneasy, and I can't decide which verdict to drop.

Two oaks that hybridize in a narrow strip where their ranges touch, but stay pretty well distinct everywhere else. I call them two species.

Two strains of E. coli that swap genes sideways all the time. I still call them one species, and I cant say why that feels different from the oaks, which bugs me.

A fossil lineage changing gradually over three million years. The ends look nothing alike and probably couldn't have interbred, but there's no single generation where a parent couldn't have interbred with its own offspring. I'd still split the ends into different species.

So the question: is there one species concept that gets all of these, or close? Or does this pattern just amount to pluralism? If you had to pick a single concept — biological, ecological, phylogenetic, morphological, whatever — that captures the most of my verdicts, tell me exactly what the principle says, which verdicts it saves, and which ones it forces me to give up. And to test whichever account you settle on: what does it say about two frog populations that can produce viable tadpoles in a lab but never encounter each other in the wild, and about two cryptic species that look identical to a field guide but won't mate with each other?
