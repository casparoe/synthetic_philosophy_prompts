I write about behavioral science and philosophy for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about whether what the Greeks called akrasia — acting against your better judgment — is really the same thing as the hyperbolic discounting curves economists fit to people's impatience in the lab. The line I keep hearing from researchers is that weakness of will just is present bias, and that the preference reversal you see in delay-discounting tasks is akrasia, measured. I'm suspicious. A fitted curve that discounts near-term rewards steeply describes a pattern; it doesn't by itself tell us whether the person still thinks, at the very moment of caving, that the delayed option is the better one. If their valuation genuinely flipped as the reward got close, that isn't akrasia in the old sense, its just changing your mind. So here's what I need: is the claim that akrasia is hyperbolic discounting testable, or wheter it's a category mistake dressed up as a finding? Design the strongest concrete test you can — a behavioral experiment with ordinary adults, real choices, nothing involving a scanner or fancy equipment — that could pull apart 'the valuation flipped' from 'the valuation stayed put and the person acted against it anyway.' Tell me what pattern of results would count for the reductionist's claim and what would count against it, and be explicit about what the test would not settle even if it came out perfectly. Then, because I don't want the piece to be over-sold, say what would change your mind on this — which argument or consideration would flip your verdict — and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
