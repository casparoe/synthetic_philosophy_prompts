In the interdisciplinary working group I'm part of, a dispute about the claim that the brain is a computer has now run for over two years without either side moving, and I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement we are really having. The empirical facts are not in dispute. Both sides agree that neural signals carry information in Shannon's sense, that many circuits are usefully described as implementing algorithms, and that the brain is a noisy, analog, massively parallel physical system rather than a stored-program digital machine. What they disagree about is whether "the brain computes" is a literal description of the brain's causal organization or a piece of modeling shorthand.

One camp — call them the mechanists — holds that the brain computes literally. They grant that "computer" cannot mean "silicon stored-program device," but they insist there is a perfectly literal sense in which a system computes when a mechanism manipulates medium-independent vehicles according to a rule: patterns of spikes or the contents of working memory are transformed by neural machinery roughly as a processor transforms symbols. On their view, whether the brain computes in this sense is a discoverable fact about the system, not a choice of description; computational neuroscience is a science of actual computation in the brain, not a metaphor.

The other camp — call them the deflationists — holds that the literal claim is either false or trivial. They point out that any sufficiently regular physical process, from a falling rock to the orbit of Jupiter, can be assigned a computational interpretation, and that Shannon information is too cheap to distinguish brains from anything else. What makes a laptop a computer, on their view, is not its dynamics alone but its design history and the semantic contents we assign to its states, and the brain lacks exactly that. So "the brain computes" is at best a useful way of saying that the brain can be fruitfully simulated or modeled computationally.

The frustrating thing is that the two sides keep making the same moves. The mechanists accuse the deflationists of conflating computation with the simulation of computation; the deflationists accuse the mechanists of smuggling in an interpreter to decide which physical states count as vehicles. My working hypothesis, which I accept may be too quick, is that "computer" is doing at least three jobs at once — (i) anything simulable by a Turing machine, (ii) a mechanism that actually manipulates discrete, medium-independent vehicles, and (iii) a functional kind partly constituted by design history and semantic interpretation — and that the camps are answering different questions under one name. But I doubt the disambiguation is the whole story, because the camps still disagree, even after the senses are separated, about whether the brain in fact manipulates vehicles and about whether "manipulates a vehicle" can be spelled out without appealing to an interpreter.

Give me a committed diagnosis, not a survey of possible readings. Take a definite position on what this disagreement most fundamentally is — a merely verbal ambiguity, a dispute over standards of explanation, a clash of bedrock intuitions about the observer-relativity of function, a hidden empirical question about whether the brain has discrete vehicles at all, or something else — and defend that diagnosis rather than laying out options. Then state what would count as progress given the diagnosis. Format the response as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
