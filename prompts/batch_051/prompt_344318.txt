Analyze the 2006 reclassification of Pluto by the International Astronomical Union as a case study in whether the sciences carve nature at its joints. For orientation: the IAU defined a planet, roughly, as a body that orbits the Sun, is massive enough for gravity to have pulled it into a roughly round shape, and has cleared its orbital zone of comparable objects. Pluto meets the first two conditions but not the third, so it was put into a new category, dwarf planet. I want the analysis to run in both directions. Through the lens of natural kinds: is that third, dynamical condition the sort of property that could mark a genuine joint in nature, or is it an extrinsic fact about a body's surroundings rather than about the body itself? Then use the case to test the theory: what does a contested classification, settled by vote and still resisted by many planetary scientists, do to the idea that science discovers kinds rather than stipulates them? Does the lesson stop at planet, leaving supposed natural kinds like chemical elements and biological species untouched, or does it put pressure on the whole idea that a single correct carving exists? And say explicitly where a natural-kinds framework is silent or misleading about what actually drove the controversy.
