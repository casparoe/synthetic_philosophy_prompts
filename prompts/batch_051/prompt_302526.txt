I am marking a third-year undergraduate essay in distributive ethics. Below is the question and one student’s submission. I would like you to propose a mark and to provide the detailed, point-by-point comments that justify that mark; please do not give an overall judgement without explaining each point.

Question: Explain the levelling-down objection to egalitarianism, and discuss the extent to which prioritarianism succeeds in avoiding it.

Student answer:

Egalitarianism, in the form relevant to the objection, is the view that inequality is intrinsically bad. More precisely, it holds that people’s being worse off than others through no fault of their own is bad in itself, independently of its further effects. The levelling-down objection points out that if this is right, then reducing the better-off to the level of the worse-off without improving anyone’s position would still be an improvement in one respect, simply because it removes inequality. The objection says this cannot be correct: a change that benefits no one and harms at least one person cannot make an outcome better in any respect. Hence equality cannot be intrinsically valuable. Egalitarians may reply that they regard equality as only one value among others, so that levelling down can be better in one respect while being all-things-considered worse; but Parfit’s point is that a view that avoids even this implication is preferable, other things equal.

Prioritarianism, by contrast, holds that the value of a benefit to a person is greater the worse off the person is. It tells us to give priority to the interests of the worse-off, but not because equality itself matters. Since prioritarianism assigns no intrinsic value to equality, it avoids the implication that levelling down is good. This is why Parfit regarded the priority view as preferable to egalitarianism.

There is nevertheless a residual problem for prioritarianism. It may justify making a better-off person worse off in order to benefit a worse-off person even when the better-off person loses more well-being than the worse-off person gains. In such a case total well-being declines, and the only justification for preferring the new distribution is the priority weighting given to the worse-off. That looks like levelling down under another description: an outcome with less total well-being is preferred to one with more, for no reason other than the shape of the distribution. So prioritarianism does not wholly escape the levelling-down objection; it merely relocates it from equality to priority.
