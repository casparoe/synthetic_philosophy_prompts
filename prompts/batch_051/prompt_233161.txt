I am writing a paper on a long-running impasse in the philosophy of action, and I need a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement the participants are actually having, rather than a verdict about which side is right.

The dispute concerns whether a person engaged in genuine deliberation can coherently assign probabilities to her own still-open choices. One camp holds that deliberation crowds out self-prediction: to deliberate between A and B is to treat both as live options under one's control, and assigning, say, probability 0.7 to choosing A is already to step outside the choice and regard oneself as a mechanism whose output is about to be revealed. On this view, the thought "I will probably choose A" cannot be combined with seriously deliberating about whether to choose B, and someone who assigns credences to her own unresolved options has either already decided, or has stopped deliberating and begun forecasting herself.

The other camp replies that this is a philosophical taboo with no formal basis. We constantly hold partial beliefs about our own future conduct—whether we will finish the paper before the deadline, keep a resolution, or lose our temper—and many of these beliefs persist while the conduct is still up to us. On this view, a rational agent can have a subjective distribution over the outputs of her own decision procedure without ceasing to be an agent, and refusing to do so merely exempts self-directed outcomes from an otherwise general theory of probability.

What frustrates me is that the debate seems to have run for decades without either side moving. Each side accepts the other's examples but reads them differently: the first camp treats "I'll probably be late again" as evidence that the speaker has not actually decided to be on time, while the second camp treats it as a mundane and perfectly rational self-prediction under uncertainty. The participants agree on the formal probability theory and on the relevant phenomenology; they disagree about what those shared materials show.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a hidden factual disagreement about the mind, and not a small theorem waiting to be proved, but a disagreement about which stance is constitutive of genuine deliberation—a first-person stance of self-governance versus a third-person, predictive stance toward oneself—and that it therefore behaves more like a dispute about regulative ideals than about the content of any belief. Is that right? Or is there a hidden empirical question, a verbal equivocation, or an overlooked formal result doing the real work? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here—a distinction, an experiment, a decision-theoretic result, a reframing of the question—or should we expect no movement, and why?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating of the question.
