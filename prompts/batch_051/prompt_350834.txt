§1. The Hayekian picture, roughly. Prices are supposed to be a kind of distributed knowledge technology: a change in a price encodes alot of dispersed local information, and you don't need to know why it changed in order to respond sensibly. The miracle is that the knowledge is somehow "in" the price, not in any one head.

§2. The no-trade picture, roughly. Aumann's agreement theorem says rational people with common priors can't agree to disagree; if their posterior beliefs become common knowledge, they must be the same. The Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem pushes the same spirit toward markets: if rational common-prior agents start from an allocation that's already efficient, new private information shouldn't generate trade—because an offer to trade itself leaks the would-be seller's information, and the price adjusts until the counterparty no longer wants the trade.

§3. The tension I want to press. These two pictures can't both be true without an asterisk. If the price system gets its wisdom from trades that move on dispersed information, but rational common-prior agents have a built-in Groucho Marx problem (I don't want to trade with anyone who's willing to trade with me), then either (a) sophisticated traders are the wrong place to locate the wisdom—the aggregation happens through the unwashed mass of noise and liquidity traders; or (b) the epistemic defense of markets quietly depends on heterogeneous priors, overconfidence, or some other cognitive wart; or (c) the Hayekian story is about something else entirely—say, economizing on what anyone needs to know, not aggregating everything into a single number.

§4. The upshot if I keep it. I think the interesting version is (a) and (b) together: the price system's epistemic achievements might require a standing supply of folly—traders who trade when common-prior theory says they shouldn't. Which would mean the rationalist case for markets and the behavioral-finance case against individual traders are weirdly codependent. But I'm worried this collapses into the old "noise traders provide liquidity" point, in which case the essay isn't new, just rediscovered with more words.
