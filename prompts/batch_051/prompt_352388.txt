A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about what the right epoch for our civil calendar would be — in effect, what year zero ought to be — and neither of us moved. His position is that anchoring the year count to a supposed religious event, as the traditional system does, makes the calendar a parochial inheritance rather than a neutral instrument; a rational calendar would re-anchor its numbering in something genuinely universal or at least deliberately secular, so that people are not silently endorsing a sectarian starting point every time they write a date. My position is that no choice of origin can be correct or incorrect, because the origin of a year count is a pure convention, and the only things that matter are continuity, coordination, and the practical cost of switching; the existing epoch is therefore no worse than any alternative, and reform is pointless disruption. We agree on the underlying facts — the traditional scheme has no year zero between 1 B.C. and A.D. 1, the dating of the commemorated event is at best approximate, and a year number is an index rather than a measurement of the world's age — yet the argument only circled. My working hypothesis is that we were using 'right' in two different senses: he seemed to mean fitting the calendar's proper function or public meaning, while I meant something closer to answerable to the facts, so we may have been talking past one another. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this disagreement verbal, is it a substantive difference of evaluative criteria, is it the symptom of an ill-posed question, or is it something else? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress — an explicit agreement on what a calendar's epoch is for, a formal criterion for comparing proposals, or a reasoned dissolution of the question?
