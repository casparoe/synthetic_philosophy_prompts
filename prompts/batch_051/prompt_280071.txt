I have been collecting proposed solutions to the paradox of tragedy — understood broadly as the paradox of painful art, the question of why we deliberately seek out and value works that reliably make us sad, frightened, or appalled — and the list I have so far is this. (1) Purgation: the work arouses our pity and fear and then drains them away, and the resulting relief is the source of the pleasure. (2) Conversion: the artistry of the work, its plot, verse, staging, or performance, is itself so pleasing that it transforms or overpowers the painful material, making the whole experience an enjoyed one. (3) Distance and control: because the represented suffering is unreal and the audience can stop at any time, the negative affect loses its sting and becomes a safe, even exhilarating, exercise of the emotions. (4) Make-believe or quasi-emotion: the audience does not undergo ordinary, full-fledged fear or pity at all, but only make-believe or attenuated counterparts, so there is no real displeasure that needs explaining away. (5) Meta-response: the first-order sadness is accompanied by a second-order pleasure, such as satisfaction in one's own capacity for fellow-feeling or admiration for the artist who moved one. (6) Cognitive and moral value: the true object of appreciation is the insight, understanding, or moral seriousness the work supplies, and the painful emotion is instrumental to that reward or a cost worth paying for it. (7) The value of negative emotion itself: at least when framed aesthetically, the sadness is not a cost to be outweighed but an ingredient of a richer experience that is itself directly valued.

This list, or minor relabelings of it, is everything I keep finding, and I want to know whether it is actually exhaustive. Is there an eighth solution to the paradox of tragedy, genuinely distinct from these seven? By genuinely distinct I mean that it identifies a different psychological mechanism or a different object of positive response, and is not a rephrasing, a special case, or a conjunction of items already on the list. To make the question hard rather than a matter of finding an easy qualification, test any candidate against a deliberately unaccommodating case: a short, competently made, non-narrative work — a ninety-second film of a face weeping — that offers no moral or cognitive content and that the viewer has already seen several times, so first-time novelty, narrative resolution, and information gain can do no work. If you believe there is no eighth solution, then say so and argue that any proposed candidate must collapse into one of the seven, indicating which item absorbs it and why.
