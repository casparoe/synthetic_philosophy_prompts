Karl Popper's The Poverty of Historicism makes the following argument. The future course of human history depends heavily on the future growth of scientific and technological knowledge. But we cannot now predict the future growth of knowledge, because to predict a future discovery would, in substance, already be to make it. It follows that we cannot predict the future course of human history, and that the historicist search for laws of historical development rests on mistaking conditional trends for unconditional laws. On this view, the most a rational social science can offer is piecemeal, conditional, testable intervention, not large-scale prophecy.

A later development complicates this position. Since roughly 2010, competitive forecasting projects have shown that teams of forecasters using explicit numerical probabilities, aggregation methods, and regular updating can produce calibrated, above-chance forecasts of geopolitical and social events months ahead—elections, conflicts, disease spread, and similar society-level outcomes—without knowing the future discoveries that will shape those events. Machine-learning and ensemble systems have extended this to short- and medium-run forecasting in economics, epidemiology, and conflict early warning. None of these systems claim universal laws of historical development, but they do predict bounded segments of the historical process before they unfold.

Assess whether Popper's argument survives this development. In particular: (1) Which premise of the argument, if any, does the existence of skilled social forecasting actually put under pressure? (2) Can the argument be restated so that it still excludes what Popper meant by historicism—unconditional, large-scale prophecy of the long-run shape of future society—while remaining compatible with calibrated, probabilistic forecasting of bounded social outcomes? Or does that restatement quietly abandon the original claim? (3) If the original argument does not survive intact, give the strongest repaired version you can defend: a precise statement of what is and is not predictable in historical and social processes. Argue for your assessment rather than merely surveying alternatives.
