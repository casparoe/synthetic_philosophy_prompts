I've been arguing for years with a friend who insists that foreign aid from rich countries is a failed idea: too much of the money is consumed by donor agencies and middlemen, too much of what remains is diverted or wasted by recipient governments, and too little ever reaches the people it was meant to help. From this he concludes that rich countries don't owe poor ones nearly as much as the idealists say, because in practice sending money does no good. I've never had a clean answer. Lately, though, I've been reading about an alternative that seems to sidestep his strongest claims: small no-strings cash transfers sent directly to poor households through mobile-phone payment systems, with the household rather than a ministry or a foreign NGO deciding how to spend the money. The results reported for these programs look much harder to dismiss than the old image of food aid rotting on a dock.

So here is what I want to think through. The standard case against foreign aid appears to rest on a specific premise about routing: aid has to pass through donor agencies and recipient governments, and so it inevitably gets captured, wasted, or distorted on the way down. If money can now go from a donor to a poor household's phone at very low overhead, does that argument still have force, or does it shrink into a much narrower claim about government-to-government aid in particular? I want to know which exact premise the direct-payment development undercuts, whether the anti-aid argument can be repaired in a new form — for example, by arguing that direct cash transfers have their own limits, side effects, or hidden costs — and what, if anything, survives of the original claim that rich countries cannot do much good by sending money to the poor. And if the original argument does collapse, say what that does to my friend's conclusion about what rich countries owe.
