I am trying to pin down what is distinctive about choices that are hard because the relevant values are incommensurable, as opposed to choices that are merely difficult because we lack information, because our preferences conflict, or because the alternatives are close in value. I have been using the following rough criteria for a hard choice in the incommensurability sense: (a) at least two distinct dimensions of value are in play, and neither alternative is better than the other on all of them; (b) the alternatives are not determinately ordered, being neither better nor worse all things considered and also not exactly equal; (c) there is no common scale or determinate rate of exchange that would yield a uniquely correct trade-off between the relevant values; and (d) the difficulty would survive full information and ideal rationality, so it cannot be dissolved by discovering further empirical facts or evaluative truths.

Consider a case. A small private university receives an unrestricted bequest and its faculty senate must choose between two uses of the money that cannot be combined: (A) rebuilding and equipping its humanities seminar rooms to support a new discussion-centered first-year curriculum, or (B) expanding its need-based scholarship fund so that twenty additional first-generation students can enroll without taking on extra debt. The senate agrees that both projects serve central commitments of the university, but they draw on different values: the quality of teaching and intellectual formation in the first case, and access to higher education in the second. The committee has full information about enrollment projections, classroom usage, and financial need, and the disagreement persists. Members reject a cost-benefit reckoning, saying that there is no defensible rate at which improved seminar space trades for a debt-free place for a disadvantaged student, but they are also unwilling to call the options simply equal. In a further wrinkle, when someone proposes spending slightly more on A to include a modest faculty-development program, everyone agrees that A would be somewhat better along its own dimension, yet nobody is moved to say that this improved A is now better than B.

Does this case qualify as a hard choice by my four criteria, or does it sit at the edge of the concept and thereby expose tensions in the criteria themselves? Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Items one through four should address respectively whether the case satisfies criterion (a), (b), (c), and (d); item five should give your overall verdict on whether this is a hard choice in the incommensurability sense and what, if anything, the case shows about how the criteria would need to be revised.
