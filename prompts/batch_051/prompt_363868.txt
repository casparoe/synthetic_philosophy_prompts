A confessionally Reformed denomination adopts the regulative principle of worship as an enforceable institutional rule. Its constitution paraphrases the principle this way: public worship may include only those elements expressly commanded in Scripture, and whatever is not commanded is forbidden. To make the rule operational, the denomination creates a Worship Review Board. Any element proposed for the Sunday service — a song, an instrument, a gesture, a video, a testimony, a choreographed processional, a children's segment, a period of silence — must be submitted in advance. The board decides by a two-thirds vote whether the element is expressly commanded in Scripture. If it is, the ruling is published with the proof texts and becomes binding precedent; if it is not, the element is banned from the service. The constitution adds one exception: circumstances of worship, such as lighting, seating, amplification, and service times, are not themselves acts of worship and are not governed by the rule, provided they do not function as elements. A minister who violates a ruling is subject to discipline, up to removal. The board's precedents are public, and the denomination plants congregations in settings with very different inherited worship forms, all subject to the same board.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than a moral position. Analyze how it would actually operate. What would it incentivize among ministers, musicians, laypeople, and board members? How would it be gamed — for example, through the element/circumstance distinction, through creative readings of what counts as commanded, or by placing contested material just outside the service proper? What second-order effects on the denomination's worship, theology, and politics would you expect over several decades? Where does the mechanism fail most predictably, and are those failures fixable by changing the process, or do they follow from the structure of the rule itself?
