I have been turning over a parallel between two impossibility results and cannot tell whether it is more than a family resemblance. Arrow's theorem says that no procedure for aggregating individual preference rankings into a social ranking can satisfy all of the following when at least three alternatives are in play: unrestricted domain (every possible profile of individual rankings is admissible), Pareto (if every individual ranks x above y, the social ranking ranks x above y), independence of irrelevant alternatives (the social comparison of x and y depends only on how individuals rank x and y), and non-dictatorship (no single voter's ranking dictates the social ranking regardless of the others). The CAP theorem from distributed systems says that a distributed data store cannot simultaneously guarantee consistency (every read receives the most recent completed write), availability (every request to a functioning node receives a response), and partition tolerance (the system continues to operate despite arbitrary message loss or delay between nodes); since partitions cannot be wished away, the live trade-off under a partition is standardly said to be between consistency and availability.

The generic affinity is obvious: both results say that a system which takes many inputs and must produce a single coherent output cannot have all three attractive properties at once, and both invite the practical question of which property to sacrifice. I want to know whether anything more exact holds. Is there a principled mapping between the two triples of properties, comparable in precision to the Curry–Howard mapping between proofs and programs? When I try to construct one, it feels forced. Consistency lines up with independence of irrelevant alternatives if both are read as constraints on how local facts compose into a global judgement, but then Pareto has no natural counterpart, and partition tolerance becomes non-dictatorship only as the leftover. I also cannot see what on the distributed-systems side plays the role of unrestricted domain, or of Arrow's insistence that the output be a total order rather than a weaker structure. If no mapping can be made exact, does the analogy still do genuine work? For example, would treating a network partition as an agent whose preferences the system must accommodate, or treating a dictator as the single node through which all ordering constraints flow, correctly predict an otherwise non-obvious theorem on the other side? Or are these two results only united by the fact that both get summarized as trade-offs among three desirable things? I would like a verdict: shared structure, loose analogy, or the word impossibility doing more work than the mathematics.
