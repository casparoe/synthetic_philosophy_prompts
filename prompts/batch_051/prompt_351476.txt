I'm a retired chemist who got into political philosophy about a year ago, and I've fallen in with a reading group whose favourite pastime is testing each others' intuitions on little tax-and-property cases. One member, Miriam (a retired magistrate), is the star of these sessions because she's weirdly consistent, and we write her verdicts down on index cards. We spent part of last month on Nozick, so the Wilt Chamberlain example keeps coming up. So far she says: it's just that Wilt ends up with a far larger income than most people when thousands of fans voluntarily pay a little extra to watch him play, and the resulting inequality is not by itself an injustice. It's also just for a squatter to fence and farm abandoned land, provided there's plenty of equally good land left for others. A burglar's loot is unjust, and if the stolen goods or their value can be traced, they must go back to the original owner's descendants however long ago the theft was. A farmer who honestly earned his land may give a corner of it to a neighbour who helped with the harvest, and it's nobody's business but theirs. But a tax on above-median wages, redistributed as an equal basic income to everyone, she says is unjust because it amounts to forced labour.

Next week's case is this: a woman inherits a large house and a pile of savings from her parents. The parents bought the house and built up the savings honestly, and left everything to her in a valid will. The daughter is, frankly, a bit of a layabout and and has never done a day's work towards that estate.

I think Miriam's track record fits at least two rules: a historical-entitlement rule of the sort Nozick runs (just acquisition plus just transfer, with rectification where either fails), and a desert rule (you're entitled to what you've earned through your own effort and choices), and maybe a third that just says no coercion, no fraud, and wrongs have to be undone. Those rules part company over holdings that are procedurally clean but unearned, which is exactly what next week's case is. So: which rule or rules actually fit all her verdicts, which one is she most plausibly running and why, what would she say about the inheritance case under each plausible rule, and what's your best prediction? And name one further case that would finally tell the rules apart. Please answer in British English.
