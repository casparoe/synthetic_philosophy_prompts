I have no academic training in philosophy, but for the past few years I have been reading and arguing on my own about how we should rank causes and spend limited resources, and I have settled into a position I think of as suffering-focused. In two weeks I am giving a talk at my local effective altruism meetup — around forty people, mostly software engineers, data scientists, and public health workers. They are comfortable with expected-value reasoning, cause prioritization, and rough cost-effectiveness calculations; they are not philosophers, and they are suspicious of positions that sound gloomy, anti-natalist, or hard to defend in public. Several of them are parents or planning to have children, and many already give to both global health and farmed animal welfare.

My thesis, in one paragraph, is this: when we compare causes and decide where to give money or spend our careers, only expected reductions in intense, involuntary suffering should carry weight by default. Positive well-being — additional lives lived above a decent threshold, ordinary contentment, satisfaction from achievement — should enter the comparison only as a tiebreaker between interventions that are roughly equal on expected suffering reduced. I am not claiming that happiness has no value, and I am not claiming that it would be good to eliminate all sentient life. I am claiming that positive value is a different, lower-priority currency, and that a world with much more net-positive experience can still be worse than a smaller world with somewhat less aggregate well-being but much less severe suffering, if severity is tracked separately. In the talk I plan to defend this concrete tradeoff rather than retreat to a vague version of the position: I would not trade away the prevention of one person's year of unrelenting severe pain for adding a decade of ordinary contentment to a thousand already-decent lives, and I think our marginal money should follow that verdict.

My rough argument is: (1) the strong asymmetry we feel between the urgency of relieving pain and the optionality of adding pleasure is not a bias to be corrected but a clue about value; (2) standard expected-value comparisons flatten this asymmetry into a single scale, which makes preventing future happy lives look commensurable with preventing future severe pain; (3) once the two currencies are tracked separately, tradeoffs like the one in the previous paragraph stop looking like close calls; and therefore (4) the burden of proof should rest on anyone who wants to spend scarce resources creating or protecting additional positive lives rather than preventing severe suffering in existing human or non-human lives.
