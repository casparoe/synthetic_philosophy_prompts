I am drawn to the view that free will is, at bottom, the capacity to notice when one is acting on a fixed routine and to interrupt or revise it in light of new circumstances. The view earns its keep by making the Sphex wasp case come out right: the wasp re-enacts its burrow-inspection sequence whenever its immobilized prey is moved, because it cannot step outside its own routine, whereas a free agent can. The view is graded, naturalistic, and requires no contra-causal powers. But I have a firm intuition that the posited capacity is not sufficient. A self-monitoring system that detects that it is cycling through a failing rule and switches to another does exactly what the view asks, yet I would not describe it as free, not even to a small degree. Is this intuition merely an artifact of how we imagine machines, something a defender of the view should debunk? Or does it show that a condition is missing—something like authorship of one's own standards, consciousness, or the right causal structure—and that the view should be amended or replaced? Give the strongest case for each move and a negotiated verdict, including whether there is a reading of the word free on which both the theory and the intuition survive.
