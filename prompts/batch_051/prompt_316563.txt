Last night a friend and I argued at length about panpsychism and the combination problem. She is a committed micropsychist: she holds that the fundamental constituents of matter each have some extremely simple experience, and that the real difficulty is explaining why only certain aggregates of these micro-subjects combine into a unified larger subject. By the end of the argument she had committed herself to a series of judgments: an electron has minimal experience; a granite boulder is not, as a whole, a subject of experience; a single human neuron kept alive in a dish is not a unified subject; a termite colony is not a unified subject despite its coordinated problem-solving; a thermostat is not, as a whole, a subject of experience; a dog is a unified subject; an octopus is a unified subject; an awake adult human is a unified subject; and a large language model is not a unified subject. The case we could not settle is a silicon-based system whose components reproduce, neuron for neuron and synapse for synapse, not merely the input–output behavior of a particular human brain but its internal causal architecture, recurrence, synchrony, and signal timing. What does her existing set of commitments force her to say about this silicon system? I care less about the bare answer than about the principle of subject-combination she appears to be running, the rival principles that fit her recorded judgments equally well, how those principles diverge on the silicon case, and which additional case would best discriminate among them. If her prior judgments genuinely leave the matter open, say what further commitment she would be adopting with each available answer, and give your best guess as to which answer she would accept on grounds of fit with what she has already said.
