Question:
Explain the contractualist objection to interpersonal aggregation. In particular, say whether Scanlon's individualist restriction implies that numbers never count. Distinguish between two cases: (a) a policy that relieves a hundred people's mild headaches by causing one person a severe migraine, and (b) a rescuer who can save either one drowning stranger or five. If what Scanlon should say differs between the cases, explain what accounts for the difference.

Student's answer:
Scanlon's contractualism says an act is wrong just in case it would be disallowed by any set of principles for the general regulation of behavior that no one could reasonably reject. The key point for the aggregation objection is the individualist restriction: you can only appeal to the reasons of individuals, never to the summed weight of many different people's reasons. So a principle that lets a large number of people avoid mild headaches by imposing a severe migraine on one person is reasonably rejectable by that one person. Each headache-sufferer's reason is much weaker than his reason to avoid the migraine, and you're not allowed to add up those weaker reasons until they outweigh his. Contractualism therefore rejects the standard cost-benefit framework people associate with utilitarianism.

By the same logic, numbers should be irrelevant in rescue cases. If I can save either one stranger or five strangers, each of the five has exactly the same kind of claim on me that the one has, and I can't sum their claims because the individualist restriction forbids it. So Scanlon is committed to saying I should be indifferent between saving one and saving five, and the fair procedure is to flip a coin. That's Taurek's position, and Scanlon's framework is basically just a cleaner argument for it. Ultimately this shows Scanlon's contractualism is the separateness-of-persons objection turned into a full moral theory: once you grant that reasons are had by individuals, all interpersonal aggregation is illegitimate, and consequentialism of any sort is out.
