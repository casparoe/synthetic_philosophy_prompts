I am scheduled to give a department colloquium next month, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a polished defense. My thesis is a contemporary version of Russell's eliminativism about causation. I will argue that the ordinary philosophical concept of cause has no legitimate role to play in the fundamental sciences; that the law of causality, as Russell maintained in his 1912 Aristotelian Society address, is a leftover from an earlier stage of thought that survives mainly because it is thought harmless; and that what actually does explanatory work in mature science are differential equations, conservation principles, and structural relations rather than some worldly relation of producing. I intend to defend this through three main moves: a semantic argument that ordinary causal talk is too context-sensitive and anthropocentric to track a single relation; a physical argument that fundamental theories do not quantify over causes and can state their empirical content without causal vocabulary; and a diagnostic argument that much of the contemporary causation literature is a set of verbal disputes carried over from an outdated picture.

The audience is the part I am worried about. The department includes two philosophers of physics, one a Lewisian Humean about laws and one a structural realist who is suspicious of metaphysics but may think my view overstates the relevance of fundamental theory; a philosopher of biology sympathetic to Woodward-style interventionism who will resist treating fundamental physics as the standard for all causal talk; two metaphysicians who defend a powers ontology and regard causal production as irreducible; a historian of early modern philosophy working on occasionalism who will likely hear my argument as a late descendant of Malebranche's critique of creaturely causation; and a Russell specialist who knows the original texts better than I do. The chair is a Kantian ethicist with no stake in metaphysics but a strong interest in whether my position can accommodate causation in action, agency, and moral responsibility. Please draft the prep sheet I will bring into the room: the eight or so objections I am most likely to get from this particular audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased roughly as the person would actually put it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
