I've been trying to get clear about what it means to say that a state has joined something — a customs union, a confederation, a common market — and I find myself caught between two pictures that both seem right but pull in opposite directions.

One picture, which I'll call the bundle view, says that sovereignty is not an all-or-nothing property but a loose package of separable powers: setting an external tariff, controlling the entry of persons, minting currency, concluding treaties, adjudicating disputes, and so on. Joining an association is then just the transfer of some of these powers, under specified decision rules, to a common body. The appeal of this view is that it supplies a single, continuous language for everything from a narrow free-trade area to something almost federal: all membership arrangements are just points in a high-dimensional space defined by two questions for each power — who decides it, and with whose votes. On this picture, joining is not mysterious: some competences move from one box to another, and nothing further is added.

But I cannot shake the intuition that some moves cross a line the bundle view cannot draw. Imagine three states that begin with a modest customs union, then add a court whose rulings bind them even against their own constitutional courts, then adopt a common currency, and finally agree that no member may withdraw without the others' consent. At some point in that sequence I want to say that the members are no longer sovereign in the sense they were before — not that they have handed over a few more items on the list, but that they have changed constitutional kind, because they no longer control the boundaries of the arrangement itself, including who may leave and who has the final word on the limits of the common institutions. The bundle theorist replies that this is exactly the sort of illusion the theory was designed to dissolve: I have singled out two or three especially dramatic competences and invested them with quasi-magical significance, while in reality there is no threshold in the world, only a continuous gradient of pooled authority.

I do not want a verdict, and I am not asking which side wins. I want to know what each resolution costs. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes — for example, a debunking explanation of why losing control over exit and final adjudication feels categorical even though it is just a salient bundle? What would it take for the theory to be amended so that certain thresholds are treated as real, and what would such an amendment leave of the bundle view's continuity and tidiness? And is there a reading on which both survive — one account for the logic of delegated powers, another for the identity over time of a polity — and how would I tell whether that is a genuine reconciliation or merely a relabeling of the two sides?
