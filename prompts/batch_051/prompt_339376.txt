In my course on medieval Jewish philosophy we have spent several sessions on Maimonides' treatment of the sacrificial cult, and one idea has stayed with me long enough that I am trying to test it against a concrete case. The view, as I understand it from the assigned readings, is that the Torah's elaborate system of animal sacrifice was not the ideal form of worship but a concession to human limitation: the Israelites, emerging from an idolatrous environment, were habituated to express devotion through concrete sacrificial rites, and the commandments redirected that entrenched habit toward God rather than demanding a purely spiritual worship for which they were not yet ready. Prayer and intellectual love of God would have been the primary aim; sacrifice was a kind of second-order accommodation meant to carry the community from where it was to where it ought to be.

I want to use this view as a lens on one historical episode: the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE and the subsequent reorientation of Jewish worship around prayer, study, and halakhic observance rather than a central sacrificial cult. On the concessionist reading, this should look almost like the completion of an educational arc: the scaffolding of sacrifice had done its work, its institutional substrate was gone, and the community moved closer to the worship that was supposed to be primary all along. But the historical and liturgical record pulls in the opposite direction. The destruction was experienced and commemorated as a catastrophe rather than a graduation, and the daily liturgy repeatedly petitions God to restore the Temple service and its offerings. So I would like this episode analyzed carefully and honestly through the Maimonidean lens: what does the lens genuinely explain about the transition, what does it misdescribe or quietly pass over, and what does the episode in turn reveal about the theory? In particular, can the view coherently treat sacrificial worship as a temporary concession while the tradition in which Maimonides stands continues to long for its restoration, and while, as I understand it, Maimonides' own legal code still looks forward to the reinstatement of the offerings in the messianic age? Is that tension real, or should the theory be refined, perhaps by distinguishing the historical reason a commandment was introduced from the normative status it retains once legislated, or by allowing that what begins as a concession can become an intrinsically valuable obligation over time?
