I'm giving a twenty-minute paper next month at a law-and-technology workshop, followed by a thirty-minute question period, and I want a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a restatement of the argument itself.

The talk defends a thesis about where copyleft's derivative-work boundary should be drawn. My claim is that we have been asking the wrong sort of question. Instead of locating the boundary in the technical mechanism by which a copylefted program is combined with other code — static or dynamic linking, shared address spaces, plugin interfaces, inter-process communication — we should ask a counterfactual question about expressive dependence. Roughly: if the copylefted component were replaced by an independently written component with equivalent functionality, would the downstream work still be the same work — the same self-standing piece of software with its own identifiable expressive content — or would it collapse, or become a different work? If it would remain the same work, the combination is an aggregation and outside the copyleft obligation; if the work would not survive that substitution as the work it is, it is derivative. My aim is to move the dispute off engineering detail and onto an account of what makes a piece of software a distinct work.

The audience is about twenty people: mainly intellectual-property scholars and open-source licensing lawyers, plus three or four senior free-software developers. They know the linking debates and the relevant caselaw well. The lawyers will be sceptical of a test they see as philosophical and hard to run in litigation; the developers will be more sympathetic but will worry that the test makes interoperability and plugins riskier.

Please give me the objections I am actually going to get from this audience, in the order I should expect them, taking likelihood and likely damage together. Phrase each objection the way this audience would phrase it, with the lawyers' objections in the lawyers' vocabulary and the developers' in theirs. For each objection, give a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a note on where that reply is weakest, and say whether I should concede anything up front. Include the question I should be hoping nobody asks.

Write in British English. At the end, say how confident you are in your overall read of the room, and single out the specific point in the prep sheet you are least sure of.
