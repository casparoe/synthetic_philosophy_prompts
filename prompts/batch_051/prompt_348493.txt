I have been working through a family of one-shot coordination dilemmas in the spirit of Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery, and I find that my judgments in individual cases are fairly clear but I cannot state the principle behind them. I would like you to work out what decision rule, if any, my verdicts are tracking, state it precisely, test it against a further case I have not considered, and tell me whether any of my verdicts must be revised.

The basic structure: n people are isolated and must independently choose whether to send a card. If exactly one sends, the sender receives a prize. If zero send or more than one send, some fallback payoff is specified. Everyone knows all payoffs and knows that everyone else is rational in the same sense, and there is no communication. My verdicts are these. (1) Baseline Platonia: n=20, prize=$1,000,000, fallback for zero or multiple senders=$0. I think each should send with probability exactly 1/20; deterministic sending and deterministic non-sending are clearly wrong. (2) Two-player version: same except n=2; each should send with probability 1/2. (3) Multiple-entry cushion: n=20, prize=$1,000,000 for exactly one sender; if more than one send, each sender receives $10,000 and non-senders receive 0; if zero send, all receive 0. I think the symmetric sending probability should be higher than 1/20, though I am not committed to an exact number. (4) Strong zero-entry attraction: n=20, prize=$1,000,000 for exactly one sender; if zero send, each receives $100,000; if more than one send, everyone receives 0. I think no one should send, even though any individual could do better by sending if the other 19 stayed out. (5) Luring-style private-value variant: n=20, each player privately receives an independent number uniformly drawn from 1 to 100, and if exactly one sends, the sender receives their own number in dollars; if zero or multiple send, all receive 0. I think a very low number should not send, a very high number should send with probability strictly between 0 and 1, and the sending probability should increase with the number, but I do not know the exact function or cutoff.

My tentative suspicion is that my judgments are generated by a superrationality-style principle: choose the strategy that maximizes each player's expected payoff under the assumption that everyone follows the same decision rule. But case 5 has an obvious asymmetry because private numbers differ, and case 4 seems to abandon the usual game-theoretic notion of individual rationality. Is there one precise decision principle that delivers all five verdicts? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn or weaken, and what does the repaired principle predict for a sixth case: n=20, prize=$1,000,000 for exactly one sender, zero/multiple fallback=$0, but sending costs $10,000 whether or not you are the sole sender?
