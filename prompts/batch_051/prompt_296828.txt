I serve on the Emergency Measures Review Commission of a parliamentary democracy. We review executive orders issued under the emergency provisions and either let them stand or annul them. Over the past three years, our rulings have been these:

1. We let stand a nationwide lockdown ordered after a prior authorizing vote in parliament, with a 30-day expiry, full judicial review of its substance, published epidemiological modeling, and uniform application across the country.

2. We let stand, with conditions, a border closure ordered while parliament was in recess and without a prior vote. It expired after 14 days; the government notified parliament's health committee within 48 hours and scheduled a ratification vote before the expiry date. The stated basis was that a novel variant had already been detected among arriving travelers.

3. We annulled a regional night curfew issued under the emergency clause of an ordinary public-order statute. It had no expiry date, applied to an entire region, was issued without publishing any analysis of its necessity, and permitted judicial review only of the formal regularity of the order, not of its substance or proportionality.

4. We annulled a set of travel restrictions that applied only to districts in which one ethnic minority forms the majority, while comparable districts with majority-ethnic populations were left unrestricted.

5. We annulled ministerial orders closing schools and banning assemblies, issued through ordinary administrative powers rather than the emergency framework. The ministry said the situation did not warrant a formal declaration of an emergency, yet the orders were stricter than some measures we had reviewed under the emergency provisions; they were issued with no published necessity assessment and no expiry date.

Next week we decide the following matter. The executive has ordered a 21-day suspension of non-essential travel between regions, on the basis of hospital-capacity projections showing a collapse within roughly three weeks if movement is not reduced. Parliament is in recess and was not recalled before the order took effect. The order lapses after 21 days, is open to full judicial review, and the government has scheduled a retrospective ratification vote for the fifth day of the suspension. The projection model is published, and the order applies to all regions alike. The same model indicates that a two-week voluntary reduction in travel, if widely followed, would achieve most of the projected benefit. The government's legal service says the order satisfies every textual requirement of the emergency statute; the live question is whether it meets our standards for a legitimate emergency measure.

What should we decide if we want to be most consistent with our own precedents? Give me a recommended disposition (let stand, let stand with conditions, or annul); the precedent that is our closest analog and why; the distinct rules that fit all five of our past rulings and what each rule predicts for this measure; and an honest statement of which general principle we would be adopting with each choice. Finally, name one further case that would best discriminate among the rules this measure leaves open.
