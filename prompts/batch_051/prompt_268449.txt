For a seminar I'm calling "Knowing Yourself," I want a sorting instrument — a which-position-do-you-actually-hold quiz with a scoring key — on views about when self-examination is worthwhile and when it curdles into rumination. It should sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they like, so no item should just ask whether introspection is good for you.

Build it from the logical space first. Here are the distinctions I suspect actually separate the positions; your job is to test each one against a small set of sensible positions and see which deserve a branch: (a) past-directed vs. future-directed attention; (b) whether the person has a stopping rule or an open-ended "why am I like this" loop; (c) whether the inquiry is instrumental — aimed at a decision or change — or whether it's become a way of postponing action; (d) whether the target of attention is something inside one's control; (e) whether the output is treated as discovered fact or as a story one constructs; (f) whether the process is affectively hot (replaying distress) or cool (curious observation).

Leaves I want at minimum: a virtue-epistemic view (self-examination is a skill: good when it tracks truth and is governed by a stopping rule, bad when it's idle); a narrative view (good self-examination is future-directed construction, bad when it curates the past); a process or "defusion" view (the content matters less than whether the inquiry is being used to act or to avoid acting); an anti-introspectionist view (the outputs are mostly confabulated, so the harm is in crediting them); and a Stoic-leaning view (the line is whether the rehearsal concerns what is up to you). Add one or two more if the space demands them, but don't multiply leaves just to fill boxes. Mark any unoccupied combinations of answers and say, briefly, whether each looks incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Use concrete cases, including at least: a person who replays a social faux pas for days; the nightly journaler "getting to the bottom" of her moods; a fixed twenty-minute worry window; a Stoic-style negative visualization about losing one's job; a charitable donor who has analyzed her motives until she can't tell altruism from status; a griever going over her last conversations with the deceased; and one person who decides to stop asking "why am I like this" and instead asks "what should I do next." Feel free to adjust them. Include at least one case where two of your positions would give the same answer, and then try to find a third case that splits them.

The quiz itself should be eight to ten items, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto your leaves. Bake the rumination-vs-reflection distinction into the cases rather than naming it in the questions — my students won't know it, and it does a lot of work. Audience is sharp undergraduates who've read Montaigne but no technical psychology. Flag any pair of positions your final quiz cannot really seperate, and say what kind of case would split them if one exists.
