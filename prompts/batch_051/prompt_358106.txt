I've been turning an argument over in my head and I'd like an expert philosophers take on it. The argument is the usual one from moral disagreement: look at how wildly moral codes differ across times and places — slavery, honor killing, cousin marriage, blood feuds, what counts as fair. If there were objective moral facts, you'd expect people to eventually converge on them, like they mostly do with math. The best explanation of all this persistent disagreement, so the argument goes, is that there aren't any objective moral facts; there's just whatever each culture came to accept. I'm simplifying but that's the shape of it.

Lately I've been reading about the WEIRD problem in psychology: a large share of the research on moral psychology comes from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic subjects — often college undergrads — and studies in small-scale and non-Western societies have shown that a lot of what looked like human universals doesn't replicate. Some of the most dramatic cross-cultural differences seem to come from bad translations, loaded scenarios, or researchers treating their own WEIRD responses as the neutral baseline. And there's another wrinkle: newer work suggests that underneath the variation, cultures may be tuning up and down the same handful of basic moral concerns rather than inventing values from scratch.

So the question I want help with is: does the argument from moral disagreement survive that? I am not asking whether moral realism is true in the end. I'm asking what the WEIRD development does to the argument itself. Which premise does it actually pressure — the premise that disagreement is as deep as people advertise, the premise that disagreement is best explained by there being no objective facts, or something else? Does the argument die, does it just get weaker, or does it survive because enough real disagreement remains after you correct for all the WEIRD artifacts?

Please take on the role of a moral philosopher who knows this debate well. Think carefully, and start by laying out the strongest version of the argument before deciding where the WEIRD stuff bites. If the argument survives, tell me which remaining disagreements are doing the work and why they're enough to matter. If it needs a repair, give the repaired version and say exactly what it can and can't support anymore. And be honest if the original version was already shaky before any of the WEIRD research showed up.
