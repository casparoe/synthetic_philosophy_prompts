This is about as basic as source criticism gets, but I keep finding the criterion surprisingly fuzzy in practice. Give me a working definition of an 'independent source,' as in the usual rule that a claim about the past is on firmer ground when it rests on two independent sources rather than two sources that are secretly one. Then run your definition against these cases and tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer:

- two handwritten copies of the same chronicle;
- a chronicle and an inscription in which the same king gives his own version of the same campaign;
- two annals from different monasteries that both mention a famine, but the monks are known to have exchanged letters every year;
- a chronicle describing a city burning and a layer of ash in the archaeology dating to the same decade;
- a later biography that we can show silently paraphrases an earlier biography for whole chapters;
- an oral tradition written down by an ethnographer in the 1920s and a colonial official's report saying he heard the same story in the same village.

I suspect 'independence' is doing several jobs at once — separate chains of transmission, separate kinds of evidence, separate interests or agendas. Dont just label each case independent or not; tell me what principle does the classifying, where the definition breaks, and which of your verdicts you'd defend least confidently.
