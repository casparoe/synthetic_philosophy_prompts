I'm sketching an essay called (provisionally) "thought experiments are just in-distribution generalization," and I'd like comments before I sink more time into it. Below is the current shape of the argument; I'm more interested in whether it's right than whether it's novel.

The standard puzzle: how does someone in an armchair learn anything about the world just by imagining? Thought-experiment fans usually point to the logic. Galileo doesn't have to drop two balls off a tower; he can tie a light stone to a heavy stone and show the Aristotelian view generates a contradiction. The conclusion seems to follow from premises that are just obvious.

My line is that the logic is the least important part. The "obvious" premises are doing all the work, and they're obvious only because they're compressed regularities from experience. The falling-bodies case only works because we already know from a lifetime of dropped and thrown things that heavy stuff doesn't systematically pull ahead of light stuff in free fall. So when Galileo asks us to imagine the tied stones, we run the scenario on an internal model that experience already trained, and the contradiction shows up as a feature of that model. Logic is just the user interface on top of the simulation.

That re-frames the standard question. Instead of "how can armchair thinking tell us about the world?", the question becomes "how did our armchair get a model this good, and when does the model stop being reliable?" And that's an empirical question about the origins and limits of trained intuition.

The view predicts something testable-ish: thought experiments should work where our internal model approximates the target domain and go wrong exactly where it doesn't. That matches the record, I think. Newton's bucket and Einstein's elevator run on spatial and mechanical intuitions that track the relevant physics. Quantum mechanics and special relativity are graveyards of thought experiments because the internal model has no gears for superposition or the invariance of light speed. The Bohr–Einstein debates look like Bohr saying "your simulator wasn't trained on this distribution, stop trusting it," and Einstein replying "a good simulator should generalize further than that."

Where I'm stuck: is this just old empiricism with "trained model" taped on? What does the new language actually buy? I want it to buy a criterion for when to trust a thought experiment, which the armchair-rationalist story doesn't give. But I can't tell if that's real or just a vibe.

Specific things I'd like comments on: (1) Is there a thought experiment that yields scientific knowledge while being genuinely out-of-distribution for prior experience, in a way that would break this? (2) Am I smuggling in the conclusion when I say the premises are compressed experience, or could I show it for the Galileo case without circularity? (3) Does the view handle thought experiments that work as reductios against a theory rather than by simulating the world, or do I need a second mechanism? (4) wether the whole thing is interesting or just a restatement.
