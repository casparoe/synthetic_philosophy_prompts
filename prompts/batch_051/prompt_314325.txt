I have been trying to think through Locke's story about property: that the earth was given to human beings in common, that a person makes part of it their own by mixing their labour with it, and that appropriation is bounded by the bar against letting things spoil and, more importantly, by the proviso that one's taking must leave enough, and as good, for others. The familiar criticism is that the theory is a blank cheque for enclosure; the familiar defence is that it is actually a constraint on exactly that. I want to test both readings against one historical episode rather than through the standard abstract examples.

Take the English enclosure movement, especially the parliamentary enclosures of the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, though earlier piecemeal enclosures may enter where they matter. The salient facts, as I understand them, are these: common fields and wastes carried customary rights of grazing, gleaning, and gathering fuel or turf, held by villagers who often had little or no formal title; enclosure, whether by private agreement, by Act of Parliament, or by force, extinguished those rights and converted the land into private consolidated holdings, sometimes with modest allotments or compensation to some commoners and often with effectively nothing for the poorest; the enclosed land was then hedged, drained, and turned to more profitable uses such as sheep pasture. I do not need more historical narrative than this; I need the theory.

Please approach this as a political philosopher working on the history of property theory, and think carefully. Begin by stating the specific test Locke's account would impose on an enclosure: what would have to be true of the land, the labour, the spoilage constraint, and the proviso for an enclosure to count as a legitimate original acquisition, or, where the claim is that common rights were themselves property, for their extinguishing to be justified? Then run the historical case through that test as honestly as you can. Which enclosures pass, which fail, and where does the theory go silent because the actual facts are not the kind of facts it tracks — for instance, the difference between formal title and customary use, the role of compensation and coercion, and the difference between genuinely improving land and merely excluding people from it? Finally, reverse direction: does the episode confirm the reading of Locke as an apologist for expropriation, confirm the reading of him as a critic of it, or show instead that the theory was built for an imaginary pre-monetary world and cannot carry the weight of a commercializing countryside? If the case exposes a real defect or ambiguity, be specific about which commitment of the theory would need to be changed or abandoned.
