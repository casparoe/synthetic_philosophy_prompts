Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*, Book III, chapter 6, contains the following passage:

‘But even death, we should hold, does not in all circumstances give an opportunity for Courage: for instance we do not call a man courageous for facing death by drowning or disease. What form of death then is a test of Courage? Presumably that which is the noblest. Now the noblest form of death is death in battle, for it is encountered in the midst of the greatest and most noble of dangers.’

And shortly afterwards:

‘Also Courage is shown in dangers where a man can defend himself by valor or die nobly, but neither is possible in disasters like shipwreck.’

Now consider this case. A 43-year-old office worker, with no military or emergency-services training, is on a commuter train when a bomb explodes. The carriage fills with smoke and flame. He helps several passengers out onto the track, then twice returns into the smoke to carry unconscious people clear. On his third return he collapses and dies of smoke inhalation. There is no combatant, no weapon, and no possibility of defending himself by force; the danger is suffocation and fire rather than an enemy.

Apply Aristotle’s criteria from the quoted passage to this man. Is he courageous in the proper sense, or only courageous by analogy, as Aristotle says of those who face illness or storms at sea? Work through each criterion honestly: the mean between fear and confidence; the restriction to a noble death or some sudden peril that threatens death; the exclusion of deaths like drowning and disease; and the further condition that one must be able to defend oneself by valour or die nobly. Where the man fails a criterion, say whether that is because the criterion has force or because Aristotle’s account has been shaped too narrowly by the paradigm of the citizen-soldier. Finally, state which criterion, if any, you would amend, and what that amendment would commit you to about other cases, such as firefighters, pandemic nurses, or people who die resisting armed attackers in peacetime.
