I am a novelist, fluent in the working vocabulary of my craft: I think of a word's many uses the way I think of a character's appearances across scenes, of register as costume or voice, of connotation as atmosphere, of semantic drift over time as revision across a serial, and of the lexicon as a cast of recurring figures. I want you to restate, in that vocabulary, the main positions in the debate over polysemy and semantic range — whether a word such as note (a sounded pitch, a written message, an undertone) or grace (elegance, divine favor, a reprieve, an ornamental flourish) has one meaning covering all its uses, several related meanings, or no standing meaning at all.

Writers on language and lexicography divide roughly five ways: a word stores one core meaning from which its uses derive; a word stores a small structured set of related senses; unrelated senses are simply different words that happen to share a form; there is no fixed core or boundary, only overlapping family resemblances among uses; a word has no standing meaning, only what each context supplies.

Restate each of those five positions, one per numbered item, as precisely as you can in my craft vocabulary. For each, say what translates cleanly, what had to be bent or fudged, and what has no counterpart in my way of thinking about a word's life through a book. Do not smuggle my craft vocabulary's assumptions into the translation: if my way of thinking treats a word as nothing over and above its appearances, flag where that is doing the arguing rather than being argued to. Within the fifth item, deliver the verdict on the residue as a whole: is my craft vocabulary missing something the positions see, or does its resistance show that some of those positions were less clear than they looked?

Format: exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no concluding paragraph; the fifth item should both translate the final position and carry the verdict on the residue.
