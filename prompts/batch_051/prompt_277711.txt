For the past several months I have been reading through the social-choice literature on rights, and I have been struck by how little the interpretive debate around Amartya Sen's liberal paradox has moved since the early 1970s. The formal result itself is not in dispute: with an unrestricted domain, a weak Pareto principle and a minimal assignment of decisiveness over personal choices to just two individuals can produce a cycle in the social ranking, as in the familiar Lady Chatterley case, where one person's claim to decide whether he reads a book and another's claim to decide whether she reads it cannot be jointly respected by any social choice rule that also honors unanimity in preferences. What has remained stuck is the lesson. One camp treats the theorem as showing that rights should never have been modeled as conditions on a social preference relation; rights, on this view, are better represented as constraints on the set of admissible outcomes or as game forms that assign individuals control over which alternatives may arise, and once they are represented that way the paradox dissolves. A second camp treats the theorem as a problem for the Pareto principle rather than for rights: if meddlesome preferences about other people's private choices are screened out as inadmissible inputs to aggregation, then unanimity over the remaining preferences is compatible with liberal decisiveness after all. A third camp treats the paradox as revealing something wrong with the social-choice question itself, namely, that it tries to capture what a procedure may legitimately decide by ranking states of affairs rather than by asking what it is for a person to have a protected sphere of action in the first place.

I am not asking which camp is correct but for a diagnosis of why the disagreement itself has this structure. Is it a verbal dispute, a dispute about which formalization is faithful to the concept of a right, a dispute about what social choice theory is for, a hidden normative dispute about whether non-welfarist considerations may enter the theory, or something else? My working hypothesis is that the three camps are largely answering different questions rather than contradicting one another—one an institutional-design question, one a question about the proper domain of the Pareto principle, one a question about the nature of a right—but I suspect this diagnosis is too charitable, since the camps do seem to make incompatible claims about whether a permissive Pareto principle and liberal rights can coexist. I would like you to assess that hypothesis against what each side actually says, and then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, a clarification of the question, or a reason to expect no convergence at all.
