I chair the ethics subcommittee of a university's animal care and use committee. Each month we get perhaps a dozen research protocols involving animals — mostly rats, mice, and pigeons in behavioral neuroscience, but occasionally dogs, farm animals, and wild birds in field studies. We have ninety minutes per meeting, our members range from a neurobiologist to a veterinarian to a philosophy lecturer to a community member, and we lack a shared framework: one member treats animal suffering as intrinsically bad, another treats only human interests as grounding constraints, and the rest vote case by case. I have been reading Kant and would like us to use his view as our working framework. As I understand the view: non-human animals are not rational ends in themselves, so we have no direct duties to them; still, how we treat them matters morally, because cruelty toward an animal weakens the sympathetic dispositions we need in order to do our duties to human beings, and kindness toward animals can strengthen those dispositions. Duties regarding animals are thus indirect duties to humanity, including duties to ourselves. On this view, using animals as means is permitted when the end justifies the use, but inflicting pain for amusement or out of hardheartedness is not; and the decisive question about any painful procedure is not what the animal is owed, but what the practice does to the human agents and to their capacity for sympathy.

Write me a reusable decision procedure our subcommittee could actually run on a protocol: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure returns a clear no, where it approves with conditions, and where it must stop and hand the decision to a person because the next step is a judgment call or a values question the doctrine itself cannot settle. Be explicit about which Kantian distinctions have to survive the simplification (for example, direct versus indirect duty, and cruelty versus instrumental use) and which can be dropped for a committee that is not doing philosophy.

Then run the procedure on three proposals: (a) a psychology lab proposes to food-deprive rats to motivate them in a maze study, then euthanize them afterward; (b) a veterinary lab proposes to surgically induce osteoarthritis in dogs to test a new analgesic, keeping the dogs for six months and then rehoming them; (c) an ecology lab proposes mist-netting wild songbirds for banding, knowing that some birds will be injured or killed in the nets. Say explicitly where the procedure yields an answer the committee will find uncomfortable, and what the procedure cannot do (for example, vindicate the Kantian doctrine itself against a utilitarian or an animal-rights member). End with a single line beginning 'Summary:' giving a one-sentence statement of the procedure's operative principle.
