I work for a nonpartisan civic-research foundation that advises city councils on whether to replace first-past-the-post elections for their councils. We review two or three such proposals a month, every recommendation must be signed off by our board before it leaves the office, and the stakes are high: cities rarely revisit an electoral system for at least a decade once it is adopted, and a bad recommendation becomes public.

The board has adopted a working principle from which I am not allowed to depart. The decisive defect of first-past-the-post is a translation failure: a party's share of council seats can diverge sharply from its share of votes, so some voters carry far more influence than others over the partisan composition of the council. Any acceptable replacement must substantially close that gap. But the board also insists that, wherever feasible, each geographic ward should keep a named councilor who is directly accountable to it. Where the two goals cannot both be fully met, the loss of ward accountability must be treated as a value choice for the elected council to make, not as a technical detail our staff settle quietly.

I need a single decision procedure that our staff, none of whom have formal training in social choice theory, can actually run in under two hours on any proposal before it reaches the board. Please write the procedure as a sequence of questions to be asked in order. For each question, give four things: (i) a one-line test the analyst is applying; (ii) what counts as a sufficient answer to pass; (iii) what answer fails the proposal outright; and (iv) where the procedure must stop and refer the matter to the council because the remaining issue is a conflict of values rather than a factual or technical one. Include a short initial screen that rejects a proposal within fifteen minutes when it cannot possibly satisfy the board's principle, and mark every step at which the procedure hands a decision to a person rather than to the checklist. The procedure should explicitly catch systems that promise proportionality but blunt it through thresholds, district sizes, or list formulas that quietly recreate the vote-seat gap.

Then run the procedure on the following three proposals:

(a) a pure closed-list proportional system: one citywide district, voters choose a party list, seats are allocated in proportion to each party's citywide vote, with a five percent threshold and no ward representatives;

(b) a mixed-member proportional system: half the seats are elected from single-member wards by first-past-the-post and half are allocated from party lists so that the overall seat shares match each party's vote shares;

(c) single-member wards using preferential voting, with voters ranking candidates within each ward; this preserves ward-level contests but is not designed to make each party's seat share proportional to its vote share.

For the procedure to be trusted inside the organization, at least one of these applications must return an answer our board will not like. For each application, show where the procedure stops, what it passes or rejects, what it sends to the council, and in what terms. End by stating what the procedure cannot decide, where it will systematically misfire, and why those limits are better handled by a person than by a checklist.
