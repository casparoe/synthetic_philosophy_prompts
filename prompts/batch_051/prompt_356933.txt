In my epistemology class we've been doing higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, and in section my TA gave this summary that I've been turning over. Here it is, as close to his words as I remember:

"Epistemic akrasia is when you believe something even though your evidence doesn't support it — like weakness of will, but for belief instead of action. Almost everyone agrees this is irrational, for basically the same reason weakness of will is. The standard argument is: suppose you take a drug that makes most people bad at a certain kind of reasoning, and then you reason your way to some conclusion. Your evidence now includes the fact that you're probably unreliable at exactly that kind of reasoning, so of course you have to lower your confidence in the conclusion. Higher-order evidence is just ordinary evidence about your own reliability, so the only real debate is how much to lower your confidence, not weather you should lower it. Anyone who says you can rationally believe something while also believing your evidence doesn't support it is basically defending wishful thinking."

I can't shake the sense that something in there is off. I could've swore one of our readings mentioned people arguing that akratic states can sometimes be rational, or at least that a blanket "never be akratic" rule has weird self-undermining consequences, so the "almost everyone agrees" part might be too quick. But my TA is a grad student and he might just be compressing. Could you go through this paragraph and tell me which parts a careful epistemologist would accept, which parts are subtly misleading, and what a defender of the view he's dismissing would actually say? I'd also like to know whether the drug example really establishes the conclusion he draws from it, or whether it looks different once you separate the uncontroversial core from the stuff that's actually disputed.
