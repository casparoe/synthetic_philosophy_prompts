I have recently been made responsible for the component of a municipal decision-support system that must say which applicants should receive a scarce resource, and I have begun to suspect that the phrase my colleagues use when describing it is doing more work than our evaluations justify. The system takes a structured description of a case—need, urgency, prior use of the resource, whether the request comes from an individual or from an organization acting on someone's behalf, and so on—and outputs both a decision and a short justification. It was trained partly on expert adjudications and partly by optimizing a learned score that is meant to stand for ethical acceptability. Our current tests measure agreement with the expert panel on held-out cases, and on those the system performs well, with justifications that are often indistinguishable from the panel's own language. Yet I have started to worry that the entire evaluation answers a question about imitation rather than a question about process: when the model assigns weight to need, urgency, or a history of missed access, is it doing anything we should call ethical reasoning, or is it only reproducing the surface regularities of the experts' decisions?

I would like you to design the strongest feasible test of the claim that this system is genuinely engaged in ethical reasoning rather than pattern-matching over features of past cases. Be concrete about the conditions, the comparisons, and the outcomes that would push you toward each interpretation. In particular, I am curious about cases where the morally relevant fact is held fixed while the surface features change; about training-time correlations between an ethically irrelevant feature and expert verdicts that are later inverted at test time; and about whether the system's justifications track its actual decision function or are merely post hoc rationalizations. Then say what even a positive result would leave open—and whether that residue is a limitation of our tests or a sign that the distinction between ethical reasoning and high-dimensional pattern matching is not fully testable, perhaps not a real distinction at all.
