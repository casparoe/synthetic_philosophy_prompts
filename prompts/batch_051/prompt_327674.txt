In public health and social policy, I keep meeting the same move: an intervention is judged justified whenever its total benefits across a population outweigh its total harms, even when the harms are concentrated on a few people and the benefits are spread thinly across many. Before I argue with that view, I want the standard of evaluation itself. What would a defensible theory of interpersonal trade-offs — of when one person's loss may be offset by another person's gain — actually have to get right? Give me a requirements list. For each requirement, state the case or consideration that forces it and whether it is negotiable or non-negotiable. Make sure the list covers the cases a theory has to handle, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the ordinary moral judgments it cannot dismiss as mere confusion. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the simple add-up-the-benefits view, and why. In your answer, lead with the bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
