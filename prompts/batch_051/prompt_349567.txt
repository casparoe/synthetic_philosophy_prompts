I'm drafting the call for a special issue of a comparative constitutional law journal on interregna and constitutional gaps, and the editors want a real agenda, not a survey. The territory I mean is: the written rules run out or malfunction—the head of state dies with no surviving successor, an election is postponed and the old parliament's mandate expires, a prime minister loses confidence and no alternative can be assembled, the court that would referee all of it has been packed or is boycotted. We have room for six or seven long articles and a few short responses, and I have to justify the priorities to a board that's skittish about the topic just becoming crisis case studies.

Suppose you're setting the agenda. Which three or four open questions or lines of work should the issue center, and in what order? I'm less interested in the list than the defense: for each, why it's upstream of other work, how tractable it is for people doing legal and political theory without field interviews or datasets, and whether an answer would actually change what drafters write or what acting governments do in the gap. Also say what would move something down your ranking—for instance, if courts increasingly treat these disputes as non-justiciable, or if messy transitions in established democracies keep resolving through unwritten caretaker norms without anyone invoking the text.

One thing I keep going back and forth on is whether the field should finally clean up the distinction between a genuine gap (nobody thought to specify X), a deliberate silence (drafters chose not to specify X), and a failed default (they specified X and X broke). Is that worth making the spine of the issue, or is it taxonomy that doesn't bite? And are there subtopics people love in this area that you'd deliberately leave out because they eat up space without changing anything?
