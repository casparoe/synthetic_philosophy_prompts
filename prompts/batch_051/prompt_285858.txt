After a long argument with a friend about the ethics of voting, I still cannot tell exactly what principle she has been applying, and I want to work out whether her prior rulings commit her to a verdict in a new case. She insists she is being consistent, and I have kept notes on the hypotheticals we discussed.

Her past rulings were as follows.

1. In a high-stakes general election in a dead-heat district, candidate A is, by the voter's own lights, clearly better than candidate B, though A supports one policy the voter regards as unjust but not a grave moral wrong. She ruled that the voter has a duty to vote for A, and that a protest write-in for a third candidate with no chance of winning would be impermissible.

2. In the same election, with the same candidates and the same policy views, the voter's district is projected to go to A by 25 points. She ruled that the voter has no duty to vote for A; a protest write-in or abstention would be permitted.

3. In the same election, the district is projected to go to B by 25 points. She ruled the same way: no duty to vote for A, and a write-in or abstention is permitted.

4. In a high-stakes dead-heat election, the voter regards the two major candidates as morally indistinguishable, with no policy differences significant enough to choose between them. She ruled that the voter has no duty to vote for either, and the ballot is discretionary.

5. In a dead-heat local election with trivial stakes, she ruled that there is no duty to vote at all; the vote would be purely expressive and may be skipped.

6. In a high-stakes dead-heat election, the voter is well informed, rightly judges one candidate better, and faces only ordinary inconvenience in going to the polls. She ruled that the inconvenience does not cancel the duty; the voter must turn out and vote for the better candidate.

The new case is this: a small municipal council election with roughly 300 eligible voters, where turnout is low and a single ballot is frequently decisive. The voter regards candidate A as clearly better than candidate B on most domestic policy, but both candidates support a measure the voter considers a grave moral wrong; A would administer it only somewhat less harshly. The voter wants to write in a third candidate who rejects the measure outright, knowing the write-in cannot win. What would my friend say?

Please predict her verdict, identify the principle or principles that would make all six earlier rulings cohere, say which earlier case is the closest analog and why, and explain what she would be committing herself to with each available answer. If the earlier rulings underdetermine the new case, say so explicitly, and state the further hypothetical that would settle which principle she actually holds.
