I've got a thought-experiment design problem for you. I want a torture case, but not the standard ticking bomb where everyone either converges or just digs in. I want it built so that a threshold deontologist comes out more permissive than an act-utilitarian: the threshold deontologist says the coercive interrogation is permissible becuase the threshold is clearly crossed, while the act-utilitarian says it isn't, because the expected consequences of using it are worse than refraining. And I want the full delivery: lay out the scenario, then show the reasoning for each theory seperately, and make sure the verdicts actually fall out of the theories rather than being tacked on at the end.

Some constraints. The bomb has to be real and about to kill a lot of people, and the detainee has to genuinely know where it is. Using coercive interrogation has to be the only option with a realistic chance of getting the location in time, but no perfect lie detection, no magic, no science-fictional tech. Don't make the utilitarian forbid torture by simply declaring that it won't work—the expected-value calculation should come from ordinary, realistic probability estimates and from the kind of costs a real act-utilitarian would be expected to count: misleading information that sends responders the wrong way, blowback, recruitment effects, institutional precedent, whatever you think does the real work. Also don't give the deontologist a cheap version where only a nuclear exchange crosses the threshold; use a threshold that a serious reconstruction of threshold deontology would accept for a bomb this size.

Then do the post-mortem. Identify the one structural feature of the case that does the separating work—I'd guess it's something about which considerations each theory lets into the decision, or how they each treat a small probability, but if it's something else say so. Say what happens if you remove that feature: does it collapse back into the standard case where the utilitarian is the permissive one and the deontologist is the squeamish one? Finally, give the attack: how would a defender of the act-utilitarian verdict go after the threshold deontologist's reasoning here? Would they say the deontologist is quietly doing expected-value reasoning with made-up numbers, or that they're being insensitive to the difference between a tiny chance and a decent chance? And what would the deontologist say back?
