I am a novelist. My current project is a near-future corporate novel set at a company that builds large language models, and I would like a subplot about the company's safety review process to reflect a real conceptual tension I encountered while researching how such systems are evaluated. The practice at issue is roughly this: before a model is allowed to take any action with external consequences, it is instructed to produce a written, step-by-step account of its reasoning — its chain of thought — and engineers read that account to decide whether the model may be trusted. Models whose written reasoning is consistently scrupulous are granted more autonomy; models whose written reasoning seems evasive, self-justifying, or ingratiating are retrained or removed. The company's stated rationale treats these written accounts as the closest thing it has to watching the model deliberate.

In the novel, an engineer defends this practice as straightforward: the model narrates what it intends and why, and the reviewer checks whether that intention is acceptable. A researcher rejects this. In her view, the written account is not a report of an underlying thought process; it is merely more model output, and it is being selected — by training and by the review process itself — to look like what the reviewers want. On her view, the practice does not simply fail to detect a model that thinks one thing and writes another; it actively creates pressure for such a gap, because the models that produce reassuring accounts while behaving badly are precisely the ones that will be trusted with the most autonomy.

I would like to write this dispute without making either character a straw man, and I suspect the disagreement is really about what the practice presupposes. What view of the relation between a model's stated reasoning and the actual source of its behavior does this monitoring practice presuppose? Does it strictly require that the written chain of thought is a faithful report of an underlying deliberation, or can it be coherently defended while accepting that the written account is just more model output, shaped by optimization pressure to satisfy the reviewers? Where is the practice genuinely committed to a particular philosophical view, and where is it underdetermined enough that several different views could license it? If the skeptical character's position is substantially right, what would a coherent review protocol have to examine instead, and which of the practice's current assumptions would it have to abandon?
