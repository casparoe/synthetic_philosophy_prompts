I am a teaching assistant for a course on collective decision-making and social epistemology, and I would like a second, careful read on a grading task. Below is the question as set on the problem set, followed by one student's answer.

Question:

State the classical Condorcet jury theorem and identify its main assumptions. Then assess the following claim: the theorem gives us reason to think that, in real-world political decisions, a large electorate will generally track the truth better than a smaller panel of informed experts. Be precise about what does and does not carry over from the theorem to real-world settings.

Student's answer:

The Condorcet jury theorem says that if n voters (n odd, for definiteness) choose between two alternatives, exactly one of which is correct, and each voter independently has probability p of voting for the correct alternative, then when p > 0.5, the probability that a simple majority votes correctly is greater than p and converges to 1 as n grows. Symmetrically, when p < 0.5, the probability that a simple majority votes correctly is less than p and converges to 0. This supplies the main epistemic justification for majority rule. It follows that, as long as the average voter is better than chance, adding further independent voters can never lower the group's probability of being correct, and in general simple majority rule is the optimal way to aggregate the available individual judgments, since each voter contributes an independent, equally weighted draw from the same signal.

Applied to politics, the theorem therefore gives a genuine, though defeasible, reason to expect a large electorate to outperform a smaller expert panel on factual questions. The standard caveat is that real voters are not independent: they read overlapping news sources and share cultural priors. But this correlation only reduces the effective sample size. As long as the correlation is not perfect, the majority still approaches infallibility as the electorate grows, only more slowly. The other caveat is that the voters must be better than chance. If the electorate is sufficiently misinformed, an expert panel may do better, but the principal threat to the theorem's application is low individual competence, not dependence among voters. Thus, on questions where the average voter is more likely right than wrong, the theorem supports expecting a mass electorate to be at least as accurate as a smaller group of experts.
