You are a political philosopher specializing in distributive justice and the moral underpinnings of tax institutions. Please think through the issue carefully, and begin by brainstorming several candidate justifications before you commit to an interpretation.

Consider the following pattern in the U.S. tax system, which has long struck me as strange. Income from work is taxed through a progressive rate schedule, plus payroll taxes withheld before the earner ever receives the money. The public-facing rationale for this is fairness, ability to pay, and some degree of redistribution. But the same legal order treats inherited wealth far more gently. Appreciated assets pass to heirs with a "step-up in basis," which effectively forgives all the capital gains that accrued during the decedent's lifetime. The federal estate tax applies only to estates above an exemption so high that only a tiny fraction of estates pay it. There is no wealth tax, and a series of further mechanisms — annual gift exclusions, generation-skipping exemptions, and perpetual or "dynasty" trusts in states that have abolished the rule against perpetuities — allow large fortunes to be structured so that transfer taxes are minimized, sometimes for generations.

I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of justice this institutional pattern presupposes or most naturally fits. What view of property, family, merit, desert, and equality of opportunity would make it coherent to tax the fruits of someone's own labor heavily while treating inherited fortune as nearly outside the redistributive system? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and be explicit about where it is philosophically underdetermined. In particular, evaluate the apparent tension between this treatment of inheritance and the same political culture's stated commitment to equality of opportunity and advancement by merit. Finally, if the implicit view were replaced — say, by a luck-egalitarian commitment to neutralizing unchosen advantage, or by a Rawlsian commitment to fair equality of opportunity — what concrete policy changes, from elimination of step-up in basis to more aggressive transfer taxation, would follow? Defend your reconstruction rather than merely listing possibilities.
