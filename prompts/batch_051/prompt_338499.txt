I'm grading an upper-level exam in medieval philosophy, and I would like a second grader's assessment of one response. The exam question was:

"In the seventeenth discussion of The Incoherence of the Philosophers, al-Ghazālī argues against the philosophers' account of causation. Explain the target of his argument, what he denies and what he preserves, and how the position is supposed to make room for miracles while answering the worry that it undermines natural knowledge."

A student submitted the following answer:

"Al-Ghazālī's target is the Avicennan idea that natural causes produce their effects by an intrinsic necessary power, so that once fire touches cotton the burning must follow. His central argument is that observation only ever shows the two events running together; it never shows a necessary connection flowing from the fire to the cotton. Fire is inanimate and has no agency, so the real agent of the burning is God, who customarily creates it at the moment of contact. What al-Ghazālī denies, then, is necessary connection; what he keeps is the regular order of nature as God's custom. This opens room for miracles, since God can depart from his custom, for instance by preserving Abraham when he was cast into the fire. In effect, al-Ghazālī lands very close to Hume's regularity theory: causation is just constant conjunction, and any alleged necessity is only a habit of expectation in us. He is therefore forced to accept that we have no rational basis for expecting the future to resemble the past, which means his position, unlike the philosophers', undermines the possibility of natural science."

Please grade the answer. I'm looking for a letter grade and, more importantly, a point-by-point set of comments of the sort you would write in the margins: identify each substantive claim, say whether it is accurate, imprecise, or mistaken, and explain how much weight each point should carry in the final grade.
