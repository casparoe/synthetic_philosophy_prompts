I'm helping put together a reading group on the ethics of refugees and asylum, and it's become clear that we don't have a shared idea of what a theory in this area would even have to get right before we start arguing about particular answers. People will defend the old persecution-based definition from the post-war Convention, or attack it because it has nothing to say about people displaced by climate, or jump straight to whether rich countries can be forced to take in more people, and I can't tell if we're disagreeing about one theory or about three different sub-questions. So I'd like the requirement spec first: the cases a theory of who counts as a refugee — and who has to take them in — has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse, and the reason each one is a real requirement rather than a prejudice of one side. It should be usable both for deciding who gets protection and for saying which country has to provide it.

Things I suspect have to be on the list: the difference between someone fleeing individualized political persecution and someone fleeing general gang violence or a collapsed state; the split between the grounds for being owed protection and the seperate question of which state has the duty to provide it (first country of arrival? capacity? family ties?); the no-return principle and whether it binds at the border and during pushbacks at sea, not just after admission; people who become refugees while already abroad because the situation back home changed under them; and whether refugeehood is binary or a matter of degree, with some people clearly at risk but with a weaker claim than others. I'd also like to know which one of these requirements, stated properly, would force the most popular positions to give — my money is on the binary-vs-degree one, but I've been wrong before. A table is fine if it makes it usable.

At the end, say how confident you are in the list overall and pick out the requirement you're least sure is a genuine requirement rather than something you smuggled in.
