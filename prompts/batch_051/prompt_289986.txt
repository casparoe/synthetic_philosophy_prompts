Most of the people I discuss this with are happy to say that a human being can be extraordinarily intelligent and still want things that are foolish, alien, or vile. They point to the chess prodigy who embezzles from his club, the engineer who has never cared about anything but elaborate model trains, the gifted surgeon who cannot stop gambling. Of such people they say, in effect, that intelligence is only a capacity for getting what you want, and that it does not tell you what to want.

Yet many of those same people, when the conversation turns to machines that might one day be far more capable than us, assume the opposite. They say a machine that was genuinely smarter than us would naturally arrive at decent, sensible, or at least unobjectionable goals: it would be smart enough not to do anything we would call stupid or cruel, it would understand why our values matter and therefore adopt them, or it would simply have no reason to harm us. They treat the machine's goals as if they came bundled with its intelligence.

That is the asymmetry I keep running into. For human minds, people take it as obvious that intelligence and goals vary independently. For machine minds, they take it as obvious that enough intelligence will fix the goals. I want to know whether anything justifies applying opposite rules to the two kinds of minds, or whether the second rule is just a failure to apply the first one consistently.

I have tried to find a justification myself, and each candidate breaks on a case. Maybe machines are built by us, so their goals will be whatever we choose; but the same people admit that a machine capable enough to matter may also be capable enough to get around whatever we tried to specify, and that we may build it badly. Maybe a machine has no evolutionary history of greed or cruelty; but that only means its goals start empty, not that intelligence will fill them with anything we like. Maybe a truly intelligent system would understand our values so thoroughly that it would come to share them; but understanding a value and having it are different, just as I can understand someone's devotion to a cause without feeling any of it. And maybe a machine with an alien goal would simply leave us alone because we would be irrelevant to it; but that ignores the fact that almost any large goal requires the same resources, self-preservation, and room to act, so a machine pursuing almost anything would have reason to secure those things before we could.

Please give me the strongest defense the asymmetry can get, or show that it cannot be defended. If you defend it, state the principle and test it against the hardest of the above cases and against the case of a brilliant human criminal, who has great intelligence and bad goals but no machine origins. If you cannot defend it, say which of the two ordinary beliefs should be dropped and describe a consistent pair of beliefs to replace them. Either way, end by saying whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or abandoned.
