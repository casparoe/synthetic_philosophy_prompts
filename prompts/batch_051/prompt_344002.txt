I spent my working life in the natural sciences, much of it building and criticizing statistical models, and I have recently begun reading the philosophical literature on algorithmic fairness. In a policy magazine I found the following summary of the fairness impossibility results, which I reproduce here essentially as it appeared:

“In the mid-2010s, researchers discovered that the most widely used formal criteria for algorithmic fairness are mutually incompatible. Suppose a risk-scoring algorithm is used to decide who should be detained, hired, or approved for a loan. One natural demand is calibration: among everyone assigned a given risk score, the same proportion should turn out to be high-risk regardless of group membership. Another natural demand is that the algorithm should make errors at the same rate across groups—that is, it should not produce more false positives or false negatives for one group than another. It turns out that no algorithm can satisfy both calibration and these error-rate parities except in degenerate circumstances, namely when the groups have exactly the same base rate or when the algorithm is perfectly accurate. Since both requirements are non-negotiable in any intuitive notion of fairness, the lesson is that fairness is an inconsistent bundle of statistical constraints: every serious fairness criterion can be satisfied only by sacrificing another. Algorithmic fairness is therefore best understood as an aspiration with no coherent formal content, and institutions should stop pretending that a score can be made fair and instead concentrate on maximizing predictive accuracy, which is the one property the mathematics leaves as achievable.”

I find the technical claim plausible, but I also suspect the paragraph quietly narrows or inflates what the theorems actually show. I would be grateful for an accuracy audit of this summary: which parts are faithful to the mathematics, which are common simplifications or overstatements, where a defender of the impossibility results would push back, and whether the concluding recommendation actually follows from the theorems as they are usually stated. I am not looking for a bibliography; I want to know how to think about what these results do and do not establish.
