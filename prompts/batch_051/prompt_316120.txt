I've been going round in circles on a Rietdijk–Putnam style worry, and I'd like a second read on it. In Minkowski's lecture "Space and Time" he writes:

"Henceforth, space for itself, and time for itself shall completely reduce to a mere shadow, and only some sort of union of the two shall preserve independence."

I'm taking that as the block universe picture: the basic furniture is the four-dimensional world, and "now" is just a slice through it rather than something that brings events into existence. Here's my working list of criteria for an event to count as real, i.e. part of the world:

(a) it can be assigned four coordinates x, y, z, t in some inertial frame;
(b) it stands in frame-independent spatiotemporal relations, timelike, spacelike, or lightlike, to every other world-point;
(c) it is connected to my here-and-now by some chain of simultaneity relations, where each link is the simultaneity hyperplane of some inertial observer.

Now the borderline case. I'm sat on Earth, and the event is a pebble falling on a beach on a planet in the Andromeda galaxy two days in my future. According to my rest frame it hasn't happened yet. But there's a modestly moving observer flying past me right now for whom that pebble-fall is simultaneous with our pass. So by (c) its connected to me by a one-link chain. And if I allow a second observer moving differently, I can reach events even further off.

Question: does the Andromeda pebble-fall qualify as real on these criteria? Go through (a), (b), and (c) one by one, say whether the criteria underdetermine the verdict, and then tell me whether the real problem is with (c), in the sense that simultaneity-connected-to-me is being asked to do a job it can't do. Also answer in British English, please.
