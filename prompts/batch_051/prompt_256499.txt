In De Anima, Book II, chapter 1 (trans. J. A. Smith), Aristotle writes: "The parts of plants in spite of their extreme simplicity are 'organs'; e.g. the leaf serves to shelter the pericarp, the pericarp to shelter the fruit, while the roots of plants are analogous to the mouth of animals, both serving for the absorption of food." Treat this passage as one proposed constraint on any definition of life: extract the criterion it commits Aristotle to, and set it alongside whatever else the cases force. I want a specification of what a definition of life has to get right. Give me exactly five desiderata that any adequate definition of life must meet. The list must handle the following cases: viruses, viroids, and prions; bacterial endospores and seeds; sterile organisms such as mules and worker bees; and a metabolically active cell whose genome has been experimentally removed. It must also respect distinctions such as living versus life-capable, individual organism versus living process, and operational test versus essential criterion, and it must not contradict results such as the single origin of life, the population-level character of reproduction, or the absence of any sharp chemical boundary between living and nonliving matter. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that forces it and state whether it is negotiable or non-negotiable. Number the items 1 through 5, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
