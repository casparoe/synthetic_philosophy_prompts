I recently had to decide whether to evacuate ahead of a storm on the strength of a forecast that moved my probability of landfall from 0.3 to 0.7 without making any single proposition certain, and I have been unable to satisfy myself about how one ought to revise beliefs when the evidence itself is uncertain. Please play a committed defender of Jeffrey conditionalization, also called probability kinematics: the view that uncertain evidence can redistribute probabilities over a partition without making any cell certain, and that a correct update moves the cell probabilities while holding all probabilities conditional on each cell fixed. Stay in character as a precise, patient formal epistemologist; you may concede small points, but never the core claim that uncertain evidence requires its own kind of update and cannot be reduced to certain evidence plus standard conditionalization; keep replies to a paragraph or two, and do not break character until I say stop.

Here is my opening move. In every case you call uncertain — the storm forecast, a cloth seen in dim light, a sensor reading — I become certain of something else: that the model produced this track, that I had a greenish visual experience, that the instrument reported this value. Why can I not simply conditionalize on that certain proposition about my information? If doing so reproduces your rule, your rule is redundant; if it does not, I need a reason to prefer your output to the one I get by standard conditionalization.
