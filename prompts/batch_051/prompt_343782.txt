I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've backed myself into a corner that is making me a little crazy. I'm writing a novel built around a real dead person—someone fairly well known—and I'm inventing scenes: private conversations, motives where the record is silent, events squeezed together so the book has a shape. That's just what historical fiction does, obviously, but a friend sketched an argument at me after some biopic made her angry, and now I cant refute it, even though its conclusion would set fire to most of what I love. I need you to tell me exactly where it breaks, or what I have to give up to escape it.

Here's the argument as I reconstruct it:

(1) When a novel or biopic puts invented dialogue, motives, or incidents into the mouth and head of a real dead person, many readers and viewers walk away with beliefs about the actual historical person, not just about the fictional version of her.

(2) At least some of those beliefs will be false. This isn't a failure of research; it's structural. You cant turn a life into drama without filling gaps the record leaves open, flattening contradictions, and giving interiority to a mind you can't get inside.

(3) Knowingly adding falsehoods to the common memory of a real named person—falsehoods about what she did, said, wanted, believed—is a way of wronging her. It treats her actual life as raw material to be rewritten for audience effect, and it weakens her grip on who she gets remembered as.

(4) That wrong doesn't simply lapse at death. If it did, we'd have to say there's nothing wrong with deliberate, knowing lies about the dead, and that seems monstrous: surely the dead can be slandered.

(5) The usual defenses don't fix it. Calling the work "fiction" doesn't stop readers from absorbing the vivid parts into their picture of the real person—if anything, vividness makes the invented parts stick harder—and the aesthetic value can't outweigh the wrong, because most of that value could be preserved by giving the character a different name, and where it couldn't, that just shows how much the audience's false belief is doing the work.

(6) Therefore, historical fiction and biopics that invent under a real person's name are wrong, and the only honest alternatives are nonfiction that flags every guess, or full renaming into an invented character.

(7) But that conclusion is absurd. It would condemn most of the novels and films that first taught me to care about history, including ones that treat their subjects with far more tenderness and care than a lot of scholarship does.

I'd most like to keep (4), because I've spent years inside this woman's letters and I dont accept that she stops mattering the moment her pulse does. But I also can't accept (6). So which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the reasoning is valid, tell me which premise is the least costly to drop and what I lose by dropping it.

You're a professional moral philosopher who works on posthumous harm, reputation, and the ethics of narrative. Please take your time, steelman each step before criticizing it, and then hand me a precise diagnosis: the exact step where the argument fails (or the exact premise that has to go), why it looked so plausible, and what philosophical price I pay for a version of this that still lets me write the book with a clean conscience—if any does.
