Hi, I've been reading Walter Bagehot's The English Constitution and I keep coming back to a distinction he makes early on. He says something like, the constitution has two kinds of parts — one that attracts the public's loyalty and reverence, and another that actually does the work of governing. I'm paraphrasing because I don't have the book open, but the idea stuck with me. In Britain the first part seems to be the monarchy and all the ceremony around it, while the second part is the cabinet and Parliament, where the actual decisions get made. He seems to think this split is not just a compromise but a kind of design: the impressive part absorbs public attention so the practical part can operate. My question is weather this configuration has a name in constitutional theory, and whether it's regarded as a healthy arrangement, a problem, or just a neutral description. Also, does it still apply to modern constitutional monarchies like Denmark or Japan, where the monarchy has even less influence than in Bagehot's day? I'm not after a bare label — I want to know what the distinction is really tracking, where its boundaries are, and whether people have argued it's a feature or a flaw.
