I sit on a small research-ethics committee for a neuropsych lab, and we're genuinly stuck on one. Our human-subjects policy says: 'No investigator may administer an experimental procedure to a participant unless the participant has voluntarily given informed consent. Informed consent requires that the participant understand, in language comprehensible to them, what the procedure involves, its foreseeable risks, and that they may withdraw at any time.' Straightforward, until this case landed on us.

A potential participant is a split-brain patient, complete callosotomy for intractable epilepsy. His left hemisphere controls speech and the right hand. On that side he seems fully on board: he reads the form, asks good questions, signs with his right hand, and says he wants to participate. But when we test his right hemisphere, using the left visual field and left hand, his left hand points to 'no' on a yes/no card, pushes the consent form back across the table, and one time wrote 'don't' when given a pencil. The patient then says he has no idea why his hand did that, and that he personally still wants to do the study. The study is low risk, lateralized word and picture tasks with EEG, no new surgery.

Does the policy count him as having consented? I care less about the practical outcome than about what the exact words 'the participant' have to do to reach each answer. Argue it from the plain text, from the purpose of consent, from what a reasonable reader of the policy would take it to mean, and from what the people who wrote it would say if they saw this case. Then tell me which approach should win, and what the whole mess shows about whether the rule was ever able to handle a person whose hemispheres disagree, or whether it even makes sense to say there is one person consenting here.
