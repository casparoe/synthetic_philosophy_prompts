I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I tripped into this while researching a character who's a monomaniacal birdwatcher and now I can't get it out of my head. A friend walked me through an argument that seeing a white shoe confirms "all ravens are black," and I want to use it in a scene where a character justifies a wild claim by saying something unrelated "technically confirms" it. Problem is, I need to know where the argument actually gives out before I put words in anyone's mouth. Here's how I've reconstructed it:

(1) Seeing an F that is G confirms the generalization "All F are G." A black raven confirms "All ravens are black."

(2) "All ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "All non-black things are non-ravens." Same claim, contraposed.

(3) Whatever confirms a statement confirms anything logically equivalent to it.

(4) So a green apple — it's non-black and not a raven — confirms "All non-black things are non-ravens."

(5) By (3), then, the green apple confirms "All ravens are black."

(6) And this generalizes: white shoes, my coffee mug, a red herring — any non-black non-raven — each confirms that ornithological claim. You could pile up evidence for a generalization about ravens without ever laying eyes on a raven.

That last step seems to prove way too much. If any random object counts for any generalization it merely fails to be a counterexample to, then "confirmation" is too cheap to mean anything at all. Each step looked fine on it's own, but the conclusion feels absurd. If I had to keep one, I'd honestly rather keep (1) than (3), because I want evidence to start from looking at the things the claim is actually about. So: which step do I reject, or am I misreading what "confirms" is doing here? And whats the price of the fix? I need a diagnosis concrete enough for a novelist to explain to a character, not just a label.
