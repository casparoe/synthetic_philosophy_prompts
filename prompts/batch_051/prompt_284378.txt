I have been reconstructing what I take to be the core commitments of teleosemantics—the view that the representational content of a mental state is fixed by its selected function—and I have distilled the following four criteria for whether an internal state s of an organism or artifact represents a determinate environmental condition C. (1) Producer condition: a mechanism in the system is keyed to C, tending to issue s when C obtains. (2) Consumer condition: a downstream mechanism uses s to guide behavior that succeeds only if C obtains. (3) Selection condition: the s-producing and s-consuming mechanisms were retained—by natural selection, reinforcement learning, cultural transmission, or deliberate design—because that pattern of use reliably produced successful outcomes, and it is this history that fixes s's content as C. (4) Error condition: s can occur in the absence of C and still count as a representation of C; representing is not the same as detecting.

Now consider the following case, a version of the familiar Swampman scenario. A bolt of lightning rearranges swamp matter into a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of an ordinary adult human, S. S has no evolutionary ancestors, no learning history, and no designer, but his current physical and behavioral dispositions are indistinguishable from the original's: he walks out of the swamp, converses, answers questions, and reports beliefs, desires, and thirst. In purely current causal terms, S satisfies conditions (1), (2), and (4): he contains mechanisms keyed to conditions, mechanisms that use those states to guide successful behavior, and mechanisms that can misfire. Condition (3), however, appears to fail across the board, since none of S's state-to-condition mappings was selected by a history of successes. To sharpen the case, suppose that after S forms he learns a few new things through ordinary trial and error; those later states do have an ontogenetic selection history, while his standing conceptual and linguistic competences do not.

Does S have beliefs and desires with determinate representational content? When S says that he is thirsty, does his state mean that he is thirsty? Go through the four criteria one by one. Then take a committed position: keep criterion (3) and accept whatever it implies about S (perhaps no content at all, perhaps only a patchwork of content for the items S has learned since forming), weaken or drop criterion (3) and say what fixes content instead, or propose some other specific revision of the criteria. Defend one definite verdict rather than laying out the options, and acknowledge the strongest objection to your view.
