I write compilers for a living, so the way I think about language is basically: an expression has a denotation (what it evaluates to under the semantics) and then a bunch of runtime effects — allocation, logging, exceptions, I/O — that happen when you run it but aren't part of the returned value. Lately I've been trying to get a handle on the dispute between Davidson and the conceptual metaphor people (Lakoff and Johnson) about whether metaphors have meaning. Davidson says there's no such thing as metaphorical meaning: a metaphor means exactly what it literally means, and whatever else it does is causal, like a joke nudging you to notice stuff. Lakoff and Johnson say metaphor is a systematic mapping from a source domain to a target domain (ARGUMENT IS WAR and all that) that structures how we think, not just how we talk. I want you to restate this whole debate in my compiler vocabulary: denotation vs. effect traces, pure functions vs. side effects, maybe the way an effect system or a monad reifies effects back into values. Go through the main claims one by one — Davidson's denial of a second sort of meaning, his joke-comparison, his claim that metaphors can't be paraphrased; then the conceptual-metaphor claim about systematic source-to-target mappings that structure thought itself — and for each say what translates cleanly, what has to be bent to fit, and what has no expression at all in that vocabulary. Then the part I actually care about: is the stuff that wont translate a defect in my framework, or is it a sign that one of the two positions was less precise than it looked from the inside?
