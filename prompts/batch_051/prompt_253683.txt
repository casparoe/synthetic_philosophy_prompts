I am a novelist, and for some years all my books have been translated into another language by the same translator. I have kept a close record of her decisions, because she plainly works from a consistent method, though I cannot quite state what it is. On their face, the decisions pull in different directions — some domesticate, some preserve the foreign — and I cannot tell whether a single principle underlies them or whether I am seeing a stable compromise between competing ones. I should like you to help me reconstruct the method, and to predict how she will handle a problem in the book I am now finishing.

Here are six of her decisions.

1. In a comic scene, a character mishears a word and the joke turns on the resulting pun. She replaced my pun with an entirely different pun in her language, preserving the timing of the exchange and the character's quickness, though the objects referred to changed.

2. A poem embedded in the novel has a strict metre and rhyme scheme. She kept the metre and rhyme but altered several images, on the grounds that a faithful rendering of the images would have produced doggerel.

3. I coined a compound word for a feeling that is half grief and half relief. She did not use the ordinary words for either feeling; she coined a new compound in her language that fuses a similar pair of feelings, though its literal components are different.

4. A minor character's regional dialect signals his class and origin. She rendered his dialogue in standard language, conveying class through syntax, register, and forms of address rather than through misspellings, which she said would turn him into a figure of fun in a way I had not intended.

5. A civic office central to the novel's setting has no equivalent in her language. She kept my word for it and added a brief, unobtrusive gloss at its first appearance, rather than borrowing the nearest institution from her own culture, which she said would transplant the novel to the wrong country.

6. One character insults another with an idiom that cannot be translated word for word. She substituted an idiom from her language of comparable force and register, observing that a literal rendering would sound merely odd rather than wounding.

The problem in the book I am finishing is the ending. The novel's central theme is the relation between forgiveness and debt, and in the final chapter a dying character writes a single word that, in my language, means both 'mercy' and 'a debt that has been cancelled'. The reader is meant to hold both senses at once; the resolution of the plot depends on the doubleness. Her language has no word carrying both senses, and the two senses are expressed by unrelated words. She could render the word literally and lose one sense; she could have the character write a short phrase that makes both senses explicit, preserving the content but flattening the gesture; she could find a word in her language with a different double meaning and adjust the surrounding text so that the plot still resolves, preserving the mechanism but changing what the character means; or she could keep my word and add a note, breaking the scene.

I should like to know what she will do. I am less interested in the bare prediction than in the method. Set out the candidate rules that fit her six earlier decisions, say what each rule would require for the ending, identify which of the earlier decisions is the closest analogue, give your best prediction with the reason you prefer that rule over the others, and name a further case that would cleanly separate whichever candidate rules remain. If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer, say exactly which principle we would be attributing to her with each choice.
