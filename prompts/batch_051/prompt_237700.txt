Analyze the 2006 IAU vote that demoted Pluto as a case study for the social construction of kinds. I know the basic history — the "planet" definition built around clearing the neighborhood, the fact that only a smallish fraction of IAU members were in the room, the immediate pushback from planetary scientists — so don't spend the whole answer recapping it. What I want is the social-ontology part. The easy move is to say either "planet is a natural kind and the vote just changed the word" or "planet is socially constructed, end of story." I want you to work at a finer grain then that. Use Hacking's distinction between interactive kinds (classifying people feeds back into what they are, like "teenager" or "refugee") and indifferent kinds (the kind doesn't care what we call it, like "quark"), and then tell me whether "planet" in the post-2006 sense is one of those, a mix, or whether the Pluto case pushes us to a fourth category, something like an institutional kind that piggybacks on a natural structure. Along the way, address all three: (1) did the vote change what Pluto is, or only change the extension of a word? (2) is the real projectible kind — the underlying dynamical structure of the solar system — left untouched by the vote, or does the need to draw a threshold somewhere show that even that structure is partly decision-laden? (3) what does the episode do to the social-construction thesis: does it confirm it, mark its limits, or suggest the thesis was always aimed at human kinds and never could handle a case where nature pushes back? And end by saying whether you'd rather teach Pluto as a paradigm case of social construction or as a cautionary tale about overusing the label.
