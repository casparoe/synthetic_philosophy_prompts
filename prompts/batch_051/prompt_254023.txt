Last night I stayed up way too late arguing with a friend who’s a philosophy PhD student about whether BP itself was morally to blame for Deepwater Horizon, or just the particular managers and engineers. I was defending the idea that a company can be blameworthy as an organization even when no single employee had the full picture, because the blame attaches to how the organization was set up to make decisions—its policies, reporting lines, incentive metrics, and the way tradeoffs got framed. He kept coming back to the point that organizations don’t have mental states, so without intent or recklessness in a literal sense, “blame” is just a metaphor or disguised strict liability, and in practice it just punishes shareholders and low-level employees who had nothing to do with the bad call.

Now I’ve got to give a lunch talk to a state bar CLE audience, mostly in-house counsel and compliance officers, with a few outside corporate criminal defense lawyers. I’m going to defend a version of the claim that a corporation can be a proper object of moral blame when its internal decision structure predictably produces the harm, and that this is distinct from strict liability because it tracks something about the organization’s practical reasoning, not just the bad outcome. These people aren’t going to push back the way my friend did. They live in the world of DOJ compliance-program expectations, corporate settlements, privilege, and keeping the company from getting tagged with words that show up in shareholder suits. So I need a Q&A prep sheet for the objections I’ll actually get from this audience. Order them by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way a compliance officer or an in-house lawyer would actually put them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I’m hoping nobody asks, and tell me what I should concede up front instead of trying to win on the word “blame.”
