I am currently taking a philosophy of science seminar on causation, and I find myself pulled in two directions about a view I am otherwise inclined to accept. The view is the interventionist account of causation: roughly, X is a cause of Y just in case there is some possible intervention on X—an exogenous change that fixes other relevant variables—such that Y or Y's probability would change. Part of what attracts me to this account is that it promises to fix a problem with purely counterfactual theories, which seem to count too many relationships as causal because they do not distinguish interventions from mere observations or from changes that are themselves downstream of other causes; the interventionist account also ties causation to manipulation, the activity through which we both test and use causal claims. Yet I also cannot shake the intuition that some causes are real even where no intervention is possible, even in principle. I think, for instance, of the causal role of the early universe's initial conditions in the large-scale structure we now observe, of a person's birth circumstances in their later health, and of tightly conserved biological variables where isolating the cause by intervention would either make no sense or would unravel the system being studied. I want to say that these are genuine causal relations, not merely explanatory relations or reflections of our ignorance. If the interventionist account tells me they are not causes, I do not know whether the theory or the intuition should give way. Please present each side at its strongest, and then give me a negotiated verdict: would I be justified in debunking and abandoning the intuition, in amending or restricting the theory, or is there a reading of "possible intervention" on which both survive? Show what would justify each of those moves rather than simply choosing one.
