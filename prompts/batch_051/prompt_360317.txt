Consider the canonical way of inferring an agent's objective from its behavior: you observe which options it selects, assume that every option never selected was nevertheless available and ranked lower, and from this back out a utility function. Now vary that assumption. The agent is a hill-climber over a grid of outcomes with fixed numerical utilities unknown to you. At each step it can perceive only the eight cells adjacent to its current position, it moves to the perceived cell with highest utility, and it stops when no perceived cell has utility higher than its current one. You observe only the sequence of visited cells across many trials that start from different cells. What utility function would the standard availability-and-rejection assumption attribute to the agent, and how does it systematically distort the true one? If you are additionally shown, for each step, the set of cells that were perceptible to the agent, what features of the objective and the search process become identifiable, and what ambiguity remains? Finally, is any part of the objective unrecoverable in principle even with that extra information, and if so, which part and why?
