I cover vision science and the weirder end of the brain-interface startup world for a living, so I can handle the technical vocabulary — CIE coordinates, tristimulus values, opponent-process channels, gamuts, metamers — but philosophy of mind is not my beat. Lately I keep running into the same arguement at conferences, usually with the old 'forbidden colors' stuff (reddish-green, bluish-yellow — colors normal human vision supposedly cannot produce) dragged in as evidence. One side says a brain interface could in principle give you a genuinely new color: not a mix of colors you have already seen, not a shade you happened to miss before, but a color no human has ever experienced, a new point in the space of possible experiences. The other side says that's incoherent, because any experience a human brain produces has to be assembled from machinery that brain already has, so it has to land somewhere inside the space of humanly possible color experience; an implant can only recombine what is already there.

I want you to restate both sides of that debate in the vocabulary I actually work in. For each side, take the central claim — that a genuinely novel color experience is possible, or that it isn't — and translate it into colorimetric and vision-science terms: tristimulus values, the dimensionality of color space, opponent channels, gamut boundaries, metamers, and the difference between a stimulus and a color experience. Then give me the residue report: for each side, which claims translate cleanly, which have to be bent out of shape to fit, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. And for the residue, I want your honest verdict — is my vocabulary missing something important about experience, or was one side of the debate less precise than it sounded once you forced it to say what it means? Finish by telling me whether 'could there be a new color?' has a determinate answer in colorimetric terms, and whether that answer settles the philosophical question or just changes the subject. Hard cap: 900 words.
