In debates about verse translation there is a recurring analogy between translating a poem under formal constraints and performing a musical work from a score. On this picture, the source poem is like the score: it fixes certain parameters exactly—meter, rhyme scheme, stanza shape, often the sequence of images or argumentative moves—while leaving a great deal open, such as local word choice, syntax, nuance, and emphasis, just as a score fixes pitches and rhythms but leaves tempo, articulation, dynamics, and timbre to the performer. The analogy is used to argue that a translation preserving rhyme and meter is not a distortion of the original but an interpretation of it, and that the translator’s departures at the level of individual words are no more a failure of fidelity than a cellist’s rubato is a failure to play what Bach wrote. Conversely, the analogy is used to say that a free-verse paraphrase of a rhymed poem is like describing a sonata rather than playing it.

Audit this analogy. Give an explicit mapping: what plays the role of score, performer, instrument, performance, interpretation, audience, venue, and repeated performance. Then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular, consider whether the score–poem difference matters: a musical score is a set of instructions for producing sounds, not itself a completed artwork in the way a poem in its original language already is; a translation produces another durable text rather than an ephemeral event; and the target language may simply lack the phonetic or morphological resources that make a given formal effect possible in the source. For each disanalogy, say whether it matters for the conclusion the analogy is being used to support—namely, that preserving form is a species of fidelity rather than a constraint imposed at the expense of content. Finally, state whether that conclusion survives the audit, and if the analogy should be replaced or repaired, say what a better analogy would be.
