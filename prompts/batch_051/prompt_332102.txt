My university's study-abroad office runs a mandatory pre-departure orientation, and every year they hand out a one-page "Responsible Traveler" agreement that students have to sign. It tells students to learn the local norms for tipping, lining up, and crossing the street before they go, and it insists that following those norms isn't just smart self-presentation or a way to avoid fines. It says visitors owe it to the people whose city their in. But on the same page, it admits these norms are entirely local. A student who fails to tip 20% in New York has done something wrong, but a student who insists on tipping 20% in Tokyo, where tipping isn't the local practice, has also done something wrong. Cutting a queue in London is treated as wronging the people behind you. Jaywalking in Berlin at 2 a.m. with no cars anywhere is treated as a failure to follow a rule that Berliners themselves take seriously. The page doesn't claim the norms are universal, and it wouldn't say a student is bound by the tipping norms of a country she never visits. So it seems to be committed to the idea that by simply entering a place, you become bound by whichever arbitrary-seeming conventions happen to function there.

I'm on the committee rewriting this page, and I can't tell what philosophical picture is doing the work beneath the advice. Reconstruct the implicit theory of obligation the page presupposes. Specifically: does it strictly presuppose tacit consent upon entry, a duty of fair play, gratitude to hosts, a general duty to follow whatever the local norm is, or something else? Which parts of the advice are compatible with several of those theories, and which parts force one view? If we replaced that underlying picture with a purely prudential one -- these are conventions, break them at your own social risk -- what exactly would the page have to stop saying or start saying? And do the three examples actually presuppose the same thing, or does the page paper over a rift between them?
