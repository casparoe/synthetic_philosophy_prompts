I am a PhD student in philosophy working on formal ethics and decision theory, and I am putting together a one-page handout for a graduate seminar on risk, aggregation, and longtermism. Please write a decision tree that sorts a reader from their bottom-line response to Pascal's-mugging-style cases into a position in the expected-value-fanaticism debate. The reader should be able to complete the tree even if they know what expected utility theory is and have heard of the St. Petersburg paradox, but have never seen the fanaticism literature. The handout should be self-contained, with no citations or further-reading list.

Every branch node should be a claim or a concrete case answerable without knowing the jargon or the position it leads toward. Example nodes are: whether there is a finite upper bound on how good a state of affairs can be; whether a one-in-10^15 chance of saving 10^15 lives should be chosen over a certain, small harm to one person, when these are the only options; and whether a gamble that was worth taking remains worth taking once you learn that its tiny probability comes from a proof of a physical mechanism rather than from a flimsy estimate by an excitable stranger. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Begin with the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then locate the live positions in that space.

Please make sure the following positions appear as leaves, each with a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment: accepting fanaticism outright, bounded utility, risk-weighted expected utility, a probability-threshold or ignore-tiny-chances view, and at least one non-consequentialist constraint view, plus any other defensible leaf that falls out of your tree. For combinations of answers that no one actually defends, say so and note briefly whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag explicitly any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate — for example, a probability-threshold view and a risk-weighted view may answer the same sorting questions, since both refuse to let tiny probabilities dominate unless the stakes are sufficiently overwhelming. Keep the tree to no more than a dozen nodes.
