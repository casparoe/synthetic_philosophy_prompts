I am a novelist, and the vocabulary in which I think is the vocabulary of craft: premise and promise; the protagonist's want against their deeper need; the wound that organizes a character; conflict, stakes, and the price of the climax; earned versus unearned emotion; interiority and point of view; tonal register; catharsis; and the difference between an ending that resolves and one that merely stops. I have been reading philosophy on the value of negative emotions, and I would like the debate restated in that craft vocabulary rather than in the vocabulary of value theory, as faithfully as you can.

Treat this as a translation exercise, not a loose analogy. Take the main positions — roughly, an instrumentalist view on which sadness and grief are signals or motivators with no value beyond what they produce; a pluralist or fittingness view on which some negative emotions answer to what matters and are valuable as such, so that a life that felt its losses properly would in one respect be better than an otherwise identical life engineered not to suffer them; and the classic puzzle of why audiences deliberately seek out tragic art that makes them feel worse — and render each in terms a novelist would use about a character, an arc, or a reading experience. For each, mark what translates cleanly, what had to be bent to fit, and what has no counterpart in craft at all.

Then give me the residue report. My working suspicion is that craft handles the paradox of tragedy well — through catharsis, earned emotion, and the way an ending can retrospectively justify what preceded it — but that it has no natural way to state that a person's actual grief is good in itself, independently of the shape a story gives it. Is that residue a deficiency in my vocabulary, or does it expose a claim in the philosophy that was never made precise? I would rather be told where my framework distorts the position than have the gap smoothed over.
