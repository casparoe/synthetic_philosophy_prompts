Naturalists about health, with Christopher Boorse as the standard representative, hold that health is a purely descriptive matter: a person is healthy when their parts function in the statistically normal way for members of their species, and disease is just a deviation from that statistical norm. Normativists object that this cannot be right, because to call a condition a disease is unavoidably to judge that the condition is bad for the person, and that is an evaluative judgment. Since what a society judges bad changes over time, and since the chapter takes the removal of homosexuality from the DSM as its example, the chapter infers that the boundary between health and disease ultimately depends on changing social values and is, as the chapter sums it up, a social construction. The chapter concludes that most philosophers now regard the normativist side as having won the debate, with the only live question being how openly a theory should avow its value judgments. I find the summary clear, but I am uneasy about the slide from statistically normal functioning to the conclusion that the whole distinction is socially constructed, and I am suspicious of the claim that the debate is settled. Still, the writer is a professional and I am a student, so I do not trust my unease. Is this a fair summary of the naturalism versus normativism debate?
