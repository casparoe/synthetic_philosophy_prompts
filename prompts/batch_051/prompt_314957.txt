I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas — mostly animal cognition and conservation policy — and I write a weekly column. A few times a month I have to decide what to do with a study that claims some brain measurement (total size, brain-to-body ratio, forebrain neuron count, whatever) should change how much moral weight we give a species, or whether an animal gets protected. I've been burned: I once wrote that a species had toddler-level cognition on the strength of a neuron count, and a reader who actually knew the field tore the piece apart. My editor won't run something that's all on-the-one-hand-on-the-other, and I don't have philosophy training. The stakes are real: a bad column can help kill an animal-protection bill, or hand a cull a clean conscience. So I need something I can actually use.

What I want is a reusable decision procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no rather than a maybe, and where it hands the decison to me because it's a values call rather than a factual one. Ground it in the strongest version you can reconstruct of the philosophical thinking about moral relevance — the deep question is whether a brain property matters in itself, or only through the capacities and interests it actually underwrites. Don't take that line for granted; build the procedure so it also catches cases where someone wrongly dismisses a brain difference as irrelevant because they used a bad measure. And be honest about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as checklist items.

Then run the procedure on three cases. One: a study reports that long-finned pilot whales have more neocortical neurons than adult humans, and the authors say we should take whale suffering more seriously than we currently do. Two: a paper shows that a tiny-brained paper wasp can learn the faces of individual nestmates, and the authors argue brain size is a poor guide to social intelligence and small-brained species get systematically underprotected. Three: a conservation agency proposes culling a large-brained invasive mammal, partly on the grounds that its intelligence is what makes it such a destructive invader — and my readers love that species, so they will hate whatever a cold procedure says.

Make sure at least one worked case ends in an answer I won't like, and say explicitly what the procedure can't settle, where no checklist can replace editorial judgement.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the procedure as a whole and single out the point you're least sure about.
