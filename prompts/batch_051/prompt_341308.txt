In my philosophy of criminal law class, the professor has handed down a term's worth of rulings on hypothetical statutes, and I still cannot tell where she sits in the Hart–Devlin controversy. Her five decisions this term, each delivered in the same compressed style, were as follows.

A statute punished duelling, even between two adults who met by private arrangement in a secluded field, and one was badly wounded. She voted to uphold it. Her explanation: consent does not extend to maiming or killing; private combat cannot be kept reliably private, since seconds, surgeons, and the public peace are drawn in; and the institution of private duelling treats life as cheap.

A statute made it an offence to sell a kidney for transplantation, even where the seller was a competent adult and the recipient would otherwise die. She upheld it. Her explanation: a seller in desperate poverty has no real alternative, so consent is unreliable, and a market in organs treats the body as a tradeable thing and pressures the poor into bearing risks that others are spared.

A city closed a theatre that sold tickets to live sexual performances whose stage was visible from a busy street. She upheld the closure. Her explanation: the performers and patrons consented, but the unwilling passer-by did not, and a person should not have to encounter public sexual performance as the price of walking through a neighbourhood.

She rejected a challenge to a ban on cockfighting. Her explanation: the birds suffer, and the spectators are not watching sport but enjoying the infliction of pain; the law is not obliged to protect a taste whose satisfaction requires cruelty.

She upheld convictions for a bare-knuckle fight staged in a pub before a paying audience, where both fighters had consented and one was injured. Her explanation: the violence was public, the excitement of the crowd made disorder likely, and the spectacle of paid violence brutalises those who watch and those who live nearby.

Next week she must decide a new appeal. A statute punishes assault causing bodily harm. The appellants are adults who met in a private, soundproofed room and, over several years and with complete consent, beat, cut, and burned one another during sexual rituals. No participant has complained; no non-participant was present, disturbed, or even aware; the injuries were transient and healed without medical treatment; and the conduct came to light only because police happened upon videotapes during an unrelated search. The state offers no evidence of public nuisance, coercion, or spillover. Its sole argument is that the conduct degrades those who do it and that a society which tolerates it cannot remain healthy, because members of a community may not treat one another as objects of cruelty even with mutual consent.

What should she decide? More importantly, which principle best explains her first five rulings—the harm principle, the offense principle, paternalism, legal moralism, or some ordered combination—and which of her earlier remarks become load-bearing once the new case is before her? Give a committed answer: a definite prediction of her vote and the best reconstruction of her underlying rule, with reasons, rather than a menu of possibilities. Then describe one further case that would cleanly settle which rule she is actually following, and say how she would come out in that further case under each candidate rule.
