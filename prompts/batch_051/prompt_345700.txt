I am an undergraduate teaching assistant for a second-year course on nineteenth-century philosophy, and next week I have been asked to lead a fifty-minute discussion section on Nietzsche's early essay about the value and harm of history for life, concentrating on the three attitudes he distinguishes: monumental, antiquarian, and critical history. The students will have read the essay once, but my sense is that most of them have come away with only the rough labels—monumental history admires great figures, antiquarian history preserves the past, critical history condemns it—and that the section will fail if it merely rehearses these definitions. I want them to understand the three attitudes as different ways of taking up the same past in the service of life, and to see why each one, taken too far or in the wrong circumstances, becomes a danger to the very life it is supposed to serve.

Do not explain the essay to me; plan the section. I need the order in which the ideas must be introduced and what depends on what, in particular whether the idea of history as serving an active, form-giving life must be established before the attitudes are presented or whether it should be allowed to emerge from them. I also need one concrete example or case that can carry the whole hour by being passed through all three attitudes, showing that the same inherited material can be appropriated in different ways; say what the example must contain and what it must avoid. Then map the misconceptions I should expect. I anticipate these: that critical history just means being negative about one's country or tradition, that antiquarian reverence is harmless and simply means caring about the past, that monumental history is straightforwardly inspirational, and that the three attitudes are exclusive types into which people can be sorted. Tell me at which point in the sequence to head off each one and how the example can be used to correct it rather than my having to stop and issue a correction. I would also like guidance on whether, and where, to introduce the role of forgetting and the need for a bounded horizon, which the essay seems to treat as a condition of acting at all but which may only confuse the taxonomy if it comes too early. Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level and say aloud that I am leaving out, and give me one question I can ask near the end that would show whether students have grasped the life-serving criterion rather than merely learned to classify.
