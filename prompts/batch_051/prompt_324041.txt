I've been going back and forth on Rawls vs. Harsanyi about choice behind the veil, and I've realized my actual problem is that I don't have a clean statement of what a rule for deciding under ignorance is supposed to do in the first place. So before I take a side, write the spec. Assume the rule has to work for a single agent facing a high-stakes, one-shot choice with no probabilities, but also for someone picking institutions from behind a veil of ignorance. What cases must it get right—the original position, a one-shot bet with no odds, a flood-control policy where the chance the levee fails is simply unknown—and what test does each case impose? Which distinctions can't it collapse: risk vs. uncertainty, a state vs. an act, utility vs. regret, complete vs. partial ignorance? Which structural constraints are non-negotiable (dominance, transitivity, invariance under redescription of the state space) and which are just tempermental preferences of one camp (pessimism, optimism, symmetry)? Then tell me which requirements you suspect can't all be met at once, and which single requirement maximin, minimax regret, and the principle of insufficient reason each fail hardest on. The joint-unsatisfiability part is what I most want to see; skip the full scorecard.
