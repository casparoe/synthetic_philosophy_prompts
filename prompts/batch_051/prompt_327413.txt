Two of my flatmates — Sasha is in a philosophy PhD and Marcus is a neuroscientist — spent most of Sunday going back and forth about Frankfurt cases, and I genuinely couldn't referee, so I'm handing it to you. They both know the standard case, so they skipped the setup, but I'll give it for context: a man has decided to do something (say, hurt someone), and a neuroscientist has rigged a device that will read an early signal from the man's brain. If the signal shows the man is about to decide not to do it, the device will make him decide to do it anyway. But the signal shows he was going to decide to do it on his own, so the device never fires. The intuition is that the man is blameworthy even though, given the device, he couldn't have decided not to do it. Sasha says this refutes the principle of alternative possibilities; Marcus says the case builds in the conclusion.

Sasha: The man decides and acts from his own beliefs, plans, and character, and the device is causally idle. What he's missing is not anything that ever figured in someone actually blaming him. The ability to do otherwise can't be what blame is about.

Marcus: But you only know he couldn't have decided otherwise because the story says a device would guarantee exactly one decision. For the device to guarantee it without ever firing, there has to be some earlier state of the man that already makes the decision inevitable. So the example stipulates that the decision was determined, then concludes a determined decision can be blameworthy. That's exactly what's supposed to be shown, not assumed.

Sasha: Not quite. The point is that what matters is the actual sequence, the real causes of the decision. If they're the right sort — his own reasons, no coercion — then he's blameworthy. The device would only have caused the decision in a different sequence, one that never happens. Why should an unrealized backup cause matter more than the real one?

Marcus: It's not that the backup matters more. It's that the principle under attack is about alternatives, and the case removes the alternative by stipulation. And even then, the man still has some alternatives in the actual sequence. He could have shown a different early signal — a flicker of hesitation, a different neural pattern before the device checked. That's precisely what would have triggered the device. Defenders of the principle can point to that flicker.

Sasha: The flicker is a desperate move. A subpersonal neural flutter the man isn't aware of and can't direct isn't the kind of doing otherwise that blame was supposed to require. If saving the principle means shrinking alternatives down to events that aren't even actions, the principle has already given up whatever made it intuitive.

Marcus: I'm not saying the flicker by itself grounds blame. The dialectical problem is that the case needs the decision to be inevitable, and the only way to get inevitability is to build in a prior state that guarantees the decision. If that prior state is there, you've assumed the man is a mechanism, and then the intuition that he's blameworthy just is the compatibilist intuition. The case can't independently test the principle.

Sasha: A case can test a principle even if its setup includes things the other side eventually denies. You stipulate a scenario, get an intuition, then revise your principle. You're refusing to have the intuition because you've already decided it would be bad news.

Marcus: No, I'm saying the setup is dialectically unstable. You can't consistently say the man couldn't have done otherwise, and also say the device tracks a signal such that if the signal were different, the decision would have been different. If the signal can differ, there's a real alternative at the level of the signal. If it can't differ, the story is deterministic through and through, and you're just asserting that a determined agent is responsible.

Sasha: Suppose I grant the signal can differ. So what? The principle of alternative possibilities was about doing otherwise — acting otherwise, deciding otherwise. A microscopic neural difference that makes no practical difference to what the man does is not doing otherwise. You've changed the subject from action to microstructure.

Marcus: Right, and once you grant the signal can differ, the principle doesn't just fail. The genuine dispute becomes what counts as an alternative possibility relevant to responsibility. That's a substantive question the case itself doesn't settle. So the case isn't a clean refutation; it's an invitation to argue about something else.

Sasha: Then the principle as originally stated is at least in bad shape, because everyone agrees the man is blameworthy and everyone agrees he couldn't have done otherwise in the ordinary sense. If the only way out is reinterpreting alternative to include microevents, that's a retreat, not a defense.

Marcus: Sometimes a retreat is a clarification. If the principle was always supposed to be about the alternatives worth caring about, then the flicker forces you to say which ones those are, and that's the real question.
