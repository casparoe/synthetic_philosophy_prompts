You are a professional philosopher working on the foundations of revealed-preference theory and the meaning of utility claims. Think through the cases below before committing; brainstorm candidate principles and check them case by case.

I am trying to figure out when observing someone's choices licenses me to say they prefer something, or that it has utility for them. My gut verdicts on some cases:

- Repeated informed choice: someone has tried A and B many times, can afford either, and picks A every time. I accept that this reveals a preference for A over B.
- The dieter: she picks the salad, then says she "really wanted" the burger. I think the choice reveals a preference for salad conditional on her dietary goal, but not that she likes salad more; the burger is what she would have preferred in the local, sensory sense.
- The addict: someone says she wants to quit, has tried to quit three times, and still smokes every day. I refuse to infer that she prefers smoking to quitting, even though the behavior is repeated and voluntary in an ordinary sense.
- The regretful chooser: after repeatedly sampling both, she chooses B; years later she says B was worse. I stick with the choice: repeated informed choice beats the later verbal report.
- The mistaken investor: he keeps choosing a high-fee actively managed fund because he falsely believes it will beat the index. I don't think this reveals a preference for the fund's actual payoff distribution over the index's; it reveals a preference for the fund as he represents it.
- Self-binding: a student puts his phone in a timed lockbox so that he will study. I think this overarching choice does reveal a preference for studying over scrolling, even though he would scroll if the phone were in reach.

The student and the addict are the pair I cannot reconcile: both involve one part of the person overruling another, and I count the student's faraway choice as his real preference but not the addict's repeated choice.

Is there a principle, stated precisely, that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? If a simple principle forces me to overturn one, say which and why. What would your principle say about an unlisted case: someone moves to a city they have never visited on a friend's enthusiastic description, regrets it within weeks, and says the move was a mistake—did moving reveal that they preferred that city to staying?
