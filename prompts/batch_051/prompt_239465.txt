I've been trying to wrap my head around a weird disconnect in how we treat the dead when they get turned into films. On the one hand, in most US jurisdictions you can't sue for defaming a dead person: a defamation claim dies with the plaintiff, so an estate can't win damages just because a biopic makes the deceased into a monster or invents awful things they never did. On the other hand, studios and publishers act as though theres something to protect after death. Producers pay estates for what the industry calls life rights, negotiate over how the person will be portrayed, and sometimes cut or soften scenes to keep the family from walking out. Archives and oral history projects tie themselves in knots about whether it's okay to release unflattering material about someone who's gone. So which is it — does a person's reputation or dignity keep mattering after they're dead, or does all of this boil down to the feelings and interests of the living survivors? I don't think the practice can have it both ways without some pretty specific backstory about what we owe the real dead. Reconstruct the implicit theory this patchwork of law and industry practice presupposes. Be strict about what the practice actually presupposes versus what it merely fits with, and note where it's genuinely underdetermined. Then tell me what would have to change, legally or in how studios and archives operate, if we replaced that view with the view that the dead themselves have interests that can be harmed. I'm less interested in the legal fine print than in the philosophy underneath it.
