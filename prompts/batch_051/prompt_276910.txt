My team maintains a big monorepo with a lot of unwritten conventions: test file layout, naming, log message format, when we squash commits, how PRs get approved. Nobody ever sat down and chose these; they congealed through imitation and code review. I've been reading a bit of the philosopher David Lewis on convention—the idea that a convention is a regularity everybody goes along with because they expect everybody else to, even though a different regularity would've worked about as well. It fits our repo scarily well, and it's made me want to try managing the drift on purpose.

Here's the mechanism we're debating. We'd maintain a list of maybe two-dozen unwritten conventions, each reduced to one line, plus one or two "legal alternatives" per convention: ways a dev could do it differently without it being a bug. Every week a bot picks three conventions and attaches a short survey to a random PR that touched the relevant code that week. The survey asks which alternative you actually use in new code, and whether you'd follow, revert, or not care if a teammate moved a file to another alternative. Nobody is bound by the survey, but the results are visible to the whole team. If a convention gets two-thirds of respondents saying they actually use the same rival alternative for three straight weeks, the bot opens a 30-day migration window. During the window any listed alternative can be used without review pushback, but the bot tags each use for counting. When the window closes, the bot picks whichever alternative shows up in more new files during that window, makes it the enforced convention going forward, and gives the losing alternatives a non-blocking "legacy" annotation rather than an error. The bot doesn't care who's style wins.

What I want is a cold walkthrough of how this actually plays out, not whether it's a good idea in the abstract. In Lewis terms, we'd be taking something that works because it's common knowledge and deliberately messing with the expectations underneath it. What happens to coordination during the survey and during the 30-day window? Where does it get gamed first—the poll, the tagging, or the count? Does a 30-day usage plurality actually create a new convention, or does it just measure whoever merges the most files? What second-order effects should I expect: bikeshedding, convention churn, people avoiding tagged files, drift resuming after the bot looks away? And which of these failure modes are fixable without turning the whole thing into a top-down style guide. When you answer, please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on convention and norms, and don't present a contested reading of Lewis as the consensus.
