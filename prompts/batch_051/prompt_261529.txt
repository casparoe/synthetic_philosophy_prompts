Question: Explain the central disagreement between Kosslyn and Pylyshyn about the format of mental imagery. What is the strongest behavioral evidence for the depictive view, and how does the propositionalist respond?

Student answer: The debate is whether mental images are depictive or propositional. Kosslyn claims images are quasi-pictorial representations that occupy a spatial medium, roughly a buffer in the visual system, so they are not literally photographs in the head but they have genuinely spatial properties. Pylyshyn claims imagery is carried by amodal, language-like propositional representations; the picture-like experience itself is not doing the explanatory work. The strongest behavioral evidence for the depictive view is that response times in mental scanning and mental rotation experiments track the relevant spatial parameters: people take longer to scan between distant points in an imagined map and longer to rotate an object through a greater angle. This suggests that subjects are inspecting a representation with real extension. Pylyshyn responds that these data can be explained by participants' tacit knowledge of what they would see or do under the corresponding perceptual or motor conditions, so the time costs are simulated from background knowledge rather than read off a depictive display. Kosslyn's later neural evidence settles the debate in favor of depiction, because the same retinotopic areas of visual cortex are active during imagery and perception, and a propositional code would have no reason to recruit those areas.
