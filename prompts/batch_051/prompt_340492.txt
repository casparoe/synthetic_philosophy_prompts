In three weeks I'm giving a talk to a mixed corporate-governance and philosophy workshop, and I want to be ready for the question period. The thesis I'm defending is that a corporation can be genuinely morally blameworthy for a serious organizational failure even when no individual employee was culpably negligent, and that this blame is neither shorthand for "somebody on the inside did wrong" nor a merely instrumental fiction adopted for the sake of deterrence. My central argument is that firms make commitments through standing decision procedures; those procedures can themselves be defective—they can pool information badly, route dissent away from the relevant decision point, or keep in force policies that no current member ever chose—and it is the firm as an organized agent, not any employee's isolated act, that properly bears the blame when such a procedure predictably produces harm. The audience will include two corporate-law scholars sympathetic to entity theories of the firm, two philosophers who are strict methodological individualists about agency, one person who works on List-and-Pettit-style group agency, and several people who suspect corporate blame is a maneuver for letting executives escape responsibility. Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order they will actually come, phrased as each of these people would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, where my reply is weakest, and the objection I should be hoping nobody thinks to ask.
