I'm a high school student in a church youth group that takes Bible study seriously, and for over a year we've been stalled on a single disagreement. Whenever we read the New Testament instructions about head coverings in worship, slaves obeying masters, or dietary restrictions, the same argument starts. One side says these commands were directed at first-century situations and are culturally conditioned; what continues to bind us is the underlying principle, not the particular form. They point out that the New Testament itself seems to draw distinctions of this sort — the apostles do not impose the whole Mosaic law on Gentile converts, and Peter is told in a vision not to treat as unclean what God has made clean — so separating a culture-bound form from an enduring principle is something the canon already does. The other side says the default should be that a command remains binding unless scripture itself clearly sets it aside, and that calling a command cultural is far too vague a test to overturn an instruction. They add that Paul sometimes grounds these instructions in creation or in the way things are rather than in local custom, which suggests he meant at least some of them to have lasting force; and they worry that once cultural relativization is allowed without strict criteria, there is no principled stopping point before reaching moral commands both sides regard as fixed. We all accept the same texts, the same translations, and roughly the same view that scripture is authoritative, yet neither side has moved, and the debate keeps returning to the same standoff.

I am not asking which side is right. I am asking what kind of disagreement this actually is. My working theory is that it is less an interpretive dispute than a disagreement about what counts as a legitimate reason to treat a command as not binding now, and that this in turn rests on bedrock intuitions about authority, continuity, and change — intuitions that neither side can argue for without already assuming its own position. Is that diagnosis correct? Or is this really a substantive hermeneutical disagreement that could be settled by an explicit set of criteria for identifying culturally bound commands, by agreed-upon test cases, or by some distinction we have not yet drawn? Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement itself, engaging with what each side actually says, and then tell me what would count as genuine progress given that diagnosis — or, if convergence should not be expected, explain why.
