Recently I have been trying to make sense of a view I have seen called tranquilism, after a stretch in which one accomplishment after another left me as restless as before. As I understand it, tranquilism holds that the only thing of final value is the absence of craving and suffering: pleasure, achievement, intimacy, understanding, and similar goods matter only insofar as they quiet craving or forestall distress, and undisturbed calm is not a mere baseline but the only ultimately good state. I described the view to two friends, and their argument went roughly as follows.

Elena said tranquilism is self-undermining: if only the absence of craving has value, then anyone who accepts the view still wants that absence; but wanting it is itself a craving, so the position generates the very thing it condemns. Daniel replied that she was conflating first-order cravings for objects and outcomes with a second-order wish to be rid of such cravings; the latter, he said, is not an object-directed craving but a posture of disenchantment, and it need not carry the restlessness that makes craving bad. Elena answered that even the second-order wish is a state of lack, and lack is a form of dissatisfaction; so on the way to tranquility one is, by the view's own standard, still suffering, which means the view cannot be endorsed except by someone it condemns. Daniel said this is like claiming a man must clench his fist harder in order to unclench it; noticing and releasing a craving is not the same as having one. Elena then moved on: granting for the sake of argument that the view is not self-undermining, she said it still undervalues. Some goods, such as being absorbed in music, loving someone, or understanding something difficult, do not merely relieve discomfort; they seem worth having for their own sake, and a theory that calls them painkillers is misdescribing human life. Daniel replied that tranquilism can allow that such goods are good, because they quiet craving, and that the fact that their value feels intrinsic does not show that it is intrinsic; not every good advertises its real function.

At that point they asked me what I thought, and I could not sort out which points had actually landed. Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: for each substantive move, say whether it is largely right, partly right, or mistaken, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who is more right overall, what both of them are missing, and whether the version of tranquilism under discussion survives their objections.
