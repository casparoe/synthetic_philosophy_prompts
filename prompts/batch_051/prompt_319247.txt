People have been fighting about the units of selection in evolutionary biology for most of my life, and as far as I can tell the fight hasn't moved in decades. The gene's-eye people say selection is really happening at the level of the gene, with organisms as the throwaway vehicles genes build to copy themselves. The old group selectionists said adaptations like alarm calls or restrained breeding evolved because they're good for the group, and Sober and Wilson later revived a more careful version where selection can happen at several levels at once and group-level selection is a legitimate part of the story. Then the inclusive fitness people sort of shrug and say Hamilton's rule already tells you when a costly behavior spreads, by tracking relatedness, so the rest is bookkeeping. Whats weird to me is that the Price equation seems to let you describe all of this at whatever level you like, and I don't see new empirical results deciding between the camps. Yet the people actually in the debate still clearly think the other side is making a real error, not just using different notation. My working theory is that the disagreement is about what a causal explanation in evolution has to do: one side wants the stable replicating thing, another wants the level at which the fitness differences actually occur, and the third wants a decomposition of total fitness. Is that diagnosis right? If it is, is the debate substantive or are these just different questions under one heading? And if it isn't, what kind of disagreement is it—factual, theoretical, or just temperamental? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the literature, and an honest answer about what, if anything, would count as progress.
