Here's a little argument I've been tinkering with, and I'd like an second opinion. It's supposed to show that honoring sunk costs is never rationally justified, not just that it's usually a bad idea:

(1) A sunk cost is a cost that nothing you now do can recover or undo.
(2) A fact can be a reason for you to act only if it concerns something your action can affect.
(3) So a sunk cost cannot be a reason for you to act.
(4) Honoring a sunk cost is treating it as a reason to act.
(5) Therefore honoring a sunk cost is never rationally justified.

I suspect the trouble is in (2), but I can't put my finger on exactly what's wrong: after all, the fact that I promised last week looks like a reason to do something now, even though my having promised isn't something any present action can affect. So: is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and under what reading of 'reason' does it fail? And if it is valid, does that force us to say that keeping faith with your past self is never a rational basis for sticking with a project you've already sunk costs into?
