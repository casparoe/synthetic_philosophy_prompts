I teach a film-and-philosophy seminar and this term I want to pair Boethius's treatment of divine foreknowledge with Denis Villeneuve's Arrival (2016). The text I have in mind is book V of the Consolation of Philosophy, where Boethius argues that God's knowledge of what we call the future does not impose necessity on human action because God does not know events as future at all: he knows them from eternity, the way we know what is present to us now, and seeing something happen does not by itself make it happen. I want you to read Arrival through that lens. The film gives Louise a heptapod language that lets her "remember" her own future: she knows, before conceiving her daughter, that the daughter will die young, and she proceeds anyway. Is Louise's position a genuine dramatic equivalent of the Boethian solution, or is it something importantly different? Work through how the two cases handle the relation between knowledge of an event and the freedom or necessity of that event. Where does the analogy illuminate what the film is doing—say, its circular logograms, its treatment of memory and choice, its final scenes—and where does it break down? In particular, consider whether Louise's access to the future is still temporal foreknowledge, even if experienced as memory, and whether that matters: does the film end up implying a fixed block of events that Boethius would have wanted to resist, or does it show how foreknowledge and choice can coexist without fatalism? Be specific about scenes, and say plainly where you think the film pushes back against a Boethian reading rather than illustrating it.
