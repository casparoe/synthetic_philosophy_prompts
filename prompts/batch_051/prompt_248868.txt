A friend and I spent an evening arguing about superrationality, and by the end he had constructed an argument that seems to prove far more than either of us meant to claim. I cannot locate the step where it goes wrong, and I would like you to tell me which premise or inference to reject, and what rejecting it costs.

Here is the argument as he left it.

(1) Two agents face a single, symmetric Prisoner's Dilemma with no communication: each would individually do better by defecting whatever the other does, but each would be better off if both cooperated than if both defected.

(2) If two agents are equally rational and face the same decision problem, then any consideration available to one is available to the other. Consequently, if there is a decisive rational argument for an action, both reach it; they cannot rationally arrive at different actions unless some difference between their situations grounds the difference.

(3) In this dilemma, the only outcomes that do not posit an unexplained asymmetry between the players are both cooperating and both defecting. Of those, both cooperating gives each player more.

(4) Each player may therefore reason: if I cooperate, the other will cooperate, since cooperating is what an equally rational player does in my position; if I defect, the other will defect for the same reason. My real choice is between both-cooperating and both-defecting.

(5) The usual argument that defection is dominant treats the other player's choice as independent of mine, but that independence claim is false between equally rational players: their reasoning is shared, so their moves are linked.

(6) But the reasoning in (4) and (5) generalizes. A million equally rational people face a symmetric public good: if everyone contributes, each gains more than if no one contributes, though each alone would be slightly better off free-riding whatever the others do. By the same linkage, if I contribute, everyone contributes; if I do not, no one does. Among the matched outcomes, contributing is better.

(7) Therefore, in any symmetric collective action problem, a rational agent should contribute whenever universal contribution is better for each than universal non-contribution — regardless of the number of people involved, how small the individual effect is, or how unlikely future interaction is.

(8) That conclusion is more than either of us can accept: it appears to make free-riding irrational in every symmetric commons problem at every scale, and to turn a point about two-player games into a general principle that anyone may treat their own action as decisive for what all equally rational people do.

My friend insists the first three premises are nearly self-evident and that the only alternative is to give up the claim that equally rational agents in identical circumstances choose alike. I suspect the slide occurs around (4) or (6), but I cannot say exactly what is wrong there, and my attempts to weaken those steps without thereby destroying the original superrationality result feel like special pleading. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
