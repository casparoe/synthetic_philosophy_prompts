I'm a graduate student in political theory, and a state senator's policy staff have asked me to think through a bill they're drafting—not to check its fiscal estimates, but to map its conceptual and institutional logic. The bill is being presented as a third path between police reform and defunding: it leaves department budgets intact but internalizes the cost of force. The mechanism, as drafted, has four parts. First, every police–civilian contact involving force beyond unresisted handcuffing is a reportable "force event." Second, the state runs a public mutual to cover force liability, on the expectation that private insurers would exit the market. Third, each department pays an annual premium per 1,000 citizen contacts, set by a formula that weights force events, sustained misconduct complaints, and civil settlements or judgments. Fourth—and this is the part the sponsors call the whole point—the premium is paid by diverting up to 40% of the employer contributions that would otherwise go into officers' defined-benefit pensions, so the cost is borne inside the department's own long-term compensation rather than by general taxpayers; a department whose premium exceeds that 40% ceiling may not operate patrols and must contract with county or state police for three years.

I'd like an account of how this would actually play out, not whether it's good in the abstract. In particular: what it incentivizes for patrol officers, command staff, police unions, municipal budget offices, and the actuaries running the state mutual; how it would be gamed or evaded—say, underreporting or reclassifying force, suppressing complaints, manipulating contact numbers, pushing force into neighboring jurisdictions or into special units, or gaming the pension mechanics; where it fails in ways serious enough to be worse than the status quo; and which of those failure modes can be patched without gutting the scheme. Lead with a one-sentence bottom line, then unpack. Keep the whole thing under 900 words.
