The levels-of-selection controversy has a peculiar feature: after decades of argument, the rival positions often turn out to be formally intertranslatable. Kin-selection and group-selection representations can be converted into one another through the Price equation, and defenders of the gene's-eye view and of multi-level selection frequently accept the same underlying mathematics while continuing to describe what natural selection is 'really' doing in incompatible terms — one side treating genes as the units that matter and organisms as mere vehicles, the other treating selection as acting at several levels at once. The dispute persists even where no one expects different observations. I want a diagnosis of this impasse rather than a verdict: is there a residual empirical disagreement hiding somewhere, a disagreement about which mathematical representation tracks the real causal structure, or a disagreement about the word 'selection' and what an explanation is supposed to do? Then say what, on that diagnosis, would count as progress — a formal result, a new kind of data, or a conceptual distinction that could actually move someone — or argue that the debate has no terminus and explain what that would show about its scientific status.
