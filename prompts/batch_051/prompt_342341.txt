For my seminar on epistemic responsibility we've been doing Clifford and James, and I have to write a short dialogue between a Cliffordian and a Jamesian arguing over whether you can believe something on "passional" grounds when the evidence doesn't decide it. I dont want the usual canned exchange about "insufficient evidence"; I want the whole thing to turn on this bit from W. K. Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief": "The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him." Write maybe 700-ish words where the Cliffordian presses that passage against James's defense of voluntarily adopted faith, and the Jamesian actually answers it rather than dodging into religious examples. A smart reader should be able to see by the end exactly where the two really disagree about what "origin" includes — does willed faith corrupt the origin of a belief, or is it just a different way of acquiring one? Both characters should be competent and respond to each other's actual points, and dont let the Jamesian quietly turn into a Cliffordian halfway through.
