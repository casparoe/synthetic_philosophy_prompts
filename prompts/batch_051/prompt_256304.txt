I keep noticing how much of our institutional life is quietly organized around the assumption that there will be a human future. We fund seed vaults in the Arctic so that agriculture could restart after a catastrophe. We design warning markers for nuclear waste repositories that are meant to remain intelligible for tens of thousands of years. Museums and archives make open-ended commitments to preserve fragile objects, not for any audience alive now, but for audiences they simply assume will eventually exist. None of these projects pays off within the lifetime of anyone asked to fund them, and several are pointless unless a recognizably human civilization actually continues.

I want to know what these practices presuppose about why human extinction would be bad. The obvious part is uncontroversial: extinction would be bad because it involves billions of premature deaths, suffering, and the thwarting of individual plans. But these institutions seem to presuppose something more — that an ongoing human future matters over and above the welfare of the particular people who will exist, and that our present projects get some of their meaning from the assumed background of a continuing human story. That sits uneasily with a widespread reluctance in our moral and legal thinking to say that merely possible people have claims on us, or that anyone alive is owed a duty to keep the species going. So I want a reconstruction of the implicit theory of the badness of extinction that these practices commit us to. Which rival theories do they quietly rule out, and which would still license them? Where is the practice genuinely underdetermined between competing views? And if a society actually adopted the view that extinction is bad only because of the deaths it involves, which of these practices would have to change, and which could survive unchanged?
