I'm on the board of a small fraternal benefit society that's been running since the 1950s, and we've spent three meetings stuck on an underwriting bylaw we now have to apply to something its drafters plainly never imagined. The rule, adopted in 1973, reads: 'The premiums charged to a member shall be fixed on the basis of the experience of the class of members to which he most nearly belongs, and in forming such classes the Committee shall have regard to the age, sex, and occupation of the members.' A reinsurance broker is pitching us a machine-learning platform that scores each member individually from genomic, pharmacy, and spending data, so we could charge every member a premium matched to their own predicted claim costs. Half the board says this has to breach the bylaw, because a class of one is no class at all and there's no pooled experience to learn from; the other half says the platform just does what the rule always meant, which is put each member in the class they most nearly belong to, and we can now see what their really like. A friend who took philosophy of probability says we've rediscovered the reference class problem, and I can see why: any member belongs to dozens of overlapping classes that imply different premiums, and the old rule just quietly selects age, sex, and occupation. But I'm less interested in the general problem than in what this text actually requires. Walk me through what each approach—the wording, the purpose of the clause, what a reasonable member would take it to mean, and what the drafters would probably have said in 1973 if asked about genomic risk scores and singleton classes—would give here, and tell me which approach should win and why. Start with your bottom line in one sentence, and keep the whole thing under 700 words.
