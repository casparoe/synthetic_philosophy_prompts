Two members of a reading group I attend fell out at the last meeting over a proposal I had circulated: replacing one house of a national legislature with a chamber drawn by lot rather than elected. I have followed the sortition and lottocracy debates on the rationalist-adjacent blogs for years, so I know the stock arguments on both sides, but this exchange moved past them quickly and I want a careful adjudication before I take a view.

The defender of the proposal began by reading a passage from Aristotle's Politics, IV.9, in H. Rackham's Loeb translation as reproduced by Perseus: "I mean, for example, that it is thought to be democratic for the offices to be assigned by lot, for them to be elected oligarchic, and democratic for them not to have a property-qualification, oligarchic to have one; therefore it is aristocratic and constitutional to take one feature from one form and the other from the other, from oligarchy that offices are to be elected, and from democracy that this is not to be on a property-qualification." His point was that even Aristotle classes election as an oligarchic device, so a lottocratic chamber is not a distortion of democracy but a return to its core. The other replied that the passage says only that this is "thought to be" so: Aristotle is listing the conventional marks of constitutions precisely in order to show that they can be recombined, and the same sentence treats election without a property-qualification as "aristocratic and constitutional". So, she argued, the text shows that neither election nor the lot is intrinsically anything until the surrounding rules settle its character. The first came back that the conventional marks were not arbitrary: elections select for the notable, the well-funded and the already-organised, and the Greeks noticed this. A lot is the one appointment device that gives every citizen an equal chance to rule, which is why it was read as democratic; universal suffrage has not abolished a candidate class so much as made its dominance feel consensual. The second answered that modern elections differ in the one respect Aristotle could not see: a universal franchise plus the power to remove incumbents makes election machinery for consent and accountability rather than for oligarchy. Equality of selection is not the same as being governed with one's consent; a chamber drawn by lot can rule over people who never chose it, and its members cannot be turned out at the next ballot. That, she said, is why lot is suited to juries and audits, which must be impartial, and badly suited to law-making, which must be responsive.

I can see force on both sides and do not want to settle this with my priors. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four moves (the appeal to Aristotle; the textual rebuttal; the "candidate class" reply; the "consent and accountability" reply), rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating. Then say which side is right about which specific points and what the pair are jointly missing; where your verdict rests on contested empirical claims about elections or assembled bodies, mark those as such. Finally, say how confident you are in the overall adjudication and single out the point you are least sure of.
