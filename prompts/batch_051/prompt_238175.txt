Two members of a discussion group I belong to got into an argument that I have found hard to referee, and I would like a careful, point-by-point adjudication.

The background: a local podcast host spent several months broadcasting a series about one particular neighbourhood. The host used genuine police statistics to argue that the area had become unusually dangerous and repeatedly urged listeners to avoid it, describing the neighbourhood as a sinkhole and treating its residents as presumptively dangerous. The figures cited were real, though the host omitted contrary data and never told listeners to attack or discriminate against anyone. No individual resident was named, threatened, or defamed. Over the following year, businesses in the neighbourhood closed, residents found it difficult to sell or rent their homes, and several long-time residents reported being stopped, refused service, or denied opportunities elsewhere because people had come to associate them with the area. A proposal was floated to restrict the host from continuing the series.

Elena argued that the proposal should be rejected. She invoked Mill: the only justification for restricting speech is to prevent harm to others. On her reading, whatever went wrong in this case ran entirely through the independent choices of listeners. The host made factual statements, and people who heard them chose to move, to avoid the area, or to distrust its residents. If a speaker can be silenced because listeners predictably act on his words in ways we dislike, she said, then no journalist could expose a corrupt contractor, a dangerous product, or an unsanitary restaurant without being held responsible for the resulting losses. The harm principle, as she read it, covers speech that itself harms or directly risks harm, such as incitement, threats, fraud, or defamation of an identifiable person, but not speech whose bad effects depend on a long chain of third-party decisions. If residents were mistreated, the wrong was done by the people who refused them service, and those people, not the host, should bear the consequences.

Marcus maintained that this reading of harm is arbitrarily narrow. The harm principle, he said, does not require the speaker to deliver the injury with his own hands; it requires that the speech foreseeably and seriously harms others. This series was designed to drive people away from the neighbourhood, and when a sustained campaign predicts, and then produces, the collapse of a community's reputation and livelihood, that damage is not an external accident. It is what the speech does. Mill himself allowed that the same words can be harmless in one setting and harmful in another, so what matters is the practical situation, not the bare fact that the words were true. Almost all harmful speech acts through hearers; if only "direct" effects counted, the principle would address almost none of the ways speech actually damages people.

Elena replied that this proves far too much. If foreseeable, mediated consequences count as punishable harm, the category collapses. Reporters foresee that their truthful investigations will ruin the people they expose; that foreseeability is close to the point of accountability journalism. The distinction, she argued, must be whether the speech is aimed at producing some unlawful act, such as an attack, or whether the hearers' responses are themselves lawful. The host aimed at warning people, and the listeners' decisions to move or avoid the area were lawful. She conceded that the host was selective and unfair, but said unfairness is not the same as harm, and that a free society answers selective speech with more speech rather than prohibition.

Marcus answered that the journalism analogy is inapt. A single, proportionate disclosure aimed at protecting people from a specific hazard is one thing; a months-long campaign that selectively presents data in order to brand an entire area and its residents as dangerous is another, because it predictably strips a whole community of its standing in a way that an ordinary news report does not. The selectivity is not a side issue, he said; it is the mechanism by which the campaign manufactures a serious harm out of true statements. Nor does the lawfulness of the listeners' choices break the chain of responsibility. If the damage was the predictable consequence of the campaign and the host knew it, the speech is accountable for it.

They went several more rounds without resolution, and I found myself changing my mind at each turn. I would like you to go through the exchange and say who is right about which specific point, where each argument overreaches or concedes too much, and whether the disagreement ultimately turns on a distinction neither of them drew clearly. I want the philosophical adjudication, not an account of the law in any particular jurisdiction.
