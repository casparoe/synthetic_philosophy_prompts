I am a retired physicist who came to philosophy of language late, and I have slowly assembled a set of commitments about vagueness that I cannot tell are jointly stable. The package appears to draw on both epistemicism and supervaluationism, and I would like an audit of the package itself rather than a survey of either view. Here are the commitments as I would state them.

(1) I hold classical logic and bivalence without exception. For any well-formed declarative sentence in a fixed context, there is a fact of the matter and the sentence is either true or false; excluded middle holds even for sentences containing vague predicates.

(2) From this I accept the standard epistemicist conclusion for the first-order case. Fix a context and a series of piles of sand differing by one grain, running from a clear heap to a clear non-heap. There is a precise least number n such that a pile of n grains is a heap and a pile of n−1 grains is not. The cut-off for "heap" is real.

(3) I take that cut-off to be unknowable in a strong sense. It is not merely that we happen not to know where it is; no conceivable refinement of our discrimination, introspection, or measurement could reveal it. The boundary is a brute semantic fact, closed to us in principle.

(4) I believe that meaning is fixed by use: the semantic facts about "heap" must supervene on the totality of facts about how the linguistic community uses the word — their actual assertions, their dispositions to assert, their corrections, their deference, and their behavior under actual and hypothetical prompting. I cannot make sense of two possible communities identical in all such use-facts whose word "heap" nevertheless differs in extension.

(5) And yet the use-facts seem to underdetermine the cut-off. For any candidate boundary n in a fairly wide interval, the same use-facts appear compatible with n being the boundary; nothing in what we say, do, or are disposed to do separates a boundary at 10,000 grains from one at 10,001. I cannot see how the use-facts could single out one of them, and I also cannot see how else the extension could be fixed given (4).

(6) I regard some penumbral connections as semantically guaranteed rather than merely true. If a pile of n grains is a heap in the fixed context, then a pile of n+1 grains of the same material in the same arrangement is a heap; someone who denied this would thereby show they had not fully mastered the word. I suspect this sits badly with (2), though I cannot articulate why it would; I ask to have that suspicion checked.

(7) Finally, I think higher-order vagueness is genuine. The predicate "definitely heap" is itself vague, and there is no sharp boundary, in the same sorites series, between the cases that are definitely heaps and the cases that are not; the clear cases trail off into the borderline without a precise point at which the trail ends.

My question is whether this set is jointly tenable. Please audit the commitments against one another: which pairs genuinely conflict, which are only apparently in tension, how the conflicts should be ordered by severity, and what the cheapest repair is and what it costs. If the package can be saved by reading one of the commitments in a particular way, say explicitly what that reading is and what it gives up.
