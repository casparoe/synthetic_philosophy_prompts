In my epistemology seminar we spent several weeks on the peer disagreement literature, and I am struck by how little the two main camps have moved despite decades of argument. Both sides seem to accept the same stock cases — dividing a restaurant bill and getting different totals, two equally qualified doctors reading the same scan and reaching opposite diagnoses — and both sides acknowledge the other's arguments, yet neither concedes anything. Conciliationists say that learning a genuine epistemic peer disagrees with you about P is higher-order evidence that your first-order reasoning about P has gone wrong somewhere, so you should substantially lower your confidence or suspend judgment unless you have a special reason to think you are better placed. Steadfasters reply that this makes one's own first-order reasons disappear too easily: if the reasons for P were good before I learned of the disagreement, the peer's contrary verdict alone does not show they were bad, so I can reasonably retain my belief while recognizing the peer as my equal, perhaps with modest adjustments in especially murky cases.

What I want is not a verdict but a diagnosis. My working hypothesis is that the debate is self-referential in a way that explains the stalemate: the question under debate is at the same time the rule for how the participants should respond to each other, so there is no neutral standpoint from which to assess the disagreement itself. The conciliationist's response to a steadfast peer is already an application of conciliationism, and the steadfast response to a conciliationist is already an application of steadfastness; each side performs its view when it responds to the other. That would make the impasse a feature of the subject matter rather than bad faith or vagueness. But I worry this is too tidy and may misdescribe at least one side.

So what kind of disagreement is this really? Is it verbal, a difference about how to weigh first-order reasons against higher-order evidence, a difference about what counts as epistemic peerhood in the first place, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about rationality that neither side can argue for? Does the self-reference diagnosis hold up when stated precisely, and does it apply to both camps or only to one? And given the correct diagnosis, what would count as progress — a sharper distinction, a formal result, a change in the question, or the admission that no progress should be expected? If the last, is that a serious problem for the debate, or just what one should expect from a foundational dispute?
