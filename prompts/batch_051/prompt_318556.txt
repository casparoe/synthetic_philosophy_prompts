Last week at lunch two people I know got into an argument about some shoes I'd just ordered from one of those ultra-cheap online brands, and I mostly just listened. Now I'm trying to figure out who was actually right about what.

Maya kicked it off by saying I shouldn't have bought them, because the people making them get paid poverty wages in unsafe factories, and sending money to that is basically paying someone to exploit people.

Dan said that doesn't make sense. Those factory jobs are the best option the workers actually have; otherwise they wouldn't take them. So the workers are better off with the job than without it, and if people like me stop buying, those jobs go away and that hurts exactly the people Maya says she's worried about. The exchange is mutually beneficial even if it looks ugly, so refusing to buy doesn't help anyone.

Maya said mutually beneficial and exploitative aren't opposites. She said if someone is about to drown and you offer to throw them a rope only if they sign over their house, they end up better off than if you'd done nothing at all, but your still exploiting their desperation. "Better off than the alternative" doesn't by itself make a deal fair.

Dan said the drowning case is different for a crucial reason: the rescuer is taking advantage of a situation he controls. The shoe company didn't create the poverty; it just offers a job inside it. Exploitation, he said, requires that you had a hand in creating the bad options someone faces, or that you're using some duty to fix those options as leverage. The company hasn't done either.

Maya said that might work for any one company in isolation, but the whole point of the business model is to go find people whose best option is that bad, and our endless demand for the cheapest possible stuff is what keeps their alternatives bad. So we're not innocent bystanders to the bargaining table; we're one of the reasons the table is tilted.

Can you go through their argument move by move and adjudicate it? For each move, say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between and give a brief reason. Then say what both of them seem to be leaving out.
