I've taught the Epicurean argument for years as if it were basically sound: when death is here, the person is not, so there's no subject left to be harmed; and since we don't regard the long stretch before our birth as bad for us, there's no good reason to regard the stretch after death any differently. It has real theoretical virtues — a clean criterion for when a thing can harm someone, an end to all the murky questions about *when exactly* a person's death is bad for them, and a tidy reply to the fear of death. But I can't make it fit a firm intuition I find myself coming back to: that a 20-year-old killed before she could do any of the things that mattered to her has been harmed by her death in a way the theory can't seem to locate. I keep telling students that the deprivation account patches this up — her death is bad because it deprives her of the life she would otherwise have led, not because she suffers through being dead — but I'm increasingly unsure whether that's a genuine reconciliation or just a pun on "bad for."

Here's what I want. Lay out the strongest version of both sides: the Epicurean argument, understood as a claim about harm requiring a subject at some time, and the intuition that a premature death is a paradigm case of harm to the person who dies. Then work through what each side must concede for a stable view. If I keep the Epicurean criterion, can I still say the student was harmed in any sense that isn't just a loose way of speaking? And if I keep the intuition, what do I have to say about the no-subject point and the symmetry with prenatal non-existence — do I deny that harm needs a subject at a time, or deny the symmetry, and what does that cost? Finish with your own negotiated verdict and be honest about whether "death is bad because it makes a whole life worse" is a real solution or a equivocation.
