Hey — I’m a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I’ve been asked to give the eight-minute opening remarks at a small workshop on neural data and mental privacy. The room will be a mix: some academic philosophers of mind, a few privacy lawyers, a couple of neurotech founders, and some foundation program officers. A few of them live and breathe this stuff, but others have only seen the Neuralink headlines. I don’t want to give the standard “privacy is important and brains are special” talk. I want to build the whole speech around a passage from John Stuart Mill’s *On Liberty* that I think frames the actual problem better than most of the BCI-ethics boilerplate. The passage is:

“Protection, therefore, against the tyranny of the magistrate is not enough: there needs protection also against the tyranny of the prevailing opinion and feeling; against the tendency of society to impose, by other means than civil penalties, its own ideas and practices as rules of conduct on those who dissent from them; to fetter the development, and, if possible, prevent the formation, of any individuality not in harmony with its ways, and compel all characters to fashion themselves upon the model of its own. There is a limit to the legitimate interference of collective opinion with individual independence: and to find that limit, and maintain it against encroachment, is as indispensable to a good condition of human affairs, as protection against political despotism.”

Here’s what I’m wrestling with. Mill is talking about social conformity — “prevailing opinion and feeling” — not about the state reading your thoughts. But if you run that argument forward into a world of consumer neurotech, it starts to look like the deeper threat isn’t the government jackboot. It’s the slow normalization of companies, schools, insurers, and even friends treating your attentional and emotional life as just another data stream to optimize. I want the audience to feel why “cognitive liberty” is not just a legal slogan, but something Mill would have recogonized as the same fight — while also being honest that his harm principle doesn’t cleanly map onto neural data. I don’t want to pretend Mill has all the answers.

Please act as a professional philosopher and speechwriter. Start by brainstorming two or three different angles I could take for the speech, and then draft the full thing. Make sure the passage isn’t just quoted and dropped — the speech should really turn on what Mill means by the “tyranny of the prevailing opinion and feeling,” and why that might be scarier than the magistrate when the thing being encroached on is your inner life. The tone should be warm and journalistic, not academic, and there should be one or two jokes that a neuroethicist would appreciate but that don’t alienate the lawyers in the room.
