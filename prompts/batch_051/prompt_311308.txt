I'm a software engineer and I end up thinking about long-running systems, so maybe I'm primed for this. The Fermi paradox argument, as I understand it, runs like: if intelligent life isn't extremely rare, then over billions of years at least one civilization would have spread across the galaxy—self-replicating probes, big visible engineering, radio leaks, whatever—and yet we see nothing, so something must be filtering life out somewhere. The key premise seems to be that an advanced civilization tends to expand and stay visible. But I've started to doubt that premise for a reason that comes from how computation actualy scales. Once a civilization is mostly digital—uploads, simulations, AI, whatever—its main activity is computation, and the physics of computation pushes you toward being small, dense, and cold, near a cheap energy source. Spreading your compute across lightyears gives you enormous latency and next to no bandwidth between nodes, which is a terrible architecture if the interesting computations need tight coupling; you wouldn't shard a database across stars. So maybe advanced civilizations deliberately converge on inner space instead of outer space, and the galaxy looks empty because everyone is huddled around their own star running simulations and radiating next to nothing. Does the Fermi argument survive this? I want to know exactly which premise gets hit: is it 'expansion is the default', 'visibility is the default', or 'at least one weird civilization is enough'? And can the argument be repaired by saying only one civilization has to break the trend, or does that just leave a much weaker puzzle?
