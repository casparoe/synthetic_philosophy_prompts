My philosophy of mind prof has been pushing the Searle–Dennett original-vs-derived intentionality thing all term, and by now she's got a running list of her own rulings on examples. Her line is Dennett's two-bitser gibe: Searle insists brains have original intentionality while anything we build — a computer, a sign — has only derived intentionality, but Dennett's point, as she keeps putting it, is that that's a check drawn on an account that itself was opened by blind natural selection. The brain's intentionality is inherited from a process that had none, so calling it original is the two-bitser: you get something for nothing.

Her rulings so far:
- a traffic sign that says STOP: derived.
- my shopping list: derived.
- a honeybee's waggle dance: original, though she says the dance is evolved machinery, not a symbol the bee is using.
- a parrot trained to say "hello, gorgeous": derived — "the greeting is ours, not the bird's."
- a chess engine's queen sacrifice: derived.
- a dog flinching at a ball kicked toward it: original.
- a B cell "recognizing" a pathogen: original — she says nobody has to read the receptor; the cell reads it itself.
- a newborn rooting for the nipple: original.

The new case she's assigned us for next week: a lab writes a bacterial genome from scratch, including a chemotaxis circuit that steers the cell toward glucose. Nobody taught the circuit "glucose = good"; the engineers just wired a gradient-following loop they understand, and the bacterium now runs itself. We have to predict whether she'll call its steering-toward-glucose original or derived.

First, lay out the candidate rules that fit her track record so far — there should be at least two plausible ones — and say what each predicts about the synthetic bacterium. Second, make your best guess about her actual rule (its fine if its genuinely underdetermined) and say why you lean that way. Third, give me the single best follow-up case that would force a choice between the surviving rules, and say what her answer to that follow-up would settle.
