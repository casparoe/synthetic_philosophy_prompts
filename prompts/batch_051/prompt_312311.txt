Hey—I run a moral philosophy reading group for advanced undergrads, 12–16 people, 80 minutes. The topic is the paradox of deontology. They've read some Scheffler and think agent-centred constraints are sort of silly. I want an activity that actually makes them squirm a little: not a thought experiment we discuss, but a live game with chips or candy where they make real decisions and run into the paradox themselves. Design a sequential game around a single act-type—say, pressing a button that deducts chips from a shared charity pot. The key structural twist: if one person voluntarily presses the button, that press disables the next two people's buttons, preventing two future presses of the exact same kind. But it still deducts chips from the pot, just like the presses it prevents. Before the run-through, have the group vote to adopt a rule like "no one presses, not even to stop others from pressing," so they're committed. Then run a few rounds where someone is in the position of having to either obey the rule and watch two other people press anyway, or break it to prevent those two presses. Include roles (e.g., pressers, observers, a banker), a time line, exact chip payoffs, what to do if people start negotiating or forming coalitions, and a debrief. The debrief should map the moment of temptation to the agent-relative/agent-neutral distinction and to the question why constraints should be about the agent's own agency rather than about minimizing bad events. Also say what the game can't capture—e.g., that a real deontologist might simply deny that pressing and the two prevented pressings are commensurable violations, or that the duty isn't about reducing violation-tokens at all. No lecture up front; don't name the paradox until the debrief.
