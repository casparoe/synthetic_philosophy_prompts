I am helping a group of researchers draft an internal position paper on how to train language models to do long-horizon reasoning of the sort where a problem is decomposed into many steps before a final answer is produced — mathematical proof construction, code generation, or scientific hypothesis evaluation. The group is divided. One camp favors outcome-based supervision: assign credit only to the final result, because intermediate reward is corruptible and because only final results count in the end. The other camp favors process-based supervision: reward each intermediate step for qualities such as validity, explicitness, and non-arbitrariness, the way an instructor grades a proof line by line rather than looking only at the last line.

We want a short internal essay that does more than list the familiar pros and cons. Please analyze the following challenge in depth. Consider two learners: one reasons in a way that is almost always truth-conducive but makes a single transcription error and reaches the wrong answer; another reasons via a specious shortcut and arrives at the correct answer by luck. Outcome-based supervision punishes the first and rewards the second. So far this favors process-based supervision. But now suppose the process verifier is itself a learned model or a human rubric with finite competence: it can only reward steps that it recognizes as good, not steps that are good. Does process-based supervision then simply relocate the same problem, replacing correct final answer with step that looks correct to a fallible judge? Or does it track something genuinely distinct — for example, epistemic conduct that is worth instilling even when the final answer is false?

Write the essay this group needs. Weigh the arguments on both sides, including the failure modes of each approach: reward hacking of outcome signals, timidity or verifier-gaming under process signals, and the possibility that process supervision is outcome supervision in disguise at the level of subgoals. Reach a clear, justified conclusion about when each form of supervision is preferable. A conditional verdict is acceptable — for instance, process supervision when an independent, competence-matched standard of good intermediate reasoning is available and outcome supervision otherwise — but only if the conditions are specified precisely and defended rather than assumed.
