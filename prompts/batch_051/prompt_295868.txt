I'm putting together a unit on punishment and rehabilitation—the old Halden-versus-mass-incarceration contrast—and I want to use _Les Misérables_ (I'll let students use the musical, since most of them won't read the whole thing) as an intuition pump. My read is that Hugo runs something like a natural experiment: prison takes a man who stole bread to feed his sister's kids and gives him back to society worse—suspicous, resentful, less able to trust—while one unearned act of mercy from the bishop does more moral work than nineteen years of hard labor. Javert, then, is the retributive worldview personified, and the novel's problem with him isn't only that he's cruel; it's that he can't survive finding out a convict might have become good. I want a philosophical reading from you that doesn't reduce the novel to a policy brief. Does Hugo actually argue something—that punishment alone cannot rehabilitate, or that rehabilitation depends on being treated as more than your worst act—or does he just stack the deck with a saintly bishop and a superhuman ex-con? Is there a version of retributivism the book is being unfair to? And what would the bishop's model require of an actual prison system: what would a humane prison have to do to be the bishop rather than the galley, and is that achievable at the scale of a state? Point me to the scences you'd teach, and be honest about where the moral psychology is persuasive and where it's just melodrama.
