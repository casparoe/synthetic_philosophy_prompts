I am a high school student, and in two weeks I have to run a fifty-minute study session for my calculus class on the epsilon-delta definition of a limit. My classmates can already evaluate simple limits from graphs and tables and have a working intuition that the function "gets closer and closer" to a value; most of them expect the formal definition to be an arbitrary hoop that adds nothing to that intuition. I want to respect the intuition without letting it substitute for the definition, and I do not want the session to collapse into either "the definition is just a formality" or "memorize this string of symbols." Do not explain the definition to me; plan how I should teach it. I need the order in which the pieces must be introduced and why each depends on the previous one — for instance, whether the absolute-value inequalities have to be read as distances before the quantifiers appear, and whether the order of the quantifiers has to be motivated before it is stated. I also need the single worked example that should carry the session, and what it must have and must lack; I assume it should be a limit where the function is undefined at the point, so that the definition is not secretly requiring the function's value there, but I am not certain. Then I need the misconceptions my classmates will bring or form — I am guessing "the function must actually reach the limit value," "epsilon is a fixed small number," and "delta is just the radius of some tiny circle" are three — and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. I also want you to mark the points where motivation can legitimately carry the lesson and the points where rigor has to be insisted on, what I should explicitly leave out at this level and say so, and one final question whose answer would show me that a student has acquired a usable definition rather than just a warm feeling about "approaching."
