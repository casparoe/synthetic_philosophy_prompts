My philosophy teacher runs an optional reading group on big-picture questions, and she has asked each of us to submit a short position paper before the next meeting. I want to write mine on how the simulation argument should bear on longtermism, but before I commit to anything I would like an audit of the set of claims I find myself wanting to assert all at once. I genuinely cannot tell whether the claims cohere or quietly pull against one another.

Here is the set:

1. The far future morally dominates the near term. Since the number of future lives could be astronomically large, reducing the probability of human extinction this century by even one-tenth of one percentage point is worth more than any realistic amount of spending on present-day poverty or disease; the expected value makes that close to trivial.

2. I take the simulation argument seriously and assign roughly twenty-five percent credence to the proposition that we are currently living in an ancestor simulation, meaning a detailed simulation of this era run by a mature posthuman civilization.

3. If we are in such a simulation, simulated people count. A simulated mind has the same moral status as a non-simulated one, and learning that our world is a simulation would not make the far future of the people inside it any less worth protecting.

4. But learning that we are in a simulation would make the far future radically harder to predict: the simulators could pause, terminate, re-run, or redirect the simulation for reasons invisible to me. Since my longtermism in (1) rests on treating projections about future populations as reasonably dependable, this would substantially dampen the far-future fanaticism.

5. Yet if we are not currently in a simulation, the simulation argument suggests my descendants may create very many simulations, which would make the pool of future morally significant lives even larger than ordinary longtermism assumes. On that horn, the argument appears to amplify far-future fanaticism rather than dampen it.

6. I reject the idea that deep uncertainty requires abandoning expected-value reasoning. The right response is to prefer interventions that pay off under many scenarios, and I currently take reducing extinction risk to be the clearest example.

I want to know whether this package can stand as issued. Does it yield one answer to the question whether the simulation argument dampens fanaticism about the far future, or do its parts point in different directions? I suspect there is at least one genuine tension and at least one apparent tension that dissolves. Please rank all the tensions you find by severity, identify which specific claim has to give first and what the least costly revision would be, and then give a committed verdict on whether the simulation argument should make someone like me more or less fanatical about the far future, where fanatical means letting far-future stakes outweigh all present-day stakes. Do not just lay out options and leave me to choose.
