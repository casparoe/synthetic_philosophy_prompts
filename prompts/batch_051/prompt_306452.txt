I have been following the distributive-justice literature from the outside for years, and one dispute strikes me as both structurally important and oddly immobile: the standoff over the leveling-down objection to telic egalitarianism. The egalitarian holds that inequality is bad in itself, so a distribution that eliminates inequality by bringing the better-off down to the level of the worse-off is in one respect better, even though no one is better off and at least one person is worse off. The prioritarian takes that implication to be a reductio: they hold that value is a function of individual well-being only, with extra weight given to improvements for the worse off, so leveling down is no improvement in any respect. Egalitarians reply, roughly, that the improvement is only pro tanto and not all-things-considered, that rejecting impersonal value elsewhere costs too much, and that the intuition that leveling down cannot be good in any way is simply person-affecting prejudice.

What bothers me is that the dialectic never seems to move. Each side has a stock reply to the other's moves, nobody updates, and the literature keeps producing variations on cases rather than changes in the structure of the disagreement. My working hypothesis is that the real dispute is a background one: whether evaluative predicates like 'good' range only over how lives go for individuals, or also over properties of distributions themselves. On this diagnosis, prioritarians are implicitly committed to a person-affecting account of value while telic egalitarians allow impersonal value, and the leveling-down exchange is mostly downstream of that unstated disagreement. Is that the right diagnosis? I want a diagnosis with reasons rather than a survey of positions: is this a substantive disagreement in value theory, a verbal dispute that could be dissolved by tabooing the word 'good,' or two programs that differ only in which implication they are willing to accept? And given the diagnosis, I want to know what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a new kind of case, a change of question — or an argument that the question as posed is ill-posed and that the field should stop expecting convergence.
