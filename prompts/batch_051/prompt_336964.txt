I help run a reading group on AI alignment for advanced undergraduates and graduate students who mostly come out of the rationalist-adjacent ecosystem — we have read the sequences, we are comfortable with terms like mesa-optimization and Goodharting, and we want to move past name-dropping toward actually having the intuitions in our bones. Our next session is on iterated amplification and distillation (IDA): the picture on which you take a slow, expensive human overseer process (for example, a human consulting many copies of themselves), use it to generate decisions, then train a fast model to imitate those decisions, and repeat the cycle. The standing worry is that the distillation step does not preserve what was not legible in the observed behavior — the reasons, the values that only bind in rare cases, the competencies that look like noise from the outside. I want participants to feel that loss happen to them in a controlled way, rather than hear me assert it.

The session is 90 minutes, about 18 participants, and I can print case cards and policy sheets but cannot assume props beyond paper, a whiteboard, and a timer. Please design a structured activity — a game or simulation with roles, rules that need no real money, and a hidden scoring rubric seen only by the facilitator — in which participants run a miniature amplification-and-distillation pipeline over several rounds. Some participants should act as the slow overseer process, applying a short charter of values to concrete cases; some should act as the distiller, seeing only the decisions and compressing them into a policy for the next round; and the rest should act as the distilled policy, applying that policy to fresh cases without ever seeing the charter. The cases should be morally and practically tractable (something like insurance-claim triage for a small mutual-aid fund would be in the right spirit, if that works), and the charter should include at least one deontic side-constraint that binds only rarely and at least one competence requirement that is easy to overgeneralize away, so that drift across rounds is non-accidental.

Give me the full rules, the case set and charter, the facilitator's scoring rubric, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the IDA argument — amplification, distillation, distributional shift, hidden values, Goodharting, and the gap between imitating decisions and inheriting reasons. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the actual IDA view the simulation cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself in the final five minutes.
