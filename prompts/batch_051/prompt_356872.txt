The view I have come to hold is that the profit motive is the root cause of catastrophic and existential risk from advanced AI. By "profit motive" I mean the structural pressure on firms to maximize returns to shareholders and outcompete rivals, not individual greed. And by "root cause" I mean something stronger than "one important factor among several": I mean the thing that turns a hard technical problem into a likely disaster. I am deliberately avoiding the word "capitalism," because it bundles together markets, private ownership, and wage labor, and I want the target to be the profit pressure specifically.

The view breaks into three nested claims.

(1) Causal priority. The principal driver of unsafe development and deployment of increasingly capable AI is structural profit pressure, the need to raise capital, grow fast, capture markets, and satisfy shareholders, ahead of scientific curiosity, ordinary technical difficulty, or the intrinsic character of the technology itself.

(2) Counterfactual. In the absence of profit pressure, imagine AI developed under a democratically accountable, non-competitive, publicly controlled regime, the same technical advances would not, by default, produce catastrophe, because no actor would have the incentive to race, to scale faster than safety arguments can justify, or to deploy before alignment is solved.

(3) Prescriptive upshot. If the profit motive is the root, then the serious response must target economic structure, not merely solve alignment or impose regulation inside a profit-driven system, because profit-driven actors will capture, weaken, or outrun regulation.

I want to know what would count against this view, not the stock dismissals, but a map of its exposure.
