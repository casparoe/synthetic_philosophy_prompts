A friend and I spent the whole evening fighting about this and now I'm second guessing my slides. Next month I'm giving a seminar at an economics department, and I'm presenting the ergodicity-economics line that for repeated multiplicative gambles — say you repeatedly bet some fraction of your wealth and the outcomes compound — the usual expected-value setup averages over parallel worlds instead of over what actually happens to one person across time, and the right thing to maximize is the time-average growth rate. My claim is that log utility and Kelly-type rules fall out of the math of compounding rather than from any assumption about how much people like risk, and that decision theory should treat "maximize expected log growth" as the default for these repeated settings, not as a special case of expected utility with log preferences.

The audience is mostly macro and finance economists who think in terms of expected utility, risk aversion, and portfolio choice, plus one senior decision theorist who is going to be annoyed if I imply Savage is wrong. They're not going to object to the algebra; their going to object to the interpretation.

The quick version of my argument: wealth after n rounds is a product of per-round growth factors; take logs and it becomes a sum; by the law of large numbers, for large n the realized growth rate per round is pinned down by the expected log factor, so the path that almost surely dominates in the long run is the one maximizing expected log growth, while expected wealth gets pulled up by parallel-world outcomes the agent will almost surely never experience.
