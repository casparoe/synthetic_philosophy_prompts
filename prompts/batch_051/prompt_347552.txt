I work in a hospital's quality-improvement office, and I have recently been asked to review a vendor's 30-day readmission risk model that scores patients as low, medium, or high risk, with medium- and high-risk patients automatically flagged for a follow-up intervention. I have been reading about impossibility results in algorithmic fairness, and I keep running into the claim that a risk score cannot in general be calibrated across groups and simultaneously satisfy balance for the positive class and balance for the negative class, except under very special conditions. I do not want a survey or a bibliography. I want a concrete toy example, built to order, that makes the structure of that tradeoff visible.

Construct a toy readmission-risk scenario meeting all of the following specifications.

1. There are two patient groups, A and B, with unequal 30-day readmission rates. The outcome is binary: readmitted or not readmitted. The score has three levels, labeled by the risks they claim: low equals 20 percent, medium equals 50 percent, high equals 80 percent. A patient is flagged as positive exactly when they are scored medium or high.

2. Calibration holds exactly: for each score level in each group, the fraction of patients at that level who are in fact readmitted is exactly the claimed percentage.

3. Balance for the positive class holds exactly: the true positive rate, defined as the share of readmitted patients who are flagged, is identical in the two groups.

4. Balance for the negative class fails: the false positive rate, defined as the share of non-readmitted patients who are flagged anyway, is not identical in the two groups.

5. The scenario is realistic in these senses: all counts are whole numbers of patients in the hundreds, both groups have all three score levels, every score level in both groups contains some readmitted and some non-readmitted patients, and no score level is a perfect predictor.

The counts must themselves generate these verdicts; do not simply assert that the criteria hold.

After the scenario, give me two complete contingency tables, one for each group, showing the number of readmitted and non-readmitted patients at each of the three score levels. Then do the arithmetic check: verify calibration, verify balance for the positive class, and verify the failure of balance for the negative class.

Then tell me which structural feature of the joint distribution of score and outcome is doing the work. Address this directly: is the unequal base rate doing the work, or does the failure of negative-class balance come from something else? If you removed the working feature, such as by giving the two groups proportional score distributions, would calibration plus positive-class balance force negative-class balance? Explain why.

Finally, give me your self-check in the form I care about most: under which stricter reading of one of the criteria would your example no longer count? For instance, someone might insist that balance for the positive class must hold at every possible threshold, not just the medium/high threshold. Tell me which such refinement would change your verdict that the case satisfies the specifications, and where your construction is most likely to fail under scrutiny.
