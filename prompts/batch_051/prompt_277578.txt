I chair the review panel for a small Foundations of Learning fund, and we've got one proposal this cycle that's split the panel clean in half, so I need a decision memo to circulate before Thursday's meeting. The applicant is an evolutionary biologist who now works in an ML group. His proposal starts from the claim that gradient descent is in an obvious sense a much stronger optimizer than natural selection ever was: it can follow an actual gradient, it has a fixed, computable loss, and it gets to restart from scratch thousands of times. He then argues that this advantage is precisely the problem. Because natural selection has no fixed loss surface, it can't overfit to one in the way a trained network can, and it tends to produce systems that are robust across environments rather than tuned to a benchmark. The funded work would (a) formalize the conditions under which the selection-as-optimizer analogy is and isn't safe to use in learning research, and (b) build a worked example in which a mechanism from developmental biology — he floats phenotypic plasticity or bet-hedging, and promises to pick one early on — is imported into an optimizer and tested on a distribution-shift benchmark. Part of the panel reads this as a genuinely fresh angle; the rest think the analogy has been doing sloppy work in ML papers for years and we shouldn't pay to keep it alive. Draft the memo I'll circulate. It should recommend fund, reject, or fund with conditions, give reasons the applicant could actually respond to, and take the strongest point on the losing side seriously rather than strawmanning it. A page and a half is plenty — the committee reads it the night before.
