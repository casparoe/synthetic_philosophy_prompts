I’m putting together a 48-hour take-home essay for an upper-level seminar on Hegel’s political philosophy, and I keep returning to a paragraph that has started to feel uncomfortably relevant in conversations with a friend who served in the military and asked, with genuine unease, whether Hegel gives a philosophical license to war. The passage I have in mind is from Hegel’s *Philosophy of Right* (1821), §324. In that section, Hegel denies that war should be treated as an absolute evil or as something merely external and contingent; he suggests that war preserves the ethical health of peoples by preventing them from sinking into the stagnant pursuit of private comfort and particular interests, and he uses the image of the movement of the winds keeping the sea from corruption. The argument sits within his larger claim that the state is the actuality of the ethical life and that individuals have their highest duty as members of that state.

I would like you to write a final exam question on this passage that cannot be answered by summarizing Hegel’s doctrine or by moralizing about war. The question should force students to work out what the passage actually says, what it does not say, and whether the argument can be defended against the charge of militarism. It should also make them connect §324 to the larger structure of Hegel’s account of ethical life and the state, rather than treating the passage as a free-standing opinion. Please include a model answer of roughly 800 words and a marking scheme separating an A from a B answer by naming the specific exegetical and critical moves each must contain. The course is upper-level, the students will have read the Introduction and the whole Third Part of *Elements of the Philosophy of Right*, and the question should be aimed at about 90 minutes of concentrated writing. If you think the task as posed is confused, for example because evaluating §324 in isolation already distorts Hegel’s argument, or because asking whether Hegel’s view is militarism assumes a standard that has to be motivated, say so plainly and adapt the question instead of producing something merely plausible.
