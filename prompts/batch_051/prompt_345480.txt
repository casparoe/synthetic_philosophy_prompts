I'm an undergraduate taking a course on Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit, and we have just worked through the section on lordship and bondage. For my final paper, I want to read Kazuo Ishiguro's novel The Remains of the Day through that section. My starting hunch is that Stevens, the butler, looks at first like a textbook bondsman: his self-understanding is mediated entirely through service to Lord Darlington, he treats dignity as something achieved by subordinating himself to a great house, and he refuses or misreads the forms of recognition offered to him by others, above all Miss Kenton. But the novel seems to complicate the Hegelian account in two ways that interest me. First, Stevens's subservience is not extracted in anything like a life-and-death struggle for recognition; it is chosen and rationalized from the inside, so it is not obvious who the lord is or whether the structure is coercive at all. Second, the bondsman's labor is supposed to give him back a self-consciousness more independent than the lord's, whose enjoyment depends on the bondsman's work, yet Stevens's labor appears to hollow him out rather than realize him: his professionalism becomes a way of not seeing what is happening in the house, not just the private tragedy of his relationship with Miss Kenton but also the political meaning of Lord Darlington's appeasement-era guests. I would like a critical reading that does justice to both the novel and the Hegelian text. Where does the novel genuinely dramatize Hegel's point that self-consciousness needs recognition from another and that recognition between unequals is unstable? Where does the novel show something the dialectic misses about chosen subordination, professional dignity, and self-deception? Which scenes or relationships should I lean on, and where should I honestly admit that the novel resists the Hegelian reading rather than forcing it? End by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical account that abstract argument alone does not, and take a position on whether Stevens is best read as a failed bondsman, as a case that exposes the bondsman's silent power over the lord, or as something the dialectic has no clean place for.
