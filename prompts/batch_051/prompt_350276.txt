I run the moderation and acquisitions side of an independent publishing platform that hosts serious historical discussion and considers unsolicited manuscripts, mostly in twentieth-century European and East Asian history. Every week my team reviews flagged posts, comments, and book proposals that dispute standard accounts of the Holocaust, the Armenian genocide, or the Nanjing massacre, and we must decide quickly which are legitimate historical revisionism and which are denialism. Our policy is deliberately content-neutral at the level of conclusions: we do not remove or reject a piece because its thesis is unorthodox, and we do not take sides on any particular historical claim. What we remove or reject is denialism.

Our working principle is that denialism is not a thesis but a method, a way of handling evidence that makes a preferred conclusion immune to correction. Legitimate revisionism, on this view, makes claims that are in principle defeasible, engages the strongest contrary evidence in its context, and treats anomalies as something to explain. Denialism reverses the direction of inference: it begins with the conclusion and treats evidence as either serviceable or tainted. Its recurring features are asymmetric skepticism, which applies implausibly high standards to sources that support the standard account while accepting sources that do not with little scrutiny; a standing appeal to conspiracy, in which scholarly consensus is treated as evidence of suppression rather than of convergence on evidence; the moving target, in which a failed claim is quietly replaced by another without acknowledging the loss; and immunity to refutation, in which the author cannot say what evidence would change their mind.

The practical problem is that my moderators are not specialists in any of the relevant historiographies. They are history graduate students and well-read laypeople who have twenty to thirty minutes per case and no time for original research. They make recommendations and I make the final call on removals and rejections, but I can personally review only the cases they escalate. The decisions are high-stakes: we risk the platform's credibility, legal exposure in several jurisdictions, and the integrity of the imprint, while also risking the censorship of legitimate scholarship if we overreach. I need a reusable procedure, not a ruling on any single post, that operationalizes the principle above for a non-specialist. It should be an ordered checklist a moderator can run from top to bottom. For each step, state what counts as a satisfactory answer and what the step licenses. Mark exactly where the procedure stops with a firm verdict, where it must escalate to a domain expert or to me, and where the question is irreducibly one of judgment rather than method. Do not simplify the principle into a test that checks which conclusion a piece reaches; the entire point is that the procedure must separate method from conclusion, even when the conclusion is inflammatory or conspiracist.

Then apply the procedure to at least three invented but realistic worked cases: a post that defends an unorthodox conclusion by methodologically sound revisionist means; a manuscript that reaches a mainstream conclusion but uses denialist patterns of argument; and a denialist piece dressed in scholarly apparatus that is not obviously bad-faith. Ensure at least one worked case yields a verdict your team would dislike, and identify at least one borderline case the procedure cannot settle.
