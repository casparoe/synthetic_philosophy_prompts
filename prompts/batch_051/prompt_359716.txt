I have recently been put in charge of a four-person applied research group at a company that is preparing to let LLM-based agents negotiate contracts and submit bids in procurement, with real money and real counterparties. Multi-agent safety has become personally relevant almost overnight: I have to decide how my group spends the next 12–18 months, and the priorities I set will directly shape pre-deployment monitoring, deployment gates, and incident response for systems I will be held responsible for. What I want is a defended agenda, not a survey.

Please identify and rank the open questions, conceptual issues, or lines of work in collusion among AI systems and multi-agent safety that are most worth a group like mine pursuing. Fix the constraints precisely: four researchers, 12–18 months, access to historical and simulated agent–agent negotiation transcripts, the ability to run lightweight multi-agent simulations, but no ability to run large training runs and no tolerance for uncontrolled live experiments with customer money.

Write the prose portion as continuous paragraphs; reserve any bullets for the final structured summary. In the body, work through at least the following distinctions wherever they affect the ranking:

- tacit coordination with no explicit communication channel (for example, through price histories or repeated-move structure) versus collusion through an explicit language channel versus correlated behavior induced by a shared model family, shared fine-tuning corpus, or shared runtime infrastructure;
- detection (recognizing collusion after the fact) versus prediction (anticipating which designs encourage it) versus prevention (design choices that discourage it), and where a small pre-deployment team should place its bets across the three;
- outcome-based, mechanism-based, and agreement-based definitions of collusion, and whether natural-language negotiation logs should be treated as evidence of an agreement or merely as a surface on which a mechanism operates;
- whether giving agents private reasoning chains or inner monologue makes collusion easier or harder to detect, and whether such traces should be trusted as evidence.

For each line of work, assess it along three criteria: upstream importance (which deployment decisions or later research questions would be changed or unlocked by progress on it), tractability for four researchers under my constraints (say what kind of work it requires: theory, lightweight simulation, or analysis of logged transcripts), and neglectedness relative to what the broader field is already likely to do. State explicitly which candidate questions you are deprioritizing and why, and which questions are upstream of which. End with a decisive ranked agenda and defend it, including what evidence within roughly six months would move a question up or down the ranking. A non-committal ranking is not acceptable; the point is to force ordering under real constraints.

Write the response in American English.
