Some places have run their own affairs for decades — controlling their borders, keeping order, running courts, issuing documents their residents actually use — and yet nearly every other country refuses to treat them as states. Somaliland is the clearest example I know, though it is not the only one. At the same time, there are places whose governments can barely exercise control over their claimed territory, and nobody questions that they are states. The difference, as far as I can tell, comes down to recognition.

Two accounts of that difference keep appearing in what I read. The first, usually called declaratory, says recognition only declares a fact that already exists: once a place has a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to conduct relations with other states, it simply is a state, whether other states acknowledge it or not. The second, usually called constitutive, says this gets things backwards — the acknowledgment of other states is itself part of what makes a state a state.

This argument has been running for a very long time, and the same moves seem to be made again and again without anyone shifting. My working theory is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about any fact about the places in question, and not even mainly about the legal rules. I suspect the disagreement is about what kind of concept statehood is: whether being a state is more like being an island — a feature of the world that holds regardless of what anyone thinks or does — or more like being married, a citizen, or a member of a club, where the standing exists only because some collective body has granted or recognized it. If that is right, the impasse is about the category itself rather than about any particular case.

Is my diagnosis correct, or is a substantive legal or political disagreement hiding underneath it? I want the disagreement itself diagnosed, with the reasoning laid out, rather than a catalogue of the familiar positions. Specifically: are the two camps disagreeing about facts, about which cases count as evidence, about the meaning of the word state, about who should get to decide, or about what the category of statehood is for? Could they be answering two different questions — one legal, one political — under a single heading? Finally, given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a kind of test case, a change in practice, a formal result — or whether no progress should be expected, and why.
