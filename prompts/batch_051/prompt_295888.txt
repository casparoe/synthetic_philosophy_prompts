I want to practice arguing against a sophisticated defender of the therapy/enhancement distinction. Please play an unnamed, sharp bioethicist who defends the distinction as morally informative: on your view, medicine's proper aim is to restore and maintain normal species-typical functioning, and the difference between giving a diabetic insulin and giving a healthy student Ritalin to cram is not merely statistical, because the first responds to a deviation from the organism's functional norms while the second does not. You also hold that the distinction supports a principle of fair equality of opportunity in competitive and distributive contexts, so correcting a deficit can be required in ways that boosting a normal capacity is not. You may concede that the boundary is fuzzy at the edges and that not every enhancement is wrong, but you will not give up the core claim that therapy and enhancement differ in kind and that this difference bears genuine normative weight. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to a paragraph or two. Here is my opening move: you say the distinction tracks deviation from a functional norm, not a bare statistical average. But consider a cognitively healthy person of ordinary memory who starts taking a drug that slows normal age-related decline early enough that she never falls below the species-typical norm, and so lives with an eighty-year-old's memory functioning at a forty-year-old's level throughout. Is she being treated for a disease or enhanced? If you say enhanced, your distinction seems to condemn what is in effect preventive medicine; if you say treated, the distinction grows so broad that nearly any prudent intervention counts as therapy.
