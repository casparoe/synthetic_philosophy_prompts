So I've been going back and forth with my brother about this betting argument for why you should keep your degrees of belief consistent, and I keep tripping on the same point. The setup as I understand it: if your credences are incoherent — say you're 60% sure it'll rain and also 60% sure it won't, or your confidence in related claims doesn't add up — then in principle a bookie can offer you a bunch of bets where each one on its own looks good by your own standards, but all of them together guarantee you lose money no matter what happens. My brother says this is basically a proof that incoherent beliefs are irrational, because if you're exploitable like that then something is broken about how you think.

What I don't get is what the argument is actually trying to show. If a real bookie tried this on me, after the first bet or two I'd probably notice something's off and refuse to keep going — and anyway I'd know better than to bet with someone who seems to know my credences better than I do. If the argument needs to imagine me accepting every bet one at a time without ever looking at the whole package, then it's not describing anyone real. So is it a claim about what a smart but incoherent person would actually do in a real betting situation, a claim about some idealized version of me who has to accept every individually fair bet, or a claim about the structure of my beliefs themselves, even if no bookie ever shows up? And if it's the last one, then why do people bring up the bookie at all — couldnt they just say credences should obey the probability rules and skip the story?
