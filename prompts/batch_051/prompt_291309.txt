I keep coming back to a view about punctuality and the small promises that organize an ordinary day — I'll confirm by noon, I'll have the draft to you Friday, I'll be at the café at four — and I want it stress-tested rather than just left as a hunch. My suggestion is that these are not best analyzed as promises in the full-blooded sense, where breach creates a performance obligation and the natural response is apology plus recommitment. Instead they generate something I'll call a planning claim. When I tell you I'll arrive at four, what I've really done is invite you to build part of your schedule on my stated return. If I arrive at four-twenty-five, the core wrong is not the falsified prediction but the twenty-five minutes of planning I consumed: time in which you had ordered your tasks, attention, and expectations around my arrival and could not freely recompose them. The governing norm, on my view, is something like: don't degrade another person's planning environment gratuitously, and when you do, repair the other person's planning capacity rather than merely repairing the relationship.

The category interests me because it predicts that a wrong remains, in diluted form, even without an explicit promise. If a colleague says something will probably be ready in twenty minutes and you wait on that basis, or if an acquaintance habitually means anything up to an hour by shortly, I want to say a wrong has been done even though no determinate promise was broken. Correspondingly, the apt response to lateness would differ from the apt response to a broken promise: it isn't mainly apology for non-performance, but absorbing the schedule-uncertainty one externalized — taking the waiting on oneself next time, giving a deliberately conservative horizon, or doing the re-planning work one's lateness imposed on the other party.

I can't tell whether this is a genuine moral category or just the familiar reliance interest in promissory obligation dressed in the language of schedules. Give me an honest assessment. Is the planning claim coherent and distinct, or does it collapse into ordinary promise-keeping or ordinary duties not to waste another person's time? Where does it capture the wrong of lateness well, and where does it break down — repeated lateness, genuine emergencies, close friends versus strangers, cases where the waiting party could in fact have used the interval productively? And if it is distinct, which of the two alleged consequences — that the wrong arises without an explicit promise, and that the apt repair targets planning capacity — is doing more of the work, and which should I give up first?
