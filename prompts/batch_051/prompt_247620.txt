I'm a PhD student in twentieth-century international history, and I keep returning to the old debate over who started the Cold War. The orthodox, revisionist, and post-revisionist literatures seem to me to be answering slightly different questions under the same heading, and I have been trying to state exactly what that heading obscures. The distinction I have been working with I call "ignition" and "framing." Ignition is the first unmistakable move that, against a fixed background of constraints, pushes a relationship past the point at which organized antagonism becomes the default policy. Framing is the prior construction of that background — the alliance systems, economic arrangements, and spheres of influence inside which the first move came to look rational, necessary, or at least unsurprising. My working claim is that the two can come apart: the power that ignites a confrontation need not be the power that framed the conditions under which igniting looked like the obvious move. In the 1945–47 window one can almost swap the labels by swapping the counterfactual baseline: hold Western economic and military arrangements fixed, and Soviet consolidation of Eastern Europe looks like ignition; hold Soviet security concerns fixed, and the hardening of a Western bloc looks like ignition. The events of 1947–48 make the instability vivid, since the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan can each be narrated as the framing of a Western bloc or as the ignition of a permanent rivalry, just as the consolidation of communist rule in Czechoslovakia and the Berlin blockade can be narrated either way. I suspect this is part of why the orthodox and revisionist accounts have not refuted each other: they are assigning two different predicates to the same question.

I am aware this risks being a relabeling of proximate versus ultimate causes, or of agency versus structure, and if it is — or if it has already been worked out under a name I should know — I would rather be told that than keep treating it as new. What I want is an honest assessment of the construct itself. Is the ignition/framing distinction coherent, or does one term collapse into the other? Does it genuinely illuminate the Cold War origins debate, or does it merely push the disputed question down a level, to deciding who counts as having framed what and relative to which counterfactual baseline? I am also unsure whether asking who started the Cold War is the right question once the distinction is explicit, or whether it should be replaced by two narrower questions. Finally, give me the strongest case or specific episode from roughly 1945–1950 on which the distinction gives a misleading or false result. Please separate what is settled in the specialist literature from what is genuinely contested, and do not present one historiographical school's account as the consensus.
