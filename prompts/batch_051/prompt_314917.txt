Exam question:

"Quine's 'gavagai' example is often glossed as showing that a field linguist cannot tell whether a native word means 'rabbit' or 'undetached rabbit part.' First, reconstruct the reasoning behind that example. Second, explain whether Quine's conclusion is that the evidence merely underdetermines the correct translation, or something stronger than that. In doing so, distinguish the indeterminacy of translation from the underdetermination of scientific theory by evidence, and say which of the two the gavagai case is meant to illustrate."

Student answer:

"Quine's linguist observes a speaker of an unknown language utter 'Gavagai' when a rabbit scurries past. The linguist can tentatively translate the word as 'Rabbit!' or 'There's a rabbit!', but cannot yet tell whether it picks out rabbits, rabbit-stages, or undetached rabbit parts, because all of these would prompt the same assent in the same stimulus situations. Quine argues that the indeterminacy arises because any behavioral evidence the linguist gathers can be made consistent with any of these competing translations by adjusting the collateral hypotheses that go into the translation manual. This is different from the underdetermination of scientific theory by evidence, because in science there is a fact of the matter about which theory is true even when the evidence does not decide between them, whereas for translation Quine denies that there is any fact of the matter that would make one manual the uniquely correct one. Even a completed physics would not settle whether 'gavagai' means 'rabbit' or 'undetached rabbit part.' So Quine's conclusion is that we can never be sure what 'gavagai' really means, and that in practice translation between radically different languages is impossible."
