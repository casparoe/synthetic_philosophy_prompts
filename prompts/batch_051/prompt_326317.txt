I've got a tension I can't get to sit still, and I want to know which of my commitments should give. I'm attracted to a revealed-preference view of freedom: take away the legal and economic barriers facing a group, and what they then choose is better evidence of what they actually want than what they did under constraint. But I also have a firm intuition that I should not infer from the gender-equality paradox that women are by nature less interested in STEM, or that men are by nature less interested in caring professions. That goes from a cross-national correlation to a claim about intrinsic dispositions, and it feels like exactly the sort of conclusion that gets a pass because it's counterintuitive. Yet the paradox keeps pointing that way: in countries scoring higher on gender-equality indices, women are, if anything, less likely to get STEM degrees and some occupational preferences look more sex-differentiated, not less. Scandinavia versus somewhere like Algeria or the UAE is the usual contrast. If freer countries show larger differences, then on the revealed-preference view I'm pushed toward accepting that the differences are natural. So something has to yield: the revealed-preference view, the intuition, or the assumption that those indices track "free choice" in the relevant sense. Give me the strongest version of each move — the debunking one (my intuition is just motivated cognition against biological determinism), the amendment one (equality changes which choices get coded masculine or feminine rather than removing the coding), and the measurement one (the indices don't measure what the theory needs them to) — and say which you'd make and what would justify it. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then reason it through, no preamble.
