I write novels, and much of my working life is spent trying to render the inner lives of characters: the precise shade of a grief, a headache, a half-admitted resentment. This is why Wittgenstein's private language argument has come to matter to me, though I am not a philosopher. As I understand it, the argument shows that no word can be given meaning by a private act of pointing inwardly at a sensation and resolving to call it this; for if the only test of correct use were my own memory of what I had meant, there would be no difference between using the word rightly and merely believing I had used it rightly. The consequence, it seems, is that the novelist's task is not to transcribe something essentially private but to master a public practice: a feeling can be named only in a shared language, by way of its circumstances, expressions, and patterns of use, which explains why descriptions of inner states can fail in publicly discussable ways.

I find this account attractive because it is a genuine theory. It offers a clean criterion for a word's possessing meaning, it explains how the language of inwardness can be taught and criticised, and it dissolves the picture of a private object to which each consciousness alone has access.

And yet, when I attend to my own present grief, or to a particular stab of envy, I cannot make myself believe that what I am referring to is at bottom public. My access to the feeling seems direct and privileged in a way no reader's access could be. I can, it seems, point inwardly and mean that — this exact quality, now — and much of my craft consists in hunting for public words adequate to something already there, privately, before the words arrive. That intuition is firm, and it shapes how I write, even if the theory tells me it is confused.

These two convictions appear to be in tension. Which should yield? I do not want a bare choice. Present the strongest version of each side, then give me a negotiated verdict: should the intuition be debunked and dropped; should the argument be amended so that it rules out only a fantasy of private naming while leaving room for genuine private acquaintance; or are the two compatible on a better reading of one or both? Say what would justify each of those moves, and be explicit about what has to be given up in the move you recommend. Keep your reply to no more than 900 words.
