I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and in reporting on the algorithms now used to inform bail and sentencing decisions I have talked myself into a view I cannot find stated cleanly anywhere. I have been calling it the Same-Score Principle. Here is the claim. A predictive instrument is fair, in the only sense a predictive instrument can be fair, when each score it assigns means the same actuarial fact about every person who receives it. If a risk score of 7 is attached to two defendants of different races, both must face the same empirical probability of reoffending; if they do not, the tool is systematically lying about one of them. If they do, the tool has discharged the whole of fairness. Any remaining inequality in who is detained follows from the underlying facts about the groups, not from the algorithm.

I take this to have an inconvenient corollary. The demand that an algorithm also balance its errors across groups — that false positives strike each group at the same rate, and false negatives likewise — will typically require the score to mean different things in different groups. In the cases I have covered, the two demands pull apart most sharply when base rates differ. My principle says that is not a tragic trade-off but a clue: the error-balancing demand is asking the tool to misreport what it knows.

I recognize that this puts me against a large part of the field. I want an honest assessment. Have I merely rediscovered a familiar position under a worse name? Do the two parts of the view really entail one another, or does the corollary borrow more than the principle can lend it? And what is the strongest counterexample you can construct — a system in which every score means exactly the same thing in every group and yet the system is still unfair?
