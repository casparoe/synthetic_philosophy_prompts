I spend an unhealthy amount of time on rationalist blogs, so its possible this parallel has already been made a hundred times and I just missed it. Here's the thing though.

The core move in ergodicity economics (Ole Peters and that crowd) is that ensemble averages and time averages only coincide under ergodicity. A gamble can have positive expected value across a big ensemble of parallel copies of you, each taking it once, but be ruinous for a single copy who takes it repeatedly over time, because wealth multiplies and the ensemble mean gets dragged upward by a few absurdly lucky paths. The practical upshot is that for multiplicative dynamics you should maximize the time-average growth rate, not the ensemble expectation.

I swear this is the same shape as a fight I keep having in population ethics and can never articulate properly. Some people evaluate policy ex ante: put yourself behind a veil of ignorance, sum expected utilities over possible lives, pick the highest average. Other people evaluate ex post: look at the actual distribution of lives the policy produces and judge that distribution itself. Usually the ex ante person says the ex post person is being incoherent, since behind the veil they would have picked the very policy they now complain about. But the ergodicity guy has a comeback available: if a life is more like a single multiplicative trajectory than a bunch of independent draws, then an ex ante ensemble average across persons can be high while nearly every actual life goes badly. "Expected utility averaged over persons" looks like the ensemble mean; "what a single life is actually like over time" looks like the time average. And just as a few lucky paths dominate the ensemble mean, a high social average can hide deep misery in the tails.

So: am I pattern-matching, or is there a real isomorphism here?
