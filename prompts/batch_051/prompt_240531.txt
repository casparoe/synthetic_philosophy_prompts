A foundation I advise is deciding how to direct a three-year, $2.5 million research programme on the tragedy of the commons and the governance of public goods. The programme can support formal modeling, comparative field studies of commons institutions, or conceptual work, but not all three at serious depth, so the initial planning question is which unresolved problem should serve as the organizing spine of the whole programme rather than which methods happen to be available. We have a working list of candidate questions: whether the design principles that appear in small, long-lived commons—clear boundaries, local monitoring, graduated sanctions, low-cost conflict resolution, nested governance—generalize to large, anonymous, non-local resources such as climate sinks, groundwater, or high-seas fisheries; whether successful sanctioning in commons regimes works chiefly through material incentives or through norm internalization and reputation; whether the standard classification of goods by rivalry and excludability actually predicts which institutional failures a resource will suffer, or whether that taxonomy should be replaced by something finer-grained; and how much of the explanatory weight in successful polycentric governance is carried by polycentricity itself as opposed to the simpler principle that governance scale should match resource scale.

For each candidate—and for any problem you think belongs on the list instead—I want a ranking that is sensitive to dependencies: which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable within three years, and which would change downstream institutional design the most if answered one way rather than another. Defend the ranking with explicit criteria such as importance, tractability, neglectedness, and upstreamness; say where the criteria conflict and how you resolve those conflicts; and specify what empirical or conceptual results would most reorder your list. The deliverable is a defended research agenda, not a survey and not a reading list.
