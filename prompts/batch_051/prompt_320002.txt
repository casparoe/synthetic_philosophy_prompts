David Lewis's story about convention is that a regularity counts as a convention when a population keeps doing it because each person expects everyone else to keep doing it, it answers some recurring coordination problem, and it's at least partly arbitrary — another regularity could have worked in its place. People go along, not because it's good in any deep way, but because everyone expects everyone to go along. Now point that machinery at the decline of dueling in the early United States, and make the case go in both directions: the theory should say what the participants faced, and the episode should say which parts of the theory actually hold up.

The facts, in case you don't have them: Alexander Hamilton and Aaron Burr dueled at Weehawken, New Jersey in July 1804; Hamilton was mortally wounded and died the next day. Burr was still vice president at the time and wasn't convicted, but his political career basically imploded. Dueling among gentlemen had been widespread in both North and South, governed loosely by an imported code duello. After 1804 it declined fast in the North but hung on in the South for decades, well into the antebellum period. Throughout, refusing a challenge carried a brutal social penalty: being denounced as a coward, loss of standing, sometimes being "posted" in public.

Here's what I want worked through carefully. First, reconstruct dueling as a convention in Lewis's sense, if it even fits: who's the population, what's the recurring coordination problem, what's the regularity, and what's the alternative regularity that could have been there instead? Flag every point where the fit is strained—especially whether the "coordination problem" is really a shared one, since the parties' interests in a duel look partly opposed, and whether third parties who punish nonconformity (rather than just failing to coordinate) break the Lewis condition that preference depends only on expectation. Second, analyze the unraveling: does the Burr-Hamilton duel read as a change in expectations, a change in preferences, or a jolt to the whole equilibrium that Lewis's framework can't represent? Did the duel actually do the causal work, or was it just the loudest symptom of a shift already underway? Third, say what the North-South lag does to the theory: if the same regularity persisted in one subpopulation and collapsed in another, does Lewis's account predict that kind of patchy equilibrium, or does it just fail to forbid it? Finally, tell me whether the episode leaves Lewis's convention story looking vindicated, stretched, or irrelevant to a norm that was never really a coordination equilibrium to begin with.
