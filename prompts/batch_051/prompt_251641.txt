You are a professional philosopher of science with expertise in confirmation theory and in the role of aesthetic values in scientific reasoning. I teach philosophy of physics, and I have been collecting my own verdicts about when elegance in mathematics and physics is genuine evidence for a theory's truth and when it is merely an aesthetic preference. I want to know whether a single defensible principle underlies my verdicts, or whether some of them must be revised. Please begin by brainstorming several candidate principles and stress-testing each against every case before settling on your final answer. Answer in American English.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. Dirac's relativistic electron equation produced negative-energy solutions that looked like a defect. Dirac trusted the formalism's constrained mathematical structure enough to insist that those solutions had physical content, and the positron was discovered a few years later. Verdict: the equation's elegance was genuine evidence for the positron before it was observed.

2. Maxwell introduced the displacement current to restore consistency among the equations of electricity and magnetism; the amended theory implied electromagnetic waves, which Hertz later produced. Verdict: the elegance of the completed theory counted as evidence before the waves were produced.

3. Copernicus's heliocentric system was more systematically elegant than Ptolemy's, but at the time it did not fit the observations better. Verdict: its elegance counted at least somewhat in its favor even then.

4. Kepler's early model nested the planetary orbits in the five Platonic solids. It was strikingly beautiful and fit the known planetary distances roughly, but it was later refuted. Verdict: its beauty did no evidential work; the rough fit did all the work.

5. Before parity violation was discovered, the conviction that parity would be conserved in weak interactions was in part a conviction that nature would not be so inelegant. Parity was violated. Verdict: that aesthetic appeal was not evidence.

6. String theory is widely regarded as mathematically beautiful and internally coherent, but it has no confirmed predictions. Verdict: its elegance is not evidence for its truth; it is at most a research heuristic.

7. The Standard Model has many free parameters and is often called ugly or arbitrary. Verdict: this ugliness does not by itself count against the model's truth, given its predictive success.

My working hunch is that elegance is evidence only when the elegant structure is forced on us by constraints we already accept for independent reasons, and that optional elegance and ugliness are both epistemically inert. But I cannot make 'forced' precise, and I suspect this hunch misclassifies at least one of my verdicts above.

Please (a) state the most defensible precise version of my hunch, or replace it with a better principle; (b) identify which clause is motivated by which of my cases; (c) say what the principle implies about two further cases I have not settled — the Higgs boson before its 2012 discovery, and the ancient preference for circular planetary orbits over ellipses; and (d) tell me honestly which of my seven verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should revise those verdicts or complicate the principle. I would rather revise one or two verdicts than accept a principle that merely lists the cases.
