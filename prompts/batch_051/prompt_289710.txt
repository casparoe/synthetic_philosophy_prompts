Over the past few months I've been going back and forth with a friend who has strong rule-of-law views, and we keep landing on the void-for-vagueness doctrine as the crux. So I started keeping a list of made-up criminal statutes and my gut reaction to each one, and now I'm stuck because my own verdicts don't cohere. A city makes it a crime to make noise after ten p.m. that a normal officer would call loud and unreasonable: I'd strike that. The same city replaces it with a hard rule of 65 decibels at the property line after ten: I'd uphold it, even though the line between 64 and 65 is every bit as arbitrary as the word loud, maybe more so. A stalking law criminalizes repeatedly following or contacting someone in a way that would make a reasonable person fear for their safety: I'd uphold that. A loitering law prohibits hanging around in public in a way that supposedly shows an intent to commit a drug offense: I'd strike it, because cops can pin manner and intent on just about anyone they don't like the look of. A fraud law simply makes it a crime to run a scheme to defraud, with no definition of defraud in the text: I'd uphold it, on the grounds that a long line of cases has filled the word in.

Now the problem. I reach for fair notice to explain the verdicts, but stalking and fraud don't give ordinary people advance notice in any plain sense. I reach for no-arbitrary-enforcement, and that handles the noise and loitering cases, but the stalking law hands police and juries alot of discretion and I don't mind it there. Am I just pattern-matching to vibes, or is there one principle that captures all five verdicts, and if not, which verdict should I give up? And whatever principle survives, what does it say about a sixth case: a computer-crime law that makes it illegal to access a computer without authorization. I'm genuinely torn on that one, because scripted scraping feels unauthorized but not quite criminal to me, while authorization also seems to have a real core.
