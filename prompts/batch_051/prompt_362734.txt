As a novelist, I spend a great deal of time trying to tell the difference between two kinds of character: the one who values something for its own sake and the one who treats it as a tool while sincerely denying that he does. I want to know whether that difference can be detected from outside, or whether it is partly a matter of interpretation and self-report. You are an expert in value theory and moral psychology. Consider a concrete case: an official in a tradition-bound community who performs costly acts of ritual purity and insists — apparently without ulterior motive — that he values purity for its own sake, not for the standing, safety, or divine favor it brings. A writer I know claims the matter is testable: eliminate the instrumental payoffs one by one and see whether the costly behavior persists. Is he right? Begin by brainstorming the several things the expression "for its own sake" might mean in such a case, then design the best available observation or experiment to distinguish terminal from merely instrumental valuation of purity (or, if it makes the design cleaner, of truth-seeking or tradition). State which outcomes would support which interpretation, and then say explicitly what even the cleanest outcome would fail to settle — for example, whether it would show that the agent really holds the value terminally, or only that he is robustly disposed, or only that his self-description is coherent. Conclude with your view of what the terminal-instrumental distinction must amount to if any test is to be genuinely diagnostic.
