I'm a software engineer with basically no philosophy background, so I'll phrase this in my own terms. I went down a rabbit hole on the St. Petersburg paradox and came out with a position I'd like you to preassure-test. My position is: the paradox doesn't show expected-value reasoning is broken; it shows that a rational utility function has to be bounded above. Once utility is bounded, a game that offers infinite money-EV just has finite expected utility, and there's no puzzle. The reason I care isn't casino games, it's long-tail risk in software: security fixes, rare-but-catastrophic outages, features that could in principle cause unbounded harm. Under an unbounded utility function the EV of yet another safety check becomes infinite, which strikes me as a reductio.

What I want is a map of what would have to be true for the bounded-utility resolution to be wrong, not a literature review. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about where a bounded-utility defender should themselves concede ground. In particular I want to know:

- Which observations, choice behavior, or experimental findings would count against bounded utility, and which ones are decisive versus just awkward?
- Which formal results should move me most — for example, games with faster-growing payoffs that break any given bound, or variants where the expected value isn't defined because the series doesn't converge?
- What does bounded utility predict about real decisions that its rivals (probability weighting, rejecting unbounded payoffs as impossible, saying no casino could actually offer the game) don't predict, and where might those predictions fail?
- What's the most damaging concession a defender has to make? For example I already worry that "there exists some upper bound" is nearly empty — it doesn't say which bound, and it can be nudged upward every time a larger payoff appears.
- Has the view been insulated into unfalsifiability by that move? If so, is that a genuine problem for a consistency condition of this kind, or just the price of keeping utility theory coherent?

Give me the honest assessment: am I holding a substantive position, or mostly a truism with a long-tail-risk sticker on it?
