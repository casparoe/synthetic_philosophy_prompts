Working on a piece about early modern attempts to naturalize moral questions, I keep getting stuck on a passage near the beginning of Hume’s *Of Suicide*, where he is arguing that suicide cannot be a trespass against our duty to God. The passage, as I am reading it, runs:

“The providence of the deity appears not immediately in any operation, but governs every thing by those general and immutable laws, which have been established from the beginning of time. All events, in one sense, may be pronounced the action of the almighty: They all proceed from those powers, with which he has endowed his creatures. A house, which falls by its own weight, is not brought to ruin by his providence more than one destroyed by the hands of men; nor are the human faculties less his workmanship than the laws of motion and gravitation.”

I understand the general drift—that human actions are no less part of the divinely ordered system than inanimate events—but I cannot see exactly how the comparison licenses the conclusion he wants. Could you walk me through the argument step by step, keeping separate the claim about general laws from the claim about divine authorization? In particular, what suppressed premise about the difference between acting through general laws and acting by a particular volition is doing the main work? I would also like to know where a theologically sophisticated opponent could most plausibly press back, even if one grants Hume the picture of a deity who governs only through general laws.
