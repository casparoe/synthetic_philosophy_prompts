I keep coming back to a debugging principle: states that cannot produce any observable difference anywhere are not real states. I want to know whether applying that principle to qualia gives the following argument.

(1) If two hypotheses about a mental state could not differ in any observation even in principle, then the alleged difference between them is empty.
(2) By the standard conception, a quale is a private, intrinsic, directly introspectable feature of experience; the subject's first-person report is therefore the only channel through which information about a quale could enter any observation.
(3) Consider two coffee drinkers. Alex says that the coffee tastes exactly as it always has but that he no longer likes the taste. Blair says that she still likes the taste exactly as much but that the coffee no longer tastes the same. Each is sincere, each has drunk the same coffee over the same period, and neither can find any further introspective datum—a memory of the old taste, a felt effort, anything—that would prove her own reading is the correct one.
(4) What introspection gives each drinker is only that a change occurred somewhere in the tasting-and-liking complex. It cannot place the change on one side of the line between the taste-quale itself and the drinker's liking or evaluation of that quale.
(5) Because of (2), third-person measurement cannot place the change either; the private quale, if it exists, is screened off from every measurement except the subject's report.
(6) Therefore no possible observation distinguishes "the taste-quale changed" from "the liking or evaluation changed while the taste-quale stayed fixed."
(7) By (1), that distinction is empty.
(8) If the difference between a change in a quale and a change in one's response to an unchanged quale is empty, then the notion "qualia" has no content in its own canonical case.
(9) A notion with no content in its own canonical case is incoherent.
(10) Therefore qualia, as standardly conceived, are incoherent.
