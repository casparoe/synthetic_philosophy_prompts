Imagine a country that, starting next year, removes one hour per week from the mathematics curriculum for all students aged six to eighteen and replaces it with a compulsory class in "decision practice." Students in the class drill standardized decision katas — selecting a health plan, deciding whether to evacuate before a storm, budgeting a small windfall, evaluating a warranty — while instructors correct only procedural form, as a martial arts instructor corrects stance. They never explain the underlying theory: probability, expected value, and named cognitive biases are not mentioned until age eighteen, if they are mentioned at all. Compare this country over the following thirty years with a matched country that spends the same hours on explicit instruction in probability, decision theory, and cognitive biases but runs no drills. Predict the differences you would expect among ordinary adults in personal finance, medical decisions, and public argument. In your answer, make the mechanisms explicit; for each major prediction, state how confident you are and under what conditions — concerning habit formation, motivation, or the transfer of trained procedures to unfamiliar problems — it would fail.
