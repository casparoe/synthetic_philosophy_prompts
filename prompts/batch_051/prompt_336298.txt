I'm an undergraduate in a formal epistemology and decision theory seminar, and I have been asked to prepare an interactive exercise for next week's discussion section. The past two weeks of the course have centered on Good's theorem on the value of information: for an expected-utility maximizer with a fixed utility function, costless information can never have negative expected value, because the agent can always set the information aside when choosing an act, while any signal creates the possibility of conditioning the act more finely. Several classmates find the theorem implausible, since people pay to avoid learning things—think of unopened genetic-test results, sealed medical charts, messages one agrees not to read, or negotiators who refuse to learn the other side's deadline. The instructor's view is that the interesting work lies in deciding which of these cases are genuine counterexamples and which are just disguised costs, and she suggested that a sorting quiz built around concrete cases would force the class to commit through judgments rather than through slogans. I would like you to write that quiz.

Act as an expert in formal epistemology and decision theory, and spend some time brainstorming a large set of candidate cases before selecting the final ones. The quiz should have eight to ten questions. Each question should present a short, concrete scenario and then offer two or three response options describing what the respondent would choose or judge; no question should ask respondents to identify with a labeled position, and none should require knowledge of the names in the scoring key. The instrument should be diagnostic rather than evaluative: it should sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label sounds best to them. At least the following fixed cases must appear, though you may adapt their wording: (1) a genetic test that can reveal, with high accuracy, whether the respondent carries a gene causing a late-onset disease that has no treatment; (2) a manager who learns that a sealed report contains information about employees' union organizing and that opening it will trigger legal duties she would rather not bear; (3) a machine that will print the exact date and cause of one's death, with no way to change it; (4) a negotiator who deliberately does not read a message containing the other side's financing deadline; and (5) a professor who avoids checking whether a student's confession of plagiarism was itself copied from a website, because university policy would then oblige her to report the student. Any cases you add should isolate a single moving part—for example, whether the information can affect action, whether it affects only one's emotional state, whether the state of knowing itself would impose obligations, whether one can credibly deny knowing, or whether others can observe what one knows—rather than merely illustrating a label.

The scoring key should map patterns of answers onto at least these positions: (a) strict Bayesian orthodoxy, on which information is never bad for a rational agent and all apparent information-aversion reduces to costs of attention, computation, decision-making, risk, or anticipated actions; (b) belief-state consequentialism, on which beliefs and the timing of uncertainty resolution matter directly—dread, hope, anxiety, savoring—so information can harm a person even when it cannot alter anything the person does; (c) commitment-and-temptation views, on which ignorance can be rational because it binds a foreseeable future weakness or an obligation one expects to act badly under; and (d) strategic ignorance, on which what one knows matters in a social game because it changes what one can deny, what one reveals, and what others may demand. Where some combination in this logical space is plausibly unoccupied, report that and briefly diagnose whether the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or possible but thin. Finally, flag any positions your instrument cannot cleanly separate from one another—for instance, a very generous reading of "costs" in the Bayesian position versus belief-state consequentialism—rather than pretending the quiz has sharper edges than it does.
