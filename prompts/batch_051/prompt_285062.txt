Construct a thought experiment for the rationalist theory of war, transposed to an AI race. The framework is Fearon-style: rational actors can fail to reach a bargain that all of them would prefer to a costly fight, and the failure must trace to one of three mechanisms — private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, or issue indivisibilities.

Build the case to the following specifications.

- Two actors (labs, states, or a lab and a state) end up in a costly race to develop and deploy a specific AI capability. Both would strictly prefer some feasible bargain — a verifiable cap, a phased rollout, or a sharing arrangement with specified terms — to the race that actually occurs. Make the race costly enough that this preference is real, not cosmetic.
- The binding mechanism must be a commitment problem of the shifting-power kind, not the first-mover or offensive-advantage kind. The failure must arise because the balance of power is expected to shift against one party over time: the side that expects to decline knows the rising side cannot credibly promise to honor the bargain once its lead has compounded, so the declining side defects — races, refuses to sign, or walks away — now, while it still has leverage.
- Complete information holds: each side knows the other's capabilities, timelines, and preferences, and knows the other knows. Private information about resolve, progress, or type must play no explanatory role.
- No indivisibility: the subject of the bargain is in principle divisible or compensable. Nothing about the capability rules out side-payments, phased deployment, profit-sharing, or another division.
- Mundane stakes and near-term technology: no existential risk, no superintelligence, no science-fictional capabilities. The compounding advantage should come from an ordinary mechanism, such as data-network effects, capital, talent concentration, or infrastructure lead times.

Write the scenario itself in one paragraph. Then do the check.

- State the bargain both parties prefer, and why each would sign it if signing were actually binding on the other party.
- Explain why the bargain is not reached.
- Show, explicitly, why private information fails to explain the outcome, why indivisibility fails, and why the shifting-power commitment problem succeeds.
- Finish with an honest note on which feature of the case does the work, what the case would look like if that feature were removed, and how a defender of a private-information explanation would attack the case.

The case is meant to isolate the shifting-power commitment mechanism from the other two; a scenario that stipulates the verdict rather than generating it from the actors' incentives fails the brief.
