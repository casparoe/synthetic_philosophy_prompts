Since retiring from experimental chemistry, I have been reading the philosophy of causation, and one thesis both intrigues and irritates me. A philosopher I know maintains that there is no genuine causation by omission. On his view, when we say that a plant died because no one watered it, or that a patient worsened because a nurse failed to change a dressing, we are not reporting an objective causal relation; we are applying a norm, marking a departure from an expected or proper course of events. My scientific training disposes me to ask what observation could discipline this claim. The obvious place to look is comparative data: do ordinary causal judgments about matched act–omission vignettes shift when the background norms shift, or do they stay fixed? Please develop the strongest version of that test, or replace it with a better one. Say precisely what pattern of results would favour the claim that omissions are genuine causes and what pattern would favour the norm-relative reading, and then be honest about what the test would not settle. Where the two readings remain inseparable, is that a limitation of the method or a sign that the dispute is not fully factual?

Structure your answer in five titled sections, in this order and roughly at these lengths: (1) The Claim, Sharpened — restate the thesis so that its empirical commitments are explicit, about 75 words; (2) Test Design — give the concrete design, including data source, stimuli, conditions, dependent measure, and the predicted outcomes, about 300 words; (3) Reading the Outcomes — explain what each possible pattern would establish and how strongly, about 200 words; (4) The Residue — identify what the test cannot decide and why, about 150 words; (5) Verdict on the Disagreement — assess whether the remaining gap is a limitation of method or a defect in the thesis itself, about 100 words.
