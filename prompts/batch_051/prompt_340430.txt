Since I retired I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group, and last week two of the regulars got into it over Van Fraassen's Reflection principle. I went back and forth the whole time and now I cant tell whose right at each step. Roughly, from memory:

Lena said the principle says if I know now that tomorrow I'll have degree of belief .9 in H, then my degree of belief in H now should be .9 too; otherwise a bookie can run a Dutch strategy on me, selling me H at today's price and buying it back tomorrow at the price I've already told him I'll use, and profit either way.

Marcus said that's obviously false as stated: he's scheduled for a surgery next month, and he knows the anaesthesia will make him briefly overconfident; he'll wake up .9 confident the biotech startup he left will get bought, for no new evidence. He shouldn't adopt that belief now just because a future version of him on drugs will hold it.

Lena said that's not a counterexample, because Reflection only ranges over future selves who are at least as rational and at least as well-informed as you now. Your zonked future self isn't the kind of future self the principle tells you to defer to, any more than you'd defer to a drunk.

Marcus said once you add that restriction, Reflection is either redundant or empty. If future-you is rational and conditions on all your current evidence plus whatever comes in, then your current credence in H is already the expected value of your future credence, and conditional on that future credence being .9 your current credence is .9 — that's just the probability calculus. Reflection adds nothing. If future-you isn't like that, the restriction stops the principle from applying. So it's either a theorem or a stipulation with no application.

Lena said it isn't empty: you usually don't know in advance which future self you'll turn out to be, and Reflection forces you to keep your probabilities over your own possible future evidential states coherent before you find out. It's a cross-time coherence constraint, not a prediction.

Marcus said then it just inherits the hard question of which future selves are experts, and tells you to defer to the ones you've already decided are. It's a bookmark, not a principle.

Can you go through the exchange and say who's right on each specific point — the Dutch strategy, the anaesthesia case as a counterexample, the rationality restriction, the redundancy charge, the coherence-reply, and the bookmark jab — and tell me what, if anything, they're both missing? Don't just pick a winner; I want the scorecard for each step.
