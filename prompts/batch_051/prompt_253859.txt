I've been chewing on this idea for a while and I want somebody to actually stress-test it instead of just telling me it's interesting. I call it the 'prepaid toll' theory of sincerity. The claim is: a signal counts as reliable only if the sender pays a cost that (1) is already sunk before the receiver has to decide whether to believe it, and (2) doesn't do double duty as a bribe or a repair — that is, the cost itself can't buy the good it's supposedly signaling. So a public apology is convincing only if the groveling isn't what actually wins the forgiveness; a scientist's claim is convincing only if the costly protocol (blinding, pre-registering, throwing out the first batch of data) doesn't itself produce the result. I know this sounds like the handicap principle, and my friend keeps saying its just Zahavi restated. But I think it isn't: the handicap principle, as I understand it, says honest signals are *differentially* costly — they're more expensive for fakers than for genuine senders. My principle says the cost also has to be *non-instrumental and front-loaded*, so a rich person's donation or a smooth apology where the cost is performative self-flagellation still doesn't count, even if it is costly. The problem I keep hitting is the millionaire: if costs are relative to wealth, then tolls can just scale up until they're theatrical, and at that point it's not clear the principle predicts anyone should ever be believed. So I'd like the strongest version of my idea, the nearest existing treatments, and the three or four sharpest objections — with a verdict on whether the whole thing collapses into costly signaling plus commitment devices, or whether there's actually a sliver of a new distinction in here.
