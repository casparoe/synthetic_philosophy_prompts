I'm a retired ecologist—spent most of my career on population dynamics and source-sink stuff—and lately I've been reading about the deep human past, especially the shift from foraging to farming. I'm new enough to the philosophy and history side of this that I don't quite trust my own vocabulary. There's a pattern there I keep noticing and I don't have a good word for it. By most measures I can see, the earliest farmers were worse off than the foragers they replaced: longer workdays, narrower and more brittle diets, more infectious disease, shorter average stature, more hierarchy, more bad-year starvation. And yet the farming package spread basically everywhere, and once it arrived, foraging disappeared from the regions it took over. From an ecology standpoint it looks like an ecological trap—an organism favoring a habitat that actually lowers its fitness—except it isn't obviously lowering fitness in the bilogical sense. Farming populations grew, often fast, and outcompeted or absorbed their neighbors. So you get reproductive success and individual welfare apparently pulling in opposite directions, and the welfare-losing strategy wins by outbreeding the alternative.

I see the same shape in smaller cases: a brittle high-yield crop or livestock breed pushes out a robust low-yield one because it produces more per acre or per season, even though it needs more labor and fails more often; a dense, unhealthy settlement form outcompetes a dispersed, healthier one because it can field more people. The general structure seems to be: a strategy spreads because it supports more population growth, while making the average individual's life worse, and then it can't be abandoned because the alternatives have been crowded out or degraded.

I know there's a whole debate about whether the adoption of agriculture was, on net, a mistake. What I'm after is narrower. Is there an established name for this specific configuration—a welfare-lowering but reproductively successful strategy that outcompetes its alternatives precisely by growing faster, and then locks in? I know the terms Malthusian trap and ecological trap, but neither of them seems to name exactly this, since one is about subsistence being held down by population growth and the other is about a mismatch between preference and fitness. If there's no settled name, what would you call it, and how would you carve its boundaries against a Malthusian trap, an ecological trap, and a plain trade-off? I'm not just looking for a label—I want to know what the thing is and where its edges are.
