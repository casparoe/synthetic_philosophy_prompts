I help run an AI-policy discussion group, and we have been stuck on a question that splits people along lines nobody can articulate cleanly. Our running example is deliberately stylized. Two leading labs, Amara and Beacon, both say — apparently sincerely — that the field would be better off with a slower, safer trajectory. Each lab's internal strategy memos nonetheless conclude that it must race: cutting corners on interpretability, scaling at maximum speed, and treating any unilateral slowdown as fatal. The result is expensive, both sides call it collectively worse than the cooperative alternative, and neither will be the first to brake. We have been arguing about what is actually driving the race. Some say it is information: neither lab knows the other's true capabilities, safety thresholds, or resolve, and bluffing runs rampant. Others say it is commitment: each could credibly prefer the cooperative slowing-down outcome, but neither can bind itself or the other to a verifiable capability cap, especially when a lead would be decisive. Others say it is indivisibility: the prize is winner-take-all in a way that cannot be shared or settled. And a few people reject the whole "costly race" framing, saying the sprint is privately rational or is driven by status, prestige, and organizational momentum rather than strategic calculation.

For the next session, I would like an actual classification instrument: a decision tree that starts from one concrete, answerable diagnostic question and routes a person to the explanation they are implicitly committed to, without ever asking them to name or endorse a label. The tree should be built from the logical space first — roughly, the Fearon-style rationalist distinctions among information asymmetries, commitment problems, and issue indivisibility, together with an external branch for "there is no costly-race puzzle here" — and only then should the standard positions be mapped onto that space. The leaves must include at least: a pure information-asymmetry leaf, a pure commitment-problem leaf, a pure indivisibility leaf (with a note if you judge it unstable on its own), a combined commitment-plus-indivisibility leaf, a leaf denying that the race is collectively costly because private expected returns justify it, and an out-of-framework leaf covering prestige or organizational inertia. For any branch or combination of answers that has no occupant, add one sentence on whether the region is empty because the position is incoherent, merely unattractive, or plausibly just unexplored rather than refuted. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any concrete diagnostic question you can devise, flag that pair and say why the indistinguishability arises.

Write each node as a question a smart non-specialist could answer without knowing the taxonomy in advance — for example: "If a neutral auditor could verify in real time that neither lab had exceeded capability level C, and any violation triggered an automatic, irreversible shutdown of the offending training run, would the pressure to sprint disappear?" or "If the first-mover prize could be converted into tradable shares allocated in proportion to safety-adjusted effort, would both labs prefer to slow down?" Avoid formal notation, explain any technical distinction in plain language, and aim for six to eight leaves. Since this is a discussion-group handout, do not include a bibliography or recommendations for further reading.
