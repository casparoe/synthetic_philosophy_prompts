I keep noticing a thing with names and I can't find a word for it. It goes in two steps. A person has a name whose parts have different jobs: one part is for the individual, another part carries the family or lineage, and that second part is the one that survives across contexts — it's what people get indexed under, what their kids share, what strangers use as the handle. Now suppose that family-carrying part comes first in the person's own naming system (like a Chinese name where the single-syllable family element is up front), or suppose the last slot isn't a family name at all but a father's first name plus "son of" (like an Icelandic name where Einarsson just means Einar's son). When the name gets moved into an English form, or a database, the parts get reread under the new system's assumptions about what first and last name mean. The family-carrier gets demoted to middle-name-ish or dropped, and the wrong part gets promoted: the father's name becomes a surname, or the individual part gets treated as the family name. Then it locks in, because once indexes and bylines have the promoted part, everyone in the new language refers to the person by a string that wasn't doing that job originally. Is there an established term for this two-step process — the rereading of name-parts under foreign assumptions, and then the promoted part becoming the new public reference? Or are those two steps seperate and I should be after two different names? I'm not so much after a label as the carve: where does ordinary misspelling or mispronounciation end and this start, is it best thought of as a translation problem or a reference problem, and what are the edge cases — names with one part only, or cultures where the family-part isn't stable across generations anyway?
