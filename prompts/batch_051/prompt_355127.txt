Was talking to a friend about what 'joining' a customs union actually locks you into, and we came up with a mechanism i can't stop turning over. Suppose a customs union writes a 'sliding membership' clause into its founding treaty. Any member can unilaterally pull itself out of any single common policy area — the common external tariff, free movement of goods, regulatory harmonization, the shared dispute body — for a rolling two-year period. While opted out of an area, it looses its vote in that area's council and pays a surcharge into the union budget, calculated as a percentage of its trade with the members still inside that area. It can re-enter any time, but only after bringing its rules into line with whatever the area changed while it was gone.

Don't tell me whether this is good in the abstract; analyze how it actually plays out. Which policy areas get opted out of first, and does that pattern differ between rich and poor members? Does the surcharge deter exits or turn them into a premium service for big economies? How do the remaining members respond — do they design rules to make opt-outs costly, or to tempt states out? Does the clause stabilize membership by making disagreement cheap, or does it hollow the union into a hub-and-spoke before anyone notices? Where is it gamed first, and can those failures be fixed without membership going back to all-or-nothing? Hard cap: 600 words.
