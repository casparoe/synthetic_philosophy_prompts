I am trying to work out what I actually think about whether something can count as knowledge when it cannot be put into words. Here are my verdicts on some cases.

When I read a function and can reliably tell that it has a subtle concurrency bug, often before I can say exactly what is wrong, I would call that knowledge: I can act on it, and I am usually right. A wine taster who can reliably sort wines by vineyard and vintage but cannot articulate the rule she is using likewise seems to me to have genuine knowledge. A native English speaker who instantly knows that "the green big dragon" is wrong and "the big green dragon" is right, without being able to state the rule of adjective order, knows something about English word order even though she cannot say what it is. A strong chess player who looks at a position and just sees that White is winning, and whose snap judgments are reliably correct, also seems to me to know something, though she cannot explain why.

So far I am comfortable. But two cases give me trouble. A meditator claims to know, from years of practice, that the self has no stable, separate core, yet says the insight cannot be described or transmitted, only pointed at. I find myself resisting the word "knowledge" here. This case bothers me in particular because her judgment is the product of a repeatable practice, and many practitioners independently report converging on the same thing, which is the closest analogue to reliability I have — and yet I still withhold the label. A mystic claims to know that the ground of reality is an undivided unity that language necessarily splits into subject and object, so that it can only be gestured at. If I am honest, I cannot bring myself to call that knowledge either.

My working hunch is that I grant the first four cases because each involves a reliable, public performance that someone else could in principle check, whereas the last two make claims I can see no way to test. But I am not sure this hunch actually matches my verdicts, and the meditator case seems to expose its limits.

Is there one principle that captures all of these judgments? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and does the resulting principle leave me with anything I would not want to accept? Finally, what does the principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on: a mathematician who is strongly convinced that a conjecture is true and is reliably right about neighboring results, but cannot yet prove this one — does she know it before the proof, or only after?
