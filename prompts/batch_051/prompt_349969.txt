I am working through a draft by a colleague in my reading group, and one of its arguments has left me unable to find the failing step. She is arguing that, once we accept the standard pragmatic account of how presuppositions are updated in conversation, the distinction between what a sentence asserts and what it presupposes collapses into a difference of mere staging. I have reconstructed her argument as follows.

Think of conversation as having a scoreboard whose entries are the propositions the participants mutually treat as accepted for the purposes of the conversation. Call this the common ground.

(1) When a speaker asserts a sentence, she proposes to add the asserted content to the common ground; that content is at issue until the hearer accepts or rejects it.
(2) Many sentences also carry presuppositions: requirements that certain propositions already be in the common ground if the utterance is to be appropriate. For instance, "Dana's partner is a great cook" presupposes that Dana has a partner.
(3) When a required presupposition is missing, hearers routinely accommodate: provided the missing proposition is consistent and not controversial, they quietly add it to the common ground so that the utterance may be accepted.
(4) It follows that when a speaker utters a sentence whose presupposition p is not yet in the common ground, the normal result is that p gets into the common ground on the strength of the utterance — just as the asserted content gets in if the hearer accepts the assertion.
(5) The difference between the two cases is therefore not in whether the speaker's utterance adds an entry to the common ground, but only in how it adds it: asserted content is proposed as at issue and challengeable, while presupposed content is slipped in as already settled.
(6) But that difference concerns the manner of the addition, not what gets added: in either case, a proposition the hearer had not previously accepted ends up in the common ground because of the speaker's say-so.
(7) So, once accommodation is admitted, there is no theoretically significant difference between what is asserted and what is presupposed; presupposition is only assertion performed with different stage directions.
(8) Yet that conclusion cannot be right. We respond to a false presupposition differently from how we respond to a false assertion. If I say "Dana's partner is a great cook," and you reply "Wait — I didn't know Dana had a partner," you are not denying my asserted content; you are refusing to let me treat the presupposition as given, and you hold me responsible in a different way than if you had replied "That's false — they're an awful cook."

Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep the contrast described in (8), but if the argument forces a redescription of that contrast, I want to know what the redescription would have to look like.
