My four-year-old asked me last week why we say ducks lay eggs when the duck in our park is a boy duck, and I realised I had no good answer, so I went down a generics rabbit hole and came out more confused. Mosquitoes carry malaria is fine even though almost no mosquito I've ever met carries malaria; books are paperbacks is false even though an enormous number of books are paperbacks; peacocks have huge showy tails works although only the males have them; boys don't cry seems to be doing something weird and prescriptive rather than statistical. I'm not after a survey or a reading list. Give me the spec first: write the desiderata for an adequate theory of generic statements, the requirements it has to meet, the cases it has to get right and why each is a requirement, the distinctions it can't afford to collapse, any results from linguistics or pyscholinguistics it can't contradict, and which requirements are negotiable and which aren't. The purpose is a theory good enough to explain what ordinary speakers are committing themselves to when they trot out one of these sentences and to tell when someone is misusing one in an argument. Then say which single requirement knocks out the most obvious first-pass theories, and flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied. Continuous prose only please, no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, like an email.
