I'm preparing a talk for a monthly economic-history workshop. My thesis is that the Protestant ethic thesis is best read not as the claim that religious ideas directly caused the rise of capitalism, but as a claim about the causal force of a particular ethical outlook — the revaluation of disciplined work as a calling and of deferred gratification as a virtue — and that this reading is best articulated through Weber's idea of elective affinity, a non-law-like mutual fit or reciprocal selection between an ethic and an economic order, rather than through a covering-law account of causation. I also hold that this version of the thesis still has teeth against the modern claim that culture is merely a proxy for institutions or geography. The audience is mostly quantitative economic historians, plus two development economists who study culture and growth using natural experiments, and one political scientist who works on Weber's ideal-type method. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I should actually expect, in roughly the order I will hear them and marked by how likely and how damaging each is, with who will raise each, each phrased as those researchers would phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest, the concessions I should make up front rather than defend, and the question I am hoping nobody asks. Throughout, say how confident you are in each claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
