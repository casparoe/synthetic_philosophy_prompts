I am a high school senior and the president of my school's student government. Each semester, the district proposes one or two "smart campus" automations, and the council votes on whether to forward a recommendation to the principal, who has final sign-off; so far the proposals have included an app to replace student morning-announcement readers, an algorithm to match peer tutors with tutees, software that gives automated feedback on practice essays, and a front-desk tablet replacing a student-staffed sign-in sheet. These votes determine which student activities survive as meaningful work and which disappear, and the council currently has no shared basis for deciding them. I want a reusable decision procedure, grounded in philosophical thinking about meaningful work and automation, that the council can run in about twenty minutes per proposal with no philosophical background. The procedure should decide when an activity should stay in human hands because the doing of it is a source of meaning—autonomy, the exercise and growth of skill, social recognition, contribution to a shared enterprise—and when the meaning argument is weak enough that the automation should pass. Please include: the questions in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a definite yes or no; where it must hand the decision to a person because the disagreement is about values rather than facts; and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. In building it, preserve or explicitly reject distinctions such as the difference between automating a task and eliminating a role, the difference between meaning that lies in the product and meaning that lies in the doing, and the baseline question of what students or staff would actually do with the freed time. State which distinctions from the underlying theory you are keeping and which you are collapsing, and where the procedure will likely misfire. Then run the procedure on these three proposals: (a) replacing student morning-announcement readers with push notifications; (b) replacing the peer-tutoring intake conversation and teacher judgment with an algorithm that matches tutors to tutees and schedules the sessions; (c) using software to grade practice essays in an elective writing workshop and return feedback immediately, while the teacher uses the freed time for one-on-one writing conferences. Make sure at least one worked case produces a verdict I will not like, and explain what the procedure cannot do.
