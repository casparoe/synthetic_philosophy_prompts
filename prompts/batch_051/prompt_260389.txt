I work the claims desk at our union's sickness fund. We pay a weekly benefit when a member is off work because of "illness or injury," and my job is first pass: read the claim form, read whatever note the doctor sent, and decide if it clearly gets paid, clearly doesn't, or has to go up to our medical advisor. The manual is no help—it says "condition" in one paragraph and "disease" in another, and nobody ever defined either one. I do about forty of these a week and I have maybe 25 minutes per claim. I have no medical training and neither do the two people I trained. The trustees audit me and review the ones I reject, and we're not exactly rich, so paying things we shouldn't means higher dues for everyone.

Heres the thing I keep getting stuck on. Some claims are easy: a broken wrist is an injury, pneumonia is an illness. But we also get notes that just say "burnout," or a doctor puts down "infertility" when the person is actually taking time off for IVF appointments, or an uncomplicated pregnancy at 38 weeks where the doctor's note says she should stop standing on a warehouse floor, or a 38-year-old whose only diagnosis is ADHD and half the form was filled in by his manager. Half the time I feel like I'm not deciding whether something is a medical problem, I'm deciding whose suffering counts and what a normal life is supposed to include.

I've read around online (nothing academic) and it seems like the people who think about this split into two camps. Camp one says a disease is just a fact about the body or brain: some part isn't doing the job it's built to do, full stop, and whether the person minds it or society calls it bad has nothing to do with it. Camp two says that can't be right, because deciding what a part is "built to do" or what a "job" is already has values smuggled into it, and anyway we call things diseases partly because they cause pain or stop people living the life they want—and that's a judgment, not a measurement. Camp two also says a condition can be a disease in one place and just a difference in another, depending on what a persons life demands and what their society expects.

I dont want a lecture, I want something I can use next week. Build me a checklist or a decision tree, in plain words, that takes the facts on a claim form and the doctors note and spits out one of three results: pay, don't pay, or kick it upstairs as a judgment call. It can't just punt all the hard ones upstairs, or the advisors desk would be buried and I'd have no job. For each step, tell me whether it's doing fact-checking or value work, because the value steps are the ones the trustees will fight. Then run the procedure on at least three made-up but realistic claims and show me the steps, including one where the procedure tells me to do something I don't want to do, like pay a claim I'd rather reject or reject one I'd rather pay. And be honest about where the whole thing breaks down—where a checklist can't settle it and I'm going to have to just decide and take the heat.
