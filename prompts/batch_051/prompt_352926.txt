Two analysts in the policy research unit where I work got into an argument at last week's seminar, and I could not referee it. The immediate question was a city proposal to let residents buy and sell their assigned slots in public after-school programs, but it quickly became a dispute about whether markets corrupt or civilize character.

Elena argued that the slot market would not merely allocate slots; it would teach that public goods are rightly distributed by willingness to pay, and that lesson would not stay inside the market. Placing a price on something changes its frame: reasons of need, fairness, or civic obligation give way to the logic of the deal. She noted that incentives can crowd out intrinsic motivation, giving the example of a childcare center where fining late pickups produced more lateness because the fine reframed an obligation as a fee. The corruption worry, she said, is about habituation: a life spent pricing things produces a person who treats relationships and duties as transactions.

Marcus replied that this singled out one mechanism and ignored the civilizing one. Repeated exchange forces people to keep commitments, show up on time, honor terms, and treat strangers as people one can deal with rather than threats. Competition penalizes fraud and rewards a reputation for honesty. Compared with the actual alternative—allocation by bureaucratic discretion or patronage—markets at least train impersonal fairness and reciprocity. The vices Elena described, he said, come from badly designed incentives or from markets without competition, not from exchange as such.

Elena answered that Marcus was treating the moral preconditions of markets as if markets produced them. Trust, rule-following, and forbearance are supplied mostly by families, schools, and civic life, so the real question is whether spreading the price frame into those same domains consumes a stock of non-market motives that exchange cannot replenish. That is a dynamic question. Once people are taught that goods are commensurable and obligations can be priced, they lose both the practice and the inclination to act for non-price reasons, and the preconditions erode.

Marcus granted that markets presuppose non-market virtues, but said the relevant comparison is not a romanticized pre-commercial past. Pre-commercial societies ran on clannishness, patronage, and often violence, so learning to deal fairly with strangers is a moral gain. The boundaries Elena wants to protect, he added, are already full of calculation: parents negotiate with children and neighbors trade favors. Making the terms explicit is less corrupting than pretending they do not exist.

Elena said making the terms explicit is precisely the worry, because it signals which reasons are in play and thereby changes the relationship. The childcare case, she argued, shows the framing effect is not cosmetic. The relevant baseline, she insisted, is an attainable mixed order with protected non-market spheres, not feudalism. The true disagreement, on her view, is whether the pricing frame is self-limiting or self-expanding; she held that it self-expands, because it trains people to see all goods as fungible.

Marcus countered that this made badly designed markets bear the blame for the market as such. A slot system could embed non-price reasons—eligibility rules, priority categories, limits on resale—and thereby keep the honesty of exchange without letting willingness to pay displace every other value. If character is harmed, that is a failure of institutional design, not a property of buying and selling itself.

Elena concluded that this distinction is the very issue. A tool that habituates people to treat goods and each other as fungible cannot be fully contained by surrounding design, because the people who would defend the boundaries have partly been trained by that tool not to. Marcus, she said, was assuming the neutrality of the market rather than defending it.

I can see force on both sides. Please adjudicate: identify which specific points each has right, what both are missing, and who is more right overall.
