Two friends of mine spent most of last night arguing about photographs, and I'm supposed to referee but I honestly couldnt. I'd like a point-by-point ruling.

Rina's view was that when you look at an old photograph of someone, you are literally seeing that person, not just seeing a picture of them. Her main argument was that a photograph is produced by light bouncing off the actual person and hitting film or a sensor, which makes the photo part of the same causal chain as ordinary seeing. A telescope puts more glass between you and a moon of Jupiter, but nobody says you stop seeing the moon; the camera just adds a photosensitive surface into the chain, so photographs are transparent.

Dev said that's the wrong way to think about it. A photo is a physical object in front of you: dye on paper, pixels on a screen. That's what you see. The person might be dead or on another continent, so they aren't there to be seen. He added that being caused by something isn't enough: a footprint is caused by a foot, but looking at a footprint isn't seeing the foot.

Rina came back that a footprint doesn't preserve visual information about the foot the way a photograph preserves visual information about a person. And she said we already accept massively delayed seeing: you look at a star at night and you're seeing it as it was years ago, even though it may have since died. So "the object is gone" can't be decisive.

Dev answered that the star case still has an unbroken stream of light travelling from the object into your eye; a photograph interrupts that stream, stores a pattern, and shows it to you later. Seeing requires the live, continuous chain, not a stored trace. A photo is more like a recording than a window.

Rina then asked about live CCTV. The signal is encoded, transmitted, decoded and displayed; the light from the person doesn't hit your eye at all, yet we still say you see the person on the monitor. If live CCTV counts as seeing, then a photograph only differs in being a longer delay, and a delay can't be the difference between seeing and not seeing.

Dev granted that live CCTV is tricky, but said it at least preserves moment-to-moment covariation: if the person moves, the image moves now, so you can track them. A photograph is static; you can only track changes in the print. It's a trace you examine, not a scene you witness.

Rina's last move was that tracking is a red herring. She said your own retina is only a photosensitive surface that produces a static two-dimensional pattern that your brain then works on, so the camera's sensor or film is doing the same job outside the body. If the retina's frozen image counts as seeing, the photograph should too.

Dev replied that the retina counts because it's wired into a living visual system in real time, and anyway the analogy collapses: the photographer chose the lens, the moment and the framing, so a photograph is an intentional representation, not a transparent medium.

Rina said choosing where to point a telescope or which window to look through doesn't mean you stop seeing what's on the other side. Photographers make choices, but that doesn't break the causal chain. They more or less went in circles after that.

Please adjudicate: who is right about which specific points, where does each argument need refining or go wrong, and who has the stronger overall case? Also, what are they both missing? If 'seeing' is doing all the work here, can you spell out the principle that should decide wether a technology is transparent or not. Oh, and answer in British English please.
