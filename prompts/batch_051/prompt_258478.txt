I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about self-control, and I came away convinced that the two of us were using the word for at least three different things. She wanted to say that self-control is always admirable and that any method of getting yourself to do what you have resolved to do counts as self-control. I am less sure. I suspect that some of what we call self-control is really prudence (arranging your circumstances well), some of it is repression rather than a virtue, and some of it can be exercised in the service of something thoroughly unhealthy. We could not settle even the following cases, which is why I am putting them to you.

I should like a working definition of self-control that correctly classifies, or at least assigns a defensible verdict to, each of the following ten cases:

1. A woman with a quick temper who, when provoked in a meeting, silently counts to ten before replying.
2. A man who wants the last slice of cake, has it on his plate, and decides not to eat it.
3. A recovering alcoholic who chooses a route home that does not pass the pub, because he doubts he could walk past it without going in.
4. A person who takes a medication that blunts his cravings for alcohol, so that the impulse to drink hardly arises in the first place.
5. A marathon runner who continues through the final miles despite pain and a strong desire to stop.
6. A person with anorexia nervosa who restricts her eating despite severe hunger.
7. A child who passes the marshmallow test only because she does not like marshmallows.
8. A man who rarely expresses anger and smiles through repeated provocation, then one day responds with disproportionate rage.
9. A woman who asks her flatmate to keep the biscuits in a locked drawer and to refuse to open it however much she pleads.
10. A person who is simply indifferent to sweets and declines dessert without any struggle at all.

To my mind, (1) and (2) are clear cases of self-control, and (7) and (10) are clear non-cases; I have no settled view about (3), (4), (5), (6), (8), and (9). My friend would count all of them as self-control except, she conceded, the child who dislikes marshmallows.

Please propose a definition, run it through all ten cases, and then say which of your verdicts is least secure and whether the definition can be defended or should be revised in light of it. I should also like to know whether you think self-control is best understood as a descriptive capacity that can be put to good or bad use, or as a virtue term that applies only to some exercises of that capacity.

Please write your answer in British English.
