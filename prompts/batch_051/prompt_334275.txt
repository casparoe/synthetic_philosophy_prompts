Two members of my reading group quarreled over the short treatment of the sovereign's right of pardon in Kant's Doctrine of Right, which forms Part II of The Metaphysics of Morals and is also translated as The Philosophy of Law. In that passage Kant describes the power to pardon as one of the most precarious of the sovereign's rights. He grants the sovereign authority to remit punishment only for wrongs done to the sovereign himself, and he denies the sovereign authority to remit a penalty for one subject's crime against another subject, since doing so would wrong the subjects and extend the sovereign's authority beyond its proper limit.

One reader, N, took the passage to be retributivist at its core. On N's reading, Kant forbids the sovereign from waiving punishment for ordinary crimes because the victim has a right to see the wrongdoer punished and justice itself demands the penalty. The only exception is where the sovereign is the victim, because there the sovereign waives a right that is his own. N argued that the passage shows Kant cannot treat punishment as a social instrument that may be set aside when clemency would produce better results.

The other, M, replied that Kant's stated argument is about standing, not desert. A wronged subject has a claim against the wrongdoer, and the sovereign did not acquire that claim by becoming sovereign. A third party may not cancel it. M claimed that a consequentialist ruler could accept the same institutional rule, since allowing executives to remit punishments for private wrongs invites favoritism, corruption, and unpredictability. Nothing in the passage, M said, requires retributivism.

N answered that this misses the force of Kant's language about pardoning as a uniquely treacherous right. A forward-looking account would hold that a pardon is justified whenever its expected consequences are better than the expected consequences of exacting punishment, making the right hard to use well but not treacherous in Kant's sense. Kant's categorical prohibition is best explained by the idea that remitting deserved punishment for a wrong against a subject is itself an injustice.

M responded that the danger Kant highlights can equally be a forward-looking concern about the abuse of mercy by the powerful. The rule may be stricter than a pure act-consequentialist would adopt, but that alone shows only that Kant holds a strict view of the limits of office, not that he accepts retributive desert.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. Identify which claims are correct, which are overstated, and what interpretation of the passage best accounts for it. In particular, say whether the passage by itself gives differential evidence for retributivism over a rule-consequentialist or contractarian account of punishment, and where the decisive difference between those positions must be sought instead.
