Next month I'm giving a colloquium talk, and I want you to help me prep for the Q&A the way a conscientious opponent would, because I'm going to get eaten if I wing it. The talk is called — working title — "When is a regress vicious?" and the thesis I'm defending is this: endlessness is not what makes an infinite regress bad; what makes it vicious is that each step inherits the very same explanatory or justificatory debt, unchanged, from the step above it. The same chain, same formal structure, can be benign under one demand and vicious under another, so you cant read viciousness off the regress's shape alone. My foil is Aristotle, Posterior Analytics I.3, and I open with this passage in Bouchier's translation: "Those who assume that knowledge is not possible at all, think that it would involve an infinite regress, since one cannot know the later terms of a series by means of the earlier when such a series has no primary terms. In this they are right, for it is impossible to complete the infinite." I want to argue that the second sentence — "impossible to complete the infinite" — is doing all the work for him and is the part he should not have said, and that the better diagnosis is sitting in the first sentence, in the words "by means of the earlier." The argument runs: (1) structure-based accounts of viciousness (terminates vs. doesn't, circles vs. doesn't circle) only get cases right by silently importing a demand; (2) the real engine is transmission — whether the regress-generating relation hands down an unsatisfied condition identical at every stage; (3) that's why Aristotle's own quarrel with circular demonstration in the same chapter is the same quarrel as the regress quarrel — the debt just gets passed around instead of down; (4) so the Agrippan modes of regress and circularity are really one mode in two outfits. The audience: mostly epistemologists — two committed infinitists, a few foundationalists, one old-school skeptic who treats the five modes of Agrippa as a package deal — plus the department's Aristotle person, who will fight me on the Greek. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way these people would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Include the question I'm dreading.
