Discussions of Weber's Protestant ethic thesis often settle on a railway image to explain what kind of cause a value or religious idea can be. The picture, due to Weber himself, is roughly this: interests — material and ideal — provide the motive force of human conduct, while the pictures of the world generated by religious ideas act as the switchman who selects which of the available tracks that conduct runs along. The image is made to do real work: it licenses the conclusion that culture can be a cause in history without being an independent power source, since religious ideas shape economic behavior by routing motives that interest and material circumstance have already supplied; and it is meant to protect the thesis both from the charge of crude idealism (ideas alone built capitalism) and from the charge of crude materialism (ideas as mere after-the-fact rationalization). I keep encountering this image whenever the thesis is defended against the first charge or dismissed with the second, and I want a rigorous verdict on whether it actually works.

Please audit the analogy rather than survey what commentators have said about it. First, give the correspondence table explicitly: what in the historical process plays the role of the switchman, the locomotive, the steam, the track, the switching mechanism, the timetable? Second, separate the disanalogies that matter for the analogy's intended causal work from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Third, address at least these pressure points: (a) a switchman chooses among pre-existing tracks, whereas religious ideas sometimes seem to lay new track rather than select among existing routes; (b) a switchman is a single deliberate chooser, which may import more executive control than a diffuse religious culture actually possesses; and (c) a switchman alters trajectory but neither generates energy nor changes how much of it there is, which threatens to reduce values to triggers rather than genuine causes. Then commit: give a definite overall verdict on how much weight the switchman image can bear, whether the hedged conclusion about cultural causation survives the disanalogies, and, if the image breaks, what better analogy would do the job and why.
