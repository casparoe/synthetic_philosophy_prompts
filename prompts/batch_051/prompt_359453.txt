I'm one of nine members of a Subsidiarity Review Chamber in a small federation. Every central-government framework law that touches state or municipal powers has to clear us before it can take effect, and we review one question: does it overstep its bounds under the subsidiarity clause? Our bench memos always gesture at the comparativists — one of us keep citing the American commerce power, another the EU's subsidiarity protocol, and our chair likes Swiss municipal autonomy — but in the end we're bound by our own precedents.

Our precedents, as I'd summarize them in a memo:
1. A national food-safety standard for packaged foods, preempting state rules. Approved — food moves across state lines, and patchwork rules would impose costs and health externalities.
2. National air-quality floors, with states free to go stricter. Approved — transboundary pollution.
3. National license portability for nurses, so a license from one state is valid everywhere. Approved — labor mobility and interstate services.
4. A fiscal equalization formula transferring revenue from high-capacity states to low-capacity ones. Approved — the constitution's promise of equivalent living conditions across states.
5. Nationwide minimum standards for municipal elections, like secret ballots and a minimum frequency for council elections. Approved — a democratic floor of citizenship, not efficiency.
6. A national schedule capping municipal parking fines. Rejected — purely local traffic management, no cross-border dimension, and mere standardization wasn't enough.
7. A mandate that all municipalities do their own purchasing through one federal e-procurement platform. Rejected — the feds showed real cost savings from scale, but there was no cross-border externality, and it displaced local choice over a purely local activity.

Now a new framework law is on our docket: a national minimum standard for classroom temperatures in public schools (a heating floor in winter, a cooling ceiling in summer), paired with a five-year federal grant program to help poorer states upgrade boilers and air-conditioning.

I have to write the memo. If we want to be consistent with our own decisions, which way should we come out? Which precedent is the real closest analog — the equalization or election-floor cases on one side, or the parking-fine and procurement cases on the other? If our precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle would we be adopting with each choice? And is there a tweak to the proposal (for instance, recasting the classroom standard as a health-and-safety floor, or converting the grants into a conditional transfer with no federal standard attached) that would make the answer less close, and which rule would that tweak betray? American English, please.
