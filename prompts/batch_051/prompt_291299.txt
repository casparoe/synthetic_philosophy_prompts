As a PhD student in moral philosophy, I've come to defend a strong version of the consequentialist cluelessness challenge: the long-run consequences of ordinary actions are so intractable that no non-arbitrary probability distribution supports a principled expected-value comparison between them, leaving consequentialism without usable action guidance. Please tell me what would count against this position. I want a map of where the view is actually exposed, not a list of standard objections: give me the considerations, observations, and arguments that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Among them I'd expect at least these, but go beyond them where you think I've missed something: that some options dominate their alternatives across every reasonable distribution, so no full distribution is needed; that imprecise credences permit robust or approximate comparisons where precise expected values cannot; that the position demands a standard of epistemic precision that would equally disable ordinary non-consequentialist practical reasoning; and that the view has been qualified enough to survive any proposed counterexample. For each, say whether a committed defender of the cluelessness challenge should concede it as counting against the view, which escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible, and close with an honest assessment of whether the position, stated this way, risks anything at all.
