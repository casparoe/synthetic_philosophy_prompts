I cover intellectual history and political economy for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about whether Adam Smith's attack on Britain's American colonies generalizes into a broader argument about loss-making territories. The passage I keep coming back to is in Book V, Chapter III of The Wealth of Nations, where Smith argues in effect that for more than a century the rulers of Great Britain had amused the public with the idea of a great empire on the western side of the Atlantic, but that this empire had so far existed in imagination only. It was, he says, not an empire but the project of an empire; not a gold mine but the project of a gold mine — a project that had cost, continued to cost, and if pursued in the same way was likely to go on costing immense expense without bringing any profit, since the effects of the monopoly of the colonial trade were, for the great body of the people, mere loss rather than profit. I am paraphrasing, but that is the substance.

A colleague has convinced me that the passage contains the makings of an argument whose conclusion is far more radical than the one Smith actually draws, and I cannot tell whether the argument is valid and therefore forces an uncomfortable choice or whether something slips out of place along the way. Reconstructed, it runs like this. (1) If a political arrangement has for more than a century imposed net costs on the general population of the governing country and offers no reasonable prospect of net benefit to them, then it is not genuinely valuable to that country; it is the project of a value, not a value. (2) Britain's American colonies, by Smith's own account, fit that description: the monopoly of the colonial trade was a net loss to the great body of the people, with profits captured by a small merchant interest. (3) What is merely the project of a value, not a value, is not something a country is rationally justified in retaining or pursuing in the same costly way. (4) Therefore Britain ought to have relinquished the American colonies, or at least to have ceased pursuing the imperial project in that form. (5) By parity of reasoning, any territory or public undertaking that is a persistent net drain on the general population and not a source of general benefit ought likewise to be relinquished. (6) But that would commit a state to abandoning poor or peripheral regions, disaster-stricken areas, and loss-making public goods — plainly unacceptable, since states sustain precisely such territories and programs for reasons of solidarity, justice, or long-term security. (7) Therefore the principle behind (1) or (3) proves too much and must be rejected.

I would most like to keep Smith's conclusion about the American colonies while avoiding the wholesale liquidation of subsidized regions, but I do not know whether I can do so. Which step, if any, should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
