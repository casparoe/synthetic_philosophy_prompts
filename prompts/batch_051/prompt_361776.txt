Most state archives operate under a set of practices that are individually routine but rarely examined as a whole. Archivists appraise records for permanent retention using criteria of evidential and informational value, keeping only a small fraction of what institutions produce. The survivors of that appraisal are kept in the order in which the creating body generated or filed them — the principles of respect des fonds and original order — rather than being rearranged by subject, place, or the persons they document. Government and corporate records are collected far more systematically than the records of private households, and even within private collections the finding aids tend to reproduce the creator's own categories and vocabulary. Records that are never collected, or that are deliberately destroyed under retention schedules, leave no formal trace of their absence.

I want to know what this complex of practice presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory of evidence, memory, and historical justice under which these choices make sense. Which rival views does it quietly rule out — for instance, that the archive's task is to represent the past as it was lived by everyone rather than to preserve the traces left by the powerful? Are the practices' presuppositions even consistent with one another, given that appraisal seems to require archivists to make value judgments while arrangement and description seem to promise something like neutrality? And where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several different theories of history or justice would issue in the same procedures? Conclude by saying what would actually have to change in appraisal, description, and access if the governing idea were that archives do not simply record what happened but decide who is remembered, and that silences are themselves part of the record.
