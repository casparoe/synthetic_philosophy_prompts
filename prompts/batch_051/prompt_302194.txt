I have been reading about C. Lloyd Morgan's rule from his 1894 book An Introduction to Comparative Psychology, often called Morgan's canon. As I understand it, the rule says roughly that we should not explain an animal's behavior by appeal to a higher psychological process if the behavior can be adequately explained by a process lower on some scale of psychological development. People still argue about whether this rule is right, but I cannot tell what the disagreement is really about. Some treat it as a cautious methodological policy for doing science; others treat it as a substantive claim about what animals probably think; still others say the whole idea of higher and lower psychological processes is questionable. My hypothesis is that this debate has stalled because the sides are answering different questions under one name, and because they disagree about what makes an explanation good rather than about any single empirical fact concerning animals. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a hidden factual or formal question underneath the impasse? If the diagnosis is right, what would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a formal model, a new kind of experiment, or something else? I would also like the response to say which argument or consideration would most likely change its mind, and where its reasoning is most likely to fail.
