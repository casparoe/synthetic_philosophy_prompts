Since being appointed last autumn to the citizens' panel that reviews our city's disaster-preparedness spending, I have noticed that the deepest disagreements among otherwise reasonable members are not about the actuarial numbers but about what safety means once it has to compete with other uses of a fixed budget. We must decide whether to direct a substantial sum toward hardening the levee system against a storm with an estimated annual probability on the order of one in several hundred that would kill many people at once, or toward measures that predictably reduce routine harms such as traffic deaths, falls, and respiratory disease, which kill the same expected number of people but one or two at a time. The panel splits sharply, and I have come to suspect that the real division is not empirical but conceptual: whether the concentration of deaths in a single event should matter at all, and whether caution under severe uncertainty is a rationally defensible value or merely fear wearing a technical costume.

I would like your credence that the following proposition is true: when a society is allocating a fixed safety budget, it can be rationally defensible, not merely psychologically understandable, to be substantially more cautious, per expected death prevented, about low-probability risks that would kill many people at once than about high-frequency risks that kill the same expected number of people one or two at a time. Please give me a number, but spend most of the answer decomposing it. The sub-questions I have in mind are: whether the distribution of deaths across persons and time has intrinsic moral weight beyond aggregate expected mortality; whether genuine uncertainty about the tail of the distribution justifies a precautionary premium over and above the known expected value; whether the institutional aftershocks of a catastrophe, such as lost public trust and cascading failures, can be fully absorbed into an ordinary cost-benefit ledger; and whether some catastrophic risks are governed by deontic constraints that forbid trade-offs rather than by a logic of weighing costs against benefits. For each, give a rough probability and say how it enters your overall number. Then tell me which single sub-question your credence is most sensitive to, what evidence or argument would move it most, and, if it helps, where you think a panelist at roughly 0.2 and one at roughly 0.9 most likely diverge.
