I'm a novelist, not a historian, and for the past year I've been pestering a historian friend about period labels because my next book is set in the twelfth century and I can't figure out whether I'm describing a real turning point or just some decades inside a box later historians drew. Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it from coffees and emails, is this: the Middle Ages, she says, is a later invention, a baggy thousand-year gap with no inner unity; the Italian Renaissance is real; the Scientific Revolution is real; the Enlightenment is real. She's never stated her principle, and I can't pin it down from those four alone. It could be that she only calls an era real when the people living in it had a self-conscious sense of living through a new age, or it could be that she calls it real when there was a genuine, broad structural break, or something else I haven't thought of.

The case I can't predict her on is the so-called twelfth-century renaissance: the cathedral boom, the first universities, the recovery of Aristotle throught Arabic translators, the commercial revival, roughly 1050 to 1200. By almost any material measure that looks like a genuine, rapid transformation. But as far as I can tell, nobody inside it thought they were living through a renaissance or a new age; the label was glued on later, mostly by twentieth-century medievalists. If I brought her this case, would she say it's real like the Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution, or an invention like the Middle Ages? I care less about the bare verdict than the principle: what rules fit her track record, what each rule says about the twelfth century, which rule is the best guess and why, and what further case would settle it.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question.
