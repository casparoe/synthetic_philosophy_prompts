I write for a general-readership magazine that covers science and ideas, and roughly once a month I do one of those short pieces that takes an old scientific or mathematical text and restates it in language a modern reader can follow — what Newton was up to with fluxions, what the Greek atomists were actually claiming, that sort of thing. Every time I run into the same fight. Historians of science write in to say I'm smuggling in modern concepts and being anachronistic or whiggish; my editor and the factcheckers say that if I hedge everything with "they didn't have our concept of X," the piece dies on the page and readers learn nothing. I have decent science literacy but no historical training, I'm usually working against a two-hour deadline, and I can't chase sources — I need a working method, not a reading list.

So: turn whatever the philosophy and history of science actually says about anachronism in reading old texts — presentism, contextualism, Whig history, the idea that an old term gets its meaning from a whole background of related concepts and practices — into a decision procedure I can actually run. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure licenses a modern gloss, where it says "do not use this word," and where it has to stop and say "this needs a historian of science, not a journalist." Then run it on three cases I actually face: calling Newton's fluxions "the derivative" and his methods early calculus; saying Democritus and Lucretius had a theory of atoms; and using "force" or "momentum" for what Aristotle meant by motion and movers. Make one of the worked examples a case where your procedure tells me not to do the thing I'd naturally do, and be clear about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as method.
