Been rereading Kafka's Metamorphosis for a book club, and I cant stop thinking about it through L.A. Paul's transformative experience framework. Her idea, roughly, is that some experiences are epistemically transformative in the sense that you literally cannot know what they're like until you go through them, and personally transformative in the sense that going through them changes your values or preferences in ways you can't fully anticipate. The obvious thing is that Gregor's transformation is a weird fit: he doesnt choose it, there's no decision problem ahead of time, and the story starts after the change has already happened. But the decision problem is everywhere else in the story: Grete deciding how to care for him, the father driving him back into his room, the lodgers and the violin-playing scene, and the family's slow shift to concluding that the thing in the bedroom simply has to go. Those are all people making choices about someone else's transformative experience (if it even counts as one) without having had it themselves.

What I'd love is a careful reading that works through the novella using Paul's two distinctions. To what extent is Gregor's transformation epistemically transformative for him—does the story actually dramatize the idea that he couldn't have known beforehand what it would be like, or does it partly undermine that by keeping him recognizably Gregor inside a bug's body? Does the story treat the change as personally transformative, and for whom—Gregor alone, or the whole Samsa household? Does the novella end up agreeing with Paul that third parties are in a specially bad epistemic position about someone else's transformation, or does it read the family's reaction as a moral failure rather than just an epistemic blind spot? I don't want a stock essay about alienation; I want the reading to stay honest about where Kafka resists the framework, and to say what the story adds that the philosophy misses.
