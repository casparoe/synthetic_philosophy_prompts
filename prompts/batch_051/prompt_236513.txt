Deplatforming has become an ordinary feature of public life. A speaker whose views are judged hateful, harassing, or dangerous — sometimes unlawful, sometimes merely offensive or controversial — is removed from a social media platform, disinvited from a campus lecture, or dropped by a publisher, not by the state but by private institutions exercising rights of property, contract, and association. To the person removed, however, the experience has the familiar shape of punishment: public stigma, loss of livelihood and audience, and a visible warning to others. My question is what theory of punishment, if any, this practice presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can. Does deplatforming make most sense as retribution, on which the speech makes the speaker deserve exclusion? As general deterrence, on which others are warned away from saying or holding such things? As incapacitation or harm prevention, on which the speaker's reach is removed so the speech cannot continue to do damage? As denunciation or moral education, on which the community publicly reasserts which views are beyond the pale? Or as none of these — not punishment at all, but a private actor's exercise of association that merely has punitive side effects? Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes, what it is merely compatible with, and where it is philosophically underdetermined. Then say what would have to change in either the practice or its public defense if we replaced its implicit theory with the strongest rival account. I ask because institutions so often defend deplatforming as mere content moderation while critics and targets treat it as punishment, and I suspect the disagreement is really about which theory of social sanction is quietly doing the work.
