For a course I am preparing on the ethics of historical fiction, I am assigning Peter Shaffer's play Amadeus alongside Milos Forman's film of it, and I want to pair it with the question of what, if anything, the living owe the real dead when they turn them into characters. The film's dramatic engine is the claim that Antonio Salieri, a real historical figure, schemed against Mozart out of envy and in effect destroyed him; the story is told as the elderly Salieri's confession to a young priest. The murder scheme is generally regarded as dramatic invention, and the film likewise turns Mozart into a giggling vulgarian and gives Constanze a much-reduced portrait. I would like a reading of Amadeus through the ethics of dramatizing the actual dead: does the work wrong Salieri, and, to the extent it does or does not, what kind of wrong is it — a harm to a reputation that survives its bearer, a failure of truthfulness, a violation of something like counterfactual consent, or no wrong at all because the dead have no interests left to set back? I want you to take the play's own dramaturgy seriously rather than reducing it to an illustration. One might argue, for instance, that the confession-frame marks Salieri as an unreliable, self-justifying narrator, and that a drama which announces its own distortion is less culpable than a straight-faced biopic that hides its inventions; but one might equally argue that the work's vivid images simply become the public's Mozart and Salieri regardless of the frame. Which scenes or choices would you put before my students to make each view as strong as possible, and where does the work resist or complicate both of them? End by telling me what would change your verdict — which historical or philosophical consideration, if it went the other way, would most alter your assessment — and where in your reasoning you think failure is most likely.
