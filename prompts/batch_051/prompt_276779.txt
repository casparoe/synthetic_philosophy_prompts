I've been going back and forth with a roommate about large language models—he's convinced there model genuinely understands the stuff it says, and I keep telling him it's just next-token prediction with good posture. Then I realized we were accidentally relitigating the old animal-cognition principle behind Morgan's canon. Roughly, that principle says you shouldn't explain a behavior by attributing a higher mental faculty when a lower one will do the job: don't say the dog reasoned about the latch if associative learning covers the data. Its whole point was to stop people from reading human-style thought into animals.

What throws me is that with an LLM we've actually built the lower-level story. The thing is trained by gradient descent on text; the training objective is about as dumb as it gets, and yet the resulting system solves novel problems and explains its own reasoning in ways that sure look like understanding. If Morgan's canon still applies, doesn't it force us to say: never attribute understanding to such a system, because the lower-level training story always suffices? But then the canon starts to prove too much, since a low-level neural story also "suffices" for human behavior if you squint hard enough. So I want to know: does Morgan's canon survive this development, does it need repair, or is it just the wrong tool now? Say which premise the LLM case really bites on—the idea that lower and higher faculties form a clean hierarchy, or the idea that having an available lower explanation is evidence against the higher one—and whether the repaired version (if there is one) still helps with the original job of attributing cognition to animals, or whether it now mostly applies to machines.
