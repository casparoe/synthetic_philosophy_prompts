Consider the ergodicity critique of expected-value reasoning. In a multiplicative gamble repeated over time, the ensemble average can point one way while the time average points the other. Take a concrete case: each round, an agent may stake its full wealth on a bet that gains 50% or loses 40% with equal probability. The expected per-round multiplier is 1.05, so the expected value of the agent's wealth grows without bound. But the time-average growth rate is the geometric mean of the two outcomes, the square root of 1.5 × 0.6, or about 0.949 per round; an individual who repeatedly takes this bet sees wealth decay almost surely in the long run. The critique generalizes from this: an agent who lives a single path through a multiplicative process should maximize time-average growth, not ensemble-average expectation, because expected-value reasoning is systematically misleading in non-ergodic settings.

Now change one assumption. The decision-maker is not a lone individual but a large, long-lived institution that each round divides its capital across thousands of independent and identically distributed copies of the gamble and rebalances at the start of the next round. To the extent that sampling error is small, the institution's total capital each round grows almost deterministically at the 1.05 ensemble mean, while each individual copy, left to itself, would follow the decaying single path.

Does the ergodicity critique survive this change? In particular, evaluate these three possibilities: (i) the critique still applies unchanged, because the institution's total capital is still a multiplicative process across rounds; (ii) the critique simply does not apply to the institution, because diversification has given it effective access to the ensemble, and expected-value reasoning is thereby restored for it; (iii) the critique's true load-bearing premise was never multiplication or time per se but the agent's confinement to one path of an unpooled process, so the argument must be restated in terms of path dependence and undiversifiable risk, after which it still applies to every constituent of the institution who lives a single path. Say which possibility is most defensible, which premise the development bears on, under what conditions — correlated shocks, costs of rebalancing, finite capital — the conclusion changes, and whether the argument ends up preserved, repaired, or dead for (a) the institution and (b) the individuals inside it.
