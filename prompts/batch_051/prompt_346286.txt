I coordinate a community literacy project for a language that has been spoken for generations but has no established way of being written; the few existing texts are improvised and inconsistent. In six months, an alphabet proposal must be approved by a committee of twelve first-language speakers, none of whom has formal training in linguistics. Next year a government program will require school materials and public signs in the language, and once the alphabet is published it will be very difficult to change, so the result has to be something the community can live with a decade later. I will be running the decision process, but I am an outsider with only basic conversational command of the language, and we will have one visiting linguist for only two sessions before the final vote.

I have been reading on my own about why writing systems make the choices they make, and one idea keeps returning: a script is a kind of theory of the language. It announces which sound differences matter for telling words apart and which differences are just automatic side effects of the surrounding sounds, position in the word, or the habits of speech. I have also seen the word "phonemic" used for the rule of one written mark per meaningful sound difference, and I understand that real alphabets often depart from that rule for good reasons. But I do not know how to turn this into a procedure the committee can actually run when it argues about whether one particular sound needs its own letter.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure for deciding whether a sound difference in this language should be represented by its own letter, digraph, diacritic, or other consistent written mark. It should be a sequence of questions asked in order, each stated in plain language, with what would count as a sufficient answer before moving on, where the procedure stops with a yes or a no, and where it must hand the decision to the visiting linguist or to the committee because the question is one of judgment rather than fact. Any technical distinction the procedure relies on has to be translated into concrete tests the committee can run with ordinary words from the language. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about where the procedure will misfire.

We will run this on every sound difference the committee raises, probably forty or so over the next six months, so the procedure needs to be fast in the easy cases and clear about why the hard cases are hard.

Then run the procedure on these three cases:

1. There are two consonants that I, as an outsider, hear as the same consonant, but the committee insists are as different from one another as two consonants can be. They can produce several pairs of words that mean different things and differ only by which of the two consonants is used.
2. A consonant is always pronounced one way before a particular vowel and another way in every other environment. No two words are ever distinguished only by this difference; the pronunciation follows automatically from the surrounding sounds. Some committee members still find it obvious that the two pronunciations should get two letters.
3. The language distinguishes words by high versus low pitch on a syllable. If pitch is left unmarked, many unrelated words will be written identically; if every pitch is marked, the text becomes dense and slow to type, even though speakers say context would usually disambiguate the written words.

Make sure at least one of the three outcomes is one the committee will not like, and say explicitly which one it is and why the procedure recommends it anyway. Finally, list the kinds of question the procedure cannot settle, such as visual tradition, typing, or closeness to a neighboring language's writing, and say how the committee should weigh those against the linguistically tidier answer.
