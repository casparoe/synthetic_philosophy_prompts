In my philosophy of science reading group we've been stuck on James and Peirce for a few weeks, and one of the PhD students has a habit of ending every hard discussion by saying "truth is what works," as if that settles things. Last week he used it to close a debate about whether a climate model that's built on assumptions we know are false but still predicts the data well counts as "true." The week before he used it to shrug off a question about whether a comforting but unfalsifiable belief—like "things happen for some reason"—gets to be true if it helps a grieving person get out of bed. I keep feeling like the slogan is sliding between "a true belief is one that doesn't blow up in your face when you act on it" and "truth is just whatever a community finds useful to believe," and probalby a bunch of other readings too.

I've been asked to bring a one-page handout to the next meeting laying out the distinct precise claims someone could be making when they say "truth is what works," from the trivial to the genuinely radical. For each reading, I'd like to know: would a Peircean convergence picture accept it, would a Jamesian cash-value picture accept it, and does that reading actually license using the slogan to close down the climate-model case, the grief case, both, or neither? Flag which readings are just false even if they sound pragmatist. I want it set up like a menu I can point to next time he reaches for the slogan.
