Here is my argument, which I half-suspect proves too much. (1) A student's wrong answer counts as a misconception rather than a random slip only if it is stable, in the sense that the same answer recurs across time, across different wordings of the problem, and across surface changes in the task. (2) Stability of this kind is possible only when the answer is generated by an underlying rule, model, or theory of the domain; a mere collection of associations could not keep producing the same error over that range of variation. (3) If a wrong answer is generated by an underlying theory, then there is no point correcting the answer in isolation, because the theory will simply generate it again; the theory itself has to be located and revised. (4) Therefore, for any genuine misconception, error feedback alone cannot fix the difficulty, and instruction must aim at revising or replacing an underlying theory. I want the conclusion to be true because it favors conceptual-change teaching over mere error correction, but I worry that premise 2 smuggles in a picture of the mind as more systematic than it is. Could a stable error instead be a stable but isolated fragment, something like a local rule of thumb that is stable without belonging to any wider theory? And if so, does the conclusion survive, at least in a weakened form?
