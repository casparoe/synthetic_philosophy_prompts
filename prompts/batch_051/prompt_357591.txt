Alright, here's one from my historiography seminar that I cant shake. A friend has an argument that seems to show the whole idea of avoiding presentism is incoherent, and I keep getting pulled along step by step. It runs:

(1) A historian has to select from an effectively infinite mass of evidence; you can't include everything.
(2) Selection is driven by judgments of significance — what mattered, what caused what, what deserves more than a footnote.
(3) There's no timeless, neutral standard of significance. What a historian finds significant is going to reflect the concerns, values, and explanatory obsessions of her own period.
(4) So the shape of every historical work is set by the standards of the author's own time.
(5) Shaping an account of the past by the standards of your own time just is presentism.
(6) But presentism is treated as a historiographical sin — it's the standard criticism of, say, a nineteenth-century historian who sneers at medieval peasants for being irrational by Victorian lights.
(7) Therefore either all history is presentist, in which case the criticism is toothless, or the field demands something impossible and then blames people for failing to do it.

I want to say the conclusion is too strong, and I especially want to keep the idea that the nineteenth-century historian is doing something genuinely wrong. But I can't find the step to reject. Which premise or step should go, why does it look so unproblematic, and what does each way of fixing the argument cost?
