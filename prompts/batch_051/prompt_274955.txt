Construct a single-paragraph thought experiment that exposes the instability of the distinction between a moral preference and a moral bias. The case should center on a person whose firm moral judgment about some conduct, relationship, or institutional arrangement can be plausibly redescribed in either of two ways: as a stable preference that partly constitutes the kind of life she wants to live, or as a bias that distorts her moral perception of the relevant situation. The tension you must engineer is practical as well as descriptive: if the judgment is a preference, the fitting response is some form of respect or accommodation; if it is a bias, the fitting response is some form of correction or discounting. The case must make these two responses genuinely conflict, and it must not leave room for the easy reconciliation that the judgment is simply a bit of both.

Constraints: the scenario should be realistic and set in an ordinary context such as a family, a workplace, a school board, or a neighborhood association; it should be no more than one paragraph; it may not include any stipulation that the person is biased; the judgment must be one a reasonable person could hold; and the judgment should carry genuine practical consequences for other people rather than being a merely aesthetic or private taste.

After the scenario, do the following. (i) Give the strongest available version of the preference reading, citing the standard criteria it satisfies, such as stability over time, self-identification, integration into the person's broader life plan, or willingness to bear costs for it. (ii) Give the strongest available version of the bias reading, citing the standard criteria it satisfies, such as selective responsiveness to evidence, motivated belief, or asymmetry in the treatment of otherwise similar cases. (iii) Identify the structural feature of the case that allows both readings to remain sustainable at once, and explain why removing that feature would collapse the ambiguity. (iv) Specify the smallest additional piece of evidence about the person that would break the tie in one direction, and then say whether adding it would settle the underlying conceptual issue or only settle this particular case.
