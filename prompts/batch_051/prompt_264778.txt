I have been convening a working group on neurotechnology and mental privacy, and I want you to draft the document its two factions might sign after a year of good-faith adversarial collaboration. Approach this as a professional philosopher with expertise in the ethics of neurotechnology and in privacy theory. Start with an informal brainstorming pass in which you catalogue the points of agreement, the loaded terms each side would refuse to sign, and the places where the dispute may be partly verbal; only then write the polished document.

The two factions are as follows. The neural exceptionalists maintain that neural data is sui generis: because it is causally upstream of thought, largely involuntary, and temporally fine-grained, it merits a distinct right to mental privacy, best expressed as a component of cognitive liberty. The integrationists maintain that neural data sits at the sensitive end of a single spectrum of intimate physiological and behavioural data, and that it should be governed by strengthened but familiar data-protection principles rather than by any novel sui generis right. Both factions accept the relevance of certain working distinctions: content-level privacy, which concerns particular thoughts, beliefs, and intentions, versus processing-level privacy, which concerns attention, arousal, effort, and valence; the fact that a raw signal may be innocuous by itself while yielding a sensitive inference once combined with contextual data; and the difference between cognitive liberty understood negatively, as freedom from reading or interference, and positively, as a claim to use neurotechnology.

Please draft their joint statement in three parts. Part one is a list of numbered claims both factions can sign, formulated so that neither would flinch. Part two is a disagreements table: for each residual disagreement, state the claim twice, once in the wording the exceptionalists accept for their own position and once in the wording the integrationists accept for theirs, and assess in each row whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal, supplying a shared reformulation whenever it is verbal. Part three identifies the cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one faction. I expect the document to develop at least the following candidates, though you may refine or replace them: the source-dependence question, whether holding the inferred mental content and its accuracy fixed, an inference derived from neural signals differs in moral kind, not merely degree, from the same inference derived from keyboard dynamics and eye tracking; the inert-recording question, whether continuously storing raw neural signals that no present algorithm can decode beyond attention constitutes a distinctive wrong independently of any downstream use; and the enhancement question, whether a closed-loop system that modulates mood in real time engages cognitive liberty in a way familiar persuasive technologies do not. For each crux, provide a concrete case or experiment both factions would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Keep the document self-contained: no bibliography, footnotes, or reading list.
