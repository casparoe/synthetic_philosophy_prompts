Consider an argument against the broadly Hofstadterian view that the self is a self-referential symbol—roughly, a symbol like "I" that refers to the very system that produces it. The argument was put to me as follows, and I cannot tell whether it succeeds or where exactly it goes wrong.

(1) If the self simply is a self-referential symbol, then any system containing a self-referential symbol thereby has at least the rudiments of a self.
(2) But self-referential symbols occur in things that plainly have no self: a token of the sentence "this sentence has five words," or a computer program that prints out its own source code.
(3) Therefore, if the self simply is a self-referential symbol, those very things would have at least the rudiments of a self.
(4) But they plainly do not.
(5) So the self is not simply a self-referential symbol, and the Hofstadterian picture must be missing something essential.

Assess this argument step by step. State whether it is formally valid, and if so, identify the premise that fails—unless you think an expression such as "self-referential symbol" shifts its meaning between steps. Give the strongest reading of the target view under which this objection does not connect, and say whether the conclusion in (5) still follows on that reading or whether it would need a different argument.
