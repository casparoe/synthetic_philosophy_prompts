I am a novelist writing a book whose protagonist, a retired appellate judge, holds a settled and deliberately worked-out view about a citizen's relation to the law. I want to be able to test this view with integrity — to break it if it deserves to break, or to know honestly that it does not — rather than staging only the objections convenient to a plot. Here is the view, as he would state it.

In a state that is broadly democratic and meets a threshold of basic justice, a citizen has a genuine moral obligation to obey the law as law — not merely a prudential reason grounded in the risk of punishment or the need for coordination. This obligation is pro tanto: it can be outweighed, but it is a real demand, and it extends to particular laws the citizen reasonably believes are mistaken, provided ordinary channels of political change remain available. Civil disobedience may therefore be justified only under tight conditions. It must be public rather than covert, nonviolent, communicative rather than coercive, aimed at the community's sense of justice, undertaken after ordinary remedies have been tried or are demonstrably futile, and performed with a willingness to accept the legal penalty. What justifies disobedient protest, on his view, is not that the protester's conscience is satisfied but that the act addresses a serious injustice while acknowledging the system's general claim to compliance.

I want a map of what would count against this position — genuinely against it, not against a straw version.

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward: the observations, arguments, formal objections, possible cases, and results from adjacent debates that should move a careful holder of this view. For each, say what accepting it would force the view to surrender, whether a committed defender would concede that it counts against the position or would dismiss it, and whether it is a live route of resistance or only a bare logical possibility. Include any consequence to which the view is committed that turns out unacceptable, and any case it lacks the resources to describe. I also want an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — whether the threshold of a decent state, the pro tanto qualifier, and the narrow conditions on disobedience together make it self-certifying — and, if so, whether that is a defect for a view of this kind or simply how a view in this area ought to behave.

Since the novel's engine is this judge meeting each challenge as a life rather than as a premise, give each defeater a one-line note on how it could be dramatized in a scene or a dilemma. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; everything should be followable from the case as stated.

After the prose, provide a structured summary as a JSON object with the following schema:

- "position": a one-sentence restatement of the view under scrutiny.
- "defeaters": an array of objects, each with:
    - "rank": integer, 1 for the most decisive;
    - "defeater": a short name for the defeater;
    - "kind": one of "observation", "argument", "intuition", "possible_case", "adjacent_area_result";
    - "severity": one of "fatal", "major", "moderate", "merely_awkward";
    - "what_it_would_show": what the position would have to give up if this were accepted;
    - "defender_response": one of "concede", "partially_concede", "dismiss", "explain_away";
    - "live_or_merely_possible": whether a reasonable person could actually accept this route now;
    - "dramatization_note": one sentence on how to place it in a scene.
- "insulation_assessment": a short verdict on whether the view has been insulated against falsification and whether that matters.
- "bottom_line": an overall assessment of how exposed the position is and where its single strongest pressure point lies.
