She takes as her controlling text the early section of Harry Frankfurt's On Bullshit, in which Frankfurt distinguishes the liar from the bullshitter by their relation to truth: the liar, on her reading, must know what is true in order to depart from it, whereas the bullshitter is not constrained by truth one way or the other. I will paraphrase rather than quote, but that difference is the crux of what she applies.

Here are her prior rulings, using only the labels lying, bullshit, error, or neither:

2. A car salesman tells a customer that a used car gets 35 miles per gallon; he has not checked and does not care whether it is true, because he wants the sale. She says: bullshit.
3. A scholar writes that a certain manuscript is twelfth-century; she sincerely believes this because she relied on an amateur transcription, but the dating is wrong. She says: error.
4. A dinner guest announces that the city is now the most dangerous in the country; he has no interest in whether that is accurate and wants only to sound provocative. She says: bullshit.
5. A witness testifies that she saw the defendant at the scene, knowing she did not, in order to protect him. She says: lying.
6. A student answers on an exam that the French Revolution began in 1788, sincerely remembering it that way, though the answer is false. She says: error.
7. A broken weather station sends an automated alert saying no rain is expected because its sensor failed, when in fact a storm is forming. She says: neither, because there is no speech act by an agent.
