I cover science and ideas for a living, which means people email me thier pet proofs all the time, and mostly I can spot the sleight of hand. But a logician showed me this one at a conference and I still can't name the move. He says it proves there are no truth-value gaps:

(1) Take any declarative sentence S. Either S is true or S is not true — that's just the most basic either/or there is.
(2) To say S is not true just is to say S is false. So every declarative sentence is either true or false.
(3) Now take 'She is tall,' said of someone who's a borderline case of tallness — right at the boundary, where the word itself hasn't settled an answer.
(4) If the sentence is true, then she's tall, full stop. But then the word has settled an answer, so she isn't borderline after all.
(5) If the sentence is false, then she's not tall, full stop. But then again the word has settled an answer, so she isn't borderline after all.
(6) By (2), one of those two must hold. So there can be no borderline cases: vague predicates really just hide an exact cutoff.

I want to keep both that borderline cases are real and that logic doesn't just give out at the edges, but I'm starting to think I can't have both. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
