The thesis, roughly: everyday conventions—queueing, tipping where it's customary, crossing against the light where everyone does it—aren't just etiquette; many of them generate real pro tanto moral obligations, and visitors are bound by them too, but only when (a) the convention actually solves a local coordination or respect-signaling problem, (b) it isnt a cover for exploitation, and (c) a reasonable visitor could be expected to notice the convention without being a local. So a visitor to a queueing culture should queue, and a visitor to a tipping culture should tip, but a visitor isn't obligated by a convention that's deliberately opaque, decorative, or harmful. The argument in one paragraph: conventions create settled expectations people plan around; deliberately disappointing a settled expectation you can see, where that expectation exists to solve a shared problem, is a way of using the locals' reliance as a tool, and that wrong-making feature doesn't depend on the visitor's tastes or whether they would have chosen the convention—so the obligation is content-independent in the relevant sense.
