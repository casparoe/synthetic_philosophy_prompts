I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist who has recently gotten into philosophy of mind, mostly by reading through the standard debates on mental causation, and I keep tripping over Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument. As I understand it: my intention to raise my arm is supposed to cause my arm to go up. But my arm going up already has a complete physical cause — the neural and muscular events leading up to the movement. If we refuse to say every such effect is massively overdetermined, then the mental cause looks "excluded" unless it just is one of those physical causes, and that seems to push you toward either reduction (the mental just is the physical) or epiphenomenalism (the mental does nothing). So far so good, or rather so far so bad for the mental.

Here's the parallel I can't get out of my head. I spent my career saying things like "the pressure of the gas caused the piston to move" and "the temperature drop caused the supersaturated solution to crystallize," and nobody in physics treated those macro-scale causes as competing with the exact microstate of 10^23 molecules. The microstate is surely a complete physical cause of the same piston movement and the same crystallization, yet no one concludes that pressure and temperature are epiphenomenal or that we must identify them with a particular microstate. They are multiply realized by zillions of distinct microstates. So it looks as though Kim's argument, run straight, would make pressure and temperature causally idle too, which would be absurd for thermodynamics and chemistry. I suspect the analogy breaks somewhere — maybe temperature and pressure are physical predicates in a way mental ones aren't, or maybe the relevant difference is reducibility, or maybe I'm sneaking in a different notion of "cause" — but I can't pin down where.

Could you be the professional philosopher of mind here and think through whether this is a deep structural parallel or a superficial one? Start by laying out the mapping explicitly — Kim's mental cause, the physical realizer or competitor, and the effect, against the thermodynamic analogues — then check where the structures match and where they come apart. I'd especially like to know what the parallel would predict that turns out false in one of the two cases, because that would tell me whether I'm just pattern-matching. Please dont just gesture at "levels of explanation"; tell me what would have to be true on both sides for the exclusion argument to carry over, and whether physics gives me a real independent reason to resist it in the mental case. And please say how confident you are in your verdict, and single out the point you're least sure about.
