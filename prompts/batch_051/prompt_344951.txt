I teach an upper-level undergraduate epistemology seminar, and I am writing the final exam. I would like one essay question on the relationship between Williamson's anti-luminosity argument and the KK principle, where KK is the principle that if one knows p, then one is in a position to know that one knows p. The question should be unanswerable by reciting the familiar summary that Williamson runs a sorites-style series of cases and concludes that no non-trivial condition is luminous, so KK has to go. I want it to force students to work through the argument at the level of its premises and to take a stand on what it actually shows.

Specifically, the question should require students to do all of the following:
(1) Reconstruct the argument so as to separate the epistemic margin-for-error premise (roughly, that a subject in case i knows, or is in a position to know, that condition C obtains only if C also obtains in the adjacent case) from a brute tolerance premise about C itself (if C obtains in case i, then C obtains in case i+1), and explain why the argument depends on the epistemic premise being far more defensible than the brute tolerance premise.
(2) Explain how the anti-luminosity conclusion is supposed to reach the KK principle, addressing whether the condition C must be the condition of knowing that p, and whether p is held fixed or allowed to vary across the series.
(3) Assess one natural line of resistance: that although phenomenal states such as feeling cold are not luminous, the argument does not transfer to knowledge because knowledge is factive and its boundaries track truth rather than gradable appearances. Students should have to say whether this resistance blocks the argument, merely relocates it, or changes the subject.

Please write the question as it would appear on the exam, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain and the specific misunderstandings that should cost marks. The course has a three-hour sit-down final; this question is worth 30 marks out of 100. The marking scheme should not award marks merely for using the labels luminosity, KK, or margin for error.
