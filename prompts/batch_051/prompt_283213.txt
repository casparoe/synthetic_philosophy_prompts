So I just got back from a long argument with a friend about this and I’m still turning it over. We were trying to figure out whether Darwin’s “strange inversion of reasoning” and Turing’s are actually the same move or just superficially similar. My friend kept coming back to this passage from Darwin, On the Origin of Species, Chapter XIV, Recapitulation and Conclusion:

“It is, no doubt, extremely difficult even to conjecture by what gradations many structures have been perfected, more especially amongst broken and failing groups of organic beings; but we see so many strange gradations in nature, as is proclaimed by the canon, “Natura non facit saltum,” that we ought to be extremely cautious in saying that any organ or instinct, or any whole being, could not have arrived at its present state by many graduated steps.”

His line is that this already contains the whole idea: you don’t need a comprehending designer, just a lot of accumulated small non-comprehending steps. Fine. But then he said Turing’s move in Computing Machinery and Intelligence is just the same inversion applied to the mind, and I don’t buy it. I think there’s a real difference between saying a dumb historical process can produce something that looks designed, and saying a system can display competence without any inner comprehension. But maybe I’m the one who’s confused. Can you explain how to think about this? Start from the Darwin passage and then show whether it does or doesn’t map onto Turing’s version of the inversion. I want to understand the conceptual structure, not a reading list.
