I'm sketching a chapter on decision-theoretic arguments for religious commitment, and I keep noticing that defenders and critics of Pascal's wager are never actually arguing over the same requirements. Before I read yet another reconstruction, I want the full spec: what would an adequate wager-style argument have to get right, if it's meant to be a genuine piece of practical reasoning that ends in a recommendation to practice (or try to believe)? Give me the dessiderata as a list, with the case or objection behind each, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of a particlar camp. Make sure the list at least covers: the many-gods problem and the rule for which merely possible payoffs get to count; the mixed-strategies point that if wagering gets infinite expected value then any act that leaves God a positive probability also gets it, so the argument stops singling out wagering; infinities in the utility function and whether expected utility arithmetic even works with them (finitists vs. the infinite-utility crowd); evidential vs. causal decision theory and whether the act has to cause rather than just be evidence for the good outcome; whether you can decide to believe, or whether the argument has to be retargeted at acting-as-if or going through the practices; and the step from 'can't rule God out' to 'assign God positive probability.' Then flag any subsets of these that can't be jointly satisfied — I've got a hunch that 'allow any possibility of infinite payoff to count' plus 'the wager uniquely recommends one act' are in tension, but I can't make the proof clean myself. And say which single requirement, if genuinely enforced, does the most to kill the wager and its modern descendants. Keep the weight on the criteria and their cases, not on scoring the contenders.
