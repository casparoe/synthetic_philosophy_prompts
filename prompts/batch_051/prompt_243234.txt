You're an expert in philosophy of science — please think through this carefully, and start by brainstorming the strongest version of structural realism you can before you answer.

Structural realism, as I understand it, is the view that across scientific revolutions what tends to get preserved is the mathematical structure — the equations and relations — rather than the entities, so we should be realists about the structure and not the stuff. Its two main arguments are the no-miracles argument (scientific success would be a miracle unless our theories are tracking real structure) and the pessimistic meta-induction (history shows entities get abandoned while structure survives, so be a realist only about structure).

Here's the development that's been worrying me. A growing share of the most impressive scientific success now comes from black-box machine learning models: deep networks that predict protein folding, weather, or material properties extremely well, but where no one can state what relational structure the model is actually encoding. Different architectures with different internal structures make basically the same predictions, and you can't extract a clean set of equations or relations from the trained network.

So my question is whether the structural realist's argument survives this, or whether the black-box case kills a premise it needs. The no-miracles argument seems to need some identifiable structure that the model is latching onto, and the pessimistic meta-induction seems to need structure to be the thing that survives theory change. If we can't say what the structure is, what exactly is left to be a realist about? Is there a repaired version — for instance, realism about the fact that many different internal structures converge on the same predictive regularities, or realism about real patterns in the world that the model tracks even when we can't state them — and would that repaired version still do the work structural realism was supposed to do? Or does this push us toward a kind of predictionism, and what would we lose if it did? I want to know whether the argument is dead, needs repair, or is fine, and exactly which premise the black-box development puts pressure on.
