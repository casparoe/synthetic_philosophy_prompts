I began volunteering with a mountain search-and-rescue unit earlier this year, and last week the planning group to which I was assigned faced a formal question I had previously encountered only as an abstract exercise. I still cannot resolve it.

Our search area is divided into four sectors by two boundaries: a river running east–west separates the northern half from the southern half, and a ridge running north–south separates the eastern half from the western half. Let N be the proposition that the missing person is north of the river, and E the proposition that he is east of the ridge. Before any new information, we had no reason to favor any sector, so our prior was uniform:

P(NE)=P(NW)=P(SE)=P(SW)=1/4.

We then received an authoritative conditional estimate from the air unit: if the missing person is north of the river, then the probability that he is east of the ridge is 3/4. Operationally, we are to adopt a posterior probability function satisfying P(E|N)=3/4.

One of my teammates now claims that, on textbook Bayesian principles, this message cannot move our probabilities at all. His argument:

(1) On the orthodox Bayesian view, the only way to take in information is to conditionalize on a proposition that expresses that information; if something cannot be represented as a proposition in the relevant algebra, it cannot count as evidence that changes our degrees of belief.

(2) The air unit's message expresses no proposition about the missing person's location. It does not say that he is in the north, or in the east, or in any particular sector. It only says how our probability for E should behave on the supposition that N.

(3) Conditionalizing on the fact that the message was sent would be a different move. That would treat the message as a noisy report whose likelihood must be modeled. But the estimate is authoritative for the purposes of the operation, and we have no likelihood model for the air unit's reliability.

(4) Therefore there is no proposition about the missing person's location on which to conditionalize, and orthodoxy licenses no other form of learning.

(5) So our posterior probabilities over the four sectors must remain exactly what they were: 1/4 each.

(6) But a uniform distribution yields P(E|N)=1/2, which contradicts the authoritative estimate.

(7) Thus, if we remain orthodox Bayesians, we should file the message away and deploy our search teams precisely as we would have without it.

I find (7) unacceptable. The message is plainly relevant to how we should allocate search effort, and I would rather keep the message than keep the claim that all learning is ordinary conditionalization.

After the meeting I tried to construct the missing update rule myself. The repair that seemed most natural to me was to change only what the message requires: keep the probability of the northern half unchanged at 1/2, impose P(E|N)=3/4, and leave the two southern sectors in their original proportions. This gives:

P(NE)=3/8, P(NW)=1/8, P(SE)=P(SW)=1/4.

But our operations analyst pointed out that a different rule looks just as principled: among all distributions satisfying the constraint, pick the one information-theoretically closest to the prior, in the sense of minimizing Kullback–Leibler divergence from the prior. That rule gives roughly

P(NE)=0.351, P(NW)=0.117, P(SE)=P(SW)=0.266,

and it lowers the probability of N below 1/2 even though the message told us only what holds conditional on N. I now worry that my repair is merely one arbitrary choice among several, which makes me doubt whether my teammate's argument goes wrong at one clean step or reveals a genuine gap in the standard framework.

Where does my teammate's argument go wrong, if it does? If I must reject one of its steps, which one should it be, and what does rejecting it commit me to? And is there a fact of the matter as to which of the two repairs, if either, is the correct way to update on a conditional probability?
