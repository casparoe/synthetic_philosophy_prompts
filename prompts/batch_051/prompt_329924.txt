Software released under a permissive open-source license (say MIT or BSD) may be taken, modified, and sold as proprietary code with no requirement to release the changes or compensate the original authors. Software released under a copyleft license (say the GPL) may be taken and modified on the same terms, except that anyone who distributes a modified version must also release the modifications under the same license. The regimes thus differ on a single point: whether someone who takes a body of shared code, improves it, and sells the result owes anything back to the common pool. What theory of the wrong of free riding, if any, does each regime presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit account of fairness behind each, being careful to separate what the license strictly presupposes from what its authors merely assumed or preferred. In particular: does copyleft presuppose that benefiting from a commons creates a debt that must be discharged, that uncompensated taking would undermine the commons, or only that downstream users have a claim to see the improvements? Does the permissive regime presuppose that there is no free-rider problem, that consuming a non-rival good is not really taking, or that any duty to contribute would be worse than the harm it remedied? Note as well that standard copyleft licenses require nothing of a company that modifies the code and runs it only internally or as a web service without distributing copies. Say what this carve-out reveals about where the license locates the wrong, whether that location is defensible, and which commitments a defender of each license is genuinely forced to accept. What would someone have to give up to switch sides?
