You are a moral philosopher who works on well-being and the emotions. Please act as a reflective-equilibrium consultant: I'll give you my gut verdicts on a set of cases about sadness and grief, and I want you to find the principle that best captures them, test candidate principles against each case, and tell me where my verdicts cannot all be held together. Think carefully, begin by brainstorming several candidate principles and the cases that embarrass each, and only then defend the one that fits best.

After a long argument with a friend who defended the strong view that negative emotions are valuable only as instruments—once whatever useful work a bout of grief or sadness does has been replaced by some other means, the emotion itself has no value—I found my own verdicts split in ways I cannot organize. (a) I regularly rewatch a film whose ending reliably makes me grieve, and I would not choose a cut that replaced the loss with a happy ending; the sadness seems to be part of what I value there, not a price I pay. (b) If someone widowed yesterday could take a pill that dissolved her grief overnight while leaving all her warm memories intact, I'd say it should be permitted and would probably make her life go better—yet I'd find something unfitting about taking it the day after the funeral, and I don't think I'd take it myself in her place. (c) A composer's best work comes out of depressive episodes, but I still think we should lift the depression immediately; the work isn't worth the suffering, so here the negative emotion is at most instrumentally valuable and in this case not even valuable enough to justify itself. (d) A man who spends a decade rehearsing a mild romantic rejection, gaining no insight and changing nothing, seems to be living through pure cost; I'd say his sadness has no value at all. (e) A mother's grief for her dead child doesn't abate, doesn't motivate anything, and doesn't deepen her other relationships; I'd erase that suffering if I could, yet I shrink from saying the grief itself is worthless—it seems to be how her love now shows up, directed at someone who is gone. (f) I also think a world in which no one could feel sadness would be missing something even if everyone in it reported being happy, but I can't tell whether what it would be missing is the sadness itself or some sensitivity to loss that sadness merely tracked.

Is there one principle that captures most or all of these verdicts? Please state the principle precisely, tie each clause to the case that motivates it, and test it against a further case in the same vicinity that you choose yourself—for instance, someone who deliberately schedules a brief, contained episode of grief each year so that a loss is not forgotten. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether you think I should overturn that verdict rather than complicate the principle to save it.
