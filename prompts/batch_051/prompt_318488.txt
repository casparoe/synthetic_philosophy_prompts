I've been trying to get clear on moral worth — not whether an action is right, but whether the person deserves moral credit for it. I've got a pile of gut verdicts and I want someone to either find the principle behind them or tell me which verdicts I should drop. Here they are.

Priya gives ten percent of her income to a malaria charity. She chose it by running cost-effectiveness numbers, and she says she doesn't feel much warmth toward the people helped; she just thinks it's the best thing to do with the money. I want to say her giving has moral worth, though it feels a little cold.

My cousin volunteers at a food bank weekly, but he admits it's mostly because it makes him feel good about himself and he likes the other volunteers. He says if it stopped feeling good he'd quit. I don't want to grant him the same credit as Priya, but he is doing something good.

Maya was raised to think that undocumented immigrants should always be reported. Her neighbor is undocumented. When an immigration agent comes asking, she lies and says she hasn't seen him. She doesn't do this because she's worked out that the policy is unjust; she does it because he's her friend and she can't bring herself to turn him in. She feels guilty afterwards, convinced she did wrong. I want to say this has a lot of moral worth, maybe more than Priya's donation, even though Maya believes she's doing the wrong thing.

A store clerk returns twenty dollars in extra change because the customer looks like someone who'd make a scene; she'd probably have kept it if the customer seemed like a sweet old lady who wouldn't notice. She did the right thing, but I want to say she gets no moral credit.

A man who is exhausted and grieving still visits his elderly mother in a care home every week because he believes he owes it to her, full stop. He gets no pleasure from it right now and finds it draining. I want to say his visits have moral worth.

A woman sees her husband about to step into the path of a bus. She pauses for a moment to ask herself whether she's permitted to grab him, because her first instinct is that pulling someone is an intrusion, then she pulls him back. She saved him, and she did it out of concern for his safety, but that little pause bothers me in a way I can't quite name. I'm unsure whether her act has moral worth, or whether the worth is there but something else is deficient.

My brother never lies on his taxes, but not because he thinks it through each year; it's just unthinkable to him. It's automatic. I'd like to give him moral credit for it, but it feels strange to say he acted for the right reasons when he didn't deliberate at all.

My working theory is something like: an act has moral worth just when the agent is responding to the features that actually make the act right, even if she can't articulate them. That handles Priya, Maya, the clerk, and the man visiting his mother. But it seems to break on my brother — he isn't exactly responding to features, it's habitual — and it doesn't say anything helpful about the bus-pause case. So maybe the principle needs repair, or maybe I have to sacrifice a verdict or two.

Is there a coherent account that gets all of these, or nearly all? State it precisely if you can, say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. Also, please seperate what's pretty settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. American English, please.
