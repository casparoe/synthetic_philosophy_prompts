On a forum I read, every time a deepfake story shows up in the news, the top comment is some version of "this is just Photoshop for video, calm down." The story that gets told is always the same: when photo editing got good, people said photographs would stop being evidence, but we adjusted — juries got more skeptical, newsrooms made rules, and now nobody treats a suspicious photo as decisive if there's video or documents or live witnesses to check it against. So the argument is that the panic about never being able to trust any video is overblown, because we'll do the same thing one medium over and the sky won't fall.

I've been trying to lay the analogy out and it keeps slipping. What maps onto what, exactly? Photoshop is to photos as deepfakes are to video, sure, but then the "we adjusted" part has to map over too — what plays the role of the skeptical juror, the newsroom policy, the forensic expert, the institutional adjustements? The part I can't get past is that video and audio have been the backstop you check edited photos against: when a photo looked off, you asked for the tape. If the backstop itself gets cheap to fake, what's the new backstop, if there is one? I want a full audit: the table of correspondences, the places the analogy breaks, which disanalogies actually matter for the "we'll be fine" conclusion and which are just nitpicks, and whether the analogy is doing the reassuring work or actually supporting it. If it's bad, what's a better analogy?
