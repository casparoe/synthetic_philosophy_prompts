I've been thinking through Ian Hacking's claim that some ways of classifying people are not neutral: the classification loops back into the lives of the people classified and changes them, which in turn changes what the classification picks out. People can take the label up as an identity, game the criteria, resist the label, or reshape the institutions that apply it. My working test for whether a category is an interactive kind, as opposed to an indifferent kind that simply sorts people into a pre-existing class, has four conditions. (1) The category is applied to individuals by institutions — clinics, workplaces, schools, benefits systems — not merely by theorists. (2) Being classified changes the subject's self-understanding: the term becomes a live identity or explanation. (3) That changed self-understanding changes conduct in ways specific to the label — symptoms get expressed, narrated, managed, or contested under its description. (4) The changed conduct feeds back and alters the category's own content, boundaries, typical presentation, or measured prevalence. A broken bone fails all four; the wave of multiple-personality diagnoses that Hacking made central to his account seems to satisfy them all.

Now I want to test these criteria on burnout. Burnout sits in an odd institutional position: the World Health Organization classifies it as an occupational phenomenon rather than a disease, yet it is handled with much of the machinery of a diagnosis — screening questionnaires, specialty clinics, coaches, memoirs, workplace accommodations, and a growing number of people who say "I am burned out" and reorganize their working lives around that description. At the same time, I cannot persuade myself that the underlying exhaustion is merely an artifact of the label. Something seems to happen in chronically overstressed workers — a depletion that shows up in their sleep, attention, and affect — whether or not the word is available, which is exactly what an indifferent-kind reading would predict.

Go through my four conditions one by one and say, for each, whether burnout satisfies it clearly, only partially, or not at all. Then commit: is burnout best understood as an interactive kind, an indifferent kind, or a case that shows my clean two-way distinction needs a third option? If you think the distinction needs revising, say exactly how, and make sure the revision does not collapse into the unhelpful claim that everything is a bit of both.
