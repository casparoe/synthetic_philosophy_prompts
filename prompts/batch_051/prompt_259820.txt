In the United States, a physician or researcher may not draw a single vial of blood from a competent adult without signed informed consent, and a trial that merely randomizes patients between two already-approved drugs must explain risks, alternatives, and the right to withdraw. Yet the same society legally requires that enriched grain products be fortified with folic acid, and most Americans drink fluoridated water, so that the entire population ingests substances selected for it by public health authorities without any individual consent, in order to produce small statistical benefits — benefits that, in the fortification case, accrue mainly to fetuses at risk of neural tube defects rather than to most of the people exposed. Assume for the sake of argument that each policy is reasonable on the merits; the question is what view of consent and risk could make them jointly reasonable.

Your task is to reconstruct the implicit theory — or theories — under which this pair of practices makes sense. Do the following:

(1) State, as precisely as you can, the principle of legitimate risk imposition that the public-health side appears to presuppose, and the principle that the research-consent side appears to presuppose, and say whether they are consistent.

(2) Distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with. For example, do the practices require a principle about statistical versus identifiable victims, about whether the benefits of a risk flow back to those who bear it, about the difference between intervening on a person and altering a shared environment, about the absolute size of the risk, or about the availability of exit — or are these merely features that happen to correlate with the public-health/research dividing line without doing the normative work?

(3) Identify at least one place where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: a point where several rival theories of consent and legitimate risk could each license both the strict research-consent rule and the fortification policy.

(4) Finally, say what would have to change — in clinical-research regulation, in public-health practice, or in the way the two are justified — if the operative principle were replaced by (a) a strict view that no non-consensual risk may ever be imposed on a person, and (b) a straightforward ex ante expected-benefit view that permits imposing a risk whenever the expected benefit, summed across all affected persons, exceeds the expected harm.

Please answer in American English.
