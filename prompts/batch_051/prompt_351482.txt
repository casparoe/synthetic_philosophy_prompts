I've been trying to figure out what I actually believe about moral realism, and my gut reactions on cases keep pulling in different directions. I dont want a label so much as the underlying view: is there a single position about what moral judgments are and whether they can be true or false that fits all or most of these, and where exactly does it fail? I'd like you to reconstruct the best candidate position (or two or three candidates), state it precisely, say which of my verdicts each one can't accommodate, and tell me which of my verdicts I should probably give up and why. Also tell me what your favored position says about a case I haven't listed, below.

Here are the cases.

1. Roman slavery. I think the claim "slavery was wrong even in Rome, before modern human-rights ideas existed" is true outright, not just true-by-our-lights. It feels like a discovery, like we found out slavery was wrong rather than decided to treat it as wrong.

2. The factory-farming argument. A friend and I have spent years hashing out whether eating factory-farmed meat is okay. We know all the same facts about animal suffering, climate, nutrition, everything. Neither of us is making a logical mistake. I still think he's wrong; but I also don't think there's any further fact he's missing. If you press me, there's no joint in nature that settles it.

3. Debunking by evolution. Learning how disgust and loyalty got wired in by evolution made me trust my disgust-based moral judgments less (like my revulsion at harmless-but-weird sexual practices). But it didn't make me think "torturing a child for fun is wrong" is just a local preference. The first feels debunked, the second doesn't.

4. The psychopath. Someone with no empathy says he sees no reason not to hurt people when he can get away with it. I don't think he's making a factual error like someone who says 2+2=5. Something is missing in him, but it doesn't feel like he's asserting a false proposition.

5. Depression. When I've been depressed and thought "nothing matters," it did not feel like I'd discovered evidence that all moral claims are false. It felt like the moral part of my brain switched off. That's why error theory always struck me as wrong: if all moral claims are false, depression should be a route to seeing that, and it isn't.

6. The aliens. If we met aliens whose psychology made them sincerely say that causing pain is morally good, and they were otherwise rational and informed, I don't think I'd say they're wrong the way a flat-earther is wrong. I'd probably say our moral systems just don't cover each other. But then I think about case 1 and feel like I've contradicted myself.

My working theory, to the extent I have one, is that there's a small core of harm-based claims that are facts true regardless of anyone's attitudes, and everything else is expressive or relative; but that feels ad hoc, and I want it either repaired or replaced.

What's the least-self-deceived way to hold these together? And what would that view say about a superintelligent AI with no evolved emotions that concludes torturing humans is morally neutral — is that a blind spot, an error, or just something outside our moral community?
