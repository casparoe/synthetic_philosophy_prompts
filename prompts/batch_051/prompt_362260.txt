I teach a graduate seminar on causal reasoning in the social sciences, and last week I gave my students what I admitted was a rough-and-ready definition of what it means to say an intervention helps. The criteria were: (1) participants do better overall than comparable non-participants; (2) the advantage survives when you look within the groups that should matter, so the program isn't merely attracting easy cases; and (3) there's at least a plausible story about how the program's activities could produce the improvement. Yesterday a student brought in numbers from an optional after-school tutoring program in her old district that seem to break the definition. Overall, tutored students passed the state math exam at about 81% versus about 54% for non-tutored students. But the program was not randomly assigned—kids opted in. When she looked seperately at students who had failed the same exam the previous year, tutored students passed at 40% versus 50% for non-tutored; among students who had passed the previous year, tutored students passed at 85% versus 90% for non-tutored. So within each prior-achievement group the tutored kids did slightly worse, while overall they did vastly better, because the tutored group was mostly prior passers and the non-tutored group was mostly students who had failed before.

Walk through my three criteria one at a time and say whether the tutoring program qualifies as one that helps its students. Where the criteria pull in opposite directions, say which one you trust more and why, and whether the case shows my definition needs to be rebuilt—perhaps by making "help" relative to a comparison class or a set of held-fixed characteristics. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question, in American English.
