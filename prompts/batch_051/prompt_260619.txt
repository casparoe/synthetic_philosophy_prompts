Suppose a mid-sized common-law country (call it Mercia) amends its constitution tomorrow so that sovereign immunity simply doesn't exist. The state — and every official acting in the course of duty — can now be sued in ordinary courts for any wrong, under the exact same doctrines that apply to private defendants: no crown immunity, no discretionary-function or "policy act" exemptions, no special defences, no damages caps, and judgments paid straight out of the general treasury. Officials also stay personally liable even for good-faith errors, though the state must indemnify them. Predict how Mercia is governed forty years later. I want the mechanical chain, not a verdict on whether it's a good idea. What happens to administrative decision-making once every refused permit, botched inspection, and delayed benefit payment is a potential suit? Does the bureaucracy become paralysed, or careful, or something more like an insurer? Which effects are robust, which are guesses, and what assumptions does the whole prediction hang on? And the underlying question: does the state become the kind of thing that can do wrong in a way it couldn't before, or does it just learn to price wrongdoing and carry on?
