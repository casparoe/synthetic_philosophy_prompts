I am a retired evolutionary biologist, and I have lately been reading the philosophy of biology. The adaptationism debate that grew out of Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique has unsettled something I used to take for granted: that I knew exactly what I meant when I called a trait an adaptation. I am not looking for a survey of positions; I want a working definition I can actually use, and I find the most honest way to test one is to run it against cases about which I already have firm intuitions and cases where I do not.

I want a definition that counts a bird's wing and adult lactase persistence in populations with a long history of dairying as adaptations, and that does not count the red color of blood or male nipples. It should also return a definite, reasoned verdict for each of the following: the giant panda's false thumb (the enlarged wrist bone it uses to strip bamboo), the human chin, the vermiform appendix, and the descended human larynx. My own inclination has been to define adaptation historically, as a trait whose origin was shaped by natural selection for the function it now serves. That gets the clear cases right, but it pushes the panda's false thumb into the category of exaptation rather than adaptation, and I cannot decide whether that is the correct result or a sign that the definition is too narrow. Could you propose a definition, apply it to all eight cases, say where it yields an answer you did not expect or no answer at all, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts?
