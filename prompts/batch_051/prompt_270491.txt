You are a philosopher of language specializing in formal pragmatics, conversational dynamics, and the logic of presupposition. I want you to work through a difficult case rather than summarize the literature.

In David Lewis's 1979 paper Scorekeeping in a Language Game, he defends an account of presupposition in terms of conversational score. The passage I have in mind is the one where he introduces his rule of accommodation. As I understand it, the picture is this: a conversation is governed by something like a scoreboard with components such as presupposed propositions, standards of precision, and salient possibilities. When someone says something that requires a component of the score to have a certain value, and the required value is not already there, the score tends to shift—within limits—so that the utterance comes out acceptable. For presupposition, the rule predicts that if a speaker says something that presupposes a proposition p, and p is not yet in the common ground, the score changes so that p is added, provided the addition is not too obtrusive and no one objects. That is how a speaker can introduce new presuppositions without first asking permission.

Now vary the setting. Suppose the conversational partner is not a human agent with beliefs and communicative intentions, but a large language model trained to predict likely next tokens from a vast corpus of human text. Such a system can produce fluent, context-sensitive responses, and in practice it seems to follow something like the rule of accommodation: if a user writes, Why did John stop beating his dog, the model often responds without challenging the presupposition that John has been beating his dog, and in longer exchanges it may continue to treat that content as given. But the LLM is not, in the ordinary sense, keeping a public scoreboard of accepted presuppositions. It has a context window and a learned distribution over continuations, and its score is at best an emergent or ersatz feature of that distribution.

I want to know whether Lewis's argument for the scorekeeping model survives this development, and in what form. Specifically: which premise in Lewis's argument is actually under pressure? Is it the premise that explaining accommodation requires something with the functional role of a scoreboard? Is it the premise that conversational participants must represent the score, or that they must mutually accept its contents? Or is the LLM just a new implementation of the same abstract scorekeeping architecture, with the score realized statistically? Work through what a defender of Lewis should say, what a critic should say, and whether there is a repaired version that concedes something important but is still worth defending. I am interested in the argument itself, not in whether LLMs have beliefs or consciousness.

Please begin by restating the rule of accommodation in your own words and isolating the load-bearing claim in Lewis's case, then evaluate the challenge step by step. Be precise about which parts of the original view survive unchanged and which parts would need to be weakened or redescribed.
