Suppose a state abolishes civil jury trials for all consumer and employment disputes and makes arbitration mandatory, governed by the following rules. Every licensed arbitration firm must publish each award together with a short written rationale in a public, searchable repository. A regulatory agency assigns each firm an annual "deviation score," computed by comparing the firm's awards against the median award for cases in the same subject-matter and amount-in-controversy bucket across all firms; firms in the top decile of deviation are barred from accepting new cases for one year, on the theory that persistent outliers are either biased or incompetent. To blunt repeat-player dynamics, no firm may take more than five percent of its annual caseload from any single corporate client, and each individual claimant is assigned five firms by lottery and may strike one. Costs are asymmetric: a losing individual owes nothing to the company, while a losing company must pay the individual's filing costs plus a fixed statutory penalty. Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a proposal to endorse or reject. What would it incentivize for arbitration firms, defendant companies, and individual claimants? Trace how the scheme would be gamed during its first five years and what the equilibrium would look like once participants adapt: would it converge on median justice, tacit collusion, the defense of mediocre awards, or something else? Distinguish the failure modes that could be fixed by tuning parameters such as the bar threshold, the caseload cap, or the penalty size from the failure modes that are structural to the design.
