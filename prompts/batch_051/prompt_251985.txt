I've been reading a moral philosopher's blog for years, and I've been collecting her verdicts on the wrongness of painlessly ending a life — painless in the strong sense that pain, fear, anticipatory dread, and grief to others are all stipulated away. Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it: painlessly killing a healthy adult dog because you no longer want to care for it is wrong; painlessly killing a pig or a cow for food when plant alternatives are available is wrong; painlessly killing a salmon for food is permissible; painlessly killing an oyster for food is permissible; painlessly killing a human being with profound, irreversible cognitive disability whose mental life is roughly that of a pig is wrong.

I've been trying to reverse-engineer the principle behind these calls, and two live options seem to fit, though I may be missing a third. On the first, moral status tracks an individual's actual psychological capacities — a sufficiently rich self, memory, and future-directed desires — regardless of species. On the second, human beings get full status in virtue of species membership, while nonhumans get status graded by their individual capacities. Both fit the record so far because every human she has commented on has had capacities above the relevant threshold.

The case I actually want to predict is a human being whose brain works, in the relevant psychological respects, like a salmon's: no self-awareness, no memory of a personal past, no plans or future-directed desires, but an intact capacity to feel pain. The killing would be painless, and no one depends on them. What would she say, and how wrong would she take the killing to be compared with killing a salmon and with killing a cognitively normal human? I'm less interested in the bare call than in the rule that produces it. Which principles best fit her track record, what does each predict for this case, and which do you think she is actually running? What further case would most cleanly separate the options that remain live after that one? Tell me as well what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would make you switch your prediction, and where this kind of extrapolation from a track record is most likely to fail. Finally, if the rule you end up ascribing to her lets the category 'human' do work that 'has these capacities' alone would not, say whether that amounts to speciesism or is defensible.
