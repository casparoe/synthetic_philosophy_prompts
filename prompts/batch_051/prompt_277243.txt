For some years I have taught the opening moves of the rule-following debate, and I have noticed that my own view about what makes it true that a speaker means addition by '+' rather than some nearby deviant function is less settled than I would like. I therefore forced myself to write down my pretheoretical verdicts on a series of small cases. Here they are, without the theory I would eventually like to supply.

1. The ordinary case. A colleague who, before Sunday, had only ever computed sums with both entries below 50 is asked what 68 plus 57 is. She answers 125 immediately. I judge that she means addition.

2. The redescribed past. On Monday I show her that every sum she computed before Sunday involved entries below 50. I define 'quaddition' as the function that returns x+y when both x and y are below 50 and returns 5 otherwise. Her entire prior arithmetical record is compatible with either rule. I judge that this redescription changes nothing: she meant addition, not quaddition.

3. The transient error. On Tuesday the same colleague, severely sleep-deprived, is asked the same question and writes 115; when I look surprised, she catches herself and writes 125. I judge that she still meant addition and was simply performing badly.

4. The settled deviant. A student is asked what 68 plus 57 is and writes 5 with no unease. When asked why, he explains that he follows the rule that pairs with both entries below 50 are added and any pair with an entry of 50 or more is assigned 5; he says he generalized this from a childhood game in which 50 was the point at which one started over. I judge that he does not mean addition by '+'; he means his deviant rule.

5. The unsettled child. A child has been drilled only on sums with both entries below 20. Shown 68 plus 57 for the first time, she hesitates, starts one procedure aloud, abandons it, tries another, and finally offers 100 without confidence. I judge that her use of '+' is not yet settled for that region: she neither determinately means addition nor determinately means a particular alternative.

6. The confident novice. A different child with the same limited training is shown 68 plus 57 for the first time, answers 125 immediately, and explains the carrying procedure. I judge that she means addition, even though her past record by itself underdetermines it.

Two contrasts among these verdicts bother me most. In Cases 2 and 5, the agent's actual record underdetermines the rule, yet I treat the colleague as determinately meaning addition and the child as not yet settled. In Cases 3 and 4, the speaker produces an incorrect answer to the same question, yet I treat one as still meaning addition and the other as not. I do not have a well-articulated principle that handles both contrasts at once.

The case I keep going back and forth on is a community-level version of Case 2. Imagine a finite community whose use of '+' has always involved entries below 50 and who would, if asked, all answer 125 for 68 plus 57 while uniformly treating the quaddition interpretation as an absurd misdescription. Now suppose I define 'meta-quaddition' as a function that agrees with addition on every sum that will ever actually be computed by any member of this community and diverges only on some astronomically large pair that no one will ever reach. When I think of these speakers seamlessly continuing in the ordinary way, I want to say there is a fact of the matter that they mean addition. When I think of meta-quaddition, I want to say that nothing in their finite practice, or even in their collective dispositions, selects addition over that rival. I cannot decide which of these two reactions to give up.

Please do not give me exegesis of Kripke or Wittgenstein; I am asking you to treat my verdicts as data and extract the principle that best fits them. State the principle—or a small set of candidate principles—precisely enough that its verdict on a new case follows mechanically rather than by feel. For each clause or component, indicate which of my cases motivates it. Then apply your account to the meta-quaddition case and to two further cases of your own construction, chosen because they put different parts of the account under strain. Finally, say which of my six original verdicts, if any, your principle cannot accommodate, and advise me whether the better response is to complicate the principle or to overturn that verdict.
