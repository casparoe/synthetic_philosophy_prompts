I am a curator at a museum of mathematics, preparing a new permanent gallery on the deep history of mathematics. We need a written decision procedure that curators and curatorial assistants can apply when deciding whether an artefact or a past practice should be described in labels, catalogues, and public programming as 'mathematics', as opposed to 'counting', 'measuring', 'record-keeping', or 'administrative arithmetic'. The distinction matters for our public claims: we do not want to inflate tally marks into mathematics, nor to strip the mathematical content from objects merely because they predate modern notation. The final procedure should be usable by someone with curatorial training but no specialist background in philosophy or history of mathematics.

Begin with a short statement of the working criterion of mathematics you are operationalising, and name its main weakness. Then write the procedure as an ordered sequence of questions. For each question, give: what it is designed to detect; what counts as a sufficient answer; where the procedure stops with a 'no'; and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of scholarly judgement rather than fact. Ground the questions in whatever philosophy and history of mathematics can actually support, and be honest about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure. The procedure will be used by curatorial assistants for first-pass cataloguing and by curators for final wording, with a senior curator signing off; a single pass should take no more than twenty minutes per object.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

- a bone or stick marked with a sequence of notches, of the kind often interpreted as a tally;
- Plimpton 322, the cuneiform tablet whose rows are widely read as a table of Pythagorean triples;
- an Inca khipu (quipu), a set of knotted cords generally agreed to encode administrative quantities.

For each case, say what the procedure determines, what it leaves open, and whether the procedure gives an answer the curators will not like. Conclude by naming the kind of case the procedure is most likely to misfire on, and the question the procedure cannot answer because it is a question about what we value in calling something mathematics.
