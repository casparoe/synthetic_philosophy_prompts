Some philosophers hold that certain experiences are transformative in a specifically epistemic sense: before undergoing one, a person cannot know what it will be like, how she will evaluate it, or what preferences and values she will hold afterward, because the relevant information is first-personally inaccessible—testimony, statistics, and imagination all fall short. The claim is routinely used to draw normative conclusions about the rationality of choices such as becoming a parent, emigrating, or converting. But the epistemic claim itself is seldom tested, and it is easy to suspect that it is just an armchair postulate dressed up as psychology.

Suppose you were asked to bring evidence to bear on it. Choose one canonical transformative experience and design a concrete empirical test of the claimed inaccessibility. Specify how you would operationalize the gap (for example, through prospective versus retrospective utility forecasts, confidence calibration, willingness to act on surrogate reports, or the stability of preferences across the experience), and say which pattern of results would support the transformative-experience hypothesis rather than the deflationary view that these are merely unusually difficult predictions. Then state what a positive result would rule out and what it would leave untouched: in particular, whether any such result could establish not merely that people are unreliable predictors, but that the experience is inaccessible in the sense required for the normative conclusion about rational choice—and where the residue should be located if no test can reach it.
