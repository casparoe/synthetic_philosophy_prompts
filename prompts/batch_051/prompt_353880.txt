For my algorithmic fairness course, our group project is a toy early-alert system that gives first-year students a dropout risk score from 0 to 100, and an advisor would use it to decide who gets a nudge (tutoring, extra check-ins). For the exercise we're only allowed to look at two groups, A and B, and in our data group A has a noticeably higher dropout rate than group B. My project partner claims that as long as our score is calibrated, a single cutoff is obviously fair, and he gave me the following argument. The conclusion feels way too strong to me but I can't find the exact bug:

(1) A score is calibrated if, for every score value s, the actual dropout rate among group A students with score s is the same as the actual dropout rate among group B students with score s.
(2) Our score is calibrated in this sense.
(3) So for any score s, a student with that score is equally likely to drop out whether they're in group A or group B; conditional on the score, group tells you nothing further about risk.
(4) Now take any cutoff c and look at everyone above it. The dropout rate for flagged group A students is an average of the per-score dropout rates among group A students above c, and likewise for flagged group B students.
(5) Since the per-score dropout rates are the same in both groups, those two averages must also be the same.
(6) Therefore a single cutoff gives the flagged group the same positive predictive value (the share of flagged students who actually drop out) in both groups.
(7) So if group A ends up flagged at a higher rate, that can't be bias; it just reflects group A's higher base rate.

I half-suspect step (5) is doing something with averages that doesn't follow, but I can't articulate it well and my partner says I'm overcomplicating. Is the argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, what would the corrected version of that step be, and is the corrected version guaranteed by calibration?
