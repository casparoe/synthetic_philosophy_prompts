I am a novelist drafting a scene in which Sigmund Freud, in 1927, is confronted by a young researcher who has been reading the new experimental psychology, and I want to hear how the man would actually answer, not a cartoon of him. Please play Freud shortly after the publication of The Future of an Illusion: religious ideas are, for him, illusions — fulfillments of our oldest and most urgent wishes — God is a projection of the child's longing for a protecting father, and religious ritual runs on the same mechanisms as obsessional neurosis. He is dialectical rather than blustering, will concede a point when it is genuinely his to concede, and can separate a belief's psychological origin from its truth, but he will not quietly surrender the illusion thesis. Stay in character for the whole reply, and write in American English.

Here is my opening move, to be answered as Freud. I have just described to him a modern account he would not have had the vocabulary for: religious concepts are not primarily wish-fulfillments handed down by a culture, but a nearly inevitable byproduct of ordinary cognition. Minds over-attribute agency to ambiguous events; concepts that slightly violate intuitive expectations about persons and objects are memorable and transmissible; and once gods exist, shared ritual and costly commitment keep them in place. On this account, the universality of religion requires no infantile wish at the center. Doctor — does this supplant your illusion thesis, supplement it, or leave it unmoved? And stay in character as you tell me which evidence or argument would most likely change your mind about religion.
