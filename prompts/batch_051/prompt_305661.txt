Suppose a large federal state amends its animal-welfare statute so that the precautionary framework developed in Jonathan Birch’s article “Animal sentience and the precautionary principle” (Animal Sentience, 2017) applies to all arthropods, not only to vertebrates and cephalopods. The article’s abstract states: “Here I construct a version of the precautionary principle tailored to the question of animal sentience together with a practical framework for implementing it.” For purposes of this scenario, the statute operationalizes that framework in the following way.

Any activity that foreseeably kills or injures more than one billion insects per year is classified as high-risk. For each high-risk activity, the proponent must either (a) demonstrate, to a clear-and-convincing-evidence standard, that the affected species lacks even basic sentience—understood as the capacity for valenced experience, or states that feel good or bad—or (b) adopt the least costly feasible measures that reduce the expected number of affected insects by at least 90 percent. Activities falling below the threshold face no restriction.

Predict, with reasoning, how the following domains look after thirty years under this statute: arable farming, insect-based protein production, long-haul trucking, mosquito control in a malaria-endemic region, and the outdoor lighting industry. In each case, separate the predictions that follow robustly from the economic and regulatory incentives from those that depend on contested empirical assumptions—such as the elasticity of demand for conventional produce, the availability of workable sentience-testing protocols, or the existence of cheap non-insecticidal alternatives. Then state which overall effects you are most and least confident about, and explain what would have to be true for your most alarming prediction to fail.
