Act as a professional philosopher who works on ubuntu and relational theories of personhood, and who is used to stress-testing definitions against hard cases. I want you to think carefully and avoid the standard lecture-y summary; instead work through the case slowly. Start by brainstorming two or three initial reactions to the case, including the one that feels most obvious, then set aside any reaction you can't actually defend.

Here's my working definition of personhood, pieced together from ubuntu-flavored views. Personhood is (1) something you become rather than simply are from birth; (2) something you attain through relationships of mutual recognition, care, and participation in a community; (3) a matter of degree, so someone can be more or less of a person at different stages of life; (4) tied to normative standing, so more personhood means you're owed more respect and your acts are more open to praise and blame. Crucially, I'm adding (5): the relationships have to be reciprocal in both directions. It's not enough that other people care about you or keep your name alive; you have to recognize others as persons and be recognized back.

Now the case. Consider an elderly woman, Naledi, who has advanced dementia. She no longer recognizes her children, doesn't orient to her own name, and cannot carry on any kind of exchange. Her daughter still speaks to her every day, washes her, sings at her bedside, and insists the community elders visit her and greet her by name. Neighbors bring food to the household and talk as if Naledi still has a place among them, with a history and a name that can be honored or shamed. But Naledi herself doesn't reciprocate anything; there is no recognition, response, or participation coming from her side. Does Naledi count as a person under the five criteria? Go criterion by criterion and mark the genuinely underdetermined ones. Where she fails a criterion, say whether that is because the criterion is too demanding, or because Naledi has in fact lost some personhood. In particular, do the family's and neighbors' acts of recognition count as a relation she is still "in," or is that just them curating the memory of a person? Does this case show that criterion (5) or (2) needs a dependent/cared-for dimension, or should we just bite the bullet and say a human being can cease to be a person while still being owed humane treatment?
