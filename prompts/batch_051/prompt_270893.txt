Last night I had a long and partly frustrating argument with a friend about whether the everyday risks we take when we drive are actually justified. He has been reading a popular book on risk and kept returning to a framework he insisted settled the matter. If I am remembering correctly, his reconstruction of the book’s argument went roughly as follows.

A micromort is a one-in-a-million chance of dying, and it supplies a single scale on which very different dangers can be compared. A scuba dive carries roughly 5 micromorts, a skydive roughly 8, and a general anaesthetic roughly 10, while driving 250 miles carries only 1, so routine driving looks vastly safer than the recreational risks people worry about. The book’s twist is accumulation: because people drive constantly, the average person’s lifetime chance of dying in a car crash is about one in a hundred, or 10,000 micromorts. If a statistical life is worth about ten million dollars, then each micromort is worth about ten dollars, and each mile of ordinary driving carries a mortality cost of a few cents. The book concludes that we massively discount the risk of driving: flying is safer than driving per mile, which means the most dangerous part of any plane trip is the drive to the airport, and people who fear flying while driving every day are displaying a kind of risk illiteracy. For most people, the book says, the risks of habitual driving are not worth the benefits.

I pushed back during the argument on a couple of these steps — in particular, the slide from flying being safer per mile to the claim that the drive to the airport is the dangerous part — but I could not say exactly what was bothering me, and my friend was extremely confident. I would be grateful for a careful assessment of how fair his summary is. Could you go through it part by part and separate what a specialist in risk analysis would accept, what is a simplification, what is overstated or mistaken, and where the book’s position might still be defensible even if my friend’s version of it is not; and then tell me whether the final verdict about routine driving being not worth it follows from the parts that remain once the errors are corrected? I care less about checking the precise numerical values than about knowing where the conceptual weak points are.
