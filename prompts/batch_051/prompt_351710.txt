I chair the Digital Collections and Preservation Committee of a mid-sized university library. The committee must rule on a request from our software archivist, and we are genuinely split. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting.

The request concerns Aquifer3, a groundwater-flow simulation package published in 1994 by Meridian Scientific Computing, a two-person firm. The firm dissolved in 2003, and its corporate records list no successor or assignment of assets. The lead developer, who wrote nearly all of the code, died in 2009; his will did not mention the software, and the probate estate has been closed. The other co-founder, who handled sales and documentation, has not been located after eighteen months of documented attempts by library staff, and the firm's registered agent is defunct. The work will remain in copyright for decades, but the rights holder is currently unknown and apparently unlocatable.

Aquifer3 was widely used in hydrology before 2000. It reads and writes a proprietary format, .aq3, whose specification was never published. A substantial body of public-interest studies and state water-management assessments from the 1990s report results generated with Aquifer3, and current researchers cannot open the underlying files. The archivist holds a disk image that is beginning to degrade; running the software on modern hardware also requires bypassing a copy-protection dongle or patching the executable. He asks the committee to authorize three things: making preservation copies, creating an emulated version, and providing authenticated access to the package—along with a documented parser for the .aq3 format that he would reverse-engineer—to campus researchers for reproducibility work.

Current policy permits preservation copying of works the library already holds, but it allows access to copyrighted works or the bypass of access controls only with documented permission from the rights holder or a favorable written fair-use determination from university counsel. Counsel has declined to make a fair-use determination here, calling the case novel. The archivist argues that this is a textbook orphan-works case: the owner is unlocatable after diligent search, the work is commercially unavailable and irreplaceable, and the library's preservation mission should not be hostage to an unknowable owner who has shown no interest in exploiting the software. The general counsel and several committee members reply that orphaned is not the same as abandoned: copyright passes to heirs whether or not we can identify them, non-exploitation is itself an owner's prerogative, and institutional infringement would undermine the rights system on which the library itself depends. Another member adds that treating unlocatable owners as forfeiting control by default would invite a slide into regarding anything out of print as free for the taking.

Please write the opinion as a decision memo from the committee. It should recommend a concrete course of action—approve, reject, or approve with conditions—with a specific timeline and any access restrictions you would impose. It should state the principle the library would be applying, including an account of what ownership amounts to once the owner has vanished and no successor has come forward, and it should engage seriously and specifically with the strongest consideration on the losing side. Do not treat the matter as already settled by existing law; the committee needs a reasoned institutional justification that both the losing side and university counsel can respond to.
