Below is a passage from the Introduction to Thomas Hobbes's Leviathan (1651), given in Hobbes's original spelling:

"Nature (the art whereby God hath made and governes the world) is by the art of man, as in many other things, so in this also imitated, that it can make an Artificial Animal. For seeing life is but a motion of Limbs, the begining whereof is in some principall part within; why may we not say, that all Automata (Engines that move themselves by springs and wheeles as doth a watch) have an artificiall life?"

Immediately after this, Hobbes applies the analogy to the commonwealth, treating sovereignty as the soul, the magistrates as joints, and reward and punishment as nerves. I want you to treat the analogy not as ornament but as a proposed institutional blueprint. Suppose a constitutional convention took it literally and set out to design a state according to the principle that the body politic is a self-moving engine whose parts must be laid out and connected as Hobbes describes. Walk through what this design principle would imply for the definition of offices, the flow of authority, the treatment of dissent, and the conditions under which the whole remains in motion. Then diagnose where the blueprint fails as a description of actual political life, and separate those failures that are merely limitations of the metaphor from those that are doing real work in Hobbes's argument for undivided sovereignty.

At the end, say what would change your assessment: what fact about actual commonwealths, or what objection to the priority of sovereignty, would most lead you to conclude that the machine analogy is illuminating rather than misleading.
