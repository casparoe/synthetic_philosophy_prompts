I am a novelist, and while sketching a character who refuses to open letters that might challenge something she is certain of, I kept returning to an argument I cannot fault, though its conclusion unsettles me. I half-suspect a trick, but I cannot locate it.

(1) If I know that p, then p is true, and any evidence against p points away from the truth.
(2) Evidence that points away from the truth is misleading evidence.
(3) If I know that a piece of evidence is misleading, I am rationally permitted to set it aside without examining it.
(4) I know many ordinary facts; for instance, I know that my brother is at home, since I spoke with him on the house phone an hour ago and recognized his voice.
(5) So if a neighbour tells me she has evidence that my brother is not at home, I am rationally permitted to set that evidence aside unexamined; and the same principle extends to any evidence against anything I know.

Is the argument valid as stated? If not, exactly which step fails, and under what reading of its terms? If it is valid, say what the conclusion does and does not license. Answer as exactly five numbered items, one assessing each step, each item a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
