Longtime reader of the EA and rationalist blogs here. In a recent argument about how to prioritize pandemic interventions, a commenter dismissed a cost-effectiveness model, and the broadly utilitarian reasoning behind it, by saying it 'ignores the separateness of persons.' Their substantive charge was that the model summed well-being losses across individuals, treating the planner's ledger as if a hundred people each losing one unit were morally equivalent to one person losing a hundred units. I can feel the slogan doing real argumentative work, but I cannot tell which precise claim it is meant to assert, and I suspect it shelters several different arguments under one phrase. Please take 'the separateness of persons' and lay out the distinct theses it could be used to assert in this context — for example, claims about where value ultimately resides, about the moral difference between intrapersonal and interpersonal trade-offs, about whether one person's gain may offset another's loss only with the latter's consent, and about whether the objection targets the criterion of rightness or merely the decision procedure. For each candidate reading, tell me whether it is trivially true but inert, substantive and plausible, or dubious, and whether it actually supports the conclusion that aggregative cost-benefit reasoning in policy is illegitimate. I want a menu of readings with verdicts, not one canonical interpretation.
