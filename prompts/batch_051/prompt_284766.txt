In my former life as a natural scientist, I regarded a proposed model as adequate only if it could be made to satisfy an explicit list of constraints—conservation laws, boundary conditions, consistency with the measured data—and only if one could say which constraints were physical necessities and which were merely conventions of a particular modeling tradition. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy, and I find myself wanting to apply that same discipline to a puzzle I first met as a stock example in an anthology: an ass stands midway between two indistinguishable piles of hay and, having no more reason to go left than right, supposedly starves.

I want a specification, not a survey. Write the desiderata that any account of choice without preference must meet to count as adequate—adequate, that is, for making sense of what an ordinary finite agent does when the alternatives are genuinely tied. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable because the phenomenon itself forces them on us, and which are merely the preferences of a particular philosophical camp. If some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, or can be jointly satisfied only by giving up something else we thought we wanted, say so plainly; that is the part I most want to see.

Among the demands you will need to sort through are these: the account must not simply deny that the ordinary case—picking one of two identical cans from a shelf—is possible; it must distinguish a tie in value from mere ignorance of a hidden asymmetry; it must say whether the selection is arbitrary, random, or something else, and whether that difference matters; it must not resolve the puzzle by smuggling in a preference under another name, including a preference for rules or for not deliberating further; it must explain whether and why rationality permits, requires, or falls silent about picking; it must say what the agent contributes, if anything, when a symmetry is broken; and it must respect what decision theory can and cannot say about equal options rather than dismissing the puzzle as an artifact of bad formalism.

The question I actually want answered is whether the constraints themselves are coherent. If you conclude that some widely demanded requirement must be dropped or softened, say which one an honest theory would drop first—and state what argument or consideration would change your mind about that judgment, or where your reasoning here is most likely to fail. Please keep any scoring of existing views against the list brief; the deliverable is the list itself and its internal tensions, not a report card on the literature.
