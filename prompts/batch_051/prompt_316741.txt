I am a high school student writing a paper for a philosophy-and-economics competition, and I would rather work through a precisely specified counterfactual than summarize the standard replies. The topic is the St. Petersburg paradox: the gamble whose payout doubles with each successive coin flip, so that its expected monetary value is infinite, yet actual people will not pay more than a small sum to play. The standard resolution is that the marginal utility of money declines with wealth, making the expected utility of the gamble modest even though its expected money value is not.

Now remove that resolution. Stipulate that, beginning tomorrow, every adult in one large, wealthy open economy—a country with a mature private insurance market, a state lottery, and a developed financial sector—experiences money with constant marginal utility: each additional dollar of wealth yields exactly as much additional satisfaction as the previous one, and people are aware of the change. Everything else about their psychology is left in place, including loss aversion around a reference point, overconfidence, and shortsightedness about the distant future. Preferences for non-monetary goods do not change, and the rest of the world is unaffected.

I want a reasoned prediction of the next twenty years in that country, with the mechanisms made explicit, for five areas: (a) private insurance, including straightforward auto collision coverage; (b) casinos and the state lottery; (c) household saving and the division between safe and risky assets; (d) government cost-benefit treatment of small-probability catastrophic risks such as pandemic preparedness or nuclear-waste disposal; and (e) the maximum price an ordinary person would be willing to pay to enter the literal St. Petersburg game. Do not just give bottom lines: for each area, say which effects follow from the change in marginal utility alone, which follow from the unchanged biases, which predictions you are most confident about, and which would fail if institutions adapted faster than you expect.
