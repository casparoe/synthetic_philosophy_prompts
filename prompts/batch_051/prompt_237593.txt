I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time, and usually I can follow the standard decision-theory arguments without much trouble, but a disagreement at a local meetup last week has left me genuinely stuck. I'm hoping you'll referee it.

The setup was the Ellsberg two-urn problem. Urn A contains exactly 50 red and 50 black balls. Urn B contains 100 balls, each red or black, but in an unknown proportion. A ticket costs one dollar and pays two dollars if the chosen color is drawn, and zero otherwise.

Jonah took the position that the classic Ellsberg choices — preferring the red ticket on urn A to the red ticket on urn B, and also preferring the black ticket on urn A to the black ticket on urn B — are simply incoherent. His argument was this: if you strictly prefer the known red ticket, you are revealing a probability of red in urn B below one-half; if you strictly prefer the known black ticket, you are revealing a probability of black in urn B below one-half. Those probabilities must sum to one, so the two strict preferences cannot both reflect any single subjective probability assignment. Since any coherent agent can in principle be represented by a unique additive probability, the Ellsberg pattern is out.

Dana replied that this only shows the choices cannot be represented by a single additive probability, and that she sees no reason to treat that as mandatory. Her evidence, she said, underdetermines the composition of urn B: every proportion of reds, from none to all, is compatible with what she was told. A cautious decision rule — maximize the minimum expected payout across all compositions still compatible with one's evidence — yields precisely the Ellsberg pattern. The known red ticket has an expected payout of one dollar no matter what; the unknown red ticket could have an expected payout far below that. Preferring the known ticket is caution, not incoherence.

Jonah answered that the max-min rule is less innocent than it looks. It weights the worst compatible composition infinitely heavily, he said, and it draws an arbitrary line between what counts as uncertain and what counts as known. Once you have decided to trust that urn A really is 50/50, you have made a probability judgment; applied globally, the same caution would have you distrusting that description too and refusing every ticket. And if you allowed yourself any actual distribution over urn B's composition, a symmetric one would make the expected payout from the unknown red ticket identical to that from the known red ticket. The strict preference for the known urn, he concluded, is not caution but a failure to reduce compound uncertainty.

Dana said two things in response. First, she doesn't need a symmetric distribution over urn B; the absence of a usable one is exactly her point. Second, she argued that even granting a symmetric second-order distribution over the composition, preferring the known urn does not require inconsistency; it requires only refusing to reduce compound lotteries. A known half-chance, she said, can rationally be worth more than a half-chance drawn from an unknown mixture, because the latter carries risk about the probability itself. As for the symmetry between red and black, she conceded that the pair of unknown tickets is equivalent to the pair of known tickets — buying both colors on either urn guarantees exactly one winning ticket. But that, she claimed, shows only that her valuations are not additive across tickets, not that they are irrational: what she will pay for the unknown red ticket may reasonably depend on whether she already holds the black ticket as a hedge.

Jonah said that this last admission was the problem rather than a detail. If the package is worth the sum of its parts and you value the parts at less than that sum when offered separately, he argued, you are exposed to a money pump: by your own lights you would pay less to acquire the pieces one at a time than the assembled position is worth. More fundamentally, he said, if your prices for tickets depend on what else you already hold, then your choices do not define degrees of belief at all. Without degrees of belief there is no quantitative notion of learning from evidence. The point of the Dutch-book and Cox arguments that circulate in this corner of the internet, he said, was never to formalize the psychology of caution; it was to show that a notion of evidence worth having comes with additive probabilities.

They debated past this point, the hour got late, and they asked me to referee. I found I couldn't: each seemed to catch the other in a real concession, but neither would grant the other's conclusion. I would like a precise adjudication. Go through the argument round by round and say who is right about which specific claim, where each speaker overreaches, and what important considerations both are missing. Then give an overall verdict on which of the two is more nearly right, with reasons.
