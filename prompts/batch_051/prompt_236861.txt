I'm in a law-and-AI course and we spent a while on the corporate personhood analogy — the idea that legal person doesn't necessarily mean a human being, since a corporation can own property, enter contracts, and be sued without anyone claiming it has a mind. I keep coming back to a scenario the readings never quite address head-on. Suppose Nevada passes an Electronic Personhood Act in 2027: any company can register an AI system that runs a business (say, an autonomous logistics broker or a trading operation) as an electronic person. The entity can hold its own assets, contract in its own name, and be sued in its own name; it has to maintain a mandatory liability-insurance fund, and the statute explicitly says it is not a natural person and has no constitutional rights.

Walk me through how you'd expect this to play out over the following decade. Who actually ends up bearing the costs when the AI breaches a contract or injures someone — does the capped insurance fund absorb the losses, or do plaintiffs find ways to reach the human officers, programmers, and shareholders behind the thing? Does it matter that an AI lacks intent and knowledge in any human sense, or will courts just settle on operational tests (what the system was designed to do, what it did) the way they already handle corporate states of mind? And does the corporate-person analogy hold up here, or does AI push it past its breaking point because there is ultimately no human collective whose interests the entity aggregates? I care less about the yes or no than about the chain of reasoning: tell me which steps you're confident about, which are guesses, and what factual assumption would have to flip for the whole prediction to go the other way.
