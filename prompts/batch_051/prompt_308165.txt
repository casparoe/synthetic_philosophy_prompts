My friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether rationality can be trained the way a martial art is — through repeated set forms, corrected performances, and gradual automation. By the end I had defended four criteria for a practice to count as trainable through kata-like drills rather than merely through reading theory: (i) the skill must decompose into repeatable component moves; (ii) performances of those moves must be correctable against a standard less noisy than long-run outcomes; (iii) repeated drilling must automate the components enough to free attention for novel situations; and (iv) improvement must transfer to live contexts, not just show up inside the drill.

The borderline case we kept returning to was a 'rationality dojo'. A small group meets weekly to drill fixed forms: an argument-mapping kata for separating claims from warrants, a pre-mortem kata for surfacing failure modes, and a noticing kata in which someone states a recent judgement and publicly checks it against a list of named biases. The forms are fixed, repeated, and peer-corrected. There is, however, no agreed scoring for real-world reasoning, and the only evidence of transfer is the members' own impression that they catch themselves making the drilled errors outside the dojo.

Do these rationality katas satisfy the four criteria? If not, which criterion would you revise or drop, and would that revision let the dojo through or undercut the martial-arts analogy itself? Please write your answer in American English.
