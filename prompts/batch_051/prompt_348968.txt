I spent most of my career teaching and doing biology, and since I retired I've been reading philosophy of science. The more I read, the less sure I am what the word "misconception" is supposed to pick out. Everyone in science education uses it, but it can't just mean "any false answer," because a student who has simply never been told something, or who rushes and slips, is obviously not in the same boat as a student who holds a stable little theory that contradicts what she's been taught.

So here's what I'd like from you: a working definition of "misconception" that seperates the stable, theory-like errors from mere false beliefs, gaps, and slips. Run the definition through these cases and tell me where it surprises you or forces a revision:

- A nine-year-old who says heavier objects fall faster, and when asked why, explains that the heavier one presses down harder on the air underneath it.
- A high school physics student who earns top marks on the forces unit, then a month later says a thrown ball keeps moving because the throw left some force in the ball that gradually runs out.
- A first-year biology student who, a year after being taught photosynthesis, still says plants get most of their mass from the soil.
- A seventh grader who sorts whales with fish because they live in the water and swim, but when you ask how whales breathe, she switches to mammal without much resistance.
- A student who writes 6 × 7 = 42 on one quiz and 6 × 7 = 41 on the next, and when asked, says he knew it and just rushed.
- A premed student who confidently repeats, as something she learned from a nursing assistant, that blood is blue until it touches oxygen.
- A novice solving circuits who treats current as water flowing through pipes; she gets most simple DC problems right, but the model falls apart with capacitors.

I'm confident the first one is a misconception and the arithmetic slip isn't. The middle five are where I need help drawing the line.

One more thing I'd like the definition to address. It's tempting to say a misconception has to hang together like a miniature theory — internally coherent, explanatory, stable across contexts. But a student might just have a loose bag of rules of thumb that fire in certain situations without any real coherence. Does your definition require something theory-like, or can a leaky collection of context-specific heuristics count? And I suspect the word does two jobs at once, one describing what's in a student's head and one flagging that instruction should do something about it. If you think so, say which of the cases above move between the two readings.
