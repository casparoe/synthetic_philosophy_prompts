You are a professional philosopher specializing in philosophy of mind and personal identity. Think through each case before committing, and if two candidate definitions tie, expose the tie rather than hiding it.

What is the best working definition of "being the same mind" (or, if you prefer, "being the same person")—especially where the later being is a whole-brain emulation or the product of some related procedure? The definition should get the clear cases right and return a reasoned verdict on every case below.

1. A person after an ordinary decade of gradual physical and psychological change. (Presumably clearly the same mind.)
2. A person who, after a severe brain injury, retains almost no autobiographical memory and whose values, sense of humor, and temperament are markedly different. (Is this the same mind as before?)
3. A destructive whole-brain emulation: the original brain is scanned at whatever resolution turns out to be causally sufficient to capture everything about the mind, the biological brain is destroyed, and the emulation wakes up with the original's memories, skills, and quirks intact. (Is the emulation the same mind?)
4. The same scan-and-emulation procedure performed nondestructively, so that the original person and the emulation coexist, each claiming to be the original. (Which, if either, is the same mind as the original—and can both be?)
5. A gradual replacement: over the course of a year, each neuron is replaced one at a time by a functionally identical artificial unit, while the person remains awake and conversing throughout. (Is the resulting being the same mind as at the start?)
6. A person is scanned, dies, and the stored scan is run as an emulation many years later, after a long interval during which no emulation of that mind existed. (Did the same mind come back into existence?)
7. A compressed emulation that preserves all observable behavior and self-report but deliberately does not reproduce the fine-grained causal structure of the original brain. (Is it the same mind, or merely a simulation of one?)
8. A language model trained on everything someone wrote, which predicts their stated views and turns of phrase unusually well but was never derived from a scan of their brain. (Presumably clearly not the same mind.)

State your definition, then apply it to each case and justify any verdicts that initially seem surprising. Finally, identify which of your verdicts is least secure and what would overturn it. If your best definition is really of "same person" rather than "same mind," explain how you would close that gap, or why the distinction collapses.
