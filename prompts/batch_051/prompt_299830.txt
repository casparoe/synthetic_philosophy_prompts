My housemates got into it last night about whether an ant colony should count as a single organism, and I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning at any given turn. Could you go through it move by move and adjudicate?

(1) Lena, a biology PhD student, opened with the superorganism idea. She said an ant colony is better thought of as one organism than as a bunch of ants, because it has division of labor like a body (foragers, nurses, soldiers), a specialized reproductive caste, and mostly sterile workers — so the workers are basically somatic cells. What natural selection acts on, she said, is traits of the colony, not of individual ants.

(2) Marcus, who does philosophy of mind, said she was smuggling in the conclusion by calling it a superorganism. Each of her "cells" is itself a complete animal with its own genome, nervous system, and gut. And in lots of ant species workers can lay eggs that become males, so they aren't even fully sterile. Calling the colony one organism just relabels a society.

(3) Lena came back that workers who can lay eggs generally can't found a new colony, and what actually reproduces as a unit is the colony sending out winged reproductives — like a germ line. Having a full genome doesn't make something a separate organism: your skin cells have a full genome and their own metabolism too, but nobody calls them organisms, because they're functional parts of a larger reproductive unit.

(4) Marcus said real organisms don't have the internal conflicts ant colonies have. Workers police each other's eggs; queens and workers are in conflict over the sex ratio the colony produces; workers sometimes kill the queen. If a body did that you'd call it cancer or autoimmune disease, not normal function. So the analogy breaks exactly where it matters.

(5) Lena said bodies have those conflicts too: cancer is a cell lineage going rogue, there's meiotic drive (genes that cheat their way into more than half the gametes), and maternal and paternal genes in the placenta are in conflict over how much the fetus extracts from the mother. No organism is perfectly harmonious. So the question is where on a spectrum of integration you draw the line — and ant colonies are far enough along that the line is doing no useful work.

(6) Marcus said if that's the standard, then a human plus their gut microbiome is one organism, a lichen is one organism, a coral with its algae is one organism, and maybe a firm is one organism. At some point the word just means "a group with aligned interests" and stops doing any real work.

(7) Lena said the first three aren't a reductio — biologists actually argue about exactly those cases all the time, and maybe they should count. But she'd stop short of firms, because a colony has shared ancestry and reproduces as a unit, whereas a firm doesn't.

They went around like this for another hour and never landed. I'd like a verdict on the actual arguments they made, not a general essay: which specific claims are right, which are overstatements, and who ends up with the stronger overall position once necessary corrections are made?
