I am preparing a discussion document for a working group on expert evidence, and I want you to stress-test a mechanism before I present it.

The mechanism is an attempt to give an oath real binding force through a definite, pre-announced penalty that follows automatically from the swearer's own breach, rather than through a judge's or regulator's later discretion. My model is the oath of the gods in Hesiod's Theogony. The relevant passage, from the public-domain English translation in the Perseus Digital Library, is:

"For whoever of the deathless gods that hold the peaks of snowy Olympus pours a libation of her water and is forsworn, must lie breathless until a full year is completed, and never come near to taste ambrosia and nectar, but lie spiritless and voiceless on a strewn bed: and a heavy trance overshadows him. But when he has spent a long year in his sickness, another penance more hard follows after the first. For nine years he is cut off from the eternal gods and never joins their councils or their feasts, nine full years. But in the tenth year he comes again to join the assemblies of the deathless gods who live in the house of Olympus."

Notice the features I want to borrow: the penalty is fixed in advance, it is automatic once the forswearing is established, it has an inner period of total incapacity and a longer outer period of exclusion from the community's deliberative life, and it returns the oath-breaker to full standing on a schedule rather than by earning forgiveness.

Now the proposed rule. A jurisdiction is considering a "Styx rule" for expert witnesses in civil litigation. Before filing an expert report, the expert must swear a declaration that the report contains no assertion the expert knows to be false and no omission the expert knows would materially mislead the court, and must pay a bond into escrow (a flat sum, or a multiple of the expert's hourly fee). If the judge who hears the case, or an appellate panel reviewing the point, finds by clear and convincing evidence that the expert knowingly breached the declaration, then the consequences are automatic and not discretionary: the bond is forfeited to a fund that covers court and expert costs for litigants of limited means; the expert is barred from accepting any expert work for one year; and for the following nine years the expert may not serve on any standards committee, editorial board, government advisory body, or similar deliberative body in the field, nor act as an expert witness.

I do not want a verdict on whether this is a good idea in the abstract. I want an account of how it would actually behave: whom it would filter out and whom it would select for; how expert consultancies, insurers, and litigants would adapt; how it would be gamed (through strategic accusations, conflicts on the bench, price adjustments, or manufactured claims designed to trigger forfeiture); what it would do to honest experts with uncertain or unorthodox opinions; and what the second-order effects would be on which cases reach trial and which experts are willing to testify. Then say whether the main failure modes can be fixed without turning the fixed penalty back into discretionary professional discipline, and explain what the analysis suggests about why the Hesiodic oath makes the penalty fixed and scheduled in the first place.

In your conclusion, tell me explicitly which argument or consideration would most change your assessment, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
