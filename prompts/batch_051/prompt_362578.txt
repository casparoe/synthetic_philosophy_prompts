Longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums here, and I've been trying to pin down a persistent intuition I have about the laws of nature. I recently reread the passage in David Hume's *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Section IV, Part II, where he argues that the best philosophy can do is reduce the many particular effects of nature to a small number of general causes; but the causes of those general causes, the deepest principles that would explain the general causes themselves, are entirely closed off to human inquiry and curiosity. He seems to be making an epistemological point: no matter how far our explanations push, we never reach a final, self-explaining level.

I want to see that passage put to work in the metaphysics debate. Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers. One is a Humean about laws: laws are nothing over and above the best systematic summary of the actually occurring local facts, sometimes called the mosaic. The other is an anti-Humean about laws: laws are real, irreducibly modal features of the world, something like genuine governing relations or primitive modal constraints that produce or explain the regularities, and not merely summaries of them.

Have the dialogue start from the Hume passage. The Humean claims that this epistemic ceiling is exactly what we should expect if laws are just summaries of the mosaic, and that positing hidden governors is to posit exactly the sort of thing Hume says we can never know. The anti-Humean should not be a strawman: they should respond that epistemic modesty does not license ontological deflation, and that scientific practice, especially the distinction between laws and accidents and the use of laws to support counterfactuals, presses us toward something beyond the mosaic. Make the dialogue a genuine back-and-forth: each character should respond to the other's actual moves, concede small points where appropriate, and not talk past each other. End with the positions sharpened but not adjudicated.
