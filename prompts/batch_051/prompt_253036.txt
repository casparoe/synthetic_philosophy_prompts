ok so for my jurisprudence class we've been doing the rule of law as an ideal, mainly Raz's line that it's only a negative value: law has to be prospective, open, clear, stable, applied independently and not perverted by official discretion, but (he says) conforming to those formal things is like a knife being sharp — it just makes law better at whatever it happens to do, good or bad. Fuller and Waldron push back that the ideal is thicker than that and tied to respecting citizens as agents and making them answerable.

The thing I keep getting stuck on is this. Raz's case for the formal, morally-neutral reading seems to assume law arrives as perspicuous general rules. But more and more of what used to be rule-governed practice — eligibility for benefits, bail, tax-audit flags, sometimes low-level sentencing — is getting handed to machine-learning systems that are not written down as rules. They are opaque, and they get retrained, so the operative 'rule' (really a learned function) drifts with every update. There is no perspicuous rule to be clear, stable, or prospective.

So my actual question: does Raz's arguement survive that development? Does the ideal just need restating as requirements on how such systems are built and reviewed — versioned models, notice before the model's behavior changes, interpretability or surrogate explanations, meaningful appeal to a human? Or does the development show the ideal was never chiefly about formal properties, and the correct reading was the Fuller/Waldron one all along, where what matters is law being answerable to public norms in a way citizens can follow and contest? Or is it dead, because the rule of law only makes sense for a world of published rules and we are leaving that world?

Don't just survey the options. Pick survives, needs repair, or dead and defend it. Also say exactly which premise of Raz's argument the machine-learning turn puts pressure on. Then tell me what would make you switch verdicts: which empirical fact about how these systems get audited, updated, or explained, or which philosophical argument about the point of the rule of law, would flip you. And be honest about where your reasoning is most likely to fail — like which premise your least sure about.
