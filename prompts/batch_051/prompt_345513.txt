I've been trying to come up with a test for legal personhood that doesn't smuggle in consciousness or moral status, and I've settled on something I call the Judgment-Stop Test. The claim: an entity should count as a legal person exactly when a judgment against it can stop with it. By that I mean the entity has it's own ring-fenced pool of assets — or insurance, or some equivalent — so that when a court finds it liable, the liability attaches to that pool and gets satisfied out of it, and doesn't automatically flow through to a human owner, user, or programmer unless you invoke a separate doctrine like veil-piercing. This handles the corporate case without pretending corporations have minds: a corporation is a legal person because the law gives it a separate patrimony and limited liability, so a suit against it can terminate at its own assets instead of at the shareholders. It also makes natural persons persons for a boring reason: your debts attach to your own property, not to your family's. Then the AI payoff: an AI system becomes a legal person not when it's conscious or autonomous, but when there's an asset pool behind it that can answer for its doings — say a mandatory insurance fund for a delivery drone. So a chatbot with no separate assets isn't a person, but a self-driving truck with its own liability pot could be. It even dissolves the can't-be-punished worry a bit: legal personhood was never about whether the entity can feel punishment, only about where the liability lands. I know this makes personhood sound unglamorous, basically like accounting. Hit me with the strongest objections.
