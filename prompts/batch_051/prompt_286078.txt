A machine-learning engineer in my reading group pressed the following argument on us, and after two evenings nobody has located the mistake. It appears to prove far too much, so I want a diagnosis rather than a restatement.

Fix a finite input space and a fixed training set of labeled examples, with binary labels. Take any two supervised learning algorithms, each a deterministic rule that outputs predictions for the points not in the training set. For any target labeling of the entire input space, an algorithm's off-training-set error is the number of unseen points it labels incorrectly. The no-free-lunch theorem says that if you add up an algorithm's off-training-set error over every possible target labeling of the input space, you get the same total for every algorithm.

(1) The theorem as just stated is true.

(2) Let I be this algorithm: given the training set, it selects the simplest labeling of the input space that is consistent with the training examples, using some fixed standard of simplicity and a fixed way of breaking ties, and it predicts according to that labeling at every unseen point. Let C be this algorithm: given the same training set, it selects the same simplest consistent labeling and then predicts the opposite of that labeling at every unseen point. Both are deterministic rules, so the theorem applies to them.

(3) Therefore I and C have exactly the same total off-training-set error, summed over all possible target labelings.

(4) If two prediction rules have exactly the same total error over all possible worlds, then no rational consideration can favor one over the other. Any genuine superiority of a rule would have to show up somewhere in the space of possible worlds, and by hypothesis the two rules are tied everywhere in the aggregate.

(5) Therefore no rational consideration favors the inductive rule I over the counter-inductive rule C.

(6) If no rational consideration favors induction over counter-induction, then our standing practice of expecting the future to resemble the past is rationally arbitrary.

(7) Therefore ordinary inductive practice is rationally arbitrary. But the same reasoning would show that every conceivable learning rule — however carefully designed — is on a rational par with a rule that deliberately flips its best-supported predictions. That cannot be what the problem of induction was trying to teach, so somewhere the chain overreaches.

Which step or transition should be rejected? Explain why that step initially looks forced, and then price the alternatives. I would prefer to keep both the theorem and the rational preferability of ordinary induction, but I do not want to beg the question in order to keep them.
