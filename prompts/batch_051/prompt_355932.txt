I've been teaching Abelard's ethics this term and by the end of the unit I'd talked myself into a cleaned-up version of his view, and I'd like someone to stress-test weather it actually hangs together before I write it up. The line I'm drawn to is the one Abelard is famous for, that the sin is in the consent rather than the act. Here's where I've landed, as six claims.

(1) The wrongness of what a person does attaches at the moment they consent to do it; neither the outward deed nor the desire for it is the locus of sin. So a person who has an intense desire to do something awful but refuses to consent to it hasn't sinned at all.

(2) Same logic on the other side: someone who does the right thing but for the wrong reasons—say they'd fully intended to do wrong and got thwarted—gets no moral credit.

(3) It's always a sin to act against your own conscience, even when your conscience is wrong about what the right thing is.

(4) And it's possible to act in accord with a mistaken conscience and be entirely innocent, even when the thing done would otherwise be wrong.

(5) But I also want to say that forming your conscience is itself something you're responsible for: carelessness about what you ought to know is culpable, even if there's no single moment where you looked at the truth and said no.

(6) Last, I want to hang onto the commonsense thought that the act itself isn't morally neutral. When a wrong thing gets done in the world, that's an objective harm—even if the agent is blameless—and any moral theory should have room for that.

I can't tell which of these are in real tension and which only look like it when you state them flat. So: rank the tensions among the six by severity, say which pairs only seem to conflict and why, and what the cheapest repair is in each case. And tell me if one of the repairs is more expensive than I probably think.
