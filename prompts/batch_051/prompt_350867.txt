Analyze the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990, together with the decade of judicial narrowing of its definition of disability that followed and the 2008 amendments meant to reverse that narrowing, as a case study in what makes a ground of discrimination suspect. Work with a concrete version of the standard answer: a classification is suspect insofar as it tracks (i) an unchosen or extremely costly-to-change trait, (ii) a trait that is visible or otherwise salient in ordinary interaction, and (iii) membership in a group that has been historically stigmatized, excluded, and politically weak. Treat the disability-rights campaign that produced the Act, the subsequent narrowing, and the 2008 restoration as a single episode, and do not rehearse the legislative history except where your argument needs it.

Give me a disciplined, two-directional analysis. First, according to this framework, is disability a paradigmatic suspect ground or only a marginal one, and what would that verdict have licensed or forbidden in the Act's design — for example, in the controversy over who counts as disabled and in the choice to require reasonable accommodation rather than merely to instruct employers to ignore the trait? Second, identify the points where the mapping strains or breaks: consider that disability is frequently acquired rather than innate, often concealable or medically contested, and that the Act in effect treats the built and social environment, rather than the individual trait, as what must change.

Then run the analysis in reverse. Does this episode confirm the framework, show that one criterion is doing all the work while the others are epiphenomenal, or reveal that the framework was built around race and sex and can capture disability only by distortion? If you conclude that a rival criterion does better — say, that what makes a ground suspect is that the unmarked norm is treated as the baseline of social design — state and defend it.
