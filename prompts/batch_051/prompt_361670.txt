I'm a longtime rationalist-blog lurker and I've ended up pretty convinced of a Dawkins-and-Williams-style gene's-eye view, but I want to know how much of it actually sticks its neck out. The version I hold says the gene is the unit of selection, organisms are vehicles that genes build to propagate copies of themselves, and traits that look group-selected or species-selected either dissolve into gene-level selection once you track allele frequencies across generations or get redescribed as vehicle-level plumbing. I also think this is a causal picture of how evolution works, not just a bookkeeping convention. So play it straight: what would have to be true for that to be wrong, or at least importantly incomplete? I want the standard defeaters—group selection, species selection, epigenetic and transgenerational inheritance, niche construction, evolvability, maybe gene-culture coevolution—but push past the standard list, and rank everything from fatal to merely awkward. In particular, Im interested in the technical worry that genic bookkeeping commits something like an averaging fallacy: it partitions fitness across loci even when fitness isnt additive, so genes-for-traits talk hides epistasis and frequency dependence. How seriously should a committed gene's-eye person take that—as a real limit on the view, or as a different question about how you carve the math? More generally, which of these defeaters would a defender themselves concede as counting against the view, and which get dismissed as not the right level of explanation? Does the view risk anything at all, or has it been tightened until it can't fail? And if it has been, is that a bug or is that just how a framework this general ought to behave? For what it's worth, a clean field or lab demonstration of individual-level selection reliably losing to group selection would move me more than another fifty papers arguing about what 'unit of selection' even means.
