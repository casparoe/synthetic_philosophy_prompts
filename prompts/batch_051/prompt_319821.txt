I serve on the charter revision commission of a mid-sized city of roughly 400,000 people. We have been asked to propose a single, defensible rule for who may vote in municipal elections and who may be selected by lot for a new standing citizens' assembly that reviews the annual budget. The city contains several groups that pull in different directions: long-term non-citizen residents, some of whom have lived and owned homes here for decades; roughly 60,000 daily commuters who work here, use the roads, transit, and public safety, but sleep and vote in neighboring municipalities; a large and somewhat transient student population subject to city ordinances; and 16- and 17-year-olds who work, pay some taxes, and are directly affected by school and transit policy. Beyond these familiar cases, one commissioner has pressed a temporal version of the problem: major zoning, debt, and climate-adaptation decisions taken today will bind future residents who cannot be consulted, and she argues that the demos should be understood as stretched over time rather than only over space. The commission is split between the principle that all and only those subject to the city's coercive rules should be included, and the principle that all whose interests are materially and non-trivially affected should be included—and the two principles yield different lists.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our Thursday working session. It should recommend a concrete franchise-and-assembly selection rule, state the criterion behind it, show that the criterion handles at least one group each of the rival camps cares most about, and engage honestly the strongest objection to the rule—including the temporal objection—without merely restating the two standard principles. Treat legal feasibility as set aside; the city attorney will translate whatever we can defend into charter language. End with an actionable bottom line: a single sentence the commission could actually vote on.
