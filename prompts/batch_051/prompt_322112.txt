I was recently denied boarding on an oversold flight, and even after being compensated I kept turning over the structure of the arrangement rather than my own inconvenience. The airline had knowingly sold more tickets than seats, exposing every passenger to a small chance of losing their seat, but no passenger had been individually asked to accept that risk; the acceptance was folded into the fine print. Only those of us who actually got bumped were compensated, though everyone had been carrying the same risk from the moment of purchase. And the remedy treated the loss as something a fixed cash payment could set right after the fact, while the passengers who drew the favorable outcome paid nothing for having been put at risk. What theory of risk, harm, and consent does this practice presuppose? Does it commit the airline — and the regulators who permit this — to the view that an unrealized risk requires no remedy, and that only materialized harm counts? Or can the practice be reconciled with a view on which the imposition of the risk is itself the wrong? And is that commitment consistent with how the same legal system treats other nonconsensual risk impositions, such as firing a gun into the air without hitting anyone?
