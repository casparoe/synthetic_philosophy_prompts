I have been trying to work out which New Testament commands I should treat as binding me directly and which I should treat as addressed to a particular cultural situation, so that only something like their underlying principle survives. My verdicts on individual cases are fairly stable, but they refuse to sit under one principle, and I would like you either to find the principle or to show me which of my verdicts must go.

The instruction that believers greet one another with a kiss, repeated several times in the New Testament, seems to me culturally specific in its outward form and transgenerational only as a principle of affectionate greeting. I feel no loss in not observing it literally.

Paul's rule in 1 Corinthians 11, that a woman who prays or prophesies with her head uncovered dishonours her head, seems to me culturally conditioned. But this bothers me, because Paul supports the rule from the order of creation and from what he calls nature, and I do not ordinarily feel free to dissolve a creation-order argument into culture.

The instruction that women should keep silent in the churches, from 1 Corinthians 14, I take to be aimed at disruptive questioning, since the same letter assumes women pray and prophesy in the assembly; so I treat it as culturally specific. The instruction in 1 Timothy 2 feels different. There Paul forbids a woman to teach or take authority over a man and argues from Adam and Eve, and I find myself treating that rule as transgenerational even though I am not entirely comfortable with it. I therefore accept a creation-order argument in 1 Timothy 2 while rejecting one in 1 Corinthians 11, and I cannot state the difference between the two without sounding ad hoc. I know the usual view is that the 1 Timothy passage is transcultural precisely because of its creation appeal, but that only sharpens the problem with my head-covering verdict.

On slavery, I take Paul's instructions that slaves obey their owners, and his sending of Onesimus back to Philemon, to be accommodations to an institution the gospel was meant in time to undermine, rather than transgenerational rules endorsing slaveholding. What survives, I think, is the equal standing of slave and free and the duty of those with power to act justly. Yet this move, letting the surface command be culturally bound while retaining only its principle, is exactly the move I refuse to make for the rule about a woman teaching. Why is one command an accommodation and not the other?

Dietary law I treat differently again. The Levitical food laws I regard as a temporary boundary that the New Testament itself, in Mark's comment that Jesus thereby made all foods clean and in Peter's vision, treats as ended. They are neither culture-specific in the way the kiss is nor an accommodation in the way the slavery instructions are. By contrast, the command to love one's enemies I take to be directly binding without adjustment. And Christ's washing of the disciples' feet, with the accompanying command to do the same, I take to be binding in its principle of humble bodily service but not as a literal requirement; I do not think a congregation that literally washes feet is more obedient than one that scrubs floors or changes bedpans.

The typical moral, ceremonial, and civil distinction is too tidy for me: the New Testament does not sort its commands under those headings, and my verdicts keep crossing its lines. I am not asking for that map. I am asking whether there is a principled map behind my own judgements, or whether I am merely making unprincipled judgements case by case.

Please do three things. First, state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that comes closest to delivering all these verdicts, and state precisely which case motivates each clause. Second, tell me what the resulting account says about cases I have not listed: the Apostolic Council's rule in Acts 15 that Gentile believers abstain from blood and from what is strangled, issued as binding on Gentile converts and now almost universally not kept; and the instruction in the letter of James to anoint the sick with oil. Third, say which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would overturn those verdicts or complicate the principle to save them.

Please write your answer in British English.
