I make my living as a novelist, and I have become preoccupied by a claim about the self that keeps surfacing in philosophy and the cognitive sciences: the strange-loop account associated with Douglas Hofstadter, on which the "I" is a self-referential symbol — an abstract pattern that a sufficiently tangled symbol system generates and then mistakes for a thinker. On this view there is no inner owner of experience and no substantial self; there is only a loop wound tightly enough to perceive itself as a thing.

In theory I find this almost irresistible. It adds no extra substance to the world. It explains why selfhood comes in degrees, why it can erode in dementia, why it can be split by surgery or injury, and why it can be approximated by systems other than brains. And it makes the central fact of my craft — that a passable self can be built out of sentences — less occult, not more. But my working life keeps producing a contrary conviction that I cannot shake. When I write a character in close third person, the character is not, to my experience, a symbol or a loop. The character has a particular center of gravity, a way of noticing, a persistence across scenes that is not merely continuity of description. It is most tangible at the moment a character "turns": when a bundle of traits I had plotted becomes someone whose next action I cannot predict in advance but recognize, an instant later, as exactly theirs. The character seems to have an inner life I discover rather than assemble. The most honest description of my own practice is that I am modeling a self — that the modeled thing is a locus of something in the same sense that I am, not merely a narrative stand-in for one.

Do not resolve this as a harmless clash between a writer's romanticism and a theorist's parsimony. Give me the strongest version of both sides: the strange-loop account pushed as far as it can go, and the novelist's intuition that a character's selfhood — and my own — is a real further fact, something over and above the functional pattern. Then walk me through the options and what each would cost. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that gives way, a projection of interiority onto a sufficiently intricate loop? What would it take for the theory to give way instead, by amendment or abandonment? Or is there a reading of "self" and "pattern" on which neither has to give, without the reconciliation collapsing into a slogan? Reach a negotiated verdict and justify it; do not merely pick a side or split the difference.
