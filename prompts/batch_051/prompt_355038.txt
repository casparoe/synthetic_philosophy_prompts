I coordinate a small interdisciplinary reading group on language acquisition, and we've hit a wall because two of our members keep talking past each other. One, call her the nativist, holds that kids couldn't get from their input to adult grammar without some language-specific, innately constrained machinery; she leans on the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument, especially the fact that children respect abstract, structure-dependent rules that they couldn't plausibly extract from what they hear, and they mostly aren't corrected for syntax anyway. The other, call him the emergentist, thinks this misframes the problem: there's more information in child-directed speech and in domain-general statistical learning than the nativist allows, and surface-level generalization, analogy, and entrenchment can do a lot before you need to posit anything language-specific at all. They've argued for a year and have finaly agreed to stop going in circles and write an adversarial collaboration document instead. I want you to draft it for them.

The document should have three parts. First, a numbered list of 8–12 claims about language acquisition that both parties can sign without gritting their teeth; make them specific and substantive, not just "language is partly learned," but be honest about where genuine agreement runs out. Second, a disagreements table: for each remaining disagreement, give a one-sentence statement of the nativist's view and a one-sentence statement of the emergentist's view, both worded so the other side would say "yes, that's a fair way to put my opponent's position." Third, a crux section: identify four to six sub-questions such that resolving at least one of them would force at least one side to move. For each crux, specify a concrete kind of evidence or experimental design both sides would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts. Use the usual candidate cruxes if you want — complex yes/no questions like "Is the man who is tall here?", the anaphoric "one" construction, artificial grammar learning, homesign and the way children regularize inconsistent or degraded input, the absence of attested overgeneralizations, and the actual distributional richness of child-directed speech — but warm them up with enough detail that this doesn't read like a textbook summary. Where the argument turns out to be partly verbal, say so and reformulate it so whatever remains is a real empirical or theoretical disagreement.
