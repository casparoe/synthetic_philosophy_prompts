I'm a software engineer, and I keep running into a version of the same argument about whether competition is good for people. The concrete case in my world is the promotion and performance-review process: some colleagues say it forces people to become sharper and more disciplined, while others say it quietly turns peers into rivals and makes people hide mistakes. I don't have any formal philosophy background, so I'm not looking for a verdict so much as for a clean case that lets me see what each side is actually tracking.

Let me put two views on the table. The first says competition builds character when and because it functions as practice against a standard: it supplies fast, honest feedback about what you can actually do, raises the bar, and trains qualities like persistence, self-control, and craft. The second says competition corrodes character when and because it functions as a positional good: your gain is defined relative to someone else's loss, so it rewards envy, information-hoarding, corner-cutting, and treating other people as obstacles.

Construct a thought experiment set in an ordinary software organization in which both views deliver their characteristic verdict about one and the same competitive practice: the first should say the practice builds character in one important respect, the second should say the same practice corrodes character in a different important respect, and the case should make clear that they are not merely talking past each other. Constraints: the scenario should be a single paragraph; it must be one practice rather than a mixture of a good practice and a bad one; nobody cheats or sabotages; the incentives must be ordinary, not cartoonish; and the two verdicts must not be stipulated — they should fall out of applying each view to the structure of the case. After the scenario, do the analysis. State each view's verdict and the reasoning that produces it. Then name the one structural feature of the case that is doing the work — the feature in virtue of which the first view sees practice-against-a-standard and the second sees a positional good. Say what would happen to both verdicts if you removed that feature while holding everything else fixed. Finally, say how a defender of the second view could most honestly push back on the redesigned case rather than on your original one.
