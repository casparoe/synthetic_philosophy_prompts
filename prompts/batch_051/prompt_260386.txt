I sit on the board of a neighborhood community center. For years the center has kept a large room open every afternoon with no scheduled programming: board games, craft supplies, a piano, books, and a small garden. Our new executive director wants to replace those hours with structured workshops—financial-literacy games, résumé coaching, and art therapy with measurable outcomes—because grant reviewers keep asking what the open room produces. One board member has been reading Huizinga's Homo Ludens and keeps paraphrasing a passage in which he describes play as a free activity that stands outside ordinary life and serves no material interest or practical necessity; she argues that the open room should stay precisely because it gives people an interval set apart from ordinary life. I find her position intuitively appealing, but I worry it commits us to funding a room where people simply waste time. Is there a principled distinction between what the unstructured open room offers and what the measurable workshops offer, or is my colleague just romanticizing downtime?
