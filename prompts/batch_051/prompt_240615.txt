I'm a retired geologist who has recently gotten into philosophy of mathematics, and I've caught myself following a rule I can't tell is a principle or a prejudice.

My rule is: when a proof tells me something exists but cant exhibit the thing, I treat the conclusion as a formal nicety rather than as a claim about what's there. In particular, whenever a proof leans on the Axiom of Choice, I downgrade the result. I'll happily say "every vector space has a basis," but I flinch at "the reals can be well-ordered," because nobody can produce the ordering and I'm supposed to accept that some ordering exists anyway. Nonmeasurable sets and Banach–Tarski don't bother me as false; they bother me as artifacts of an instrument that's stronger than anything I'd count as existence.

I know this makes me sound like a constructivist, but I don't think I am one. I'm fine with proof by contradiction and classical logic. My scruple is narrower: I resist existence claims that come from a choice function doing an infinite amount of arbitrary picking all at once, with no procedure, no witness, no nothing.

So is this scruple defensible, or am I dragging a field scientist's sense of "there is" into mathematics, where exists just doesn't work that way? If it's not defensible as stated, is there a restated version that keeps the useful caution without throwing away the mathematics that depends on Choice? Or is even that restatement trying to have it both ways?

And feel free to tell me the whole question is badly posed. If the line between "exhibiting" something and "proving it exists" is not where I think it is, I'd rather hear that than get a polite answer to the wrong question.
