I am a PhD student in epistemology, and I have been working on the value problem that Plato raises in the Meno: why is knowledge more valuable than a mere true belief, given that the two seem to steer action equally well in the moment? I find the standard answer—that knowledge is true belief tied down by an account of the reason why—unsatisfying as it is usually stated. Stability, by itself, seems neither necessary nor sufficient for whatever makes knowledge distinctively valuable: a stubbornly held true belief can be extremely stable, and a well-grounded true belief can be abandoned at the first hint of counter-evidence. Still, I do not want to give up the sense that Plato is pointing at something real.

I have been experimenting with a replacement I call revision capital. The proposal is this. A bare true belief is a one-shot asset: it is correct under the exact conditions in which it happened to form, but it encodes little or nothing about how far those conditions extend or when they would fail. Knowledge, by contrast, is true belief plus a stored route. To know that p is to hold p in a way that tracks the conditions under which p remains correct and the conditions under which p should be revised or abandoned. On this view, knowledge is more valuable than true belief because it pays out across a neighborhood of nearby possible circumstances: the knower adjusts appropriately to small changes—new symptoms, changed terrain, conflicting testimony—where the mere true believer either preserves the truth for the wrong reasons or abandons it for the wrong reasons. For example, two physicians both correctly prescribe a drug to a patient; one has memorized the protocol, while the other knows why the drug works for this condition. When the patient turns out to have an unusual contraindication, the first physician mechanically repeats the prescription and harms the patient, while the second revises the treatment. Their initial true beliefs were equally useful; the knowledge was more valuable because it was revisable in the right ways.

I would like an honest assessment of this idea. Is revision capital a genuine distinction, or have I simply relabeled justification, understanding, or a virtue-theoretic competence? I want to know which two or three existing positions it is nearest to, and where it diverges from them. Most importantly, stress-test it: give me the clearest counterexample in which someone has revision capital in my sense but does not know, and one in which someone knows but lacks revision capital. And say where the proposal stops being distinctive—where, after charitable reconstruction, it collapses into a view that is already on the table. Please write the response in American English.
