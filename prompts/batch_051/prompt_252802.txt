Design a runnable classroom activity on the philosophical question behind statutes of limitations: whether the mere passage of time can change what it is permissible or required to do about an old wrong. The setting is a university seminar in moral and legal philosophy, with about eighteen final-year undergraduates, and you have ninety minutes. The activity should be a structured deliberation, role-play, or staged case rather than a lecture. Participants should come away seeing a distinction without having been told it in advance: the difference between time mattering intrinsically (the age of a wrong itself alters what may be demanded or punished) and time mattering only derivatively (time matters insofar as it tracks or brings about fading evidence, changes in the offender, the growth of reliance interests, and the value of closure). The rules, roles, and choices should be arranged so that participants run into this distinction through what they are asked to decide, and it should be named only afterwards in the debrief.

The activity should include at least one stage that isolates the intrinsic question by holding the derivative factors fixed: a case in which the evidence is fully preserved, the offender is recognisably the same person, no one has relied on the delay, and yet the wrong is old. In that stage, participants should be made to say what, if anything, still changes.

Lay out the activity in enough detail to run it: the number and length of stages, all materials, the exact roles or decisions, and any voting or forced-choice mechanisms. Then give a debrief that maps key moments in the activity onto the intrinsic/derivative distinction and the main arguments for each side; it should also show where each step in the participants' reasoning could be resisted and what it would cost to resist it. Say what is likely to go wrong the first time the activity is run and how the facilitator should handle it. Be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply, and about where the staging tacitly favours one side of the distinction. In your guidance for the facilitator, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
