After a long argument with a friend about whether sentience—and the moral status she ties to it—is all-or-nothing or a matter of degree, I wrote down the verdicts she reached on the cases we discussed. We had agreed on a rough factual background for each case, and our disagreement was only about what those facts imply. Her verdicts use a rough 0–10 scale, where 0 is no sentience at all and 10 is the full sentience of a healthy adult dog.

- Dog: 10. A centralized brain, rich nociception, flexible learning, and clear pain and pleasure behavior.
- Octopus: 9. A large but partly distributed nervous system, with sophisticated learning and problem-solving.
- Honeybee: 4. A compact central brain, sophisticated learning, and injury-responsive behavior.
- Roundworm: 1. A few hundred neurons, a compact nerve ring, nociceptor-like cells, and simple associative conditioning.
- Sea anemone: 0. A diffuse nerve net, no brain, only simple contraction reflexes and non-associative habituation, as stipulated.
- Oyster: 0. Dispersed ganglia, sluggish reflexes, and no associative learning, as stipulated.
- Jellyfish: 0. A diffuse nerve net, no brain, reflexive swimming and feeding, and no associative learning, as stipulated.
- Venus flytrap: 0. Rapid trap closure and habituation, but no neurons.
- Thermostat: 0. A bimetallic strip responding to temperature.
- Nociceptive robot: 0. It has a damage sensor that initially produces only reversal, and later reinforcement learning shapes avoidance of places associated with sensor stimulation; she was not moved by the learning.

We did not finish before reaching this new case. It is a stipulated, hypothetical marine worm with no central brain: its nervous system is a chain of dispersed ganglia, each performing local sensing and local reflexes, with slow distributed signaling across the body and no single integrating structure. It has nociceptor-like sensory neurons. It learns to avoid a mild shock after a single exposure, generalizes that avoidance to chemically similar cues, and trades off shock risk against food quality and hunger, shifting its choices as hunger changes.

Before we next meet, I want to predict her verdict. Please (i) reconstruct the principle or principles that are each consistent with her entire record, stated precisely enough to apply to a new case; (ii) state what each candidate principle predicts for this worm on the same 0–10 scale; (iii) give your best guess about her actual verdict and identify the closest analog among the earlier cases; and (iv) name one further case, distinct from the worm, that would cleanly separate the leading principles, with each principle's prediction for it recorded in advance. Finally, be explicit about what would change your mind: which consideration would lead you to a different prediction, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
