A country whose fertility rate has hovered near 1.4 children per woman for two decades abolishes its child allowances and replaces them with the following system. Every citizen receives one birth permit upon turning twenty-one, and no further permits are ever issued to that citizen. Registering a live birth with the state consumes one permit, which is then canceled; two parents may pool their permits, and either may present one when registering a child. The designers describe the allocation as replacement-level, since a couple each holding one permit can register two children without acquiring anything further. Permits may be bought, sold, gifted, or bequeathed; they do not expire during the holder's lifetime; they confer no parental rights or obligations; and the government neither guarantees a resale price nor buys back unused permits.

Analyze this as a mechanism rather than as a moral proposal. Assume that desired fertility, apart from the permit requirement, stays near 1.4 for the first decade. Walk through the incentives it creates for people of different ages, incomes, and family intentions; how a secondary market would form, or fail to form; who would gain and lose while the market price remains low; and what would change if desired fertility unexpectedly rose to 2.5. Then say where the system would break down first (hoarding, speculation, unregistered births, births abroad to avoid the permit, or something else) and whether those failures could be repaired without abandoning the device entirely. Finally, say what the scheme's behavior reveals about what a tradable birth permit can and cannot do for a below-replacement society.
