Here is the exam question: "Moral foundations theory finds that political liberals and conservatives weight a shared set of moral foundations differently—liberals relying chiefly on care/harm and fairness/cheating, conservatives drawing more evenly on loyalty, authority, and sanctity as well—and that moral reasoning is typically post hoc. Some have taken this descriptive psychology to support a parity thesis: that neither side can claim to be morally or epistemically more justified than the other. Explain what in the theory could be thought to support that parity thesis, and assess whether the theory actually licenses it."

Here is the student's answer: "Moral foundations theory identifies five, or on a later version six, universal and evolutionarily prepared foundations of moral judgment: care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation, and later liberty/oppression. Survey evidence shows that self-identified liberals rate care and fairness as much more important than the binding foundations, whereas self-identified conservatives assign moderate importance to all of them. Haidt's account also says that explicit moral reasoning usually comes after an intuitive judgment and rationalizes it. What matters is that this replaces philosophical claims about which political side is right with a causal explanation of how each side gets its convictions. Once we understand that each side's judgments arise from a different weighting of normal, cross-culturally recurring intuitive foundations, disagreement is unmasked as a difference in how the same machinery is calibrated rather than a difference about a shared moral reality. This gives empirical support to the parity thesis: it shows that neither side is more correct, because both are simply instances of human moral psychology doing what it routinely does. Since the data confirm that the liberal and conservative profiles are each internally coherent, parity is an empirical finding rather than a normative leap, and any attempt to rank one profile above the other lacks scientific warrant. Moral foundations theory therefore supports treating left–right moral disagreement as closer to a disagreement about which flavor of ice cream is preferable than to a debate in which one party has reason on its side."
