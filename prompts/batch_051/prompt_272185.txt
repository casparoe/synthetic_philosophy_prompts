I co-host a philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends a broadly Reformed account of predestination, in the line running from the later Augustine through Calvin: election is unconditional and prior to any foreseen faith or merit; saving grace is efficacious and cannot be finally resisted; and yet the non-elect are genuinely responsible for their sin and justly condemned. In earlier debates she has handled the usual objections with one maneuver. When pressed on the Arminian charge that this makes God arbitrary, makes human freedom an illusion, or makes the gospel offer insincere, she says the objector is collapsing two explanatory levels—"God authors the decree and the order of salvation, while the creature authors the act and its moral quality"—and that because God works through rather than against the human will, no competition between the two arises. I need eight to ten interview questions that keep that distinction from doing so much quiet work, so that she has to say exactly what it commits her to.

Please include at least one question, each with a follow-up phrased to anticipate the reply she is most likely to give, on these three pressure points. First, whether the freedom she wants to preserve requires the ability to do otherwise, and whether "could have done otherwise" is the right test of responsibility for a will whose fallen inability is, on her own account, self-incurred rather than natural. Second, the problem of equal ultimacy, pressed as a dilemma: if the decree of reprobation merely passes over the non-elect in view of their sin, election begins to look conditional; if it does more than pass over, how is God not the author of sin—and may she remain agnostic about the intra-Reformed question of the order of the decrees (whether the decree to permit the fall precedes or follows the decree to elect), or does the decree/act distinction only work if one particular ordering is assumed? Third, the Arminian alternative itself: whether election conditional on foreseen faith, with resistible grace, really secures divine aseity—God's independence from anything outside himself—and human freedom, or instead reintroduces a human contribution to salvation that the Augustinian side has always charged is incoherent. Then add further questions of your own where her position looks weakest, keeping every question on the metaphysics of the view rather than on exegesis or church history.
