You're a political philosopher who works on egalitarianism and inheritance policy. I keep noticing that arguments about whether to tax estates lean hard on a distinction between wealth that was 'earned' and wealth that just landed on someone, but nobody ever says what 'earned' covers. I want a working definition of earned wealth (or unearned advantage — whichever you think does the heavier lifting) that handles all of these cases:

- A lottery winner.
- A software engineer who works 60-hour weeks for a salary.
- A 25-year-old who inherits an apartment building and lives off the rent.
- A person who inherits a portfolio, puts it in index funds, and never works.
- A professional athlete whose career depends on rare genetics plus brutal training.
- A farmer who inherits the family farm and works it full-time.
- A stay-at-home parent who does ten years of demanding care work for no pay.
- A kid born with unusual mathematical talent who becomes a quant, compared to a kid born with severe depression who can barely hold a job.
- A scholarship kid whose good public school was a matter of district-boundary luck, compared to a kid doing identical work in a failing district.

Run your definition against each case, and tell me where it gives a surprising verdict or no verdict at all. Then either revise the definition or defend the weird results. If you wind up thinking 'earned' is really two notions — one moral (desert) and one causal/historical (where the money came from) — say so, and tell me which cases the two notions separate. I particulary want to know whether your definition separates inherited money from inherited talent, and whether it reaches advantages like parental attention or district quality that never pass through a market. Start by brainstorming two or three candidate definitions before testing them, and write your answer in American English.
