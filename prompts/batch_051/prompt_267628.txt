The Orwellian and Stalinesque accounts of the color phi phenomenon seem to be in a permanent standoff in the philosophy of consciousness. The setup is fixed: a red spot flashes, then a green spot flashes a short distance away, and subjects report seeing a single spot move across the gap and change color roughly halfway along its path. The Stalinesque account says the experience itself was edited before it entered consciousness — the subject really did consciously see the moving, color-changing spot, because conscious experience arrives late enough to incorporate the second flash. The Orwellian account says the subject first consciously saw the red spot, then the green spot, and only afterward had the memory revised into the smooth, color-changing motion; the edit is in memory, not in experience. Both stories make the same predictions about everything a subject can report or do, and no experiment has separated them. One influential diagnosis, associated with Daniel Dennett, is that the dispute is a distinction without a difference, an artifact of picturing consciousness as a single place or moment where experience is "final." I lean toward that diagnosis, but I would like it sharpened. Is this a factual disagreement that merely happens to be untestable, a verbal disagreement about where experience ends and memory begins, or a difference about what an explanation of the report must do? And on your diagnosis, what would count as progress — an experiment, a formal result, a conceptual distinction, or a principled decision to dissolve the question?
