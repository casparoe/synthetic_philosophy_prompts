I am a PhD student in moral psychology, and I keep running into the guilt–shame distinction in different literatures. The usual contrast is this: guilt negatively appraises an act and tends to motivate repair, while shame negatively appraises the whole self and tends to motivate withdrawal or reputation management. I want your credence in a proposition, not a survey of positions. The proposition: a society with a functioning legal system could, within about one generation and without coercion, deliberately shift its moral-emotional regime from guilt-based to shame-based socialization, and the shift would on balance improve morally relevant outcomes such as reparation, norm compliance, and reduced repeat wrongdoing. I am at roughly 0.4; a colleague in social psychology is at roughly 0.7. Give your number, then decompose it into the sub-questions that carry the weight: the probability that guilt and shame are distinct enough to be independently targeted; the probability that their standard motivational profiles are stable across cultural contexts rather than artifacts of a particular modern sample; the probability that social arrangements run upstream of the emotions rather than downstream or epiphenomenally; and the probability that the improvement standard is objective enough for the proposition to be truth-evaluable at all. Assign each a rough probability with a one-line justification, state how you combine them, say which of us you are closer to, and identify the one sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to and what evidence would most move it.
