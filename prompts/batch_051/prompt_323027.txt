I keep encountering the expression "the ghost in the machine" in philosophy of mind discussions, and I want to know what, precisely, it is supposed to amount to.

The most recent occasion was a reading group on Ryle's diagnosis of category mistakes. I suggested that a voluntary action should be analysed as a bodily movement plus a distinct inner act of will that initiates it. Another participant dismissed this by saying I was "still looking for the ghost in the machine." He plainly took the phrase to be a refutation of my proposal, but when I asked what the flaw in it was supposed to be, he only repeated the phrase, and I could not tell whether he meant a logical point about categories, an explanatory point about vacuous causes, or a deflationary point about inner episodes generally.

Lay out, as a menu of distinct readings, the precise claims that "the ghost in the machine" could be making when it is used in that sort of context. For each candidate reading, indicate whether it is a near-platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or a claim that is probably false or confused; also note which readings are faithful to Ryle's own complaint about category mistakes, as opposed to more behaviourist, verificationist, or eliminativist glosses that have become attached to the slogan. Then say which of these readings would actually do the argumentative work my fellow participant needed the phrase to do against the proposal of a distinct inner act of will, and which readings, if that is what he meant, would overshoot or fall short of that target.

Please answer in British English.
