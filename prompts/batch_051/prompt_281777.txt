Take a state whose public-memory institutions work in three directions at once. In its schools, the curriculum treats the national past as a storehouse of founding struggles and exemplary figures: children memorize dates, reenact patriotic scenes, and are taught that the present generation inherits and must be worthy of a great past. In its preservation policy, the same state funds local historical societies, archives, landmark boards, and language-revival programs, with the stated aim of keeping old things intact for their own sake. In its reckoning policy, the same state periodically convenes truth commissions and issues official apologies that investigate and publicly condemn earlier institutions and figures, sometimes the very figures the school curriculum celebrates, in order to free the present from their continuing effects.

Use Nietzsche's triad as the lens. By monumental history, understand the use of the past that seeks examples of greatness in order to inspirit present action; by antiquarian history, the use that reveres and preserves the past for the sake of continuity with what has been; by critical history, the use that hauls the past before a tribunal and condemns it so that present life can judge and proceed. Reconstruct the philosophy of history this institutional pattern presupposes: does each of the three practices presuppose one of these modes, and do the three practices jointly presuppose a single view about how the past serves or harms present life? In particular, when the same state both celebrates founding figures and later condemns them, does that require a coherent account of why both the monumental and the critical relation to the same past serve life, or is the practice philosophically underdetermined among several incompatible accounts? Finally, say what would have to change in these institutions if one took seriously Nietzsche's warning that each mode has its characteristic pathology: monumental history hardening into myth that invites blind imitation, antiquarian history into preservation as an end in itself, critical history into a destructive habit of condemnation that no longer serves the living.
