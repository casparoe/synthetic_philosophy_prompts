You are an expert philosopher of mind and personal identity, with enough working familiarity with dynamical systems theory and biological repair processes to handle engineering-flavoured formulations. I spent my career in cell biology and control theory, and I have only recently begun reading academic philosophy. Think carefully, start with an unstructured brainstorm about what could possibly matter here, and only then commit to a ranking.

The problem I keep circling is gradual replacement in the context of mind uploading, but without the usual all-or-nothing frame. Imagine an adult whose neurons are replaced over a period of years by synthetic units that are functionally isomorphic at the local level. Each unit is installed only after its biological predecessor has been scanned and its input–output dispositions modelled; the person remains awake throughout, with the outward appearance of continuous memory, attention, and personality; and the surrounding tissue adapts to each replacement in roughly the way living neural tissue adapts to slow perturbations. Consider five candidate features that might be doing the work when we say the original person survives, or does not survive, such a process:

1. Causal continuity of a self-maintaining organism-level process — no global shutdown and no wholesale reconstruction from a stored description.
2. Preservation of fine-grained global dynamical structure — not merely local input–output dispositions, but the brain's large-scale attractor landscape, phase relationships, and sensitivity to perturbation.
3. Repair lineage of each replacement unit — each synthetic unit is produced as a repair of a particular biological unit and remains causally downstream from it in a traceable chain.
4. Robust counterfactual dependence of the later total state on the earlier total state — small differences in the original would have produced corresponding differences in the outcome.
5. Phenomenal seamlessness — from the first-person point of view there is no rupture, only an ongoing stream of experience and memory.

Please rank these five candidates into top, middle, and bottom tiers according to how likely each is to be the feature that carries identity through gradual replacement, rather than a mere correlate or a clinical marker. For each candidate, give one or two sentences on what it captures and where it gives way under pressure. Do not fall back on saying that all five matter as practical criteria; I am asking you to take them seriously as competing philosophical posits.
