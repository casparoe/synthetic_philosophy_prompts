I am a novelist. Before each novel I generate several possible story-worlds for the same material, and I must choose one. My usual criterion is whether a story pleases me or has an earned ending, but I suspect that is not the right test. In the Theodicy and the Monadology, Leibniz argues that God actualizes the best of all possible worlds not by maximizing local pleasure or moral comfort, but by actualizing the world that holds together the greatest amount of reality: the most variety, order, and composable perfection. I want that principle turned into a decision procedure I can run at the outline stage to choose among candidate fictional worlds as if they were Leibnizian possibles. Please write the procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to me as a judgment call, and where it declares two worlds incomparable. Keep it usable in a week at my desk, without technical vocabulary. Then run it on these three candidates for a novel about an island community that must either build a sea wall or relocate inland. World A: the wall is built; the village survives but becomes a static museum-enclave; the plot follows a local engineer whose success costs him his family. I naturally prefer this world because it has a clean tragic arc. World B: the settlement is abandoned; the community disperses; the novel follows four characters whose lives become more varied and capacious in diaspora, but the island culture disappears. I find this world bleak and resist it. World C: the islanders attempt a floating settlement that half-works and half-fails; the novel is sprawling, with no single hero and no resolution. I suspect this world may be richest in composable variety, but it feels unmanageable. Show me what the procedure says, and mark where it cannot decide.
