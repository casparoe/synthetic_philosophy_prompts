I have been trying to formalize a heuristic I rely on when thinking about data—the presumption of independence—and I have worked through an argument that seems to show the presumption leads to conclusions I know are unwarranted. I would like you to examine the argument step by step.

A researcher screens 1,000 candidate pairs of genes, testing each pair for statistical interaction with a phenotype. Her working rule is that each pair is presumed independent until the data strongly contradict that presumption. For each pair she computes a p-value under the null hypothesis of no interaction, which she identifies with independence. One pair, call it pair 17, yields p = 0.001. She reasons as follows.

(1) The presumption of independence says: for each pair, accept the null hypothesis of independence unless the data strongly contradict it.

(2) Under the null hypothesis of independence for pair 17, a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed has probability 0.001; this is what p = 0.001 means.

(3) A probability of 0.001 is very small, so the result for pair 17 was very unlikely under the null hypothesis of independence.

(4) Therefore, the result strongly contradicts the null hypothesis of independence for pair 17.

(5) Therefore, by her working rule, the researcher should reject the presumption of independence for pair 17.

Each of these steps seems plausible to me, and (4) is exactly the kind of move I habitually make with p-values. But I also notice this: if all 1,000 pairs are in fact independent, then, assuming the 1,000 tests behave independently under the null, the probability that at least one pair yields p ≤ 0.001 is 1 − 0.999^1000, roughly 0.63. So the procedure would reject the presumption of independence for some pair most of the time even when every pair is genuinely independent. That makes me think one of the steps above is doing something illegitimate, but I cannot say exactly which premise to amend or what the corrected principle should be.

Please tell me which numbered step, if any, is the first one that fails, and why. If the fault is better described as a misapplication of one of the steps rather than the falsity of its explicit wording, say that. Then state the corrected principle, and explain whether an uncorrected single-test p-value ever suffices to overturn a presumption of independence in a screening study. End your reply with a final line containing only \answer{step N} for the first failing step, or \answer{valid} if the argument is valid as stated.
