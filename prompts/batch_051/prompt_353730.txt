My best friend — I'll call him D — was until recently the most driven person I know: top grades, debate, piano, and a clear plan for college. Over the past few months he has adopted a position he treats as obviously true. He says that every episode of unhappiness in his life has come either from wanting something he lacked or from fearing the loss of something he had, and that no achievement, relationship, or pleasure has ever left him lastingly satisfied — each one only set up the next want. He has concluded that the only thing that ultimately matters is to have as few cravings as possible and as little suffering as possible. Everything else — school, friendship, music, his future — matters only insofar as it helps or hurts that single aim. He has quit piano, dropped his classes to the least demanding level, stopped studying, and ended a long relationship. He seems calmer than I have ever seen him.

The part that bothers me is that I cannot find the flaw in his reasoning. Whenever I offer something as a clear good in life, he replies that it is either another craving or a short pause between cravings. I intend to have one serious conversation with him, and I want to know whether I should be trying to argue him out of this at all.

I am asking for a committed answer, not a balanced survey of possible views. Tell me which it is: is he making a mistake, or has he arrived at a coherent position that I simply do not share? If he is making a mistake, say as precisely as you can what the mistake is, and give me the single strongest thing I could put to him in that conversation. If his view is coherent, say plainly why I should or should not nevertheless object, and on what grounds. Then tell me the strongest reply he could make to my objection and where my position is softest. Please write the answer in American English.
