I'm a novelist. A few years ago my late mentor's unfinished novel turned into a proper mess: the estate and a publisher were negotiating a "completed" edition while a fan community was circulating their own ending online, and nobody could agree on weather the fan version was tribute or theft. I ended up with strong feelings on both sides, which is why I want to see what two good-faith camps would actually put their names to after a real collaboration, not who wins.

The two camps I have in mind:

- The Stewards: once the person or entity who could exercise a creative property right is gone — the author dead with no traceable heirs, a studio dissolved with no successor, someone who has publicly washed their hands of the work — the work should stop being treated as owned in any morally load-bearing way. What remains is a custodial duty: preserve the thing, attribute it honestly, don't misrepresent it, but otherwise let people revive it, patch it, re-release it, finish it. They'll point to abandoned software that only survives because fans kept maintaining it.

- The Trustees: an absent owner is not the same as no owner. The unfinished novel still belongs to someone — the author's heirs, the estate, the chain of title however messy — and making derivative works without authorization is still using somebody else's property, even if nobody's around to complain. They'll grant that keeping an orphaned book in print might be one thing, but a stranger writing its final third is another.

Draft the joint statement of a year-long adversarial collaboration between these two camps. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table, then a cruxes section, then the tests.

Specifically:

1. The agreed claims should be substantive and phrased so a hardliner on either side would actually sign — not "both sides love art." If the camps have been talking past each other because they use a word differently (I suspect "ownership" and "abandonment" are two), the statement should sort that out explicitly.

2. The disagreements table should lay out each residual disagreement seperately, in neutral framing, with each side's own view worded the way that side would accept — no strawmanning.

3. The cruxes should be the sub-questions that, if settled, would genuinely move at least one side.

4. For each crux, give a concrete case or test both camps agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about that case.

Things I'd love the document to actually work through, because they kept knotting up our arguments: the difference between preserving a work and building a new one out of its parts; whether the dead author's known wishes carry weight and whether they can ever be outweighed by living readers; whether a defunct studio is morally different from a dead person; and whether "no one is harmed" is a real argument or just a way of not thinking about the harm. It's fine if the final statement leaves some of these open, but each open one should show up as an exact disagreement or a crux, not vanish into euphemism.
