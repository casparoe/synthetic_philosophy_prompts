I just had a long argument with a friend about imaginative resistance. He kept insisting that when readers balk at a story that stipulates, say, that a character's gratuitous cruelty is beautiful or morally right, they are merely squeamish: they don't want to imagine it, not that they are unable to. I lean the other way. The resistance seems to track a real difference between descriptive stipulations, which we accept almost automatically, and evaluative stipulations, which we treat as though the author lacked authority to make them. But the argument left me unsure whether the phenomenon is a limit on imagination, a limit on authorial authority, or a principled refusal to let a fictional norm leak into real judgment — and I don't think the can't-versus-won't question can be settled by simply asking people how it felt.

Design a 75-minute activity for an upper-level aesthetics seminar of 14 students. The activity should have them accept a tight series of descriptive stipulations and then confront evaluative ones, arranged so that the resistance, and whatever distinguishes the two cases, shows up in what the participants do or refuse to do, before the facilitator names it. By the end they should come away seeing the phenomenon as a problem about authority or permission rather than mere lack of acquaintance with the imagined content.
