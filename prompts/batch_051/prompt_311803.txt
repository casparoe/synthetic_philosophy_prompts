I am chairing a working group on well-being and public policy, and after two years of argument the group has sorted itself into two camps. One camp — call them the ladder realists — holds that the annual world-happiness rankings are measuring something real and reasonably comparable across countries. Respondents are asked to place their lives on a 0-to-10 ladder anchored at the worst possible life and the best possible life; the national score is an average of those self-assessments; and the usual reporting explains country differences with six factors: income, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom, generosity, and low perceived corruption. On their view, a country that improves those factors and rises in the table is doing something genuinely worthwhile, and treating a high rank as a domestic goal is no more misguided than treating low unemployment as one. The other camp — call them the construct skeptics — holds that this is a rank built from a single, culturally loaded self-report item that asks respondents to judge their lives against an undefined best possible life, with country differences then decomposed into explanatory factors only distally related to anything anyone means by happiness. For them, chasing the ranking is a Goodhart-style category error: an institution instructed to maximize a score will learn to maximize the score, not the thing the score was meant to proxy.

The surprise is how much the two camps already agree on beneath the heat, and how many of their rows were terminological. I want the document they should have written together: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration. It should open with a numbered agreed-claims section whose claims are substantive, not just procedural — about what the rankings literally are, what the ladder item can and cannot capture, what a country-level average of self-reports does and does not tell you, and what tends to happen to any measure that becomes an official target. It should then set out the residual disagreements, each one phrased in shared neutral terms but with the precise wording each side would accept for its own view; no strawmen and no unearned concessions. Finally, it should identify the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — and, for each crux, specify a concrete case, dataset, or policy trial that both camps agree bears on it, recording each camp's prediction in advance.

My own suspicion is that the real cruxes are, first, whether the ladder item recovers a stable life evaluation or constructs a momentarily available judgment on the spot; and second, whether optimizing the six predictors after they have been adopted as targets would still track whatever the ladder measures. You need not confine the cruxes to my two, but please state them precisely enough that the camps are no longer arguing past each other about whether a ranking is a measurement problem or a policy problem.
