I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and my dissertation rehearses the dispute between mechanist and anti-mechanist accounts of mathematical thought. I have come to accept a fairly specific version of the Gödelian anti-mechanist conclusion associated with Lucas and Penrose. The version I hold is this: for any consistent formal system F that I can understand, I can in principle follow the Gödel construction for F and thereby come to recognize the truth of an arithmetical sentence — F's Gödel sentence, or the statement of F's consistency — that F cannot prove, so long as F is in fact consistent. From this I draw the conclusion that no single such formal system, and by extension no single Turing machine, has a set of arithmetical outputs coinciding with the set of arithmetical sentences I can in principle recognize as true. That is the position I would like stress-tested. I am not claiming that this establishes an immaterial mind, nor that every aspect of human cognition is in-principle uncomputable; I am claiming only that my capacity for mathematical recognition outruns any one consistent formal system that can be presented to me.

What would count against this position? Please map its exposure rather than mounting a one-sided attack or defense. Concretely: which premises or inferential steps would have to fail for my conclusion to be unsupported, and for each, what would the world — or the human reasoner — have to look like for that to be the actual point of failure? I want these sorted by how decisive they would be: distinguish considerations that would be fatal to the view from those that would merely force a qualification. I also want to know which defeaters are live given what we currently know about human cognition, logic, and computational systems, and which are merely logically possible escape routes. What should a committed defender of the Gödelian conclusion concede counts against it — for example, idealizations about my own consistency, the gap between the reasoner I can in principle be and the fallible mathematician I actually am, or the suspicion that the argument trades on an equivocation between the system and the reasoner reflecting on the system? And I want an honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated against refutation by those concessions, and whether that would be a problem for a view of this kind. If I were trying to decide whether to abandon the view, which one or two kinds of evidence or argument should move me most?
