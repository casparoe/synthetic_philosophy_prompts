I'm in an IP-adjacent seminar and also do freelance dev work, so I've hit both sides of this. The problem is orphan works and abandoned software: stuff that's clearly still owned but where the owner is unreachable, so nobody can legally use, fix, or maintain it. A classmate floated this registry idea and I can't decide if its clever or a slow-motion trainwreck, and the TA wouldn't engage with it.

The mechanism: a public-ish nonprofit runs a use-and-escrow registry. To use an orphaned work or fork a dead piece of software, you first run a documented search according to the registry's checklist — copyright office records, package managers, WHOIS/DNS history, obituaries, company filings, and so on — and submit the log. If the registry accepts it as diligent, you pay a flat fee and then 2% of gross revenue the work generates over the next 10 years into an escrow account tied to that specific work. If nobody successfully claims it within 10 years, the work gets a default registry license: anyone can use it with attribution, and the accumulated escrow, minus admin costs, goes into a fund for preserving other dead media. If a previously unreachable owner does show up before the 10 years are up, they get the entire escrow and then choose either (a) to keep your use going under a standard license and keep collecting the 2%, or (b) to shut your use down, but you're immune from damages for past use and get a six-month wind-down. For software specifically, any patches or derivative changes you make while using it must be deposited in the registry; if no owner emerges, those get released under the same default license. Non-registration doesn't shield anyone: using an orphan work outside the registry still counts as ordinary infringement.

Analyze this as a mechanism, not a moral question. Who would actually use it, and who would avoid it? What does it incentivize among users, among genuinely unreachable owners, and among owners who are findable but stay quiet or only surface in year nine? How would it get gamed — shell claims, search-laundering, owners deliberately vanishing to run out the clock, or the registry quietly colluding with repeat users? What happens to the definition of a diligent search after a few years of adversarial use, and where does the whole thing break first?
