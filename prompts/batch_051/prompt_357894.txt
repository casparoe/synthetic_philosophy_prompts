Consider the following reform proposal. A country currently has an established church, and its tax authority collects a church tax from registered members, with membership recorded mainly through infant baptism. A commission proposes to abolish formal establishment while keeping the state in the business of collection and the churches in the business of welfare provision. The new mechanism works as follows. Every adult taxpayer is automatically enrolled in a solidarity levy equal to 1.5% of their income-tax liability; the levy can be avoided only by filing an opt-out, which must be renewed each tax year and cannot be filed through ordinary tax-return software. The proceeds go by default to the religious community in which the taxpayer's parents registered them at birth, or to a secular public welfare fund if no such registration exists. A taxpayer may instead direct the whole levy to any registered beneficiary — religious or secular — or split it among at most three, but the levy is never refundable to the taxpayer. To become a registered beneficiary, an organization must certify that at least 40% of its annual expenditure goes to hospital, burial, poverty-relief, or refugee services that are open to everyone regardless of membership, must publish audited accounts, and must not donate to parties or candidates. The registering authority is legally barred from assessing doctrine, so any organization satisfying these tests qualifies. The state publishes aggregate beneficiary shares annually but never publishes individual designations. Treat this as a mechanism rather than a moral position: what would it incentivize among the formerly established church, minority religious bodies, secular charities, and incumbent politicians? How would each actor try to game it? Where is the mechanism most likely to fail — administratively, fiscally, or in its effects on religious membership and practice — and which of those failures could be patched without collapsing the scheme into either full separation or a de facto re-establishment?
