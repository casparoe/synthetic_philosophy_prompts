One of my students is writing a paper on monetary institutions and keeps invoking Aristotle's discussion of money in Nicomachean Ethics V.5, where Aristotle says that money came to be as a guarantee for future exchange, acts by convention as a representative of demand, and makes goods commensurable by measuring them against a single standard. The student claims that the modern practice of central-bank inflation targeting, combined with courts' enforcement of nominal debt contracts, presupposes that Aristotle was right: a common currency really does make heterogeneous goods commensurable. I am not sure the practice presupposes anything that strong. Walking through the practice concretely—a debtor must repay the face value of a loan in legal tender even if the currency's purchasing power has changed, and the central bank treats that currency as the unit of account while also acknowledging that its value drifts—what implicit theory of value and commensurability is actually built into it? Does it presuppose that money succeeds in making distinct needs commensurable, or only that it is instrumentally necessary to act as if it does? Does it also presuppose that exchange value is conventional rather than natural, and are all parts of the practice consistent on that point? I would like a committed answer: reconstruct the implicit theory, say which rival accounts of money's function the practice rules out, and identify any places where the practice is underdetermined between Aristotle's stronger and weaker claims.
