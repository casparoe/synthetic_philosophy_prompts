I direct the program team at a private foundation that makes about thirty-five grants a year in education, mental health, criminal justice, and organizational development. We are revising our pre-award screening because we keep funding methodologically polished projects whose findings never seem to change practice in the field. In his 1974 Caltech address "Cargo Cult Science," Richard Feynman writes: "Then a method was discovered for separating the ideas—which was to try one to see if it worked, and if it didn’t work, to eliminate it." We want that sentence turned into a reusable decision procedure. Specifically: produce a checklist my program officers can run in one hour, without a statistician, before a grant goes to the board. The checklist should determine whether a proposed project, or the field it belongs to, has a genuine error-correcting loop—a mechanism by which a wrong guess is likely to be found out and discarded—rather than only the outward forms of testing. It must handle delayed, noisy, and indirect feedback, and it must flag the steps that are judgment calls rather than mechanical tests. Then apply the checklist to three compressed proposals we are actually arguing about: (1) a teacher-training program whose main outcomes are teacher self-report and observed fidelity to the new technique, with no measure of student learning; (2) a preregistered randomized trial of cognitive-behavioral therapy for probationers, with all outcomes reported, including null results; (3) a ten-year study of restorative justice in a single city, without a comparison group, that will collect interviews, administrative records, and school discipline data. Include at least one case where your procedure returns an answer my colleagues will not like. The deliverable should be a step-by-step checklist a nonspecialist can use repeatedly, with the hard cases marked and an honest statement of what the procedure cannot do.
