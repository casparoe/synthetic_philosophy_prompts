You are a professional philosopher who works on the theory of interpretation and the semantics of written texts. Please think carefully about the structure of the disagreement before you answer. Begin by reconstructing, as charitably as you can, what each side must minimally commit to, and before settling on a diagnosis, sketch at least two candidate diagnoses and test each against the constructed case below.

A debate over authorial intention and textual meaning has run for decades without much movement. One camp—call them intentionalists—holds that the meaning of a written text is fixed by what its author intended it to mean: interpretation is fundamentally the recovery of an author's communicative intention, and a text means what its writer meant by it. The other camp—anti-intentionalists—holds that once a text is made public, its meaning is fixed by what the text itself licenses a competent reader to attribute, given the relevant linguistic conventions and publicly available context; the author's private, unexpressed, or unsuccessfully realized intentions are at most clues, not constituents, of the text's meaning. Each side has stable replies to the obvious counterexamples. Intentionalists say an anonymous pamphlet has a determinate meaning because some actual person made it with some intention, which we may recover only imperfectly; anti-intentionalists say that a poet's hidden pun, which no competent reader using only the public text and its context could recover, is not part of the poem's meaning, however plainly it was part of the poet's mental life.

The puzzle is not merely that the two sides disagree about cases. It is that the disagreement returns even after every fact about a case has been stipulated. Each side treats the other as making a conceptual mistake rather than an empirical one, and each accuses the other of changing the subject.

My working hypothesis is that the two camps are using the word "meaning" to pick out two different things, and that the real dispute is over which thing should own the word. On this picture, intentionalists want "meaning" to name what a writer communicates by an act of writing, so that interpretation is a form of purpose-recovery; anti-intentionalists want it to name what a publicly available text licenses any suitably placed reader to understand, so that interpretation is a form of rule-following applied to an artifact. But I am not confident, because the two notions can genuinely conflict rather than pass each other by, and I want that conflict examined rather than papered over.

Consider a constructed case the standard examples do not settle. A writer drafts a paragraph arguing that a certain policy is legitimate. A second person, who never sees the draft, deletes every third sentence and replaces it with a sentence produced by a fixed mechanical rule that takes no account of content. By chance, the resulting text is grammatical; a competent reader cannot tell it has been altered, and the text now appears to argue that the policy is illegitimate. The original writer later reads the altered text without knowing about the alteration and says, "That is exactly what I meant to say." Is the meaning of the resulting text the author's now-avowed intention, the compound causal process that produced the marks, or what a competent reader would recover from the public text? Different answers seem to track different prior choices about what meaning is, which is precisely what makes me suspect we are watching a disguised dispute about the role of a concept rather than a dispute about any fact.
