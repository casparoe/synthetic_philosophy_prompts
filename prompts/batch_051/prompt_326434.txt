Consider the claim that a single event—this particular house flooding next year—has a probability. Three interpretations of probability suggest different criteria for that claim. On the frequentist criterion, the event must fall under one privileged reference class, and its probability is the limiting frequency of such outcomes in that class. On the subjectivist criterion, the probability is a coherent, precise degree of belief that an agent actually holds; it is a feature of the agent's outlook, and different agents with the same evidence may rationally assign different values. On the propensity criterion, the relevant chance set-up must itself possess a stable objective disposition of determinate strength, independent of what anyone believes.

Now take this borderline case. An insurer charges a premium corresponding to a 0.04 probability that one particular house on a river bluff will flood in the next year. The house belongs to several seemingly natural reference classes with sharply different historical flood rates: houses in the county (0.005), houses on this river (0.02), houses on this bluff at this elevation (0.06), and houses of this age with this foundation type (0.09). The river's flow is chaotic and increasingly altered by upstream construction, and two hydrologists looking at the same data regard 0.02 and 0.08 as equally reasonable. Does this house have a probability of flooding next year? Answer criterion by criterion, note where each criterion underdetermines the verdict, and say whether the case shows that the criteria—or the phrase "the probability of this event"—need revision. Finally, state which argument or evidence would most likely change your verdict.
