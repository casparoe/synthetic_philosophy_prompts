I'm taking a political philosophy course on nationalism and secession this term, and I've ended up with an argument that started as a defence of Catalan and Scottish independence and now seems to prove way too much. I can't locate the step I should give up.

(1) A people has a right of self-determination, which includes the right to choose its own political status up to and including independence. (2) Scotland, Catalonia and Quebec are peoples by any reasonable criterion — distinct language, institutions, history, territory, and a sense of themselves as a political community. (3) So if a clear majority of such a people votes for independence in a free and fair referendum, the parent state is morally required not to block it.

The trouble is that (3) doesn't actually lean on the thick features in (2). It seems to apply just as well to a region of Catalonia where a majority voted to stay inside Spain, which could then hold its own vote to secede from the new Catalan state; to a single wealthy city that votes to leave its country; to a neighbourhood that votes to secede from the city. At each stage the local minority of the previous stage re-describes itself as a people and invokes the same principle, so secession could go all the way down. (4) That would make any stable political order impossible, which no plausible theory of political morality accepts. (5) So either (1) is false, or there is a principled difference between a nation and a neighbourhood majority, and I can't state that difference without it looking gerrymandered to deliver the cases I liked.

There's also a circularity bugging me. To know whether a referendum shoud count, you first have to know who 'the people' is; but that is exactly what the referendum is supposed to settle. If the seceding region gets to define its own electorate, everyone can secede; if the existing state gets to define it, nobody can. Some people in my seminar say self-determination only grounds secession as a remedy for serious oppression, but the states in question are broadly liberal democracies, so that would just make all three original cases illegitimate, which feels like throwing out the baby with the bathwater. Which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (2) than (1), but maybe that's just sentiment.
