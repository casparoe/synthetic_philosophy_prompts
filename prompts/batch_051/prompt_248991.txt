My friend Dana has stronger opinions then anyone I know about when partiality is a virtue and when it's just a bias. For years I've been trying to reverse-engineer the rule she's running, and I've kept a list of her verdicts because I can't extract the principle from them. Here's the list I'm most confident in:

- She says it was totally fine to skip the river-cleanup shift you signed up for so you could help a close friend through a last-minute move on eviction day.
- She says a café owner was fine to give a part-time counter job to her nephew without advertising the opening, even though posting it would probably have turned up someone more experienced.
- She says an alum was fine to endow a scholarship restricted to graduates of her own mediocre high school rather than put the money into a general fund for first-generation college students, saying people are allowed to love their own.
- She says a mayor was wrong to appoint her brother to a parks job over a stranger who scored far higher on the civil-service exam.
- She says a professor was wrong to give the single paid lab spot for the summer to her own research assistant, who had a decent but not outstanding record, over a stronger outside applicant, on the grounds that the RA had been loyal.
- She says a surgeon was wrong to move a friend a few spots up a liver-transplant list even though the difference in medical urgency was small.
- She says a juror was wrong to vote to acquit a friend who was, on the evidence, guilty of assault, because she couldn't stand the thought of him in prison.

The new case is this. A different café owner posts a barista job openly, interviews four strangers, and hires her cousin, who has six months of café experience, over a stranger with four years of experience and much better references. There's no formal hiring policy, it's an at-will state, and nothing illegal is going on. The stranger is clearly more qualified on paper and ends up unemployed for two more months.

I can see at least two rules that fit Dana's track record. One says partiality is fine in private, discretionary domains and only becomes a bias once you're in an institutional role with explicit impartial standards—civil-service hiring, grading, transplant queues, juries. The other says partiality is fine as long as it doesn't deprive a specific outsider of something they'd otherwise have had a fair shot at, which would make the new hire wrong. Does she bless the new case? Give me a prediction, the best statement of the principle she's really running, and one additional case that would cleanly separate the candidate rules.
