I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been spending some of my free time trying to understand the technical side of AI alignment. The area I keep returning to is low-impact AI: the project of building agents that can pursue a goal without making large changes to the world beyond what the goal requires, so that a misspecified goal causes bounded damage. The part I most want to understand — and the part where I would like to make a contribution if I can — is the definition of impact itself. Before you can penalize side effects, you need a way to measure them, and it is not obvious how to define a measure of side effects without first solving the whole problem of what humans value. If you already knew exactly which changes are bad side effects, you would already know what to optimize for. In software terms, a function's side effects are defined relative to a specification of its intended interface; here there is no specification of the world's intended state, so it is unclear what the analogue of the interface even is.

From an engineering perspective this looks like a dependency problem: several other open problems in the area seem to need an impact measure as a prerequisite, but perhaps some of them need only a weak or partial notion of impact, or perhaps some do not need one at all. I want a prioritized agenda rather than a survey. I am not looking for a reading list or citations; I want your own analysis.

Here are the candidate problems I can already see, though you should feel free to add ones I am missing:

1. The baseline problem. Impact has to be measured relative to something — a counterfactual in which the agent did nothing, a known-safe default policy, or a set of acceptable alternative actions. How should that baseline be chosen without importing a full account of what matters, and when do different baseline choices change the resulting measure in practice?

2. The representation problem. Should impact be measured over the full physical state of the environment, over a learned compressed or feature representation, or over a small set of designated audited variables? Any choice can miss side effects that fall outside the representation, so the question is which choice fails safest and what guarantees can be given about off-representation effects.

3. The aggregation and composition problem. Should impact be attributed to individual actions, to short time windows, to trajectories, to policies, or to capabilities? Can a global impact budget be decomposed into per-subsystem or per-objective budgets and recombined with meaningful guarantees, or is impact inherently holistic?

4. Reversibility and optionality as a proxy. Could impact be defined not in terms of how bad a change is but in terms of how hard it is to undo and how much it shrinks the set of future options available to the agent or to humans? This is appealing because it might sidestep the need for a full value theory, but it may also misclassify certain kinds of change.

5. The effect of information and exploration. Should acquiring information or exploring count as impact? If it does, low-impact agents may be uselessly passive; if it does not, you may need a separate story about why information-seeking is or is not dangerous.

The prioritization I am asking for is anchored to a concrete situation. I can give the problem about eight hours a week for the next year, mostly evenings and weekends. I can build and test small toy environments, formalize subproblems precisely enough to code them, and produce a small open-source artifact such as a benchmark, a counterexample, or a clean implementation of one measure. I cannot produce novel theoretical mathematics, and I do not want to spend the year writing literature reviews.

Which of these open problems — or another one I have not listed — is most worth settling first, in the sense that the largest number of other questions in low-impact AI depend on its answer? Give me a dependency-aware prioritization: which problems are upstream of which, which are tractable under my constraints, which you would deprioritize because settling them would not change much downstream, and what would most change your ranking if the assumptions behind it turned out to be false. Make the criteria you are using explicit — for example importance, tractability, neglectedness, and dependency — and defend the trade-offs between them.
