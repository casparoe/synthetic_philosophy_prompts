In the value-theory portion of our department seminar we have spent much of the term on the problem of moral dilemmas, and I have left the discussion with a suspicion I would like tested rather than confirmed. The two camps in the room are, roughly, the no-dilemma camp and the tragic-conflict camp. The no-dilemma camp takes apparent dilemmas to be an artifact of incomplete information or an unfinished ranking of obligations: on any completed moral picture, they insist, one demand weighs more than the other and there is always a unique all-things-considered ought, even if limited agents cannot always identify it. The tragic-conflict camp points to cases — the combatant who leaves a dependent parent to oppose an occupying army, the official who can save only one of two parties, the physician with two patients and one ventilator — and says that in these cases something is violated no matter what the agent does, that the agent is not merely uncertain but genuinely subject to two real demands, and that the regret or residual guilt that attends even the best available choice is evidence of an unmet obligation. A third position surfaces in the margins: that obligations can conflict as reasons while the all-things-considered ought remains contradiction-free, so the so-called dilemma dissolves into a conflict of considerations rather than of verdicts. This middle position is often treated as obviously correct in our room, and its adequacy has never really been tested.

What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict on which camp is right. Is this a verbal dispute about the word ought — all-things-considered ought versus pro tanto ought — or a substantive dispute about whether obligations come in a complete commensurable ranking? Is it a disagreement about whether retrospective emotions like agent-regret are evidence of anything, a hidden formal question about whether ought A and ought B can each hold while ought (A and B) does not, or a conflict of bedrock intuitions about tragic cases that neither side could argue for without begging the question? Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a sharper account of moral remainder, a change of question, or something else. If the right diagnosis is that nothing should be expected to move, say so and say why, but do not settle for that conclusion unless the structure of the stalemate actually forces it.

My working hypothesis is that the two camps are not arguing about one question. The no-dilemma camp, I suspect, is really theorizing about what the all-things-considered verdict of a competent agent must look like, while the tragic-conflict camp is theorizing about the structure of the demands themselves, and each side's notion of obligation is already loaded in its own favor. I would be dissatisfied with a diagnosis that just says the dispute is verbal or that the sides are talking past one another; I want the specific difference in what each side means by obligation, or in what each side treats as data, or in what each side requires an adequate account to preserve, and an account of where exactly the two positions come apart.
