I serve on the research ethics committee of a teaching hospital. We meet twice a month and must usually reach a decision on each protocol in a single sitting; our decisions are binding, are written up, and can be overturned only by an appeal panel. The committee works from a short code of four principles:

1. Consent: adult participants must give free and informed consent; research on those who cannot consent requires surrogate consent plus additional safeguards.
2. Risk: risks to participants must be minimized and reasonable in relation to expected benefit; no protocol may impose serious irreversible harm on a participant purely for the benefit of others.
3. Fair selection: no group may bear a disproportionate share of research burdens without a proportionate share of benefits.
4. Honesty: deception is permissible only when the study cannot be done otherwise, the harm is minimal, and debriefing is immediate.

Several times a year, our reaction to a concrete protocol conflicts with what the principles say. A rescue-style trial makes us want to loosen the risk principle; an online manipulation we would normally regard as trivial is forbidden by the honesty principle as written; a study of an illegal practice divides us between a protective rejection and the charge that we are being paternalistic. The discussion then becomes unstructured: one member says the principle is too blunt, another says the case reaction is mere salience or squeamishness, a third warns that approving this case would commit us, by parity, to cases we previously rejected.

We need a reusable written procedure for deciding, when a case judgment and an endorsed principle conflict, whether we should (a) follow the principle and set aside the case judgment as unreliable, (b) amend or replace the principle, or (c) allow the case as an exception while leaving the principle in place. The procedure must be usable in under fifteen minutes per case by members with no philosophical training. It should draw on philosophical work on reflective equilibrium, on the debate between generalism and particularism about whether principles or case judgments are more trustworthy, and on what simplicity and transitivity or parity constraints demand when principles get amended. In particular, we need:

- a way to tell when a case reaction is likely distorted by vividness, identifiability, omission bias, or moralized disgust, rather than being a genuine datum;
- a way to tell whether a principle is earning its keep or is just a summary of our favorite cases with a protective coating;
- a test for whether an amendment to a principle is a genuine refinement or an ad hoc patch that would make the code gerrymandered;
- a parity or commitment check that tells us what else we are committed to if we settle a case the way we are inclined to;
- explicit markers for the steps at which judgment cannot be eliminated and a person has to take responsibility.

Then apply the procedure to these three protocols:

1. A first-in-human dose-finding trial for an advanced fatal disease. Participants have exhausted standard treatment. The intervention carries a substantial risk of irreversible organ damage with little expected direct benefit at the doses tested; its value is mostly information for future patients. The committee strongly wants to approve, on the ground that these patients have no acceptable alternatives; the risk principle, read literally, would reject it.

2. An online study that gives some participants false feedback that they scored in the bottom 10 percent on a loneliness scale, in order to study how acute social distress affects generosity. The manipulation is brief, mild, and followed by a full debrief; consent omits the false feedback because knowing would defeat the manipulation. The honesty principle, read literally, would reject it.

3. An interview study with people who inject illegal drugs, evaluating a harm-reduction program. Participants face a small additional legal and social risk from being connected with the study, but the community strongly supports the research. Some members want to reject it under the risk and fair-selection principles in order to protect participants; others answer that the participants themselves judge the risk to be worth taking. We also need to know whether the procedure requires us to reopen a previous decision in which we rejected a similar observational study with undocumented residents on protective grounds.

Begin directly with the procedure itself, opening with a one-sentence statement of its default decision rule and then the numbered steps; no preface and no restating of the question. After the procedure, give the reasoning behind the steps, and then work through the three cases, including at least one case in which the procedure gives an answer we will not like. End by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do.
