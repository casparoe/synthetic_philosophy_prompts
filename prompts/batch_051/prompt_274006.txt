A familiar argument in debates about how much risk a society should tolerate runs like this: resources for safety are finite, so every measure chosen has an opportunity cost in the safety measures not bought elsewhere; the right question is therefore comparative—whether the measure reduces total expected harm on net—rather than whether some particular risk is acceptable in isolation. The argument needs at least two things to be true: that the relevant harms can be put on a common enough scale for the comparison to make sense, and that decision-makers can tell when a measure controls a risk rather than merely moving it to people the metric does not see. Now consider a city where public safety is managed by real-time scoring models. Phone-location data, camera feeds, and transaction records are combined to assign every person, crowd, and event a continuously updated probability of being involved in serious violence, and police are dispatched automatically to wherever the product of probability and expected severity is highest. The system is defended as the purest version of the comparative argument: it optimizes expected harm in real time. Does the familiar comparative argument survive this development? If it does, say which premise still does the work and what would have to be true for the city's system to inherit it. If it does not, identify exactly where the argument breaks and say what principle should guide the city instead. Answer in no more than 750 words.
