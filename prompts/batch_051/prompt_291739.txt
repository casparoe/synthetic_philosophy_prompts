The exam question was:

"Define metaphysical grounding, and distinguish it from causation and from supervenience. Then, for each of the following candidate grounding claims, say whether it is true or false and give a one-sentence reason: (1) the fact that the rose is crimson grounds the fact that the rose is red; (2) the fact that the rose is red grounds the fact that the rose is crimson; (3) the fact that p and q grounds the fact that p; (4) the fact that p grounds the fact that p or q; (5) the fact that the atoms are arranged table-wise grounds the fact that there is a table."

Here's the student's answer, slightly condensed from the original:

"Grounding is the non-causal 'in virtue of' relation: a fact is grounded when it obtains because other facts obtain, in the specifically metaphysical sense of because. It's asymmetric, irreflexive, and transitive, and it relates facts. It differs from causation because it's synchronic and doesn't depend on the laws of nature, whereas causation is diachronic and lawlike. It differs from supervenience because supervenience is a purely modal covariance relation: A-facts supervene on B-facts when no two worlds can differ in A without differing in B. Grounding is stronger than supervenience, since it adds an asymmetric explanatory direction, so supervenience is really a species of grounding minus the explanation. On the cases: (1) true—being crimson necessitates being red, so the rose is red in virtue of being crimson. (2) false—being red doesn't necessitate being crimson, so there's no necessitation and no grounding. (3) true—the conjunction has both conjuncts as constituents. (4) true—the disjunction is the weaker fact, so p is enough for p or q. (5) true—this is just the standard composition case: the table isn't anything over and above the atoms."
