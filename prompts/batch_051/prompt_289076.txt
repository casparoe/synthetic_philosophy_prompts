ok so in my philosophy club we got into whether not doing something counts as causing something, and my friend used the argument below about a fern i let die on my windowsill. i said i killed the fern by not watering it, and he said that actually can't be literally true, and gave me this:

(1) The fern died because I didn't water it, so an omission caused its death.
(2) But not watering something isn't an event. Nothing happened; there was just water missing from the pot.
(3) If omissions cause anything, the causing is done by the absence itself, not by the person who failed to act.
(4) So the lack of water in the pot caused the death whether we describe it as my omission or as the omission of some random person in Brazil. The absence is literally the same either way.
(5) So that random person in Brazil caused my fern to die, even though they never knew it existed. Same for everyone else who didn't water it, and for every other thing that didn't happen to it.
(6) That's absurd. A cause has to be actually hooked up to its effect somehow.
(7) So omissions can't be causes. But then my not watering it didn't cause it to die, and "i killed it by not watering it" is just false, which feels wrong too.

i cant tell where the trick is. did he slide something in at (3) or (4) by deciding that my omission and a stranger's omission are the same absence? or do i actually have to bite the bullet and say causes are everywhere and people only mention the ones they care about? i'd definately rather keep (1) and (6) if i can, but i want to know what that costs.
