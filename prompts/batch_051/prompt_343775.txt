Me and my sister have been going back and forth about Still Alice — the film (I've also read the book) where Alice Howland, a linguistics professor with early-onset Alzheimers, records a video for her future self telling her to swallow the pills once she can't pass the little memory quiz. The scene I can't get past is later, when the disease is far along and she finds the video, seems to understand it just long enough to try, and then can't see it through — the pills end up on the floor. My sister reads it as her earlier self getting betrayed: the person who made the decision deserved to have it followed. I keep thinking the film is more ambivalent than that.

I want to read the film through the advance-directive debate: whether, and why, the earlier self gets to bind the later, very different, self. Specifically, what does it actually show, dramatize, or complicate in the precedent-autonomy view, the current-interests view, and the thought that everything turns on whether Alice-then and Alice-now are the same person in whatever sense matters? Is the Ulysses-contract framing apt here, or does it quietly assume the continuity it's supposed to earn? And does the later Alice's apparent contentment in some scenes function as the film's argument against just deferring to the video?

I don't need quotes from the script or a bibliography — just a close reading from the major scenes as you know them. I'd rather you flag where your reading overreaches or leans on a contested read of a scene than pretend it's airtight.
