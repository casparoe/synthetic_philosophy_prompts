I'm running a seminar this term on why famines happen, and the framing question is whether they're natural disasters or political crimes. My students have sorted themselves into two camps that talk straight past each other. One camp (the 'entitlement' camp, after Sen) treats famine as essentially a distributional failure: people starve because their command over food collapses, even when food is available somewhere in the system, so famine is a policy failure — a political crime, or near enough. The other camp (call them the 'supply' camp) accepts that maladministration can worsen a famine, but insists the underlying cause is a genuine collapse in the availability of food — the potato blight in Ireland, the wartime disruption and the cyclone in Bengal, the appalling harvest years around the Great Leap Forward — and that to call all that 'political' is moralising with hindsight. I'd like you to write the document these two camps could sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. First, a numbered list of claims both camps can endorse about what famines are and what the three cases actually show; I want it to run from the obvious ('blight, bad weather and war genuinely happened') to the substantive ('a famine is not just any food shortage but starvation on a mass scale, and X follows from that'). Second, a disagreements table. For each dispute: state it neutrally; then give each camp's own formulation in wording it would accept; then state the crux — the sub-question an answer to which would force at least one camp to revise something. Where possible, propose the comparison, test or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on that crux, and record each side's prediction in advance.

Push hard on two things, because they are where I think the real work is. Is some of the fight verbal — does 'political' mean caused by politicians, preventable by institutions, or blameworthy, and how much turns on which sense each camp is using? And which proposed tests would not actually discipline either side, because the counterfactual history can never be known; I'd rather the document admit that than dress a hunch up as a test. I suspect the substantive cruxes are things like: in Ireland, whether grain and livestock leaving the country during the worst years shows there was food the starving could have been entitled to, or whether the blight had destroyed so much of the poor's own food supply that no feasible policy could have averted mass death; in Bengal, whether the famine was driven by the loss of Burmese rice and the cyclone, or by wartime inflation, speculation and hoarding that priced rural labourers out of the rice that existed; and in the Great Leap Forward, whether the weather or the grain-procurement and communal-dining policies carry the causal weight. Sink your teeth into those, and add any cruxes I've missed.
