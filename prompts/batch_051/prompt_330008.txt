Okay so I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm working on a near-future book where a coalition of countries starts injecting sulfates into the stratosphere to keep temperatures down, then a war knocks out the logistics chain and they have to decide whether rebuilding the deployment counts as keeping the planet on life support or as the thing that made the war inevitable. My protagonist keeps coming back to the idea that the whole project was rotten from the start because of moral hazard: once the button existed, cutting emissions stopped being treated as urgent, so the merely possible fix made the underlying problem worse. I honestly lean that way myself. But I don't want the antagonists to be straw men.

So take that position — that the moral hazard worry is the strongest, maybe decisive, objection to solar geoengineering — and tell me what would count against it. I don't mean "somebody could just disagree." I want the actual mechanisms, evidence, and argumentative moves that should move a thoughtful defender of the position, and a clear sense of which ones a committed moral-hazard person would have to concede as real damage versus wave off as missing the point. In particular, how does termination shock fit in? I half-remember people arguing that the two worries pull in opposite directions, because if you know the masking could snap back you can't treat the stuff as a substitute for mitigation, and that seems like it matters enormously for whether the moral-hazard objection actually works. And then theres the free-driver idea — that the problem isn't that nobody will act, but that a single mid-sized country (or even a rich guy) could act unilaterally and force everyone else to live with it, which relocates the problem from "temptation" to "who has the remote." Does that reframe the moral hazard objection, deflect it, or actually strengthen some version of it?

Ranked from fatal to merely awkward would be ideal. And be honest if you think the moral-hazard position only survives by being kept so vague that it can't fail. I want the strongest case the antagonists could make, not the one I can already beat.
