Absent special grounds for suspicion, we treat a person's report that they are in pain, or that they are currently visualizing a red triangle, as settling the question: asking "what evidence do you have for that?" would sound like a category mistake. But we take a very different attitude toward a large class of other reports from the same first-person channel. If someone tells you they chose a job for the intellectual challenge rather than the salary, or that their criticism of a colleague has nothing to do with the colleague's having contradicted them, you are likely to treat the explanation as revisable — the speaker may be self-deceived, motivated, or honestly confused about their own motives. Likewise, a person's statement that they hold no racial bias is routinely given far less evidential weight than their statement that they have a toothache. Both are sincere, first-person claims about the speaker's own mind, and both reach us through the same social practice of taking one another's word. Is there a principled line between the self-reports we accept as authoritative and the ones we treat as hypotheses, or is this asymmetry an unprincipled habit we should give up? Test any proposed line against the following cases: a report of one's current mood versus one's current pain; the claim "I am now deliberately imagining a white bear" versus the claim "I rarely have anxious thoughts"; and a chess player's answer to why she made a move, given immediately afterward versus a week later. Give the strongest principle you can find, apply it to these cases, and say whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or rejected.
