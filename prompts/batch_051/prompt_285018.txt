Decision-theory people keep telling me the St. Petersburg game is settled, but I'm not convinced the standard move actually does what it claims. Setup, to fix terms: flip a fair coin until it comes up heads; if the first heads is on the nth flip, you get 2^n dollars. Here's the argument I keep running into:

(1) Money has diminishing marginal utility—each extra dollar matters less then the last.
(2) A rational agent maximizes expected utility, not expected cash.
(3) Diminishing marginal utility makes the expected utility of the game finite; with log utility, for instance, the expected utility is Σ 2^{-n} log(2^n), which converges.
(4) So a rational agent just assigns the game some finite price and refuses to pay above it; no paradox left.

I half-believe this, but step (3) feels like it's getting more out of "diminishing marginal utility" than the phrase entitles it to. Is the argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, and what extra assumption do you actually need to fix it—and does the fixed version dissolve the original paradox or just change the subject?
