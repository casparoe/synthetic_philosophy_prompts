i'm in a 20th century philosophy seminar and we just spent a week on Moritz Schlick's essay on the meaning of life. he says the question what is the meaning of life is loaded, becuase it treats life as work: a means to some final end that keeps receding, so meaning is always postponed to a future that never arrives and the present gets drained of value. his counter is that meaning lives in play — activity done for its own sake, where the worth is in the doing rather than in any product or goal — and that the person who lives this way stays young no matter their age, while someone who treats everything as work grows old inside that instrumental logic. i want a sonnet where the octave gives the work-picture as convincingly as possible (tasks, aims, arriving somewhere, value always stored in what comes next), and the volta at line 9 flips hard into the play-picture (present-tense, self-contained, done for itself, no outside purpose). the closing couplet should land on what Schlick thinks the original question gets wrong: life does not need a final task, it needs to become the kind of activity that no longer asks what it is for. use a petrarchan-ish rhyme scheme so the turn sits naturally between octave and sestet, keep it to 14 lines, and make it precise enough that someone who has actually read the essay would recognize his view rather than a generic live-in-the-moment poem. it also has to work as a poem, not an essay with line breaks.
