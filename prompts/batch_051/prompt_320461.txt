I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, and in two weeks I have to run a single seventy-five-minute section on gratitude for first-year undergraduates, most of whom have never read moral philosophy. I want the session to build to one problem rather than to survey the topic: gratitude seems to require a definite object—this benefactor, that act, the benefit received—yet each candidate object is shot through with luck and with a past that neither party authored, so it becomes unclear what the gratitude is for and how far back along the causal chain it is supposed to reach. Do not write the explanation itself; plan how I should explain it. In what order must the pieces be introduced so that the problem arrives as the students' own discovery rather than as something I handed them, and what does each step depend on from the one before? Choose a single carrying example for me to introduce early and vary through the hour, and say what it has to have and what it must not have if it is to do that work. Then map the misconceptions I should expect—I anticipate gratitude-as-repayment, gratitude-as-nothing-but-a-warm-feeling, the slide toward being grateful to luck or to the universe itself, and the deflationary conclusion that once luck is admitted gratitude is simply irrational—and specify at which point in the sequence I should head each one off rather than correct it afterward. Say explicitly what I should leave out or decline to settle at this level, and give me one final question that would tell me whether students have grasped the problem itself rather than merely remembered the examples.
