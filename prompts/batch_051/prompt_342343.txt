I cover science and ideas for a living — mostly evolutionary biology and the philosophy around it — and I'm working on a piece about the old complaint that natural selection is a tautology. While reading a recent popular book on the philosophy of biology, I copied down a paragraph that struck me as to tidy. It said something like this:

"For decades critics said Darwin's theory was circular: 'survival of the fittest' just means 'survival of whoever survives,' so natural selection explains nothing. Philosophers of biology put this worry to rest in the late 1970s with the propensity interpretation of fitness. On this view, an organism's fitness is not what actually happens to it — that can come down to luck — but a probabilistic disposition, rather like the bias of a coin. An organism with fitness 2.0 is one that would be expected to leave two offspring on average if its life could be run over and over in the same environment. Because fitness is defined prospectively, in terms of expected reproductive output rather than actual success, the claim that fitter organisms leave more offspring is a substantive empirical claim, not a definition. The tautology objection is now generally regarded as solved."

I dont know enough of the philosophy to see where this goes wrong, but my reporter's instincts say the book is overclaiming. Before I repeat any of it in my piece, give me an accuracy audit. Which bits are right, which are acceptable simplifications for a general reader, and which are subtly mistaken or overstated? I'd especially like to know what a defender of the propensity interpretation would say to the worry that the tautology problem just reappears when you ask how we can measure the propensity independently of actual reproductive success. And the bottom line for me: is it fair to tell readers the problem is solved, and what should I hedge? Please answer in British English.
