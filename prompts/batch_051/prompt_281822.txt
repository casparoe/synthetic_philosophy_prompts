After a long argument with a friend about why promises bind, I came away dissatisfied with both of the positions we had been defending. He held the expectation view: a promise binds because it leads the promisee to form plans and rely, so the wrongness of breaking it is essentially the wrongness of letting someone down. I resisted this because it makes the obligation hostage to how much the promisee happens to rely, and it seems to leave no obligation in cases where the promisee forgets, declines to rely, or dies before performance. But I also do not think promises bind simply because of their good consequences, or simply because breaking them would erode a useful practice.

In the course of the argument I found myself reaching for a different picture, which I have been calling the lien view of promising. The idea is that a promise is an exercise of a normative power that encumbers a future act of the promisor in roughly the way a lien gives a creditor a legal interest in an asset I still own. When I promise you that I will perform some act, I transfer to you a claim-right over that performance while retaining the practical power to perform or not. I have not yet given you the act itself; I have given you a right in the act. That would explain several things at once: why only the promisee, and not a third party or my own regret, can release me; why the obligation survives the promisee's inattention or non-reliance, because the right does not live in the promisee's psychological state; and why breaking a promise is a distinctive wrong, closer to a normative trespass against the promisee than to a failure of benevolence, a false prediction, or a harmful lie. It also predicts that the promisee may legitimately insist on performance even where forgone performance would leave everyone materially better off, since he is enforcing a right he already holds rather than maximizing an outcome.

My friend objected that this picture over-juridifies promising. A lien, he said, is exactly the wrong model for promises between intimates or friends: it turns a personal commitment into a piece of property and imagines the promisor as handing over part of his future will. I suspect the objection is really to the legal imagery rather than to the underlying structure, but I am not sure.

I want an honest assessment. Does the lien view say anything more than that promising imposes an obligation on the promisor owed to the promisee, which everyone already accepts? Is the transfer of a claim-right over one's future action a genuine explanation, or just a restatement of the fact to be explained? Where does it break? I can see several possible failures: it may over-juridify low-stakes promises and promises in close relationships; it may imply, implausibly, that all promise-breaking is the same kind of wrong; it may have difficulty with promises extracted by coercion, deception, or grave need, where the relevant act was performed but the promise intuitively does not bind; and it leaves open the deeper question of why anyone has the normative power to create such a right in the first place. I would like to know what the view gets right, what it gets wrong, and whether it is genuinely distinct from the expectation, assurance, and practice views or collapses into one of them once the legal metaphor is set aside.
