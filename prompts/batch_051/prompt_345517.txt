In Chapter IV of *On Liberty*, John Stuart Mill gives the following criterion for when society may rightfully interfere with an individual’s conduct:

> The acts of an individual may be hurtful to others, or wanting in due consideration for their welfare, without going the length of violating any of their constituted rights. The offender may then be justly punished by opinion, though not by law.

Now consider a case. A private individual, with no public role, has for years written a blog of short autobiographical essays about leaving a restrictive religious community. She writes under a pen name, though she is easily identifiable from details she includes. A stranger builds a free, automated podcast that uses a synthetic voice model trained on recordings of her voice from a few publicly posted conference talks. The podcast has the synthetic voice read her blog posts aloud in a flat, faintly derisive tone. It does not falsely attribute new statements to her, does not claim to be her or to speak for her, does not threaten her, and does not reveal anything beyond what her blog already makes public. She nevertheless says it feels like being impersonated and turned into a spectacle, and she wants it stopped.

Apply Mill’s criterion to this case. Work through the passage: is the conduct hurtful to her, or wanting in due consideration for her welfare? Does it violate any constituted right? Does it prejudicially affect her interests? Where the passage leaves the answer underdetermined, say whether that reveals a limitation in the principle or merely a hard application. In particular, address whether “hurtful” in Mill’s sentence is doing more work than the harm principle is usually taken to allow, and whether punishment by opinion would be warranted if legal intervention would not be.

If the case as I have described it already begs the question, or if the passage will not bear this use, say so plainly and reframe the test.
