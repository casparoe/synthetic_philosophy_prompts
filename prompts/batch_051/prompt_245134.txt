In the literature on the rule of law, and in ordinary legal judgment, I keep encountering an asymmetry that I would like help assessing. A state that punishes people under unpublished decrees is treated as a paradigm of lawlessness: Fuller lists the failure to publicize rules among the ways a legal system can fail, and the secret decree is the stock example of a rule-of-law violation. Yet many of the same people who find the secret decree abhorrent are unmoved by a tax code or a body of financial regulation so vast, technical, and cross-referential that no lay citizen can know what the law requires without paying an expert—and often not even then. Both conditions frustrate the same value the rule of law is supposed to secure: the capacity of law to guide the conduct of those to whom it applies. Secrecy, however, is treated as a categorical defect, while complexity is treated as an inconvenience—a problem of drafting or policy, not of legality. I want to know whether that asymmetry can be justified, or whether it is an inconsistency we have simply learned to live with.

Please do three things. First, state the strongest principle you can find that would yield both judgments: that the secret decree is a fundamental rule-of-law failure while the impenetrable code is not, or at least not in the same way. Second, test that principle against three cases and say which belong with the secret decree and which with the merely complex code, and why: (i) a state that publishes every statute but only in a language none of its subjects can read; (ii) a provision that is clear in itself but whose application requires resolving hundreds of cross-referenced provisions and delegated instruments; (iii) a legislature that deliberately drafts rules in impenetrable technical prose so that only insiders can reliably comply. Third, give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, softened, or abandoned.

End with a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{keep}, \answer{soften}, or \answer{abandon}.
