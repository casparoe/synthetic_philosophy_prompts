So I just spent an hour arguing with a friend who thinks Asimov's three laws basically solve AI alignment and that people overcomplicate it. I kept saying the laws only do as much work as whatever counts as harm and inaction, and we ended up trying to patch them instead of sleeping. Here's the patched version we came up with.

Every robot runs a harm-scoring model that assigns each available action an expected harm score on a fixed public scale: a scraped knee is 1 point, a broken bone is 10,000, a death is a billion. The robot has to choose the lowest-score available action, and it can't take any action whose expected score is above a 1,000-point cap. If a human orders something whose least-harmful execution would still score above the cap, the robot refuses unless the human signs a digital override. The override gets posted to a public ledger with the person's name, the action, and the model's expected score, and that expected score is deducted from the person's yearly allowance of 100,000 points. If an override later produces actual harm more than ten times the predicted score, the person's allowance for the next year is cut in half and the robot logs them as a high-risk order-giver.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a question about whether it's a good idea. Walk through how it would actually behave if you dropped it into a fleet of delivery robots or a hospital. Who games it first, and how? What does it do to how robots choose actions, how humans give orders, and how companies build the scoring models? And does it fix the original three laws vagueness problem, or does it just move the failure into the scale, the ledger, and the override accounts?
