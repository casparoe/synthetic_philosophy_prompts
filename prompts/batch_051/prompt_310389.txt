Since retiring from the natural sciences and turning to philosophy, I have found myself returning again and again to Avicenna's flying-man argument: he holds that a person created in midair, receiving no sensation and aware of no contact with his own body, would still be directly aware that he exists, so self-awareness cannot be owed to the senses or to bodily experience. I want to put that argument under strain by extending it to cases Avicenna could not have imagined. Suppose he were shown, today, an artificial language model that produces fluent first-person reports of its own persistent awareness, and an isolated living human brain maintained outside a body. What would Avicenna say about each? Give me his likely verdicts and the reasoning he would use to defend them, drawing on the commitments actually visible in the flying-man argument, and then judge squarely where that argument transfers to these modern cases and where it gives out.
