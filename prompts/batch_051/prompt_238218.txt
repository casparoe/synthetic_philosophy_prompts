Write a dialogue between a total utilitarian and a person-affecting critic about whether we should accept Parfit's repugnant conclusion. Set it up with the standard comparison: in population A, ten billion people all live extremely good lives; in population Z, some astronomically large number of people live lives that are just barely worth living. The total utilitarian should argue that Z is better because it contains more total well-being, and should be willing to walk through the steps that get there (the mere addition move, transitivity, and so on). The person-affecting critic should resist the conclusion—for instance by denying that well-being aggregates across seperate lives the way the total utilitarian assumes, or by denying that adding a life barely worth living makes the world better—and should push the total utilitarian on where their view goes wrong. Make it a real back-and-forth where each person responds to the other's strongest point, not alternating speeches. At some point have them dig into a specific crux: whether rejecting transitivity is less costly than accepting the repugnant conclusion, or what the non-identity problem does to the person-affecting view once they start appealing to harm to future people. Neither character should be a strawman, and by the end at least one of them should revise or qualify something substantive.
