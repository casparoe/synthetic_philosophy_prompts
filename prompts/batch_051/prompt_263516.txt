As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have come to find strong longtermism more persuasive than any rival I have encountered: the view that the most important feature of an action is its influence on the far future, so that even a small change in the probability that intelligent life survives and flourishes tends to dwarf ordinary near-term improvements in human welfare. I would like to stress-test that view rather than relitigate it. What would genuinely count against it? Beyond the familiar objections from cluelessness and from fanaticism, which empirical findings, formal results, or considered judgments should most lower my confidence; which of these would be decisive and which merely awkward; and which replies available to a committed longtermist are substantive rather than ad hoc? I also want an honest assessment of whether the position, as usually defended, has been insulated by qualifications — about the unknowability of long-run consequences, the possibility that their sign flips under different models, or the refusal to discount the welfare of future generations — and whether that insulation is a reasonable response to deep uncertainty or a sign that the view no longer takes risks. In short: give me a map of the position's exposure, including what it predicts that its rivals do not and what its defenders should themselves concede.
