I've got a colleague in the economics department who keeps insisting that if a gamble has positive expected value, an individual should take it, especially when it's offered repeatedly. His argument leans on a picture where you imagine a hundred thousand identical people taking the bet at once: the average wealth across the ensemble goes up, so, he says, that's what one person facing the same bet over time should expect. He treats 'average over many people at one time' and 'average over one person over many times' as the same picture viewed from different angles. I've been reading some work in ergodicity economics that says this is exactly where the conflation breaks down: for multiplicative bets where wealth compounds, the ensemble average can be positive while the time-average growth rate of a typical individual path is negative, and the typical person goes bust even though the average is propped up by a few astronomical lucky outliers. I want a careful audit of the analogy he's leaning on. What maps onto what between the ensemble picture and the single-person-over-time picture — give me the correspondence table — where the mapping breaks, which features of the ensemble average quietly get imported into the individual case without justification, and whether his conclusion (take the positive-expected-value gamble) survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If it doesn't survive, tell me what the individual should be tracking instead.
