Before I settle on any account of where inflection stops and derivation starts, I want the full specification of what such an account must deliver, not a survey of existing answers. Write the desiderata for a theory of the inflection–derivation distinction: which clear cases it must classify correctly and what feature of each makes the classification binding, which distinctions it must respect and which it must not collapse, which cross-linguistic regularities it must be consistent with, and which troublesome cases — participles, English -ing forms, deverbal nouns, diminutives and other evaluative morphology — it must pronounce on rather than ignore. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and say which of them are negotiable and which are not. Flag any pairs of desiderata that look difficult or impossible to satisfy jointly, including whether the pressure to keep prototypical inflection and prototypical derivation apart while accommodating intermediate cases forces a graded or prototype-based distinction rather than a clean partition. Then briefly score one traditional criterion, such as obligatoriness or productivity, against your list.
