i'm a novelist, and i'm working on a book where the protagonist has to make a decision whose downstream consequences are genuinely unknowable — the sort of thing ethicists call consequentialist cluelessness: you can't even enumerate the downstream effects, let alone weigh them. I keep coming back to "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" while drafting, because it's the standard story people reach for about utilitarian tradeoffs, but i suspect it's doing weirder, more useful work than the usual reading gives it credit for.

Act as a professional philosopher who works on consequentialism and moral uncertainty, and also as a careful close reader of fiction. Think carefully — maybe start by brainstorming two or three candidate readings before you settle on the one you actually defend.

The standard thing is to treat Omelas as a case where the calculation is closed: one child's misery, a whole city's happiness, a bargain everyone can see. But cluelessness is precisely the view that real agents almost never get that kind of certainty. So here's what I want you to wrestle with: does the story presuppose exactly the certainty that cluelessness says we don't have, which would make it a misleading picture of consequentialist choice? Or does the story itself know that, and hand the real cluelessness to the ones who walk away — they leave without knowing where they're going or wether it will change anything? Maybe the philosophical drama of the story isnt in the bargain at all but in the act of leaving when the calculation refuses to close.

Give me a reading that's faithful in both directions: it shouldn't flatten the story into a thought experiment, and it shouldn't pretend the philosophy is fully vindicated by the fiction. Where does the story resist the cluelessness reading, and where does it complicate the philosophy in a way a philosophy paper wouldn't? End by saying what the story adds to the cluelessness debate — if anything — and what it gives me as a novelist that an essay on cluelessness couldn't.
