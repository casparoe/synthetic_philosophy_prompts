Design a sorting quiz, with a scoring key, for a first-year archaeology seminar on the question whether the adoption of cereal agriculture and herding was a net mistake for human well-being. The quiz should classify respondents into positions in this debate solely from their answers; it should never ask them to identify with a named school. Build the instrument from the logical space of commitments rather than from a list of authors. Its questions should draw on concrete, answerable evidence: the decline in average adult stature and the rise in dental caries and nutritional-stress markers documented from many Neolithic skeletons; the longer working hours attributed to early farmers in archaeological and ethnographic comparisons with recent foragers; the appearance of stored surpluses and sharp status differences visible in grave goods, house sizes, and settlement plans; the emergence of infectious diseases tied to sedentism and domesticated animals; and the higher fertility of early farming populations alongside their higher infant and child mortality. The diagnostic questions are where respondents genuinely split. Does "mistake" get judged against a pre-agricultural forager baseline or against later civilizations? Should the transition be assessed as a one-time adoption or as a long, path-dependent ratchet with no clean exit? Does worsened health and nutrition count as a cost when it accompanied higher fertility and eventual demographic expansion? Should rising inequality and state formation enter the verdict at all? Which counterfactual is doing the work: everyone remaining a forager indefinitely, or the transition being delayed, slowed, or managed differently? The quiz should contain seven to ten questions and be scored so that positions emerge from answers, not from self-identification; give each leaf a position name, or "unnamed" if no one defends that combination, and a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment. Note which answer combinations no one occupies and briefly diagnose why each empty cell is empty (incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored), and explicitly flag any real positions the instrument cannot separate.
