I'm teaching a graduate seminar on rational choice and longtermism this spring, and I'm stuck on the final exam question for the unit on expected value fanaticism. The stock question — "what's wrong with Pascal's mugging?" — just gets me the same essay back every year: students summarize the mugger, say the conclusion feels crazy, and then gesture at bounded utility or at ignoring tiny probabilities, but they can't say why either fix isn't obvious or what either fix costs. Could you draft an exam question that can't be answered by that move? In particular it should force students to (a) state in expected-value terms exactly what makes a decision procedure fanatical, (b) distinguish that from the ordinary thought that very unlikely but enormous benefits can legitimately dominate, the way asteroid deflection or pandemic prevention probably do, and (c) evaluate at least two candidate fixes (bounded utility, a probability cutoff, "de minimis risk," or something like that) by giving a concrete case where each fix either misfires or quietly assumes the thing it's trying to prove. The question should be doable in 90 minutes, closed book, and it should seperate students who can work with the structure of the problem from students who just label-drop.

Along with the question, give me a model answer around 600 words and a marking scheme out of 25 points built around five must-hit points, with the points each is worth and a one-line note on the typical student failure each point catches. When you write it up, start directly with the exam question text — no heading or intro — then the model answer, which should open with its bottom line in a single sentence before the reasoning, then the marking scheme. And please, no reading list or citations.
