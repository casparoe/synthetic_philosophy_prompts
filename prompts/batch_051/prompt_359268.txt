I do pricing experiments for a living, mostly little vignettes where people choose between two ways a rebate or reward could pay out, and I keep tripping over the same pattern in choice behaviour. In one pair, Option A pays £1,200 for certain; Option B pays £1,600 with a 75% chance and nothing otherwise. Both are worth the same on average, and yet most people take A. Then I give a second pair to a different group: Option C pays £1,200 with a 1-in-3 chance; Option D pays £1,600 with a 25% chance. Now most people take D. The second pair is just the first pair with both probabilities divided by three, so the trade-off between the two options hasn't changed — all that changed is that the safe option stopped being safe. I've seen the same thing with insurance add-ons and in clinical-trial consent forms, so its not just gamblers.

I've read just enough decision theory to know there's a view that a choice shouldn't flip when a common factor is applied to all the probabilities, but I don't know the vocabulary for the pattern itself. People keep telling me it's just a love of certainty, but that can't be the whole story, because in the second pair neither option is certain and the flip still happens. So heres my question: is there an established name for this pattern, and for the no-certainty version in particular? Is the no-certainty version really the same phenomenon as the certainty one, or a distinct effect? And what principle, exactly, does the pattern break? I won't be satisfied with a bare label — I want the carving: nearest concepts, where each differs from my case, and the boundary cases.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, and in British English. Use the five items for: (1) the best-fitting name, or a statement that none fits; (2) the nearest neighbouring concepts and how each differs from my case; (3) whether the certain and non-certain versions are ultimately the same; (4) what principle a rational-choice view would say this breaks; and (5) a definition or boundary cases that pin down the edges of the concept.
