I have ended up with the following cluster of commitments about hard choices and value incommensurability, and I would like someone to audit the package for joint coherence rather than defend any single element.

1. There are genuine hard choices in which neither of two options is better than the other, neither is worse, and the two are not exactly equally good. I accept Ruth Chang’s parity relation as the correct positive description of many such cases.

2. I accept the small-improvement argument as showing that the tempting fallback description of these cases as equal goodness is mistaken. If a banking job and a philosophy job were exactly equally good, then a tiny pay rise added to the banking job should make the improved banking job better than the philosophy job; but in the relevant hard choices this intuitively does not follow, so the original options were not equal in value.

3. I think rationally ideal preferences should be complete, or close enough to complete that an agent is never left without a determinate pairwise choice disposition. My main reason is that incomplete preferences seem to expose one to sequential exploitation or money-pump arguments, and they leave pairwise choice underdetermined by one’s evaluative standpoint.

4. I reject John Broome’s view that apparent incomparability is just vagueness in the trichotomous comparisons. I do not take hard choices to be cases where one of better, worse, or equal already applies but we cannot locate the sharp boundary. The phenomenology and the normative situation seem different: hard choices call for something more like making a commitment than like measuring a preexisting value fact more carefully.

5. I believe that when an agent chooses between two options that are on a par, the act of choosing can rationally give her a reason to stick with the chosen option that she did not have beforehand. This reason is grounded in her will or commitment rather than in any newly discovered antecedent value relation.

6. I also believe that rational regret is appropriate in many hard choices. Even after choosing well, the unchosen option’s value remains present and unranked relative to the chosen one; the choice was not a mistake, but something of genuine value was left behind.

Do these six commitments hang together? I would like an audit that identifies which commitments pull against each other, which pairs are only apparently in tension, and what the cheapest way to restore coherence would be and what it would cost. I am not signaling which of these I am prepared to give up; I am genuinely unsure where the package fails.
