I've been thinking about a principle I came across, called the Principal Principle, and an argument that seems to show it can't be right — which I find hard to accept. As I understand it, the principle says that your personal confidence should defer to the objective chance: if a fair coin is about to be tossed, you should be exactly 50% confident it will land heads. But consider this. (1) Objective chance is relative to a time. Before a toss, the chance of heads is 1/2; after the coin has landed, the chance of heads is either 0 or 1, because the event is already settled. (2) A fair coin was tossed an hour ago and is now hidden under a cup; I haven't looked. (3) So, right now, the objective chance that this coin landed heads is either 0 or 1, though I don't know which. (4) The Principal Principle says my confidence should equal the objective chance. (5) Therefore my confidence should be either 0 or 1 — I should be certain one way or the other — even though I have no evidence about how the coin landed. (6) But that's absurd; surely my confidence should still be 1/2. (7) So the principle is false, or at least it can't apply to anything already settled. I'd like to keep the principle, because it seems clearly right for the about-to-be-tossed coin. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
