In philosophy club the other day two seniors got in an argument about Leibniz’s best-of-all-possible-worlds idea and then asked me to settle it, which I obviously couldn't. Jordan was defending it: if God is all-knowing, all-powerful, and perfectly good, then the world he actually made has to be the best one possible, because a perfect God wouldn’t knowingly pass over a better world he could have made. Priya said that can’t be right, since you can imagine a world with a lot less pointless suffering but all the good stuff kept in; so either God couldn’t make that world (which seems to deny his power) or he didn’t want to (which seems to deny his goodness). Jordan said you can’t just subtract the suffering and keep the good stuff, because courage, compassion, freely choosing the right thing, and so on might depend on there being some evil or hardship; the best world might need those evils. Priya shot back that this makes the whole claim unfalsifiable—whenever anybody points to an evil, you just say it was needed for some hidden good, and then “best possible world” stops saying anything. She also said there might not even be a best world: for any possible world, God could presumably add one more happy creature or one more beautiful thing, so there’s an infinite ladder with no top rung. Jordan said those are two different problems. On the unfalsifiability charge, he said he wasn’t giving a just-so story; he was saying she hasn’t shown any evil is actually pointless, so the most she’s shown is that we can’t tell whether this is the best, not that it isn’t. On the no-top-rung point, he said that actually cuts against her original complaint too: if there’s no best possible world, then saying “this isn’t the best because I can imagine a better one” doesn’t show God did anything wrong, since no world could meet that bar. Priya said fine, but then Leibniz’s actual claim is still false—if no best world exists, then this world is not the best of all possible worlds, which was her point, and if “best” just means good enough, then Jordan has quietly given up the theodicy. They went in circles after that. Can you adjudicate the exchange move by move? For each specific claim, who’s right, who’s wrong, and what are both of them missing?
