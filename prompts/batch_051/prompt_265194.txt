I am the secretary of a small natural history society whose bylaws have not been revised since the early 1970s. Recently a dispute has made me think much more carefully about one clause: "A member may resign at any time by giving written notice of resignation to the Secretary, and the resignation shall take effect upon the Secretary's receipt of such notice." During a heated argument on the society's members-only message board, a longtime member posted, "That is it, I resign." I read the post within the hour, and another officer saved a screenshot, but the member deleted the post later that evening and now insists that it was venting rather than a real resignation, and that the clause was intended for a signed letter. I have to decide whether to record the resignation in the minutes and remove him from the roll. Please set out how this clause should be applied under the usual approaches to interpretation—the ordinary meaning of the words, the purpose of the provision, what the drafters would have said if asked about an online message, and what a reasonable member reading the bylaws would understand—and analyze the posting itself as a performative utterance, that is, an utterance that is itself the act rather than a report of one. In particular, does the message succeed as an act of resigning, and do its evident insincerity, its deletion, or the fact that it was a screen message rather than a paper letter matter? Why or why not?
