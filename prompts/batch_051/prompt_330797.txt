I'm a philosophy grad student, and my roommate in psychiatry keeps venting about the DSM revision process, and I've realized the thing that bugs me is philosophical. Since the 1970s the field has declassified homosexuality, folded Asperger's into autism spectrum disorder, renamed gender identity disorder as gender dysphoria, and debated adding things like internet gaming disorder — sometimes by committee vote. The reasons given in those debates are a mix: some are about new research, but a lot are about stigma, insurance reimbursement, access to services, and what a label does to the person who gets it. Meanwhile the manual is usually described as a neutral, descriptive catalogue of whatever the evidence says is there. But if psychiatrists were recording pre-existing natural kinds — the way you'd record a new species or a chemical element — you wouldn't vote to merge two of them or retire one because the label was doing social harm. So I have the persistent sense that the practice is philosophically committed to a specific view of what a diagnostic category is, but I can't nail it down.

I want you to reconstruct that implicit view, and I want you to be disciplined about it: distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, because I suspect people overstate the commitment. Then say whether the presupposed view is coherent — espeically given the 'just describing' self-image — and what concretely would have to change in the revision process if psychiatrists instead said these categories are discovered natural kinds. Don't give me a reading list or citations; I want your own best reconstruction.

- What the practice presupposes (~250 words): the implicit theory of diagnostic concepts.
