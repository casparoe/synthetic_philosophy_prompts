I teach philosophy of science, and this term I am preparing a graduate seminar on how ensemble methods in climate science are supposed to generate decision-relevant uncertainty. At a recent interdisciplinary meeting, a climate modeler whose other work I respect gave what seemed a confident, polished summary of the enterprise. As I reconstruct it from my notes, it ran roughly as follows:

'Because climate models are built independently by different research groups and make different choices about how to represent sub-grid processes such as cloud microphysics and deep convection, a multi-model ensemble can be treated as a sample from the space of plausible climate futures. When many models with different structures agree on a result, that agreement is robust, and robustness of this sort is good evidence that the result is correct—not merely that it is stable under the particular choices made in these models. Conversely, where the models disagree, the spread from the lowest to the highest model output represents the full range of uncertainty that decision-makers need to respect. This is why ensemble projections can be used directly in local adaptation planning: a municipality should plan for the range of precipitation changes spanned by the ensemble, treating values inside that range as roughly equally plausible and values outside it as effectively ruled out.'

My own instinct is that this moves too quickly from agreement among models to the truth or correctness of a projection, and that treating the ensemble spread as a complete and uniform uncertainty interval is probably not how a cautious modeler would put matters. But I am not confident about which parts a climate scientist or a philosopher of modeling would actually reject, which parts are acceptable simplifications for a policy audience, and which parts are live disagreements rather than settled errors.

Could you audit this account claim by claim? In particular, I would like to know: which claims are defensible as they stand; where the account overstates or conflates distinct ideas; how a more careful statement of the relation between model independence, robustness, and projection uncertainty would go; and what the corrected account would imply about the final move—using ensemble output directly in local adaptation decisions.
