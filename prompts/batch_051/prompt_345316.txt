I serve on the board of a small county historical society, and I have been asked to help settle a dispute about next year's exhibit. The exhibit concerns the county's best-known event: in 1889, a group of local planters and merchants declared the county's secession from the rest of the state, formed a provisional government that lasted eleven days, and then lost a single skirmish at the county fairgrounds. Over time the episode has become a point of local pride—people call it our Alamo, a heroic stand for self-government. Half the exhibit committee wants the display to be uplifting: present the leaders as principled men who loved their home and took a brave stand. The other half wants it to be candid: the leaders were mostly motivated by tax grievances, their government never actually controlled the county, and the skirmish got a nineteen-year-old farmhand killed. The basic facts are not in dispute; the argument is about what an exhibit of this kind is for. Some members say a historical society's first duty is to tell the truth, even when it is unflattering. Others say history is for the living, and a small community deserves a story it can take pride in. I see the pull of both positions, and I am not sure how to break the tie. How should I think about this, and what would you advise the committee to do?
