For a column I am working on about how reasoned skepticism is distinguished from mere suspiciousness, I have been collecting the tests that people actually propose for deciding when a conspiracy-theoretic explanation has earned the right to be taken seriously. So far my list contains the following six:

- The means-motive-opportunity test: the alleged conspirators must have had the practical capacity to bring about the event, the opportunity to do so, and an intelligible motive.
- The historical base-rate test: the claim should not be treated as automatically implausible if there are documented cases of similar conspiracies or cover-ups succeeding, or being attempted, in relevant institutional settings.
- The secrecy-burden test: the explanation should specify how many people would have had to keep the secret for how long, and why that degree of silence is organizationally realistic.
- The independent-sources test: the evidence should come from genuinely separate lines of information rather than from several retellings of the same source, leak, or document.
- The errant-data test: the theory should account for details that the received account leaves unexplained or explains awkwardly, rather than merely offering an alternative reading of evidence the received account already handles well.
- The risky-prediction test: the theory should imply something concrete about what further inquiry would find, so that it would be weakened if those expectations failed to materialize.

I want an additional test that is not on this list and is not a rewording, special case, or weighted combination of the six. Which test would you propose? Then tell me honestly whether it is any good: would it actually help separate cases where the official story is in trouble from cases where a conspiracy narrative is merely attractive?
