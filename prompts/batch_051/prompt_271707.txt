Here is an asymmetry between two things we say about pain. On the one hand, we treat the badness of pain as belonging to the sensation itself. If someone says "I am in pain, but my experience contains nothing bad, unpleasant, or worth avoiding," we do not simply accept that as a complete description of pain; we suspect the word has shifted, the sensation has changed, or the person is being glib. The awfulness of pain does not seem to us like a reaction that merely accompanies the sensation—it seems to be what the sensation is.

On the other hand, there appear to be clinical states in which exactly that accompaniment is missing while the sensation stays. A person with pain asymbolia may identify a noxious stimulus as painful, describe it as sharp or burning and locate it precisely, yet report that it does not bother her and does not make her seek relief. We do not just declare that she is not in pain, and we do not treat her as having been lucky enough to keep the sensation while losing the badness. We treat the condition as a deficit, as if something important is gone even though the missing thing appears to be the very component that made pain worth wanting stopped.

So we say both that the badness of pain is inseparable from pain and that it can be separated from pain. Can some account of pain's badness license both judgments, or is one of them a mistake? Address the hard case directly: fix a pain's sensory character and strip away the unpleasantness. Is what remains still bad, still a misfortune, still something it would be wrong to cause in someone who does not consent? Consider as well whether the asymbolic patient's deficit could be explained entirely by the loss of protective withdrawal, and whether that explanation saves both judgments or merely relocates the problem. Give the strongest principle you can find, and then say honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
