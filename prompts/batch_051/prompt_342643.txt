I was chewing over Searle's Chinese room with a mate from my philosophy of AI module the other night, and we ended up bending it untill it seemed to be about something else entirely. In the usual setup, as I understand it, a monolingual English speaker sits in a room with a rulebook that matches Chinese symbols to Chinese symbols; questions arrive under the door, and plausible-looking answers go back out even though the man doesn't understand a word. Searle's line is that neither the man nor the room-plus-rulebook system understands Chinese, because the book is all syntax and no semantics.

So change one thing. Instead of a finite lookup table, make the rulebook a generative procedure: a vocabulary, a grammar, and a set of weighted instructions for recombining stored fragments, like a small statistical model run on paper. For any input, the man can assemble a response that isn't written anywhere in the book and probably hasn't been produced before, by blending and analogising between entries. Then suppose the manual was built from a corpus of quite formal classical Chinese, the room is given a sentence in modern street slang, and it returns a pun that only works in that slang — a native speaker later calls it genuinely clever. The man still has no idea what he said.

Does that variant move the standard analysis, or does it leave Searle's conclusion exactly where it was? Specifically, does the anti-understanding verdict survive, or does the generative-recombination version give the systems reply (the reply that the room as a whole might understand even if the man doesn't) or a "novelty is just recombination" line more traction? And what would count here as the room doing something genuinely beyond its training distribution — or is "training distribution" simply the wrong concept once the device produces these out-of-sample-looking strings?
