I am a PhD student in philosophy of law, working at the intersection of statutory interpretation and Gricean pragmatics. I have been trying to decide whether a parallel I keep noticing between the absurdity doctrine and conversational implicature is a real structural identity or only a loose analogy, and I would like a careful check.

The parallel crystallized while reading Justice Brewer's opinion in Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, 143 U.S. 457 (1892). The statute made it unlawful to assist the importation of aliens under contract to perform labor or service of any kind; the church had contracted with an English clergyman to serve as its rector. Brewer writes:

"It must be conceded that the act of the corporation is within the letter of this section, for the relation of rector to his church is one of service, and implies labor on the one side with compensation on the other. [...] While there is great force to this reasoning, we cannot think congress intended to denounce with penalties a transaction like that in the present case."

The mapping I have in mind is this. In Grice, a hearer takes the literal content of an utterance, assumes the speaker is cooperating, and, when the literal content would make the contribution absurd or pointless, infers an implicature that repairs the contribution so it accords with the Cooperative Principle. In Holy Trinity, the court takes the statutory text, assumes that Congress is a rational lawmaker pursuing an intelligible purpose, and, when the literal text would produce the absurd result of penalizing a church for hiring a minister, recovers a narrowing intention. On this view, the enacted text stands to the statute as sentence meaning stands to speaker meaning; the legislative purpose stands as the implicatum; and the absurd result functions as the apparent failure that triggers the search for a repair.

The disanalogies that trouble me are these. A legislature is not a single speaker with a psychological intention, and its "intention" is a constructive attribution rather than a state the court retrieves. A statute is not an utterance occasioned in a conversation but a standing instrument that binds persons who were never party to an exchange. And the court in Holy Trinity is not recovering something Congress obliquely communicated; it is declining to let the enacted words govern a case they plainly cover.

So my question: is this parallel genuine, loose, or confused? What would have to be true of statutes and legislatures for the Gricean apparatus to transfer rather than decorate, and what exactly does the parallel predict about statutory interpretation that turns out false?
