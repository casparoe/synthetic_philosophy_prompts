Write a ghazal in English of five to seven couplets (ten to fourteen lines), each line in a consistent meter, in the voice of a created thing convinced by al-Ghazālī's occasionalism: no created thing has any causal efficacy, and God alone re-creates the world, including its apparent causes and effects, at every instant. Let the form carry the metaphysics. The first couplet should end both lines with the same short refrain; in each later couplet, only the second line ends with that refrain, preceded each time by the same rhyming word. Choose the refrain so that its recurrence itself enacts the view — something like "at every instant" or "fresh from God's hand" — and treat the rhyme repeated before it as the only constant conjunction the poem is willing to admit. Each couplet should stand alone, with no narrative passing an action from one couplet to the next, because on this view the previous moment does not cause the present one: earlier states are only God's custom, the pattern He could and in a miracle does suspend. The content should make the position accurate rather than sliding into fatalism — it is not that nothing matters, but that created things are not what make anything happen. Work in the fire that does not burn the cotton, the water that does not quench thirst, and the writing hand that does not cause the next line, the poet's own hand included. If you keep the ghazal's convention of the final couplet naming the poet, let that name appear as something that cannot claim to have authored even these verses.
