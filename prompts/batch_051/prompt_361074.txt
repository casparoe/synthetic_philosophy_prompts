I am a doctoral student in the philosophy of physics, working on the interpretation of quantum statistics. I have been reading Schrödinger's *Science and Humanism*, the published version of lectures he delivered in the early 1950s. In one passage he argues, without using the later technical vocabulary, that an elementary particle is not an individual: if an electron is observed here and another electron later observed there, the question whether it is the same electron has no physical meaning. The labels 'this' and 'that' are, he says, things we bring to the description rather than features of the world. Particles of the same kind, on this view, are more like entries in a tally than like members of a population of persisting objects.

Here is the parallel I cannot decide about. In the lambda calculus, bound variables are likewise not individuals. Alpha-equivalence tells us that the term λx.x and the term λy.y are the same term: the names x and y are mere placeholders, and renaming them across the whole binding makes no difference. Asking whether the x in one occurrence is 'the same variable' as the x in another is, within the formalism, an ill-formed question unless one first fixes a convention about scope. Permuting labels does not produce a new term, just as permuting indistinguishable particles does not produce a new quantum state.

I suspect this is not merely a loose analogy, because both cases seem to arise from a common structure: a quotient of a space of labelled configurations by a group of label permutations. But I also suspect the lambda-calculus case is purely syntactic while Schrödinger is making an ontological claim. Is there a genuine structural identity here, or am I pattern-matching? If there is, does anything transfer from one side to the other—for instance, does the distinction between bound and free variables shed light on why particles of different kinds cannot be permuted? Please state the mapping precisely, say what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer, and name at least one thing the parallel predicts that fails on one side. I would appreciate the answer in British English.
