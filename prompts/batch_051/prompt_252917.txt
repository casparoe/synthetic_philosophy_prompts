I'm a software engineer who maintains trust and safety tooling for a large source-hosting platform, with no formal background in philosophy. We are building a feature that lets a maintainer file a conduct report against another user for public behavior: hostile code review comments, reneging on a merge agreement, or a sustained pattern of low-quality contributions. A recurring problem is the hypocrisy counter-flag: the target of a report responds that the filer has done the same kind of thing in their own public activity, so the filer has no business pressing the report. Reviewers currently handle these objections inconsistently, and I want a standing check built as a distinct triage stage before the factual contents of a report are examined.

I have come across the phrase 'standing to blame' and the idea that hypocrisy or complicity can remove a person's standing even when the underlying accusation is true. I want that idea turned into a decision procedure I can hand to an on-call reviewer. The question at this stage is not whether the reported behavior occurred, and not whether the filer's past behavior excuses anyone; it is whether this filer is in a position to press this particular report publicly. Reports can trigger public badges, temporary review bans, or account restrictions, so a mistaken or hypocritical report carries real reputational harm.

Give me a reusable procedure: a step-by-step checklist or textual flowchart that takes the filer's public history, the target's public history, and the relationship between them, and outputs one of a small set of verdicts, such as: has standing, standing reduced (report down-weighted or reframed), or no standing (report rejected or converted to a private note to moderators). The procedure must be runnable by a non-philosopher reviewer in about twenty minutes per case, with an appeals path for the filer and the target. Make the procedure specify at least the following: what counts as 'the same kind of conduct' (how similar, how recent, and whether one past instance defeats standing); whether a filer can regain standing by publicly acknowledging and repairing the behavior; what happens when the filer was complicit in the reported conduct, for example by having approved or enabled it earlier; whether an unaffected third party can ever file with full standing; and whether power asymmetries, such as a senior maintainer reporting a new contributor, should change the verdict. Mark every step that is a judgment call rather than a mechanical rule, and give the reviewer a default to use where the call cannot be eliminated.

Work the procedure through three concrete cases: one where the procedure blocks a filer I would personally prefer to let report, one where the procedure lets through a filer I would prefer to block, and one gray case whose verdict genuinely depends on a judgment call. Say plainly what the procedure cannot do: what it will not detect, what it will systematically miss, and what kinds of unfairness it would reproduce.

After the prose procedure, append a valid JSON object (with short string values only) summarizing the procedure, using exactly these fields:

- standing_outcomes: array of strings, the exhaustive verdicts and their one-line meanings
- steps: array of objects with keys step, test, if_yes, if_no, judgment_call
- standing_defeaters: array of objects with keys defeater, operational_trigger, effect
- worked_cases: array of objects with keys case, facts, verdict, decisive_step
- residual_judgment_calls: array of objects with keys call, why_unavoidable, default_policy
- limits: string, one sentence on what the procedure cannot do

The prose should carry the reasoning and the edge cases; the JSON is a reference summary, not a replacement for the prose.
