I keep running into an argument that seems to show self-deception is impossible, and I cannot decide whether it is a real proof or a trick of wording.

(1) If someone deceives another person into believing p, then the deceiver believes that not-p is the case and intentionally causes the other person to believe that p is the case.
(2) Self-deception is the special case of deception in which the deceiver and the target are the same person.
(3) So if a person deceives herself about p, then, in her role as deceiver, she believes that not-p is the case, and, in her role as target, she comes to believe that p is the case.
(4) One person cannot consciously hold both the belief that not-p and the belief that p at the same time, since they contradict each other.
(5) Therefore, self-deception is impossible.

Yet I also think I have caught myself doing it: for example, deciding that someone is interested in me when the evidence clearly points the other way. So either the argument is valid and I must deny that self-deception ever really occurs, or some particular step is false. Please tell me exactly which step fails and why, and whether the repair preserves the ordinary notion of self-deception or merely replaces it with something weaker like biased reasoning.
