I teach an upper-division philosophy of law seminar — fourteen students, one 80-minute meeting — and I want to run a session where they don't just read about self-amending legal systems but actually get caught inside one. Design me a classroom game in the spirit of Nomic: the class starts with a tiny set of rules and an amendment procedure, can change any rule by vote including the amendment rule itself, and tries to keep a simple underlying game playable while doing it. I want them to run into the trouble themselves, before anyone says the words 'self-reference' or 'rule of recognition': the amendment that amends how amendments count, the rule that tries to entrench itself against repeal, the question whether a vote taken under the old procedure stays valid when the procedure changes mid-vote, and the moment everyone disagrees about what the rules actually are. No lecture up front.

Give me the initial rules and the underlying game, with a scoring or win condition so they have some skin in it; the voting/amendment mechanics (ordinary classrooom materails only, nothing digital); a minute-by-minute timeline for the 80 minutes; a debrief that maps what happened onto primary versus secondary rules, the rule of recognition, self-application, entrenchment and immutability clauses, and clashes between higher and lower rules; and an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce — real enforcement, officials outside the room, a legal system with an actual history — and therefore what I'll have to supply verbally. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it, and how to lean into the chaos rather than shutting it down. I assume it'll either fizzle or become a procedural shouting match.
