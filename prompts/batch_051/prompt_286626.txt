I'm a reliability engineer by training, and I spend most of my time running blameless postmortems for a company that operates payments infrastructure. The vocabulary I live in is safety science and incident analysis: chronic versus active failures, drift into failure, normalization of deviance, tight and loose coupling, balancing and reinforcing feedback loops, single points of failure, redundancy and graceful degradation, just culture, observability, alert thresholds and alarm fatigue, error budgets, and the line between maintenance and toil. This is not borrowed terminology; it is how I actually reason. Lately I've been reading in a tradition I had always written off as soft: care ethics. As I understand it, care ethics makes roughly these claims. Morality was built around an image of independent, interchangeable persons who consent to obligations and follow general rules; care begins instead with particular people embedded in specific relationships marked by asymmetric needs and capacities. Some of our weightiest responsibilities arise not from choice but from being enmeshed with others. Attention and moral emotions — concern, patience, shame when we fail to notice — are forms of moral knowledge, not biases to be controlled for. And the particular features of a concrete situation matter more than the uniform application of a principle.

I want a systematic translation of care ethics into my vocabulary, not an essay of engineering metaphors. Take its main commitments one by one — the critique of impartial, rule-based duty; the idea that relationships impose responsibilities without consent; the epistemic status of attention and emotion; the priority of the particular over the general — and restate each in terms of coupling, feedback, drift, alarm design, chronic failure, just culture, observability, maintenance, and error budgets. Be explicit about what carries over cleanly, what has to be bent to fit, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. Then give me a residue report: whenever the translation distorts or simply fails, tell me whether the engineering vocabulary is missing something, or whether the care-ethics claim was doing more rhetorical work than philosophical work. Flag the points where the translation only succeeds by smuggling in engineering assumptions that care ethics would reject. I want an honest verdict on my hunch that care ethics is basically "blameless postmortems for people." Maximum 900 words, in American English.
