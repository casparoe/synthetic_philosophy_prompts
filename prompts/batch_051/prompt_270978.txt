I'm a science journalist and I've spent the last few weeks obsess over the multiple-discovery argument -- Newton and Leibniz both inventing calculus in the late 1600s, Darwin and Wallace both hitting on natural selection in the mid-1800s, and so on. One camp says this shows discoveries are pretty much inevitable once the groundwork is there: the ideas are "in the air," and if Newton or Darwin hadn't existed someone else would have found the same thing around the same time. The other camp says this is a sampling illusion: we fixate on the cases that happened to happen twice, ignore all the insights that only ever happened once (or never happened at all), and paper over the fact that Newton's and Leibniz's calculi, or Darwin's and Wallace's versions of natural selection, were actually pretty different objects embedded in different research programs. I've argued both sides at different points, and at this point it feels like the debate has been stuck for decades with smart people on both sides talking past each other.

Please act as a philosopher of science who also has a historian's instincts. I want you to diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, not to referee who's right. Before you commit to a diagnosis, brainstorm at least four or five different hypotheses for why this debate might be stalled -- e.g., is it verbal, is it a hidden empirical question, is it a difference in what counts as evidence, is it a disagreement about how to individuate discoveries, is it just competing intuitions about whether history is made by structures or by individuals? Then pick the one (or the combination) you think is most defensible and run with it. In your answer, I'd like: (1) the strongest version of each side's claim, stated so its own advocates would accept it; (2) your diagnosis of the real structure of the disagreement; (3) the sub-questions, if any, that would actually have to be settled; and (4) an honest assessment of whether anything -- better data, a sharper distinction, a different question -- could move the debate, or whether we should expect it to stay stuck. Dont just give me a survey of the history; and please skip reading lists and citations, I want the argument itself.

One hypothesis I keep coming back to: the word "inevitable" is doing two jobs at once. It's a claim about timing and base rates (given a certain pool of knowledge and tools, the probability that at least one person hits on the idea within a rough window is high), and it's a separate, more loaded claim about credit (individual genius doesn't matter much because someone else would have done it). The second claim is what people actually care about, but it's being defended with evidence that only bears on the first. Am I onto something, or is that too clean?
