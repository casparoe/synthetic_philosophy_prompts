For my school's sixth-form philosophy club, I am putting together a worksheet on what makes consent valid. I would like you to produce the core of it: a decision tree that sorts cases into three categories — valid consent, expressed-but-invalid consent, and no consent at all. The audience is sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds with no background in the literature, so the tree should work from concrete diagnostic questions rather than from theory labels or citations.

Build the tree from the logical space of three defect factors rather than from a list of positions. Take each factor as a yes/no question a person could answer about an ordinary case without knowing any theory:
(a) deception — was something about the act, the other party, or the likely consequences hidden or misrepresented?
(b) coercion — did the person choose under pressure, and was that pressure a threat from the other party, a threat from a third party, or a structural constraint such as poverty or an emergency?
(c) competence — could the person, for this particular decision, understand, retain, weigh and communicate the relevant information?
Lay out the eight combinations of yes/no answers, then turn them into a tree by fixing an order for the three questions. State which order you chose, and say one concrete thing the order changes.

Before those three factors, include a first split on whether there was any expression of agreement at all. This is where the two familiar positions about expression must go: the 'no means no' view, on which only an actual refusal cancels consent and silence can count as agreement in the right context, and the 'yes means yes' view, on which nothing short of an affirmative agreement counts. Make clear which branch each commits to, and include the intermediate view that contextually meaningful silence can count as agreement.

Positions that must appear as leaves, or be flagged as unreachable by a three-factor tree:
- the view that only deception about a matter the consenting party would consider material invalidates consent;
- the view that any deception that causally contributes to consent invalidates it;
- the view that only threats to do something the threatener has no right to do invalidate consent;
- the view that any pressure leaving the person with no acceptable alternative invalidates consent;
- the view that competence is decision-specific and scalar, and the rival view that it is a single on/off status; and
- the view that a transaction can be invalid because it is substantively exploitative even when none of the three defects is present. Show where this last view forces a fourth branch, and state the question that branch asks.

Route at least these invented cases through the tree and say what each returns on each major branch: a patient in severe pain who tells the surgeon to 'do whatever stops the pain'; a student who hands over their revision notes because a classmate threatens to expose an embarrassing secret; a person joining a drug trial after the recruiter omits a rare side effect; a homeowner selling a family painting for far below its value during a flood evacuation; and an older person with early dementia agreeing to a loan after having the terms explained slowly and choosing among options with supportive relatives present.

For each leaf, give the position's name in plain English (or 'unnamed' if nobody defends it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Diagnose the combinations nobody seems to occupy: say for each whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where the tree cannot separate two positions, flag that explicitly rather than pretending it can.

Aim for roughly ten to fourteen leaves, and keep the prose suitable for a sixth-form audience.
