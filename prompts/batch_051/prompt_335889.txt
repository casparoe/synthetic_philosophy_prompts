I've been going back and forth with a mate about what "choosing rationally" even means in a one-shot, symmetric game where there's no communication and everyone knows everyone else is equally rational. The standard story is expected utility: pick the option with the highest expected payoff given your beliefs about what the others will do. But some puzzles seem to pull against that. Take the Platonia dilemma: twenty people each get a letter, and each can either send a telegram or stay quiet. If exactly one person sends, that person gets a million pounds. If nobody sends, nobody gets anything. If more than one person sends, nobody gets anything. No communication is allowed. If you think no one else will send, you should send; if you think exactly one other person will send, you shouldn't; if you think two or more will send, it doesn't matter. There's also the Luring Lottery variant, where the prize is so enormous (or the cost of sending so small) that the temptation to just send "in case" feels overwhelming, even though the logic of the symmetric situation might say otherwise.

I'd like a working definition of rational choice in these symmetric one-shot no-communication cases that gets the following cases right: (1) the two-player prisoner's dilemma with cash payoffs; (2) the original Platonia dilemma with twenty players; (3) the Luring Lottery, where the payoff to being the sole sender is huge, but if more than one sends everyone gets nothing (or maybe a small loss); (4) the same dilemma with only two players; (5) a case where players dont know how many others are playing; and (6) a case where one player has a tiny payoff asymmetry, like she cares slightly more about money than the others. Propose the definition, run it through all six cases, and say which of your verdicts feels least secure. I'm especially curious whether your definition ends up licensing mixed strategies, and whether it treats the Luring Lottery as different from the original Platonia dilemma.
