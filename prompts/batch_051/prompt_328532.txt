I keep ending up in the same argument with a colleague of mine, an urban planner, who insists that technologies are never neutral and that some artifacts have a politics built into their very geometry. Her favorite example is the set of low overpasses on the Long Island parkways that Robert Moses is said to have specified so that buses, which carried mostly poor and Black riders, could not pass under them on the way to Jones Beach; whether or not the story is fully accurate, she uses it to argue that a bridge can be a political decision set in concrete. When I press her, however, she slides between two claims: a narrower one, that some particular artifacts were deliberately designed to exclude or to control, and a broader one, that certain technologies carry a politics in themselves, independently of anyone's intentions, because of the social arrangements they require, reward, or quietly make seem natural. Behind the broader claim I can hear a kind of technological determinism; she resists that word, but the idea seems to be that the built environment fixes the shape of what can be done more than we fix it.

I want to stop this slide by asking what evidence could actually decide the matter. Design the strongest test you can of the claim that an artifact has politics, in either the narrower or the broader sense: what would you observe or measure, what result would count for the claim and what against, and how would you distinguish a property of the artifact itself from a property of its designers, its rules of use, or the society around it? Be honest about the residue, too: what would even a positive result leave conceptually unsettled, for example whether the politics is truly in the object or only in the system that produced and uses it? Please write your answer as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter.
