I am an undergraduate currently taking a historiography seminar, and I would like help making sense of a divided practice in my discipline. On one side, working historians make causal claims constantly: that the Black Death weakened manorial bonds in Western Europe, that the failure of the 1848 revolutions smoothed the way for unification from above, and that the printing press was among the conditions that made the Reformation possible. On the other side, the same field treats counterfactual history as unserious entertainment rather than as a method of inquiry. The same seminar that approved those causal claims dismissed the question of what would have happened had the Spanish Armada landed as a parlor game rather than a research project.

I want to know what this divided practice presupposes. Does the dismissal of counterfactuals commit historians to a regularity view of causation, or to an INUS-style view in which causes matter without requiring difference-making, or to a background belief that the canonical outcomes were overdetermined and would have arrived by some other route anyway? In class we also kept apart two things that seem relevant but may pull in different directions: the claim that a long-run direction was near-inevitable (for example, that something like industrial production would spread across parts of nineteenth-century Europe), and the claim that the particular path was highly sensitive to small events (which region industrialized first, through which firms and ports, and on what timetable). I cannot tell whether the discipline's anti-counterfactual norm presupposes the first claim, the second, both, or neither.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of historical causation and inevitability that allows accepted causal talk to coexist with the rejection of counterfactual history. Please distinguish carefully what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably alongside, and mark the places where the practice is underdetermined among rival theories. Finally, say what would have to change in how history is written and taught if the field instead accepted a difference-making view of causation, on which the claim that C caused E is committed to something about the nearest world in which C did not occur.
