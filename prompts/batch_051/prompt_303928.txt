I'm drafting a short-answer problem for a philosophy of mind course on perception, and I want it to test whether a student can distinguish a genuine change in perceptual experience from the effects that merely come along with it—not whether they can reproduce a definition. For the purposes of the problem, take 'cognitive penetration' to mean this: a perceptually relevant cognitive state, such as a belief, expectation, concept, or desire, causally influences the phenomenology of perceptual experience itself, over and above any influence it has on where or when the person attends, on what they remember when answering, or on how they interpret or judge what they saw. The opposing modularist view says early perceptual processing is sealed off from such states: apparent cases of penetration are always re-describable as changes in attention, memory, or post-perceptual judgment, or as intra-perceptual adjustments of the sort involved in ordinary perceptual learning.

Construct a thought experiment that runs against the usual grain of this debate: a realistic workplace scene—for instance a radiologist reading a chest image, an air-traffic controller scanning a radar screen, or a technician reading an instrument panel—in which a lay observer would find it overwhelmingly natural to say that the expert's hard-won knowledge has changed how the display looks, but in which a careful cognitive-penetration theorist should nevertheless decline to claim penetration, because the effect is best explained by a mechanism she herself classifies as non-penetrating. Constraints: keep the scenario to one paragraph; no illusions, hallucinations, drugs, brain stimulation, or non-human perceptual systems; the expert's relevant belief must be true, useful, and acquired through ordinary experience. After the scenario, do the following in order. First, state precisely what the expert reports seeing differently, and explain why a lay observer would call this a direct effect of knowledge on experience. Second, give the modularist's re-description of that same change: say which confound or intra-perceptual process does the work, and say how one could in principle test that re-description. Third, give the careful penetration theorist's own reason for withholding the claim, using her terms rather than the modularist's. Fourth, identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, offer the strongest counter-argument available to someone who still suspects penetration, and then specify the minimal change to the scenario that would force the modularist to work hardest—and why that change is exactly what an ordinary workplace setting, unlike a psychophysics laboratory, tends not to provide.
