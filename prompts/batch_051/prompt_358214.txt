I am compiling a list of independent arguments for the view that an individual facing repeated multiplicative, non-ergodic wealth processes should maximize the long-run time-average growth rate of wealth (a Kelly-type, geometric-mean rule), and that maximizing expected wealth—and appealing to expected utility as an independent criterion—is at best a derived special case. The arguments I already have are the following:

1. Non-ergodicity. Under multiplicative dynamics the ensemble average and the time average of wealth diverge; because an agent lives along exactly one realized path, the ensemble mean is not the quantity that describes what that agent will actually experience.

2. Overbetting and ruin. Expected-wealth maximization prescribes bets or leverage whose high arithmetic mean depends on a tiny set of enormous winners, while almost every realized path busts; maximizing time-average growth avoids near-certain ruin.

3. The St. Petersburg puzzle. The apparent infinite value rests on taking the ensemble mean of a one-shot gamble; when one computes the time-average growth rate of repeated play, the paradox dissolves, so the puzzle is an artifact of using the wrong average.

4. Evolutionary selection. A strategy with a systematically lower time-average growth rate will tend to be displaced over long horizons by a higher-growth strategy, so long-run survival filters for time-average growth maximization regardless of the agent's stated preferences.

5. Ergodicity transformations. Concave utilities such as logarithmic utility are best understood not as brute psychological risk aversion but as mappings from non-ergodic multiplicative wealth dynamics to an ergodic additive process, which suggests expected-utility theory is a derived special case rather than an independent foundation.
