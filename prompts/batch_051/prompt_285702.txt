My labmate gave me this argument and I can't tell if it dissolves weakness of will or quietly assumes its conclusion. (1) An act is akratic only if the agent acts against her own better judgment at the time of acting: she judges, all things considered, that she shouldn't do X, and does X anyway. (2) Hyperbolic discounting shows the subjective value of a delayed reward spikes as the delay goes to zero, so at the moment of temptation a small immediate reward (the cigarette, the snooze button, the pint) really is valued above the larger later one that looked better from a distance. (3) If at the moment of action she values X above the alternative, then her all-things-considered judgment at that moment favors X. (4) So in the usual temptation cases there is no standing judgment that she ought not to do X for her to act against. (5) Therefore akrasia as traditionally understood is impossible: what looks like weakness of will is just time-inconsistent valuation, with "her" at different delays seeing different magnitudes. (6) But people do sincerely say, while doing the thing, "I know I shouldn't and I'm doing it anyway," and that sure seems like acting against their better judgment. Which step or premise is doing the shady work?
