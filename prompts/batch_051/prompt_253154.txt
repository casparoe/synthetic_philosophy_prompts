I'm giving a faculty workshop at a law school defending a version of negative retributivism: desert should work only as an upper bound on punishment, never as a lower bound. Consequentialist considerations (deterrence, incapacitation) can justify punishing less than a person deserves, or not at all, but they can never justify punishing someone more than they deserve, and they can't justify punishing the innocent no matter how much deterrent value it would have. My pitch is that this keeps what's right in retributivism while dodging the usual objections — it never demands punishment, so it can't be a rationalization for vengeance or conflict with mercy — while still putting a hard stop on the scariest consequentialist results. The audience is mostly empirical criminal law scholars who are quietly consequentialist (they think deterrence and incapacitation do the real justificatory work and regard retributivism as mysterian or as post hoc rationalization), plus two Kantians who think a ceiling without a floor makes desert optional in a way that abandons the whole point of retributivism. Give me a Q&A prep sheet: eight or so objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way these people would actually put them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is softest, what I should concede versus hold, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
