I've seen rationalist discussions treat Hume's argument against miracles as an early Bayesian argument, but they often blur two claims that I suspect come apart, and I want a careful reconstruction rather than a textual exegesis. The first claim is local and probabilistic: for any particular miracle report, the posterior probability of the miracle is negligible because the report is more easily explained by ordinary error, deception, misremembering, or coincidence than by a violation of a well-established regularity; the posterior would rise only if the prior on the miracle or the witness's reliability were already so high that rejecting the report did more violence to one's background model than accepting the miracle. The second claim is global and rule-like: no possible body of human testimony, however large, varied, and apparently reliable, could ever rationally establish a miracle, because an established law of nature already summarizes all relevant evidence and must be treated as a fixed constraint. Please explain how these two claims differ in what they imply about multiple independent witnesses, corroborating physical traces, and the possibility of revising a purported law of nature in light of recalcitrant testimony. In particular, explain what additional commitment is needed to move from the local claim to the global one, and whether a Bayesian can consistently accept the local claim while rejecting the global one.
