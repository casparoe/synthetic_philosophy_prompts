A friend who's been reading up on Dutch book arguments backed me into a corner the other day and I can't find the exit. He claims the same argument that's supposed to show rational credences obey the probability axioms also shows something much stronger: that a rational person can never expect their own degree of belief to move in any particular direction. That feels obviously too strong — it would make a lot of ordinary learning irrational — but I can't see where his chain breaks. Here's the argument as he gave it, with my glosses.

(1) Your degree of belief in A just is the price at which you're indifferent between buying and selling a ticket that pays $1 if A and nothing otherwise. (2) If there's a set of bets, each of which you regard as fair at the time you take it, and together they guarantee you lose money no matter what happens, then your beliefs are irrational. That's the Dutch book test. (3) Now suppose I'm currently 0.1 confident that I'll be able to fly, but I'm certain that in an hour I'll be 0.9 confident of it — say I know I'm about to take a brainwashing pill that works regardless of the truth. (4) A bookie can then buy a $1 ticket on "I can fly" from me now for 10 cents, and sell me the same ticket back in an hour for 90 cents; each bet is fair by my lights at the time, but my combined payoff is negative 80 cents whether or not I can fly. (5) So unless my current credence equals any future credence I'm certain I'll have, I'm exploitable, hence irrational. (6) Trading on the same idea with bets conditional on whatever my future credence turns out to be, any expected drift is ruled out too: rationality requires that my current credence equal my current expectation of my future credence. (7) But that's the part that can't be right. It tells me to be 0.9 that I can fly right now, before the pill, and more generally it says I can't rationally expect my future self to be predictably biased, drunk, tired, or misled. So Dutch book arguments prove too much.

I'd rather keep the Dutch book test for ordinary probabilism than swallow Reflection, but I can't tell which premise to blame. Maybe the slide from (5) to (6) is doing the work, or maybe a rational person can just refus the second bet. Where does this go wrong, if it does? Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion.
