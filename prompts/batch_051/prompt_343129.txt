I work as the sole data scientist on the product team at a mid-sized music streaming service. Every quarter the VP of Product asks me to translate our behavioral logs into claims about what listeners actually want: whether they want more novelty or more familiar catalog, whether they value broad discovery or a smaller curated set, and whether they prefer more control over what plays next or more passive, lean-back listening. Those claims determine how we spend a six-figure licensing budget and which features get built or killed; the VP of Product signs off on them, and the decisions are hard to reverse. I have about one day each quarter to do the analysis, I have no ML research team to lean on, and I am usually working from observational logs generated under our existing recommendation policy, since I can only occasionally get a small A/B test approved. I am also the only person in the room who thinks in terms of revealed preference, Goodhart's law, and inverse reinforcement learning rather than marketing personas.

I need a reusable procedure, a checklist in words with steps to run in order, for moving from observed behavior to defensible claims about listener preferences. It should be runnable by someone with my background (statistics and SQL, not reinforcement-learning research) within that one day, and it should explicitly mark the places where the theory underdetermines the answer and the choice is a judgment call rather than something the data settle.

Your procedure must handle at least these harder cases:

- The logs are generated under our current recommendation policy, so an apparent preference for familiar music can be manufactured by a recommender that mostly shows familiar music. I need a step that separates listener taste from exposure.
- The usual inverse-reinforcement-learning problem: many reward functions would make the same observed behavior optimal, so behavior alone underdetermines preference. Say where the identifying assumptions come from, and what we should do when we cannot justify them.
- Listeners are noisy and sometimes satisficing or impulsive; not every skip is a revelation about stable values.
- Survey responses and behavior can point in opposite directions. The procedure should dictate how to treat each rather than letting me pick whichever story I find more convenient.
- Preferences have a time horizon: a listener can prefer a familiar song right now and a discovery-oriented catalog over the next year, and the metric that looks intelligent at session level may be the wrong one at churn level.

Then walk the procedure through three concrete cases from a typical quarter:

1. The skip rate on algorithmically ranked playlists is much higher than on search-based or user-selected listening. Product leadership wants to conclude that listeners dislike algorithmic discovery. What should the procedure actually let us conclude?
2. A hide-this-artist feature is used heavily by a small set of power users and ignored by the median listener. We want to know whether the median listener wants that control too, or whether the power users are an unrepresentative fringe.
3. The procedure appears to show a strong preference for familiar catalog, which is the opposite of the company's current bet on discovery. I want the procedure to deliver whatever answer it delivers, including the one my VP will not want to hear.

Finally, say what this procedure cannot do: which claims about user preferences we should stop making without a randomized experiment, a targeted survey, or a model assumption we currently cannot justify.
