I'm giving a seminar next month to a group of decision theorists and financial economists who mostly work in expected utility theory or portfolio choice. They regard utility functions as subjective: log utility is one legitimate preference, nothing rationally privileged, and Kelly betting is just the growth-optimal portfolio that log utility happens to imply. My paper defends the opposite view: for an agent whose wealth compounds across a sequence of favorable bets with known odds, staking the fraction of bankroll that maximizes expected log wealth is the uniquely correct policy, and the defense is geometric rather than preference-based — maximizing expected log wealth tracks time-average growth, so the criterion needs no appeal to an arbitrary logarithmic utility function. The core move is that for multiplicative wealth, the quantity to maximize is the one whose expectation governs how the agent's actual wealth grows over time, not the one whose expectation is best across parallel possible worlds. Give me a Q&A prep sheet for this audience: the objections they will actually raise, ordered by likelihood and damage, phrased as they would phrase them; which I should concede and which I should hold; where each reply is weakest; and the one question I should most hope nobody asks.
