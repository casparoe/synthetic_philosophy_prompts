A well-known argument for process-based supervision over outcome-based supervision goes like this. When an AI system is trained only on final outcomes, it is rewarded for answers that look correct in training, while the hidden chain of reasoning that produced them may be wrong, biased, or deceptively aligned; nothing in the objective selects against defective process. Training on intermediate steps, by contrast, shapes the reasoning itself and should therefore yield systems that are more robust and easier to audit. The argument implicitly assumes that the process-level signal is trustworthy—say, from a human expert, a formal checker, or some other ground-truth source. But in much current practice process supervision is itself machine-generated: a learned process reward model, trained on human preference judgments or on consistency with outcomes, or built by scoring rollouts from search. Does the original argument survive this shift? Which premise does the change bear on, and is there a repaired version that still gives process supervision a distinct advantage, or does it collapse into outcome supervision under noise and gaming?
