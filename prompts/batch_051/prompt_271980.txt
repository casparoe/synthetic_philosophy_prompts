I'm putting together a unit on Hume on miracles and I want to close it by reading a scene from Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov through that lens. The set-up: the elder Zosima dies, and a lot of the monks and townspeople half-expect his body to stay uncorrupted as a sign of his holiness; instead it begins to stink almost immediately, which sends the hopeful into crisis and gives the cynics a field day. On a Humean reading, the crowd is treating a miracle or its failure as evidence about sanctity, and Dostoevsky can be read as making the Humean point that betting your faith on a violation of the ordinary course of nature is a bad idea. But Dostoevsky also plainly wants Alyosha's faith to survive the scandal, and the thing that actually restores him is Grushenka and a dream, not a reevaluation of the evidence. So is the novel dramatizing Hume, pushing back against him, or both? Give me a reading of this scene and its fallout that takes both the argument and the novel seriously: where the Hume lens illuminates what Dostoevsky is up to, where it distorts it, and what a Humean should say to Alyosha afterward. Also flag what's settled in Hume scholarship versus what's genuinely contested, since I'll be passing this on to students.
