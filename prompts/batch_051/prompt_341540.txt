I'm running a small seminar on Frankenstein (Mary Shelley's novel, not the films) and I keep circling back to Galen Strawson's basic arguement against moral responsibility. The short version as I understand it: to be truly morally responsible for what you do, you'd have to be truly responsible for the character or mental makeup that leads you to do it; to be responsible for that makeup, you'd have to be responsible for the things that formed it; and carrying that back means you'd ultimately have to be causa sui, the cause of yourself, which is impossible. So nobody is ever ultimately responsible for anything, whatever we say in everyday blame talk.

I'd like a genuine critical reading of the novel through that lens, not just the novel as an illustration of the argument. Which scenes and characters actually dramatise the regress Strawson is pointing at — Victor, the creature, both? Where does the novel seem to push in the other direction, and is there a way of reading its language of guilt and blame as compatible with Strawson rather than just naive? Is the creature the best test case, or does Victor's position as the one who literally creates a person complicate things in a way Strawson's argument isn't about? I'm especially interested in the passages around the creature's education — the De Lacey family, the books he reads — and the moment where he explains how he became what he is. Does the novel treat that as an excuse, a diagnosis, or both? And does Shelley track anything like the difference between ultimate responsibility and the ordinary compatibilist sort of responsibility, or does she just run them together?

The thing I most want is for you to say honestly where the novel resists the Strawsonian reading — where a careful reader would have to admit it dosen't fit or pushes back — and where it adds something to the philosophical debate that the argument alone can't show.

No need for quotations or page numbers, paraphrase from memory is fine. And please write the whole answer in British English.
