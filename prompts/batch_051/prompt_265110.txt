I design the curriculum for a small training firm that runs two-day continuing-education courses for mid-career professionals — compliance officers, engineers, and junior lawyers. I lay out about one new course a month, broken into 90-minute units, and hand the finished plan to subject-matter experts who are not trained teachers. I also have to get each plan signed off by the client's learning-and-development lead before the course date. The stakes are real: clients pay several hundred dollars a seat, some courses satisfy mandatory continuing-education requirements, and every instructor defaults to a dense information-transfer lecture, because that is the only format they trust.

I want the whole curriculum sequenced around Whitehead's educational rhythm: Romance, an initial stage of vivid and somewhat unstructured engagement with the subject matter; Precision, the disciplined stage of exact definitions, rules, and practiced skill; and Generalization, a later return to open application once the precise tools have become habit. Whitehead also warns against inert ideas, knowledge that is merely received and stored rather than used. Give me a reusable operational procedure — not an exposition of Whitehead, but a checklist or decision sequence in words — that an untrained instructor and I can run in about an hour per course. For each phase it should give observable tests for whether the phase has been reached, a rule for when to move to the next phase, and an explicit statement of where judgment cannot be reduced to a rule. It should handle the hard cases: topics that seem to have no Romance phase, units whose content is exactly Precision and where Romance feels like theater, and courses where genuine Generalization cannot happen inside the two days.

Then apply the procedure to two concrete cases, and add a third of your choosing. The first is a unit on data-protection principles for compliance officers, whose obvious treatment is a lecture on the text of the regulation. The second is a unit on reading derivative payoff diagrams for junior finance analysts, which feels like a bare vocabulary list with no conceptual center. For the third, choose a case from a catalog like ours — our best-reviewed all-day regulatory update, which is pure information transfer with no skill practice, is a good candidate — and show me the unwelcome answer the procedure gives, including what I should say to the client and to the subject expert. End by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do: the cases where it will misfire and when Whitehead's rhythm is the wrong template.
