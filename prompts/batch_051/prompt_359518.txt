I'm in a debate club and were preparing a topic on sex work policy, and I keep going in circles on the Nordic model. The usual justification is basically: the state shouldn't punish the person selling sex, because they're usually the exploited one, but it should punish the buyer and any third party, because demand is what creates the market and trafficking follows demand. So if you shrink demand, you shrink the whole industry, including the worst parts, without giving victims a criminal record. That argument seems to me to assume a world where most of the industry is street-based or brothel-based: there's a pimp or manager, the seller is visibly in a bad spot, and the buyer is a client physically present. But now a huge chunk of the market is online—OnlyFans, camming, solo workers using platforms to screen clients and sell content—where a lot of sellers work alone, set their prices, keep the money, and never meet anyone in person. The "buyer" becomes a subscriber or viewer, and the "seller" looks less like a victim and more like a solo small business.

So here's what I want to know: does the Nordic model's core argument survive that shift, or was it always resting on a picture of the industry that only covers part of it now? If it survives, show me exactly which premise carries over to platform work and why the demand-side logic still binds. If it doesn't, tell me which premise the internet broke and what a repair would look like, or whether the argument should be limited to in-person sex work and something different should apply online.

Please answer like a policy analyst who actually knows the Nordic model and also knows how digital sex work works, and who cares more about being precise than defending a side. Start by steelmanning the demand-side argument in your own words and writing down the hidden assumptions you find in it, then check each one against the platform case.
