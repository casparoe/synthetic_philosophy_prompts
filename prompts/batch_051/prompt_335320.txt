I'm a novelist, and I've been turning a small real-life itch into a character trait, but I cant tell whether the trait has a name or even whether it's one trait. The character keeps a sort of ledger where she records what she currently believes about a big decision and what she expects to believe about it in a year, and she treats the two entries as separate witnesses — not as equals exactly, but she weighs each one by how much evidence and how much pressure she thinks the relevant self had. So she'll say things like "I'm eighty percent sure I should leave him now, but I can already see that a year from now I'll think it was a mistake, and I don't think future-me is wrong, exactly, she's just a different witness." Meanwhile the real-life version: a friend spent months certain a move was right and simultaneously certain that after one hard winter she'd call it a mistake, and she didn't treat that as a problem in either direction.

The itch is that this seems to cover several different things at once. If I know I'll be drunk, or in fresh grief, or in front of an audience, I'm allowed — probably required — to refuse to line my current view up with that later view. But if I know that later me will have strictly more evidence, and I still won't line up with her, that feels like a different, maybe worse, thing. And if I do line up with a later view that I expect to be the result of pressure or fatigue, that feels like a third thing, like I've given a lobbyist a vote. Is there an established principle that says your current take should already reflect the take you expect to have, and a name for the failure or refusal to do that? I dont want just the name; I want the carve. What are the nearest established concepts, which of my three cases does each one actually cover, and where is the line between "not deferring to your future self because you have more evidence" and "not deferring to your future self because you're just treating yourself as a stranger you can outvote"?

Structure the answer in four titled sections, in this order: "The basic move" (about 80 words) — say plainly what the character is doing. "Nearest names" (roughly 150 words) — the two or three established concepts this overlaps with. "What each captures and misses" (roughly 150 words) — match the concepts to my three cases and flag the misfits. "Edges and a name" (roughly 120 words) — tell me whether this trait is irrational, where its borders are, and give me a name I could put in a novel without it collapsing on a second reading.
