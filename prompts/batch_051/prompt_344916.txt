For most of my career I worked as a research scientist, and I have only come to philosophy in retirement. In my reading on the philosophy of statistics and on the evaluation of artificial intelligence systems, I keep noticing what looks like the same distinction appearing in two places, and I cannot yet tell whether the resemblance is deep or superficial.

In statistical inference there is a long-standing fault line between judging an inference by the properties of the procedure that produced it and judging it by the properties of the particular result actually obtained. The first approach asks whether the method has the right long-run properties — coverage, calibration, unbiasedness, error rates across repeated use. The second asks whether this interval is informative, whether this estimate is plausible, whether these data genuinely support this conclusion. A procedure can meet its advertised 95% coverage exactly and still deliver an interval that is useless or misleading in the case at hand; a careless procedure can, by accident, deliver exactly the right answer.

In discussions of how to supervise modern AI systems, a similar-sounding distinction is drawn between outcome-based supervision, where the system receives feedback only on its final answer, and process-based supervision, where it receives feedback on the intermediate steps of its reasoning. Here, too, one option supervises the product and the other supervises the method by which the product was made.

The proposed mapping is straightforward: long-run properties of a procedure stand to an AI system's chain of reasoning as case-level judgment of a result stands to final-answer supervision. Both fields must decide what to trust when the truth is not directly available, and both worry about a gap between how good the process is and how good the output is.

I would like to know whether this is a genuine identity of structure or only a loose analogy. If it is genuine, what would have to be true of both sides for the lessons of statistical inference — about reference classes, optional stopping, model dependence, the limits of calibration — to transfer to the design of process-based and outcome-based supervision? And what does the mapping predict that turns out false on one side? One possible break already troubles me: in statistical inference the true value is almost never available to compare the result against, which is precisely why one retreats to properties of procedures, whereas in at least some AI training regimes the outcome can be checked exactly. Please tell me whether that difference is fatal or repairable.
