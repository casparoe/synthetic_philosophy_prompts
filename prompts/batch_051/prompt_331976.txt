A friend of mine edits a small documentary channel and keeps running into the same confusing boundary. When she re-uploads a terrorist propaganda clip with her own commentary and an on-screen banner identifying it as critique, the platform leaves it up; the identical clip posted raw gets removed, and sometimes even the commented version gets removed if the banner or the context is missing. I've noticed the same logic in how fact-checkers work: a newsroom will embed a politician's false campaign ad inside an article that debunks it line by line, and the platform will keep the article up while it restricts the ad itself. So there's this widespread carve-out: otherwise banned propaganda and disinformation is allowed to circulate as long as it is being reproduced for the purpose of critique, education, or documentation.

Im trying to figure out what theory of harm this practice actually presupposes. Does the carve-out only make sense if you think the harm of propaganda is mostly a matter of people being duped by it, or of the speaker's endorsement, rather than of the message itself being amplified and repeated? Or does it presuppose the old counterspeech idea that bad speech is fixed by more speech, and that viewers can reliably tell critique apart from endorsement? But then the platform is also counting the debunked video's views as watch time and running ads against it, which looks like profiting from the exact amplification it claims to be mitigating. Reconstruct the implicit theory of speech and harm that these carve-outs rest on, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. And then tell me: if the real harm of a piece of propaganda were just exposure and repetition, regardless of how it is framed, which parts of this practice would have to change, and could any version of the critique exception survive?
