I'm a software engineer working on a donation-recommendation product that asks users to rank moral trade-offs, such as whether to prioritize animal welfare over global health or long-term future causes over present poverty. In testing, I keep noticing that people's rankings are unstable: changing the order of the options, the numeric scale, or the wording of a cause can flip a user's top choice. I can't tell whether I'm capturing something the person genuinely values or just a framing artifact, and a friend in philosophy has suggested there may be no bright line between a moral preference and a bias, only a set of practical tests people use to tell them apart.

Suppose I can implement approximate proxies for these six tests: (1) endorsement under reflection, meaning the person would retain the disposition after learning how it arose and considering serious alternatives; (2) cross-contextual robustness, meaning the disposition is stable across changes in framing, mood, and option order; (3) motivational signature, meaning it shows up as an explicit reason in deliberation rather than only as an automatic reaction; (4) higher-order fit, meaning it coheres with the other values the person explicitly avows; (5) origin quality, meaning it does not trace back to misinformation, manipulation, or trauma; and (6) temporal stability, meaning it persists over months or years. Please rank these six tests from strongest to weakest as diagnostics for separating a genuine moral preference from a bias, assign each to a tier from S down to D, and give a one-sentence justification for each placement. Then say which two tests you think are doing the most independent work, whether any subset of them could be treated as a reasonable sufficient condition for calling something a preference rather than a bias, and, if you can construct one, describe a realistic case in which a mere bias would still pass the entire battery.
