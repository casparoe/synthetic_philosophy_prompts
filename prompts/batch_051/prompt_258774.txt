I serve on a working group that has been asked to comment on a draft regulation for automated vehicles, and I would like a mechanistic analysis of the proposal rather than a moral evaluation. The draft attempts to operationalize the doctrine of double effect — roughly, the principle that harms are easier to justify when they are foreseen side effects of pursuing a good outcome than when they are intended, or used as the means by which the good outcome is produced. Under the proposal, no driving policy may be deployed until it has passed a side-effect certification. The manufacturer submits the policy together with a specification of the objective it was trained to optimize. A regulator then runs the policy over a large battery of collision scenarios. In any scenario in which the policy brings about serious harm (say, steering toward a motorcyclist in order to avoid a larger group of pedestrians), the regulator applies a counterfactual test: it reruns the scenario with the harmed individual removed from the scene while holding everything else fixed. If the policy performs substantially the same maneuver in the absence of that individual, the harm is classified as a side effect and is permitted. If the maneuver changes once the individual is removed — indicating that the individual's presence is doing real work in the policy's choice — the harm is classified as an intended means, and the policy is rejected. Certified policies receive partial immunity from liability for collision harms, while uncertified policies face strict liability.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than a moral position. What does it incentivize among manufacturers in how they specify objectives, construct training scenarios, and select which policies to submit for certification? How would it be gamed — through re-describing objectives, training on adversarially chosen scenarios, or otherwise? What would happen in collision types absent from the test battery, and where does the scheme fail first? If you propose fixes, say whether they would address the failure modes without quietly gutting the intended-means/side-effect distinction the rule is trying to enforce.
