Question: Explain the Dutch book argument for probabilism. Your answer should (i) say what it is for a set of credences to be Dutch-bookable, (ii) show how a bookie can exploit at least one violation of the probability axioms, and (iii) note one important limitation of the argument.

Student answer: The Dutch book argument aims to show that rational degrees of belief must obey the probability axioms. A set of credences is Dutch-bookable if there is a collection of bets, each priced in accordance with the agent’s degrees of belief, such that the agent is guaranteed a net loss regardless of the outcomes. For instance, if c(A) > 1, a bookie can sell you a bet that pays $1 if A; you pay more than $1 for it, so no matter whether A occurs or not, you end up with less than you paid. Similarly, if c(A) < 0, a bookie can buy from you a bet on A at a negative price, which means you pay him to take the bet, again guaranteeing loss. For finite additivity, if A and B are mutually exclusive and c(A∨B) < c(A) + c(B), the bookie sells you bets on A and B and buys a bet on A∨B from you; the initial transaction nets the bookie c(A) + c(B) − c(A∨B), and the payoffs cancel in all worlds, leaving you with a sure loss. The argument also extends to countable additivity: if you are merely finitely additive but violate countable additivity, the same construction can be applied to a countable partition of the union, so rational degrees of belief must be countably additive. A limitation is that the argument assumes you are willing to take either side of any bet at your fair odds, but real agents may reject bets for reasons unrelated to credences.
