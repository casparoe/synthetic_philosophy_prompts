In a course on cultural evolution, I keep hearing the claim that culture is a genuinely Darwinian process, and the argument usually rests on the analogy between memes and genes: cultural variants, like alleles, are copied with modification, compete for a limited supply of hosts and attention, and spread or die out according to their environment, so cultural change can be studied with tools adapted from population genetics. I would like an audit of how far this analogy actually holds. Give the mapping explicitly: what corresponds to the gene, the replicator, the vehicle or phenotype, mutation, and differential fitness? Then identify the respects in which the mapping breaks down, for example the fidelity and discreteness of copying, blending or transformative transmission, the inheritance of acquired characteristics, the absence of anything like a germ line or clear generations, and uncertainty about what unit is being selected. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion (that cultural change is selection acting on replicators and that meme is more than a metaphor) from those that are merely incidental. Does that conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it? If the gene analogy cannot bear the weight, what would a better analogy or model be? Please write your answer in American English.
