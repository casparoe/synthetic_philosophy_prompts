I'm an undergraduate in an epistemology seminar, and I'm drafting a term paper on whether Nozick's truth-tracking account forces one to give up epistemic closure, or whether the same pattern of verdicts can be had without taking on all the commitments of the tracking view. In service of this, I have been recording my professor's rulings on cases all term, since she tends to signal her position through examples rather than stating it outright. Her record so far: in a normal zoo enclosure, seeing a zebra by its stripes is knowledge; on that same visual basis, believing that the animal is not a cleverly disguised mule is not knowledge; in the fake-barn county, no knowledge; in the lottery case, no knowledge; reading a reliable thermometer under ordinary conditions, knowledge; the stopped clock that happens to read correctly, no knowledge; a true belief resting on a false intermediate premise, no knowledge; and a reliable stranger's testimony, knowledge, absent any reason for doubt. She has told us she is drawn to Nozick's tracking theory, but she also assigns Dretske and will not say whether she thinks the two positions make the same predictions after all.

Next week she plans to present a case of her own and poll us before revealing her view: a grandmother believes her grandson is in good health because her daughter has just told her so over the phone, and the grandson is in fact healthy. But it is stipulated that if he had been seriously ill, the daughter would have told her the very same reassuring thing. Predict how my professor will rule. Then articulate the principle that best fits her whole record — Nozick's tracking theory, understood through its sensitivity condition, a Dretske-style relevant-alternatives rule, or something else — and say which rival principles fit the record roughly equally well. Finally, name a further case, not yet on her list, that would most decisively distinguish those principles.
