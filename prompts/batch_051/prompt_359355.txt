I hold a position I'll call reconstructive memetic realism: the claim that "meme" is more than a metaphor, because cultural variants — melodies, catchphrases, rituals, tool-making techniques — form a Darwinian population with heritable variation and differential persistence, so that selection operates on the variants themselves. I grant the standard complications: cultural copying is reconstructive rather than high-fidelity, variants blend and merge, and the boundaries between variants are often imposed by observers rather than given by nature. I am not defending atomistic replicators. What would the world have to look like for this weakened view to be false? Which patterns in transmission-chain data, which statistical signatures of cultural change, which formal results, and which arguments should lower my confidence? Distinguish the defeaters a committed defender would concede are genuine costs from those they would dismiss as attacks on a straw version, and rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Say what the view predicts that rivals do not — for instance, accounts on which cultural stability is explained by shared cognitive biases rather than by descent. Also say whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so whether that matters. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on cultural evolution, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
