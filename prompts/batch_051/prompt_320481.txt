I write near-future fiction, and I've caught myself using a plotting trick as a general policy for what to believe about real technologies. When I meet a new technology, I don't ask what the evidence makes likely. I ask what's the worst plausible way it could go wrong, and then I treat that tail as my default expectation until someone can show the specific mechanism is impossible. Last month it was solar geoengineering: I skipped the median model entirely, fixated on the monsoon-shift-and-famine scenario, and started talking about it as if that were simply what was going to happen. My partner says I've turned the precautionary principle into an epistemic rule and that I'm trading naive optimism for a bias toward vivid, story-shaped catastrophes (which, fair, I am literally paid to produce). I see their point, but I also think there's an asymmetry: if I'm wrong about the tail, I'm suprised; if the optimists are wrong, people die. Is this defensible as a belief-forming policy? Is there a version of the rule that keeps the caution without making me predictably wrong about near-term futures?
