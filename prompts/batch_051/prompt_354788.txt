I keep coming back to Douglas Hofstadter's argument that machine translation is shallow. As I understand it, his claim is that a system which learns to translate by extracting patterns from large collections of human-translated text does not understand what it is translating: it recombines surface patterns, so its output can be grammatically smooth while missing what the passage means, and this missing understanding shows up in failures on idioms, ambiguity, and wording that requires a conceptual grasp. He made this case against the translation software of his era, and the systems he examined were far weaker than today's. What I want to know is whether the argument survives the shift to current large language models, which are also trained on enormous text collections but produce translations that are fluent and context-sensitive, often handle the very cases Hofstadter treated as revealing, and are claimed by some people to have formed internal representations that amount to understanding. Does his argument carry over unchanged, or does the new system undercut one of its premises, and can the argument be restated so that it still has force? If you think it is dead, identify the premise that no longer holds and say what a system would have to do for Hofstadter's objection to apply to it again.
