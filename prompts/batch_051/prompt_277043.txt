Question: Explain the disagreement between an expectation-based account of promissory obligation, of the kind associated with T.M. Scanlon, and a social-practice account, of the kind associated with John Rawls. Then explain how each account would treat a one-off assurance made between two people who share no standing social practice of promising, and say which treatment you find more plausible.

Student's response: Scanlon's expectation account locates the source of promissory obligation in the moral relation between the two parties. Amara's promise binds because she deliberately leads Ben to expect that she will perform, and Ben organizes his plans around that expectation; the wrong of breaking the promise is the harm or setback Ben suffers when an expectation she induced is disappointed. No social practice is needed for a one-off assurance to bind, because the morally relevant facts are all facts about what passes between the two individuals.

Rawls's practice account denies this. Promising is a move within a public institution constituted by rules, and an individual promisor's obligation is one of fair play: whoever voluntarily accepts the benefits of a just practice must accept its burdens. The practice of promising is justified by the trust and reliable coordination it produces across a society, and this productive value is what makes any particular promise obligatory for those who invoke the practice. It follows that outside a shared social practice of promising, uttering the words I promise would generate no promissory obligation; it would amount only to a private resolution. The one-off assurance case therefore favors Scanlon, because it confirms that a deliberately induced expectation can bind even where no institution exists.
