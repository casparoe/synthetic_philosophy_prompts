I'm in a jurisprudence seminar and the professor has spent the whole term going through hard cases about when a court should treat a rule as law. She's clearly trying to make us work through the Hart–Fuller fight from the cases rather than slogans, but she refuses to tell us her own position, and for the final problem set we have to predict how she'd answer a new case. Here's the track record I've kept of her one-line verdicts:

1. Junta decree case: a junta that seized power in violation of the constitution issued a decree, never published it anywhere, and its own hand-picked tribunal enforced it against a dissident. The old constitution is back in force. Prof says the decree was never law and the regular courts should treat it as void.

2. Informer case: a woman reported her husband for defeatist talk under a 1944 statute that was public, general, and clear; he was executed. The new court now has to say whether the statute was law at the time. Prof says it was law at the time, though monstrously unjust, and that if we want to punish the informer we should do it openly with a retroactive statute rather than pretend the old statute never was law.

3. Absolute ruler case: a ruler issues commands that change daily, are often secret, sometimes contradict each other, and occasionally demand the impossible; his palace courts just apply whatever he said most recently. Prof says this isn't a legal system at all, and none of his commands are laws—not because he's a tyrant, but because there's no functioning body of general, stable, knowable rules.

4. Park bylaw case: a bylaw prohibits vehicles in a municipal park, and the question is whether a bicycle counts. Prof says the word has an uncontroversial center (a car) and an open fringe (a bicycle, a scooter, a maintenance truck), and at the fringe the court has to decide by working out what the bylaw is for—so the decision isn't just mechanical.

5. Unjust tax case: a duly enacted, published, prospective, general statute imposes a special tax on an ethnic minority to fund a war. Prof says it's law, and its injustice is a separate question; officials should apply it unless some other constitutional provision bars it.

New case: the legislature passed a criminal statute through every ordinary step—majority vote, speaker signs, president signs—but the government never published it in the official gazette and never made it public by other means. Two years later a person is prosecuted under it for conduct that was legal before the statute was passed. The constitution is silent on whether publication is required for a statute to become law, and the court must decide whether to treat it as law.

What would the prof likely say about the new case? I can see at least a source-based-positivism reading of her record, a Fuller-style procedural-natural-law reading, and maybe a substantive natural-law reading she's already rejected. Which of these rules best fits the whole record, what does each predict about the unpublished statute, and whats your best guess about how she comes down? And if the record plus the new case still leaves two readings open, what further case would you ask her about to settle it?
