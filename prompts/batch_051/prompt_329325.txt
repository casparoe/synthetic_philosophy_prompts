I've been kicking around a view I call Sunk Cost as Promissory Residue. The core claim is that honoring sunk costs is not automatically irrational, because a sunk cost is more than a number in a ledger: it is the trace of a decision your earlier self made for reasons. If you now abandon the project just because the costs are behind you, you treat those earlier reasons as if they never had authority over you, and that is a form of diachronic disrespect — a bit like rewriting the past so the person who chose the project counts for nothing. On this view, finishing a book you are not enjoying, sitting through a film you bought a ticket for, or continuing a degree you have come to dislike can all be rational, not because the past expenditure changes future payoffs, but because abandoning it incurs an identity cost: it severs continuity with the self who chose it. I am not saying the money matters; I am saying the earlier choice functions like a promise to your later self even when no promise was made.

I know this crashes straight into the standard objection that only marginal costs and benefits matter and the money is gone whether you keep going or not, so the only real question is whether the next hour is better spent reading or walking out. And I am aware this sounds like giveing the fallacy a fancy new name. But I want the strongest version of the case against it, not a dunk.

So: what are the three strongest objections to treating sunk costs as promissory residue, stated in their most damaging form? Is any of them fatal, or are they just uncomfortable? Also, does this already exist under a better name — it feels adjacent to ideas about the self over time or treating one's past self as another person, but I cannot tell whether it is actually distinct.
