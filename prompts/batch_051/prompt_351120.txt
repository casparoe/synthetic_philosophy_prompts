Next week I'm giving a department seminar at an economics department on something I've been calling multipolar traps — the Moloch idea, where each actor optimizes locally and the result is a stable equilibrium nobody wants, because any unilateral deviation makes the deviator worse off. My thesis is that these are not just negative externalities or commons problems, and that the standard economist fixes (Pigouvian taxes, Coasian bargaining, assigning property rights) systematically fail for the cases I actually care about: AI capability races, platform enshittification, arms races, maybe grade inflation. My move in the talk is to argue that the distinguishing feature of a multipolar trap is that the bad outcome is one of several stable equilibria of the same underlying game, and the actors can see the better equilibrium but can't coordinate to it, while a textbook externality has a single equilibrium with a deadweight loss. I also claim the trap tightens as the number of actors grows, which is why some proposed fixes make it worse. I'm trying to convince the room that multipolar trap earns its keep as a distinct category rather than a rebranding of stuff they already know. The audience is a mainstream econ department: mostly applied micro and public finance people, a couple of theorists, people who care about identification, comparative statics, and whether a model has a clean policy lever, and no one sympathetic to AI-risk or the rationalist blogosphere. I'm not going to say the word Moloch because they'd roll there eyes.
