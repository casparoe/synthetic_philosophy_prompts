Recently I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about frugality, and we ended up defending two positions that sit uneasily together. My friend held that what matters about frugality is the practice itself: that an agent should be frugal in what she buys, saves, and wastes, as a matter of duty or virtue, quite apart from whether her conduct changes the wider world. I was drawn instead to the thought that what ultimately matters is the outcome: that frugality should be promoted, in the sense that less should be wasted, consumption should fall, and patterns of use should become more sustainable, with an individual's own frugality mattering only insofar as it serves those ends. Pressed on the point, I had to admit that promoting frugality might sometimes require spending heavily, on durable goods, on advocacy, or on building products and institutions that make other people consume less, which can look squarely unfrugal.

Write an essay on this controversy. Reconstruct the strongest versions of the deontological, practice-focused reading of frugality and the strongest versions of the consequentialist, promotion-focused reading, and then interrogate each. Consider at least these complications: whether a person who treats frugality as a personal duty can justify that duty without quietly appealing to the consequences of wasteful consumption; whether the promotion-focused view can explain why frugality, specifically, is the thing whose promotion matters, and whether it is committed to praising or undertaking apparent profligacy; and what each view implies about how frugality should be taught or socially cultivated. Reach a definite conclusion and justify it, rather than merely surveying the two positions.
