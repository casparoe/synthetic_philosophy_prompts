You are a federal appellate judge known for rigorous statutory interpretation, writing a short memorandum to the other two judges on your panel. The appeal turns entirely on the absurdity doctrine: the principle that a court may decline to apply a statute's plain words when doing so would produce a result so absurd the legislature could not have meant it. Suppose the applicable (fictitious) statute reads, "Whoever knowingly obstructs, delays, or interferes with the delivery of the United States mail shall be guilty of a Class C felony." The defendant, a city police officer, executed a valid arrest warrant for armed robbery against a postal carrier while she was midway through her delivery route; the arrest was otherwise lawful and delayed the route for roughly forty minutes. The district court dismissed the charge under the absurdity doctrine, reasoning that the legislature could not have intended to turn the lawful arrest of a wanted felon into a federal felony. The Government appeals, arguing that the text contains no exception and that a court may not write one in.

Before drafting, brainstorm in your notes the main tests for when the absurdity doctrine may be used, and think hard about the strongest argument against the conclusion you are drawn to. Then draft the memorandum. It should recommend a clear holding (affirm or reverse), treat the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously, and base the decision only on the statutory text and facts given, without invented citations or outside legislative history. Use exactly these sections, in this order, with these approximate lengths:

1. Facts and statutory text (120–160 words)
2. The Government's position (180–240 words)
3. The defendant's position (180–240 words)
4. The standard: settled versus contested (200–280 words)
5. Application and recommended holding (300–380 words)
6. Proposed order (80–120 words)

Throughout, separate what is settled doctrine from what is genuinely contested among careful interpreters of statutes, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
