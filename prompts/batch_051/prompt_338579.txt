Walter Bagehot's The English Constitution (1867) draws a distinction between the dignified parts of the constitution and the efficient parts of the constitution. I have been trying to use a less-quoted thread in his treatment of monarchy—his claim that one of the crown's chief advantages is that it makes the state intelligible. As I understand him, Bagehot argues that ordinary people need a simple, visible, human symbol of political order, and that the monarchy provides this in a way the abstract machinery of parliament and cabinet cannot; the sovereign gives the multitude something to see and understand, and thereby attaches them to a constitution that would otherwise be too remote and complex. I want to test this strand of his theory against a real episode.

The episode is the 1975 Australian constitutional crisis. The salient facts as I know them: on 11 November 1975, the Governor-General, Sir John Kerr, dismissed the Labor Prime Minister, Gough Whitlam, appointed the opposition leader, Malcolm Fraser, as caretaker prime minister, and dissolved Parliament. Whitlam's government had been unable to get supply through the Senate, but it still retained the confidence of the House of Representatives. Kerr acted in his capacity as the Queen's representative, though he did not consult the Queen beforehand, and he kept his intentions secret from the prime minister. Whitlam denounced the dismissal, but Kerr's action held, and the election that followed produced a landslide for Fraser.

Please give me a careful mapping of the 1975 crisis through Bagehot's lens: who or what plays the dignified role, and who or what plays the efficient role? Where does Bagehot's account of monarchy as a simple, intelligible symbol of the state explain what happened, and where does it break down or misdescribe the crisis? What does the episode in turn show about his claim—is it a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case he never anticipated? I am especially interested in where Bagehot's theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
