I recently retired from a career in the environmental sciences, and my recent reading in philosophy has made me reconsider a pattern I once took for granted in regulatory toxicology. For a well-studied chemical, agencies treat harm as a calculable risk: they fit dose-response models, attach confidence bounds to the estimates, and set an exposure threshold corresponding to a probability of harm deemed acceptably small. For a poorly studied chemical, the same agencies adopt a different logic: absent demonstrated safety, the chemical is treated as dangerous enough to restrict, even though no probability of harm has been estimated. The same compound can move from the second treatment to the first as studies accumulate, even though its actual toxic properties have not changed. The institution, in other words, treats known probabilities and unknown probabilities by quite different rules. What theory of decision under uncertainty does this two-track practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that ambiguity — uncertainty about the probabilities themselves, as against risk, where the probabilities are known — should be treated as a distinct kind of uncertainty, as the Ellsberg paradox is often taken to suggest? Or could a Bayesian expected-utility framework with subjective priors rationalize both tracks as ordinary expected-utility maximization, in which case the practice merely fits an ambiguity-averse pattern without strictly presupposing one? Reconstruct the implicit theory of the practice, say whether the two tracks presuppose one coherent view or two inconsistent ones, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it is merely compatible with, and tell me what a regulator committed to treating all uncertainty probabilistically would have to change — and what would be lost in the change.
