Here's a parallel I can't decide about. In algorithmic randomness the standard move is to say a finite string is never intrinsically random: any finite string could have been printed by some deterministic program that just hardcodes it, so randomness has to live in the source (the coin, the program, the infinite sequence), and a finite string is only more or less compressible. In virtue ethics there's a move with the same shape: a single action is never intrinsically virtuous, because the same outward act could come from greed or fear or habit, so virtue has to be a property of the character behind the act, and the act is only fallible evidence for it. The shared principle would be something like: when a property belongs to the generating process rather than the token, no token is ever the bearer of the property.

I can't tell whether that's a real structural identity or just the words "produced by" doing the work. If it's real, results should transfer. The transfer I most want to make is this: algorithmic randomness still has a graded notion for finite strings (Kolmogorov complexity — a string can be more or less patternless), so the analogy predicts virtue ethics should have a graded notion of act-virtue, some score for how much an act compresses under the agent's motvies. Virtue ethics mostly refuses that. Is its refusal the place the analogy breaks, or is it a sign the analogy is trying to import a quantification that ethics doesn't need? Also, Martin-Löf randomness is defined only for infinite sequences, but a human life is finite — so what would the ethical analogue of "keeps passing every test no matter how far you go" even be?

Maybe both "random" and "virtuous" are attempts to point at an unobservable disposition we only ever see through finite samples. Or maybe I'm just pattern-matching on "can't be a property of a single observed thing." Which is it?
