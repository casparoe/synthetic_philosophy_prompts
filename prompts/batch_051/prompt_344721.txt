I cover science and ideas for a living, and I've spent the past few months poking at a question I assumed was long settled: whether causation is a real feature of the world, or just a habit of talk. What I keep finding is a fight that's been running for more than a century, in which the same three positions keep returning in new cloths, and nobody seems to have moved anyone in decades.

One camp, going back to Bertrand Russell, says the notion of cause is a leftover from an older way of thinking. Physics gets by with laws, symmetries, and differential equations; the word 'cause' survives in everyday talk and the softer sciences because it feels explanatory, not because it picks out anything in the world. A second camp says causes are real but not fundamental: causation just is counterfactual dependence, or probability-raising, or the passing of a conserved quantity from one event to another, or an invariant relation you can exploit when you intervene. A third camp says all those reductions run in a circle — counterfactuals, probabilities, and interventions all smuggle in causal notions from the start — so causation has to be accepted as basic.

And the strange thing is, everybody is looking at the same examples. The barometer falls before the storm but doesn't cause it. In the firing squad, several shooters fire at once, and no one bullet is counterfactually necessary, yet each shot seems to be a cause. Smoking raises the probability of lung cancer, but plenty of smokers never get it and plenty of nonsmokers do. The examples don't settle anything because each side reads them through its own picture of what a cause would have to be.

When I ask a physicist about all this, they shrug and say causes don't appear anywhere in their equations. When I ask an epidemiologist a week later, they say causal inference is the whole point of what they do. Both are describing the same world, and both seem almost proud that the other side's concept is useless to them. That makes me suspect the stalemate isn't really a factual one.

My working theory is that the three camps aren't answering the same question. The eliminativists are talking about fundamental physics and find that the word 'cause' has no work to do there; the reductionists are trying to analyze ordinary causal thought; the primitivists are asking what the world has to be like for any causal talk to be apt in the first place. If that's right, the arguments sail past each other because each camp has quietly adopted a different standard for what would settle the matter — one wants the word to earn its keep in physics, one wants an analysis that handles tricky cases, one wants a category that can't be defined away.

But I know this could just be a convenient story I tell myself. Maybe there's a real formal or empirical question underneath the rhetoric — for instance, whether a causal model can predict under interventions without causation already built into its variables, or whether every physical explanation can be rewritten in non-causal terms without losing anything. So I'm asking for a diagnosis, not a verdict: is this a verbal dispute, three separate questions sharing a word, a genuine factual disagreement, or some mixture? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result — or should we expect the stalemate to keep running another century?
