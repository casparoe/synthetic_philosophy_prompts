Machine-learning researchers, safety teams, and technical writers now routinely describe a false or fabricated statement produced by a large language model as a "hallucination," even when the model states the falsehood with complete confidence. Yet the same field, when explaining how these systems work, insists that a language model is just a next-token predictor: it assigns probabilities to continuations and samples from them, and it has no beliefs, no intentions, and no internal state that could itself be true or false. Those two commitments look as if they are in tension. Calling an output a hallucination suggests a truth-tracking faculty that has malfunctioned; calling the model a next-token predictor suggests there is no truth-tracking faculty there to malfunction.

Frankfurt's well-known contrast between the liar and the bullshitter gives the issue a precise shape. On that account, the liar knows and cares about the truth enough to conceal or deny it; the bullshitter is simply indifferent to how things are; and error is what remains when someone is genuinely trying, and failing, to get the facts right. What does the standard practice of calling model outputs "hallucinations" presuppose about a language model's relation to truth? Does it presuppose that the model is attempting, and failing, to track the world — and is that consistent with the next-token-predictor story the same institutions tell when they write disclaimers about accuracy and reliability? Or would the Frankfurt-style taxonomy force us to classify these outputs as bullshit, or as mere error, rather than as hallucination? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely suggests, and say what would have to change in how these systems are documented, evaluated, or regulated if the field instead classified the behavior as bullshit or as error.
