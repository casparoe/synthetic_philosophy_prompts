I keep going back and forth on the socialist calculation debate, mostly because I can't tell whether my sympathy for Hayek is just a matter of taste (dispersed local knowledge, prices compressing local information into a single number, all that) or whether someone could actually bolt a working discovery mechanism onto Lange-style central planning. A friend proposed a specific institution and I want to know how it would actually behave, not whether it's right in the abstract.

Here's the mechanism. The economy is a central planning board that owns all the capital goods and sets official prices for every intermediate good and production input. State-owned firms must buy and sell at those official prices, and the board updates prices by trail and error whenever it sees a persistent shortage or surplus, Lange-style. The twist is an appeal market. Any local manager who thinks an official price is wrong can post a refundable bond and open a short, local auction for the good they manage or use, limited to their actual suppliers and customers and capped at, say, a week's worth of normal volumes. If the auction clears at a price more than X% away from the official one, the board has to revise the official price toward the auction-clearing price, and the manager gets a cut of the measured surplus over the next quarter. If the auction doesn't clear at a significantly different price, or the revised price makes things worse on some aggregate audit, the manager forfeits the bond. Auditors get to see every auction's bid-ask history, but they can't talk to the bidders about why they bid what they bid.

Walk me through what this actually does. Who learns what, who games it first, and what are the likely failure modes? Does it surface Hayekian local knowledge, or does it just create a market for challenging the board? Can the failure modes be patched without turning the whole thing into, like, just a normal market economy?
