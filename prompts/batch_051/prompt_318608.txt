I am putting together a handout for an undergraduate philosophy of mind seminar whose shared text is Douglas Hofstadter's *Ant Fugue* dialogue from *Gödel, Escher, Bach*. The discussion keeps collapsing into students naming positions rather than being sorted by what they actually think about the cases. I want an instrument that starts from Aunt Hillary's core analogy: she invites the reader to regard the ants as something like neurons and the colony as something like a brain, so that the colony can have mental properties—recognizing food shortages, forming goals, adjusting to damage—even though no individual ant has those properties. Treat that passage as the default yes-it-has-a-mind argument to be developed, qualified, or rejected, not as an authority.

Please write a decision tree or eight-to-ten-question sorting quiz. Each branch question should present a concrete case or a claim that people can assess without already knowing the philosophical labels. Include at least the following cases among the questions: a single ant removed from the colony and kept alive; the ordinary colony reallocating foragers after its food supply is cut; a full real-time computer simulation that reproduces the colony's observable dynamics; a human split-brain patient whose one hemisphere performs an action the other cannot report; and a very small group of, say, twenty ants that cannot form normal colony-level structures. Do not ask any question whose wording is just whether one thinks the colony has a mind.

The leaves should include at least the following positions: strong colony-level realism (the colony has a mind in the same way an individual animal does, though differently realized); a functionalist position that grants the colony mental states only if it meets a suitable functional specification and otherwise withholds judgment; a deflationary position treating mind-talk as a predictive device with no deep fact of the matter; biological chauvinism (only brains have minds); parts-only eliminativism (there is no colony over and above the ants to be a bearer of mental properties); and a proto-mental/panpsychist-leaning view that locates whatever mind exists in the individual ants rather than the collective. For each leaf, give one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where a combination of answers has no occupant, say briefly whether that is because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves that your tree cannot separate.
