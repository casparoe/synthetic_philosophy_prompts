In my school's philosophy society we have been discussing an idea from debates about artificial intelligence: that a machine ought to act not on our present preferences, which are misinformed, inconsistent and short-sighted, but on what is sometimes called our coherent extrapolated volition — roughly, the preferences we would all converge on if we knew the relevant facts, reasoned without bias, and had grown up and thought things through together. I find the idea attractive, but I have been trying to test an objection to it, and I would like to know whether the argument I have put together goes through. It runs as follows. (1) The entire value of an idealised preference must come from the actual preferences it is meant to refine; otherwise the theorist is not uncovering what we already, in some sense, want, but inventing new values and attaching our name to them. (2) Refining a set of preferences is not a single, forced operation, because whenever two of a person's preferences conflict — say, a preference for security and a preference for risk — there is no fact already present in those preferences that determines how far to revise each one. (3) The difficulty is only greater at the level of a whole society, since different people's preferences conflict as well, and there is no single mind whose coherence is being restored. (4) Some rule for resolving these conflicts therefore has to be chosen. (5) That rule is either derived from the very preferences being refined, in which case the derivation must already assign weights to them, which was the choice the rule was supposed to supply, or it is imposed from outside the preferences, in which case the resulting idealisation is the rule-maker's ideal, not ours. (6) Therefore, coherent extrapolated volition is either circular or imposed, and in neither case does it supply the unique, authoritative account of what we really want that its defenders claim for it. I suspect step (2) is the weak point, but I cannot tell whether weakening it would save the argument or whether the real mistake is elsewhere. Is the argument valid as it stands? If not, exactly which step or steps fail, and what would the strongest repair be?
