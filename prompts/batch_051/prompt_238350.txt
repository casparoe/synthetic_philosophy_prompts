You are advising a three-year policy research programme on virtual worlds. Its final output will be guidance for regulators and platform designers on questions such as whether virtual property should be protected the way chattels are, whether harassing an avatar is best treated as an assault on the player or as harm to a fictional entity, and whether in-game theft is a real loss or merely a deprivation of licensed access. The programme directors believe that some metaphysics will be load-bearing for these outputs, but they cannot settle everything first. I want your defended judgement about which open questions deserve the programme's two working groups.

Candidate upstream questions include the following (do not feel bound to the list; add or replace questions if it matters, but end with a definite ordering):

(a) Are virtual objects best analysed as abstract types, as concrete computational particulars, as fictional objects engaged via make-believe, or as artefacts whose persistence conditions are fixed by their causal history?

(b) Do the identity and persistence conditions of virtual objects — across server restarts, patches, duplication exploits, save states, and porting between platforms — determine whether there is a persisting thing capable of being stolen, damaged, or destroyed, or do those verdicts come from elsewhere?

(c) Is virtual harm best analysed as harm to the player's interests, harm to a character within the fiction, or a category error to be dissolved rather than answered?

(d) How much of the apparent metaphysics of virtual worlds is in fact conventional — fixed by game code, terms of service, and design intent — so that the substantive residue is smaller than it looks?

(e) Does the increasing commingling of real money and virtual items (real-money trading, play-to-earn economies, tax and estate law treating accounts as property) settle the reality question for practical purposes by institutional fiat, or does it merely add another layer of social ontology on top of unsettled metaphysics?

For each question you would pursue, give the order in which you would pursue it and the dependencies that place it there: which questions are upstream of which, which can be worked on in parallel, and which would change few downstream policy recommendations if answered either way. Justify the ordering using explicit criteria — for example, importance to the programme's guidance, tractability within three years, neglectedness, and the number of other questions depending on the answer — and be explicit about how you trade these criteria off against one another. Then identify one or two questions you would deliberately deprioritise or dissolve, and say what new evidence or argument would change your ranking. Do not present a balanced survey; the deliverable is a committed agenda, with reasons for each placement.
