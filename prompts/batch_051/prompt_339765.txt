At lunch a couple weeks ago two friends of mine got into it over a made-up betting game, and I keep turning it over. The game: two bowls of poker chips. Bowl A has 100 chips, exactly 50 red and 50 black, counted in front of everyone. Bowl B also has 100 chips, every one red or black, but nobody knows the mix — could be all red, all black, anything in between. One chip gets drawn blind. If it matches your color, you win a hundred dollars.

Omar said he'd rather take the red bet from Bowl A than from Bowl B. Lena asked whether he'd also rather take the black bet from Bowl A than from Bowl B, and he said sure. She said that pair of choices can't be right together: you can't think the known bowl is the better bet on red and the better bet on black, since in Bowl B red and black have to add up to 100 chips, so if red looks worse than fifty-fifty, black should look better. Unless, she added, he simply isn't assigning probabilities at all.

Omar said that's exactly it, and he didn't think it was a problem: with Bowl A he can price it at 50%, but with Bowl B he can't put any number on the chances, and that ignorance itself makes both bets worse. Ambiguity isn't just more risk, he said, it's a different kind of bad.

Lena came back that the chips don't care whether he counted them. If he'd pay $40 for a red bet from the counted bowl but only $30 for the same red bet from the unknown bowl, he's paying ten bucks to whoever did the counting. That's a fee for his own nerves, not a feature of the bet.

Omar replied that nerves weren't the point: someone might have filled Bowl B on purpose. If he bets red, maybe it's stacked all black; if he bets black, maybe it's stacked all red. Each bet, taken alone, has a worst case of zero, while the counted bowl's worst case is fifty-fifty. He's not paying for nerves; he's paying insurance against being set up.

Lena said but the bowl can't be rigged against both bets at once. If it was stacked to destroy your red bet, your black bet was a sure win. So he's acting like the same unseen bowl can be worst for red and worst for black at the same time, which are mutually exclusive. Omar said he wasn't combining them; he was making each choice separately by its own worst case, and that's a consistent policy.

Then Lena pushed one more time: that policy still means his two separate choices add up to treating a guaranteed $100 payout from the known bowl as worth more than a guaranteed $100 payout from the unknown bowl, because a red bet plus a black bet pays out for sure in either bowl. Omar didn't have much of an answer to that.

I honestly can't referee it, and they've both got arguments I can't shake. Go through this move by move and say who's right about which point, whether Omar's pattern of preference is incoherent or just not how we normally price bets, and what, if anything, they're both missing.
