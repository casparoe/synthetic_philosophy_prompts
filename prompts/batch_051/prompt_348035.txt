I'm a retired geologist, and after forty years of thinking about what makes a slope fail I've got interested in the philosophy of history. In my old line of work you could sometimes test a causal story — whether that storm actually mattered, whether the slope would have failed anyway. It's occured to me that historical causes are a wierd business by comparison, which is what this argument was about.

At our monthly reading group last week, two of the regulars, Clare and Ravi, got into it over the old claim that the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand caused the First World War. I've tried to reconstruct the exchange as faithfully as I can.

Clare said: "That's daft. The alliance system, the mobilisation timetables, the naval race — the thing was already primed. If Princip's shot had missed, something else would very likely have set a general war off. Calling the assassination the cause is like calling the last raindrop the cause of a landslide."

Ravi: "But the last raindrop can be a cause of a landslide. Not the whole cause, sure, but a cause. You're demanding that a cause be everything going on at once."

Clare: "No, I'm demanding that it make a difference. If the war would probably have happened anyway, shot or no shot, then the outcome doesn't depend on the assassination. At that point 'cause' has stopped doing any real work."

Ravi: "Then you've made necessary for causation. Firing squad: four men shoot, any one bullet would have been enough, none of them alone was necessary. You wouldn't say none of them caused the death. Overdetermination is a real feature of the world, and it can be a feature of history too."

Clare: "In the firing squad the overdetermination is stipulated. In history it's inferred, usually without the ability to vary anything or run the case twice. Historians can't test the counterfactual, so claims about overdetermination often dress up ignorance as structure."

Ravi: "But your counterfactual test fares no better — you can't rerun 1914 without the assassination either. And if you insist on counterfactual dependence, you'll also lose every genuine case of redundancy. More than that, we need to say what the outcome is. The assassination plausibly made a difference to the timing and the chain: a war in late July 1914, started over Serbia, rather than some other war in 1916 over something else. So even on your own standard, it mattered to the war we actually got."

Clare: "That's a retreat dressed up as precision. When people list the causes of the war they don't mean 'it affected the date'; they mean 'this is why it happened'. If your defence is that the shot changed the scheduling, you've quietly conceded that it wasn't a cause of the war in the ordinary sense."

Ravi: "I haven't conceded that. I've said 'the war' is the vague thing, and you were the one trading on it. Once we fix the outcome, the post-assassination world and the no-assassination world diverge right away, and that's exactly what you said causes have to do. Saying it only concerned the date is your phrase, not mine."

I could not work out who had won, and neither would budge. Please referee this properly, move by move: say who is right about which specific claim at each stage, what both of them are missing, and who is more right overall. I don't need a reading list or the scholarly state of the art — I want your judgement of the argument as it happened. Please also tell me what would change your mind: which consideration or bit of evidence would flip the verdict, or where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail. And I'd be grateful if you'd write the whole thing in British English.
