I'm a high school student on my school's debate team, and we're preparing for a round on whether our city should allow sixteen-year-olds and non-citizen permanent residents to vote in municipal elections. Our government teacher calls the underlying issue the boundary problem: before a democracy can decide anything through a vote, someone has to decide who belongs to "the people" doing the deciding, and that prior decision cannot itself be settled democratically without circularity. During prep, one teammate keeps invoking the slogan "no taxation without representation" to justify the non-citizen half of our case. His argument is that permanent residents pay city property and sales taxes, so they are being taxed without representation and therefore ought to get the vote. Another teammate responds that this is just revolutionary propaganda and has no real argumentative force in a modern municipal election.

I want to know what that slogan could actually be claiming if we treated it as a premise in an argument about the franchise. Please separate the distinct precise theses it might express, ranging from something nearly tautological about the conditions under which taxation is legitimate, to something much more radical about who is entitled to political inclusion. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false. Then tell me which reading or readings would actually license extending the municipal franchise to non-citizen taxpayers, and which reading my teammate is most likely relying on if his argument is going to work.

Please think as a political philosopher who works on democratic theory. Before writing, privately brainstorm at least six candidate precisifications so you don't settle for the first reading that comes to mind. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, with no preamble and without restating my question, and then lay the readings out as a menu or table with your verdicts. If the request rests on a confused premise—for example, if the slogan is better understood as a piece of rhetoric rather than a proposition, or if it is a mistake to try to extract a single principle from it—tell me that plainly instead of complying politely.
