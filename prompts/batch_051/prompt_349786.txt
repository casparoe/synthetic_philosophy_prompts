Ok, a friend in my decision theory seminar came back with this argument and now it's bothering both of us, becuase the conclusion is deranged but every step sounds right when you read it on its own.

(1) If a cost has already been paid and can't be recovered, then no matter what you do next, that cost is the same either way. So the fact that you paid it can't make any of the options you're now choosing among better or worse than the others.

(2) Being rational just means picking the option with the best expected outcome from now on, with everything relevant included.

(3) So by (1) and (2), past sunk costs should never tip a decision.

(4) A long marriage is full of already-spent history: money on the wedding, years of effort, compromises, plans laid around each other, even the person you've grown into. All of that is already gone no matter what you choose. So by (3), none of it can count in favor of staying.

(5) Same goes for friendships, for the career you've sunk a decade into, for the city you moved to: everything up to now is fully spent, so none of it is a reason for what you do next.

(6) So if you wouldn't start the relationship from scratch today, knowing only what the future of it would be like, then staying in it is just the sunk cost fallacy in sentimental clothes. Thirty years of shared history would be no more of a reason to stay than a kitchen remodel that ran over budget.

(7) But that's absurd. There has to be a difference between throwing more money at a botched car repair and continuing a marriage partly because of the shared past.

So either one of the steps goes bad, or the difference in (7) is an illusion. I'd like to keep (2), and I'd much rather lose a premise than lose the distinction in (7). Where exactly does this argument come apart, and what do I have to pay to keep (7)'s distinction? If the answer is that shared history itself is a reason, then tell me how that's compatible with (2) without secretly turning the history into a prediction about future consequences (like the other person's loyalty, or how you'd feel guilty). I want to know whether a past cost can ever be a reason that isn't just a present or future fact in disguise.
