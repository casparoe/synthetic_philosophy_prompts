I have been trying, on my own, to sort out what I believe about envy and resentment, and I have written down a set of commitments I seem to hold. Before I try to defend them to the discussion group I meet with, I want an honest consistency check. I have no training in philosophy, so the words are my own; I would ask you to assess the commitments as I state them and not by translating them into a framework I never mentioned.

The commitments are these:

(1) Envy is at bottom a vice. It is pain at another person's good, and what it characteristically wants is that the other person's good be diminished, rather than that one's own situation improve.

(2) Resentment is different in kind. It is a response to being wronged, and in feeling it I am registering a claim that the arrangement is unfair, not merely confessing a flaw in myself. I think it can be a reliable indicator that something in the arrangement is actually wrong.

(3) An outside observer cannot reliably tell these two feelings apart just by looking at the person who feels them. People often call something "envy" when it is really a legitimate complaint, especially when the person complaining is worse off and the other person's advantage came from luck.

(4) But envy is real, and it can disguise itself as a moral complaint. Not every angry reaction to someone else's advantage is righteous, and I do not want to treat all such anger as justified.

(5) Some envy, while still a vice, is excusable. Where the gap between two people's lives is very large, and the better-off person's extra advantage contributes nothing to the worse-off person's life, it asks too much of human nature to expect the worse-off not to feel envy.

(6) The right response to excusable envy is to change the arrangements rather than the people: to make the gaps small enough, or beneficial enough to the worse off, that envy is not provoked — rather than exhorting people to stop comparing themselves with others.

(7) Resentment, by contrast, is something a decent society should preserve and listen to, even when it is unpleasant, because it often carries information about where the society is failing.

I suspect that at least two of these pull against each other, but I cannot tell which, and I do not want a lecture on the topic in general. I want an audit of this specific set, in four sections, in this order:

1. "Where the set actually breaks" (about 400 words): name the genuine tensions, ranked from most severe to least, and for each say exactly what would have to give.

2. "Where it only seems to break" (about 250 words): name the pairs that look inconsistent but can be held together, and say how.

3. "What each repair would cost" (about 300 words): take each genuine tension from the first section and say what I would lose by fixing it.

4. "The cheapest single revision" (about 200 words): identify the one change to the set that restores consistency at the least cost to the rest, and say why it is cheaper than the alternatives.
