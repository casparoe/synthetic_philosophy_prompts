I'm a TA for a third-year free will course, I've got about fifty of these short answers to grade, and this one's been bugging me, so I want a second opinion before I commit. Heres the question and one student's answer.

Exam question (final, 20 points):

In lecture we set out the Basic Argument this way. (1) In any given situation, you do what you do because of the way you are. (2) So, to be truly or ultimately responsible for what you do, you must be truly responsible for the way you are, at least in certain crucial mental respects. (3) To be truly responsible for the way you are, you must have intentionally brought it about that you are that way. (4) But you cannot be truly responsible for the way you are, because that would require an infinite regress of acts of self-creation — in the end you'd have to be causa sui, which is impossible. So no one is truly or ultimately morally responsible for what they do. Explain this argument in your own words, and in particular say whether its force depends on the truth of determinism.

Student's answer:

Strawson's argument goes like this. Premise one says that what you do in a given situation comes from the way you are — your beliefs, desires, values, temperament — rather than appearing out of nowhere. Premise two then says that if you aren't responsible for the way you are that produced the action, you can't deserve ultimate credit or blame for the action itself; it's just the causal outflow of something you didn't control. Premise three adds that the only way to be responsible for the way you are is to have intentionally brought it about, like choosing or cultivating your own character. Premise four says this can't actually be done: to form your own character you'd already have to be a certain way, and to be responsible for that you'd have to have formed it, and so on backwards, so you'd end up having to be the cause of yourself. That's the regress, and it's meant to be impossible to stop. Hence the conclusion that no one is truly or ultimately morally responsible.

Now, the force of the argument seems to depend on determinism being true. Premise one says your action is determined by the way you are. If determinism is false and some actions are undetermined, then an action isn't fully because of the way you are, so the argument won't get started. So I think the Basic Argument only works if the world is deterministic.

There's also a problem with premise two. It requires that you be responsible for your whole character, which is too strong. Being responsible for an action should only require being responsible for the particular parts of yourself that were relevant to that action. If you shaped those specific desires and values, that ought to be enough, even if you didn't shape everything about yourself.

The regress in premise four is the strongest part: any act of self-shaping would have to come from a self that's already there, so there's no way to be the ultimate source of how you are.
