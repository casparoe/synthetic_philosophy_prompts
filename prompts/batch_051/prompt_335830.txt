A couple of people in my mythology reading group got into it last week about a story we read, and I've been turning it over since and still don't know how to call it. In the story, a mortal named Ormenos disguises himself as the goddess's favorite priestess and gets her to swear on the Styx — the one oath even the gods can't just break — that she'll grant whatever he asks. He then asks her to hand over the hero she's been sheltering, who he intends to kill. The goddess is horrified and refuses, and the story turns on whether the oath binds her.

Leo said it obviously binds. The whole point of a Styx oath is that it doesn't stop binding just because you regret it or where stupid. She said the words. If a god could get out of an oath by saying they were tricked, no mortal could ever rely on one, because a god can always claim regret or deception after the fact. The Styx oath is the one promise that's supposed to survive exactly this.

Nadia said he was confusing the penalty with the obligation. The Styx is the enforcement mechanism — it tells you what happens to an oathbreaker, but it doesn't tell you what counts as a valid oath. An oath extracted by fraud isn't valid, because there's no real commitment; she never intended to grant that request, so refusing isn't perjury. There's nothing to perjure.

Leo came back that the commitment isn't a private mental event, it's the act of swearing. She freely said the formula knowing what the Styx does. If validity depends on secret inner intentions, then a mortal can never tell whether a god is actually bound, and that guts the whole institution.

Nadia said so now the Styx is just a trap? If it binds you even when you didn't genuinely agree to the content, then it's not an oath, it's a magical coercion device. Oaths bind because you stake yourself on them, not because a river is going to zap you.

Leo said for gods those two can't be pried apart. The Styx is what lets gods make credible commitments, and the moment you allow "I was deceived about who was asking" as an out, every god who changes their mind will suddenly discover they were deceived about something.

Nadia finished by saying he'd shifted from "she promised" to "we need the institution to be strict," and those are different. If I get you to sign a contract by impersonating someone else, we don't say you're bound, so why should gods be held to a lower standard of consent than we hold each other to?

Leo said because gods arn't mortals, and a Styx oath exists precisely to bind beings who can't otherwise be made to keep their word. Importing mortal consent norms just abolishes the thing you're trying to explain.

Could you go through the exchange step by step and say, for each point, who has the better of it and why? Then say what the right view actually is and what both of them are missing.
