You are a historical linguist and philosopher of linguistics. Begin by carefully distinguishing the two positions below, and brainstorm at least two or three candidate tests before settling on the most probative ones.

One position holds that sound laws are regular and exceptionless: once the proper phonetic environment, borrowing, analogy, and later changes have been factored out, a sound change applies to every eligible word at once. On this view, there is no coherent notion of a sound change that strikes only some words; any surviving exception shows only that the analyst's statement of the law is incomplete. Exceptionlessness, on this view, is part of the content of a sound law.

The rival position denies this. It holds that sound change can propagate by lexical diffusion, spreading gradually through the vocabulary word by word, so that at an intermediate stage two words with identical phonological environments may differ—one changed, one unchanged—with no borrowing or analogy to blame in either case.

Now suppose you have a well-documented language, historical or contemporary, in which a sound change is leaving or has left exactly such a residue: among the words sharing the same conditioning context, most show the new reflex, but a nontrivial minority do not, and the standard excusing factors have already been ruled out by independent evidence. Design tests that would distinguish the regularity view from the lexical-diffusion view. For each test, specify what result would favor each side, and then say precisely what a positive result would and would not settle about the stronger conceptual claim that exceptionlessness is part of what it is for something to be a sound law. In particular, assess whether the difficulty of separating the two positions is a practical limitation of the evidence or a deeper problem with the notion of a rule itself.
