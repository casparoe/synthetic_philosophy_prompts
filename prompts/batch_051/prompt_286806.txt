Okay, so I'm trying to think through Rousseau's idea of the general will, but I don't trust my memory of his exact wording. In *The Social Contract* he seems to say that a law only counts as an expression of the general will if it comes from the whole citizen body, applies to everyone equally, and never singles out particular persons or groups. And he says that when someone refuses to obey the general will, the community can compel that person to obey, and this is what he means by being forced to be free. I'm not quoting him exactly but that's the gist.

Now here's a case that seems to sit right at the edge. A small township votes to require every adult resident to attend a six-hour civic assembly every year and speak for two minutes on some local issue. The rule is completely general in form—it applies to everyone over eighteen, no exceptions for introverts, out-of-town commuters, disabled people, or people who just don't care. People who fail to show up get hit with a heavy fine. The ordinance was passed by an assembly of the residents, though only the more politically active ones showed up to vote on it.

My question: is this ordinance an expression of the general will for Rousseau, or does it actually violate his criteria? On one hand it's universal in form and seems aimed at making people active citizens. On the other hand, forcing someone to show up and speak isn't the same as forcing them to obey a binding decision; it might be forcing them to perform citizenship in a way that's too particular. And is fining the no-shows an instance of forcing them to be free, or is that phrase being stretched past what Rousseau could have meant? I want a committed take, not a list of possible interpretations. If I'm misreading Rousseau's concept, say so and then use the corrected version to judge the case.
