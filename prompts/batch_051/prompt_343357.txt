I sit on the steering committee of a small foundation that funds work at the intersection of ethics and emerging biotechnology, and we are putting together a one-page concept note for a six-month project on the Hedonistic Imperative: the thesis that we are morally required to use biotechnology to phase out suffering across sentient life, not merely to make it rare or mild. The question I want to see funded is not whether suffering is bad or whether the technology will ever be adequate, but something more tangled. Defenders of the imperative move quickly from the premise that suffering is intrinsically bad to the conclusion that we should engineer a world without it; but there seems to be an unexamined difference between eliminating particular episodes of suffering and eliminating the capacity or disposition to suffer, and the argument may be compelling only on the first reading while the end-state it promises requires the second. A creature redesigned so that nothing can feel like intolerable damage to it may no longer be vulnerable in the way that makes being comforted, defended, or spared intelligible as a benefit. I half-suspect this is not just a rhetorical worry but a genuine hole in the case for the imperative, because it is precisely the capacity to suffer that ties the badness of suffering to the subject whose welfare is at stake.

Turn this into a one-page proposal for the foundation. State the question precisely so that it has a determinate answer — something like whether the Hedonistic Imperative's end-state can be specified without eliminating the very capacity whose abolition it demands, and whether that matters to its deontic force. Explain why the usual positions leave it open: those who defend the imperative typically establish the disvalue of suffering or dismiss objections from human nature, while critics usually argue about consent or dystopia, and neither side analyzes the elimination/capacity distinction. Give the method month by month — which conceptual distinctions, edge cases, or argumentative framework you would work through — and why that method rather than merely collecting intuitions for and against. Say what a successful deliverable looks like for an audience that might actually fund or regulate such work, and give an honest account of what would count as failure and what could make the project too big for six months.
