I've been rereading Ecce Homo and I can't decide weather the long stretches about digestion, climate, how much he sleeps, why he won't touch beer, and his weirdly specific opinions about coffee and tea are just Nietzsche being Nietzsche, or are actually load-bearing for the philosophy. It seems to me he treats physiology as upstream of psychology, and he connects the whole self-overcoming thing to a very concrete bodily discipline, not just abstract posturing about the overman.

The thing I want worked out: there's now an entire subculture — quantified self, biohacking, healthspan people — that on paper looks like it descends from exactly that. Continuous glucose monitors so you can watch in real time how a meal changes your energy and focus; fasted training protocols; HRV tracking as a proxy for stress and recovery; and recently GLP-1 drugs, where the willpower part of appetite control gets outsourced to a weekly injection. A lot of rationalist-adjacent types have turned self-observation and self-experimentation into a practice, and the scene runs from tech-optimist tinkering all the way to something quasi-monastic: cold plunges, purity spirals around food, orthorexia, discipline as a status marker.

I want you to actually work out, from his stated views on diet, health, physiology, asceticism, and the body, where Nietzsche would land on that spectrum. Don't just give me the boilerplate answer that he'd despise last-man comfort-seeking — I think the real answer has cross-currents, because on one side he treats mastering one's own physiology as almost the paradigm of doing philosophy, and on the other side he hates the kind of asceticism that looks exactly like discipline if you squint. Would he read the CGM-and-fasting crowd as fellow aristocrats of the body, or as a new herd with a new priest class and a new set of life-denying rules? Be specific about where the boundary falls using his own criteria, and give me something like a rough probability split with the concrete things that would flip him one way or the other — above all, whether the tech is used to heighten someone's dominant drives rather than to manage or numb them.
