I have been trying to make sense of Thomas Schelling's discussion of nuclear brinkmanship, and one move keeps bothering me. The picture I have picked up from lectures is roughly this: a purely deliberate threat to launch nuclear weapons after some provocation is hard for an adversary to believe, because a rational leader, once the provocation has already occurred, would prefer not to carry out a threat that invites catastrophic retaliation. For that reason, so the argument goes, the threat only becomes credible to the extent that escalation is partly out of the leader's own hands — some risk of accident, miscommunication, unauthorized action, or a loss of control that cannot simply be turned off at will.

That is where I get stuck. If the leader's solution is to set up some mechanism in advance — a launch procedure, a preauthorized delegation, rules of engagement that make escalation genuinely possible — and then to signal its existence to the adversary, haven't we just moved the credibility problem up one level? The adversary still has to decide whether to believe that the mechanism is real, that it will not be disabled, and that the leader will not find a way to overrule it when the moment comes. And if the adversary does believe the mechanism, doesn't escalation then look like the product of a deliberate choice after all — just a choice made earlier — rather than something genuinely outside the leader's control? So my question is this: on this view, is the credibility of a nuclear threat supposed to come from a deliberately chosen probability of escalation, or from something about the situation that is not chosen at all? If it is the former, I do not see why it escapes the original problem; if it is the latter, I do not see what room is left for strategy as opposed to mere incompetence or bad luck.
