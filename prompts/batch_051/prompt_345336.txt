Question: A freegan is someone who minimizes participation in conventional markets and in animal production, in part by salvaging discarded food and other goods. Consider the following claim: when an animal product would otherwise go to waste, a person may consume or otherwise use it without moral concern, because doing so creates no additional demand for animal production. Assess this claim. In your answer, distinguish at least two different ways in which an animal product can be said to be going to waste, and discuss whether the no-additional-demand argument suffices to establish that using each kind of product is permissible.

Student answer: The student distinguishes two cases. First, meat recovered from a supermarket dumpster after being discarded. The student argues that the store's future orders track sales and inventory records rather than the contents of its dumpster, and the freegan's recovery is not observed by the store, so consuming this meat does not cause any additional animal to be bred or killed. Second, eggs from rescued backyard hens. The student argues that the hens lay eggs whether or not anyone eats them, so eating the eggs causes no additional laying; if the eggs were left alone, they would simply rot. The student then generalizes: in both cases the freegan's act is causally inert with respect to animal production, so using the product is morally unproblematic. The student considers the objection that eating discards might look like endorsing meat consumption, but sets it aside as a matter of appearances rather than a substantive moral issue. Finally, the student claims that consuming such products can even express respect for the animals, because it treats what came from them as useful rather than as worthless waste.
