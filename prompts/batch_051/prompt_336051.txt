I'm a PhD student working on LLM reasoning, and I've basically landed on a view I want you to try to talk me out of properly. The view is that process-based supervision is the right default for any AI system doing real reasoning — math, code, long-horizon agentic tasks. Not just because the process reward model results on math benchmarks looked good. The stronger claim is that a scalar outcome signal is always a lossy summary of what we actually want, and once the task is hard enough the model will find a way to satisfy the outcome without doing the underlying thing we were trying to train. Process supervision, when you can get it, is denser, it keeps the model's stated reasoning under pressure, and it feels harder to Goodhart into a black box. But I am aware I might be confusing "does well on problems where humans already know all the correct intermediate steps" with "is the better training scheme in general."

So play it straight: what would count against process-based supervision as a general claim, not just as a benchmark observation? I want the defeaters sorted, not a list. What empirical findings would have to obtain — outcome-supervised models beating process-supervised ones on out-of-distribution generalization, process rewards turning out to be gameable in ways nobody has measured yet, cheap auto-generated process feedback being basically noise — and which theoretical considerations are doing the real work, like the verifier-is-easier-than-generator point, or Goodhart's law applied to the process itself rather than the outcome? Which of those would be fatal to my view, and which would merely make it a special case or a contingent engineering choice? What should a honest defender of process supervision concede counts against it, and has the view been quietly qualified until it cannot lose? Then give me your own verdict, but also the part people usually skip: where in your reasoning is a single result or argument most likely to flip you the other way, and what would it look like if it happened.
