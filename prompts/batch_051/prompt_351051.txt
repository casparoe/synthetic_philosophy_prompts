**Exam question**

1. State the pessimistic meta-induction against scientific realism as precisely as you can.
2. Distinguish two readings of the argument: (i) as an enumerative induction over past theories, and (ii) as a base-rate argument turning on the low proportion of historically successful theories that have turned out true. Which reading should a scientific realist regard as the stronger challenge, and why?
3. Explain how a selective realist would respond to the stronger reading, and give one reason to think that response does not fully settle the matter.

**Student answer**

The pessimistic meta-induction, introduced by Laudan, runs as follows. The history of science contains many theories that were empirically successful by the standards of their day but were later rejected as false, such as Ptolemaic astronomy, phlogiston chemistry, the caloric theory of heat, and the ether theories of light. By induction over these cases, we should expect our current successful theories to be false as well, at least in what they say about unobservable entities and processes. Since scientific realism claims that the success of a mature theory is best explained by its being approximately true, the pessimistic induction undercuts that inference by showing that empirical success has historically been a poor guide to truth.

On question 2, the two readings are essentially the same argument in different dress. The enumerative reading says that because many past successful theories were false, current successful theories will probably be false too. The base-rate reading merely restates this as the fact that the historical rate of truth among successful theories is low. The sample of past theories is large and historically varied, which makes the induction strong, and a realist cannot dismiss the sample as biased without begging the question. A realist should therefore treat the two readings as a single challenge rather than two distinct challenges.

On question 3, a selective realist should respond in the way Psillos does with his structural realism. She should grant that whole past theories were false, but insist that the parts of those theories that were genuinely doing the predictive work are retained through theory change. The standard example is Fresnel's ether theory: the ether itself was abandoned, but Fresnel's equations were carried over into Maxwell's theory. This shows that only structural claims survive scientific revolutions, and therefore entity claims about unobservables should generally not be trusted. The weakness of this response is that the criterion for identifying the parts that survive is applied retrospectively: we can tell which parts of a past theory were essential only after we already know which parts were retained, so the criterion cannot be used in advance to tell us which parts of our current theories to believe.
