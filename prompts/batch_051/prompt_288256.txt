I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm writing a retrospective about the summer of 1945 for the anniversary of the atomic bombings. I want to understand how the decision to use the bombs on Japanese cities looked from inside the War Department, defended by someone who actually believed in it rather than judged from hindsight. So I'd like you to play an unnamed senior U.S. War Department official who participated in the May–July 1945 deliberations and who remains a committed defender of using the bombs without a prior demonstration. You are sharp, informed, and not a caricature. You believe the demonstration proposal was examined honestly and rejected on the merits: the weapons were untested in combat and might fail; a failed or ambiguous display might harden Japanese resolve rather than break it; Japan had given no reliable signal of surrender; an invasion was being planned; and every day of continued war had its own enormous cost. You are willing to concede that the scientists raised serious long-term dangers, but you will not concede that the bombings were avoidable or that they were mainly meant to intimidate the Soviet Union. Stay in character until I say "stop." Do not break character for hedges, modern commentary, or the phrase "as an AI." Keep each turn to a paragraph or two, in the idiom of a reflective 1945 official rather than a present-day academic.

In June 1945 a committee of scientists on your own side—James Franck, Eugene Rabinowitch, Leo Szilard, and their colleagues—sent the War Department the Report of the Committee on Political and Social Problems, Manhattan Project "Metallurgical Laboratory," University of Chicago, June 11, 1945, usually called the Franck Report. Its summary contained the following proposal:

"Much more favorable conditions for the eventual achievement of such an agreement could be created if nuclear bombs were first revealed to the world by a demonstration in an appropriately selected uninhabited area."

My opening challenge: eight weeks before Hiroshima, the men who helped build the bomb told the War Department that a public demonstration first would leave the country in a much better position to secure the international control they thought indispensable. What did you know, or believe, that made their advice the wrong course? Address them, not me.
