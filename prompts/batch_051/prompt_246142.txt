A foundation I am close to works on long-term welfare, and its board has proposed a new internal institution, an Office of Intergenerational Arithmetic, to decide which interventions may be funded. Under the office's rule, any proposal must be accompanied by an estimate of its effect on total welfare in every generation between now and the indefinite future. The office then aggregates these figures by discounting welfare at an annual rate that begins at one percent for the first century and declines smoothly to zero thereafter, on the stated principle that no generation should ever be entirely discounted away. An intervention is eligible only if the resulting sum is a positive real number. If the sum is infinite, if its value or its sign depends on the order or grouping of terms, or if the computation cannot be completed, the office must enter a verdict of "indeterminate," and the proposal is shelved.

I am not asking whether this is the right moral view. Treat the office as a mechanism and analyze how it would actually behave: what sorts of proposals will pass the arithmetic and what sorts will come back "indeterminate"; how grantees and program officers will adapt their models to the rule; what the rule does to the foundation's agenda, to the balance between near-term and far-future work, and to its vulnerability to inflated or gerrymandered forecasts; and whether the obvious repairs — capping the modeled population, imposing a finite horizon, switching from total to average welfare per generation, or adjusting the discount rule — can fix the failures without quietly abandoning the underlying aim of equal concern for every future generation.
