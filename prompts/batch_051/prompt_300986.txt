Act as a philosopher who specializes in well-being and value theory. I keep going back and forth on a view I'll call tranquilism: the only thing that ultimately makes a life go well or badly is the absence of craving and suffering. Not pleasure, not achievement, not love or knowledge except as they serve that absence. Before I read any more arguments for or against it, I want the spec. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory in this area has to meet — the cases it must get right (think about the experience machine, the suffering artist who would do it again, the drugged or lobotomized calm, the happy fool, the person whose desires all die out, maybe the ascetic who is truly unbothered), the distinctions it cant collapse (absence of suffering vs absence of craving, having a quiet mind vs being shut down, local vs global absence, intensity vs duration), and the facts about attention, adaptation, or motivation it can't just wave away. For each requirement, give the reason it's a requirement. Mark which are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's taste. Then flag any pairs you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, and say which single desideratum is hardest for tranquilism and which one it handles better than hedonism or desire-satisfaction. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivation, not a scorecard. Start by brainstorming the candidate desiderata, then settle on the list.
