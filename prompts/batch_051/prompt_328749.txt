I’m taking a course on early modern political philosophy, and we’ve been reconstructing Locke’s argument in the Second Treatise for the original acquisition of property. As I understand it, the argument moves from (1) each person owns her own body and labor, to (2) when she mixes that labor with something unowned, she joins to it something that is hers and thereby makes it her property, subject to (3) the proviso that this holds only "where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others." The framework was built for tangible, rivalrous resources—acorns, apples, land—where one person’s taking really does leave less for others.

I’ve been trying to run the same argument on a case Locke never had reason to consider: the open web treated as a commons. Suppose someone trains a large generative model on an enormous corpus of text and images that ordinary people made publicly available, and then holds the resulting system as private property. The training does not consume or remove the corpus; others can still read and use the same text and images, so the proviso looks satisfied automatically, or nearly so. Does Locke’s argument survive this changed assumption? I can’t tell whether the labor-mixing premise is now doing all the work—which would make exclusive private rights over an informational commons almost trivially easy to generate—or whether the argument has a hidden background condition, such as that the resource must be rivalrous, or that the proviso was really about preserving others’ opportunities rather than the material stock, which this case exposes. Which premise does the development bear on, and is there a repaired version of the argument that says something weaker but still worth keeping?
