I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. My daily work is about designing systems that must satisfy several constraints simultaneously, so I tend to read impossibility results as specification conflicts. I recently tried to work through Sen's liberal paradox and reconstructed it as a formal argument, but I cannot tell whether it is a genuine impossibility or whether one of my modeling choices is doing the damage. I want a precise diagnosis, not a survey.

Here is my reconstruction. A social choice procedure takes any profile of individual preference rankings over a set of alternatives and returns a single social ranking. I assume the social ranking is strict and transitive, and that the procedure must accept any logically possible profile of individual rankings (unrestricted domain).

Two conditions are imposed:

(1) Weak Pareto: if every individual strictly prefers x to y, then the social ranking ranks x above y.

(2) Minimal liberalism: each individual has at least one pair of alternatives over which their preference is decisive — for that pair, the social ranking copies that individual's ranking, regardless of what anyone else prefers. This is meant as a deliberately small private sphere: at least one binary personal choice per person.

Now apply the procedure to a two-person scenario. There is a single copy of a book, and three possible outcomes: Person 1 reads it, Person 2 reads it, or nobody reads it. Person 1's ranking is: nobody reads it > Person 1 reads it > Person 2 reads it. Person 2's ranking is: Person 1 reads it > Person 2 reads it > nobody reads it. Person 1 is decisive over the pair (Person 1 reads it, nobody reads it); Person 2 is decisive over the pair (Person 2 reads it, nobody reads it).

The conditions then force the following:

(3) Both individuals prefer Person 1 reads it to Person 2 reads it, so by (1) the social ranking must place Person 1 reads it above Person 2 reads it.

(4) Person 2 prefers Person 2 reads it to nobody reads it and is decisive over that pair, so the social ranking must place Person 2 reads it above nobody reads it.

(5) Person 1 prefers nobody reads it to Person 1 reads it and is decisive over that pair, so the social ranking must place nobody reads it above Person 1 reads it.

(6) From (3) and (4), transitivity requires that Person 1 reads it rank above nobody reads it. But (5) requires the opposite. A strict ranking cannot rank the same pair both ways, so the procedure has no admissible output.

Therefore unrestricted domain, Weak Pareto, and minimal liberalism cannot all hold. My question is whether this argument goes through as stated. If it is invalid, which numbered step fails, and what is the correct version of that step? If it is valid, which premise should I reject or reformulate — Weak Pareto, unrestricted domain, transitivity, or the modeling of a right as decisiveness over a pair — and does the result show a real tension between liberty and efficiency, or only that a private right cannot be captured by a fixed decisive pair inside a social ranking? I am less interested in the list of possible responses than in the exact point of failure and the reasoning behind it.
