I am taking an upper-level seminar in moral theory, and our current topic is whether virtues and deontic duties are ultimately derivative from the promotion of good consequences, merely useful instruments for a consequentialist agent, or independent sources of normativity. I find myself with fairly firm verdicts on a range of cases, but I cannot see the principle that holds them together, and I suspect that at least two of my verdicts conflict. My verdicts are as follows.

1. The forgotten loaned book. I promised to return a book to a classmate on Monday. On Monday she has completely forgotten about it, would suffer no harm or inconvenience either way, and I would genuinely enjoy keeping it for a few more days; nobody would ever know. I still judge that I ought to return it, and that failing to do so would be wrong.

2. Honesty as a weapon. A colleague tells a presenter an unflattering truth about his failed presentation purely because he wants to wound him, in a situation where a mild falsehood would spare serious hurt and nothing else of significance is at stake. I judge that the colleague acts wrongly, and I would not call him honest in a praiseworthy sense; I would call him cruel. Honesty, it seems, is not automatically a virtue.

3. Two feats of daring. A soldier runs into heavy fire to carry an injured child to safety and is rightly called courageous. A man runs into a burning house to rescue a worthless trinket, taking roughly the same physical risk, and I would not call him courageous; I would call him reckless. So what makes courage courage seems to depend partly on the value of the end, not merely on the facing of danger.

4. The judge anticipating a riot. A judge is about to enforce a correct but very unpopular ruling. Her best forecast is that enforcing it will provoke riots and leave everyone involved worse off than quietly bending the rule would. I still think she should enforce it, or at least that bending the rule would be a species of corruption. Justice appears to me to bind independently of the consequences of this particular act.

5. The charitable breach of promise. I have borrowed a small sum and promised to repay it, but I learn that I could donate the money to an effective charity with reasonable confidence that the donation would do far more good overall than repayment would. I do not think this makes the breach permissible; I still owe the lender. The prospect of greater overall good does not simply release me from the promise.

6. Honesty in an exploitative world. In a comprehensively corrupt society, someone who is scrupulously honest is routinely exploited; her honesty reliably makes her own life and, we may suppose, the average outcome of those around her worse. I still find her honesty admirable as a trait of character, though I hesitate to say that everyone in such a society is required to be honest.

I can formulate for myself a tempting principle: virtues are dispositions that characteristically produce good consequences under normal or typical conditions, while duties such as promise-keeping and justice are constraints that bind regardless of the consequences of the particular act. That fits cases 2 through 5. But it seems to fail on case 6: honesty under conditions where its typical consequences are bad should, on my principle, cease to be a virtue, yet I retain the judgment that the honest person is admirable. And the principle does not explain why promise-keeping should be a constraint while honesty is not. Is there a coherent principle that captures these verdicts and locates each virtue and duty as derivative from the good, instrumental to it, or independent of it? If so, what would it say about cases I have not considered, such as a duty of beneficence whose demands vary with the circumstances or a loyalty that persists even when its object is unworthy? If not, which of my verdicts should I give up, and for what reason?
