I am a retired physical chemist, and in retirement I have been reading the recent philosophical literature on metaphysical grounding and fundamentality; a research consortium has now asked me to chair the working group revising its biomedical data ontology. The group—bench scientists, clinicians, and informaticians—meets every other week and repeatedly deadlocks over which of the terms in the upper-level catalogue should be treated as primitive or fundamental and which should be treated as derivative, defined, or grounded in other terms. The decision is not cosmetic: the primitive list fixes the dependency structure of the database, determines which queries can be expressed, and controls how downstream contradictions are traced to their sources, and the scientific director must personally approve every term left primitive. So the room needs a defensible, repeatable rule, not a philosophical debate.

In my reading, the underlying distinction is something like this. One fact or entity is grounded in others when it obtains or exists in virtue of them, and something is fundamental when it is not grounded in anything further. Grounding is a non-causal relation of metaphysical priority: it is not the same as covarying in measurements, being inferentially convenient, being discovered first, being causally productive, or being stipulated as a definition. My colleagues constantly slide between this sense of fundamental and those epistemic, causal, or conventional senses, which is exactly the slide I want a procedure to police. I am not asking for a lecture, but for a test my group could actually run on a candidate term within a two-hour meeting, with no philosophical training assumed.

Please write a decision procedure for classifying a candidate term as fundamental or grounded. It should pose questions in a fixed order, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, specify where it returns fundamental, where it returns grounded, and where it stops and hands the matter to a person because the question is substantive rather than procedural. It should flag which steps turn on empirical findings rather than on philosophical analysis, and it should be explicit about the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and is merely being relocated. Then apply the procedure to three candidate terms we have actually fought over: core body temperature as against mean molecular kinetic energy; being in pain as against nociceptive, neural, and behavioral features; and the catalytic function of an enzyme as against its folded structure and active-site geometry. Please make sure at least one of those applications yields a verdict the working group will not like, and close by saying what the procedure cannot settle.
