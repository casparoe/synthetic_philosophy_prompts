I have been tutoring my nephew Daniel in arithmetic for the past several months. He has become fluent at addition: he can handle 23 + 14, 45 + 9, and scores of other combinations involving numbers below 60, on paper and in his head, and I had come to think he had genuinely learned the operation. Yesterday I wrote 68 + 57 on the page. He paused, then wrote 5, and explained that this is what happens once the numbers get that large. I began to mark it wrong, but a friend who studies philosophy stopped me. He argued that everything Daniel has done so far is compatible with his having learned a quite different operation—one that agrees with addition on every pair below 60 and always yields 5 beyond that—and, more strongly, that no amount of further testing could settle which operation he had in mind, since any finite collection of answers can be extended in indefinitely many ways that fit what he has said so far. On his view, there is no fact of the matter about which rule Daniel had been following, so I am not correcting an error but simply deciding, among infinitely many equally eligible ways of continuing, which one to enforce.

I have to teach Daniel again tomorrow, so I need to decide how to think about this. Is my friend's argument a genuine obstacle to saying that Daniel has learned addition and made a mistake, or a philosophical trick that dissolves under scrutiny? In concrete terms: should the possibility of such gerrymandered rules lower my confidence that he means addition, and if I accept that his past answers do not decide between addition and the deviant rule, what grounds, if any, do I have for correcting him rather than treating his new rule as legitimate?
