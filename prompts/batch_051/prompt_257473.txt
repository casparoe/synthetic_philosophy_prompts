In my seminar on nationalism and collective memory, we are reading Ernest Renan’s 1882 lecture Qu’est-ce qu’une nation ?, and I am having trouble understanding how the opening passage sets up his later claim about forgetting and historical error. The passage we are examining begins: "Je me propose d’analyser avec vous une idée, claire en apparence, mais qui prête aux plus graves malentendus. Les formes de la société humaine sont des plus variées." As I read it, he is saying that the idea of a nation seems obvious at first, but the ways human beings have actually organized themselves are so varied that treating that idea as simple leads to serious confusion. Later in the lecture, Renan argues that forgetting, and even historical error, is essential to the creation of a nation. This is the part that unsettles me. I had always assumed that when a community’s shared memory conflicts with the historical record, the responsible move is to let the record correct the memory. Renan seems to suggest that a nation’s identity depends on something the historian would call error, and that trying to replace that shared version with a more accurate record could dissolve the very thing it is meant to explain. I would appreciate help understanding what he is claiming. In particular, does he offer any principled way to decide when a community’s memory should be allowed to override the historical record rather than be corrected by it, or is he simply endorsing the community’s self-understanding at the expense of truth?
