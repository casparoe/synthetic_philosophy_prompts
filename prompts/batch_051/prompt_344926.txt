Here's the checklist I was taught for who gets assisted dying, more or less the standard list where I live: a terminal illness with a prognosis of six months or less; unbearable suffering that can't be relieved in a way the person can tolerate; a request that is persistent, repeated, and made voluntarily; and two independent clinicians confirming decision-making capacity and ruling out an untreated mental disorder as the main driver of the wish.

Now the case I can't shake. M is 76, no terminal illness, just moderate arthritis and hearing loss, both stable on treatment. Her husband died six years ago, her children live abroad, and most of her close friends are dead or in care. She scores normally on depression screens, has no psychiatric history, and isn't cognitively impaired. For about four years she has said, in writing and in person, that her life is finished — not with despair, but as a settled judgement that the life she wanted has been lived and she doesn't want to be carried through years of decline into dependence on strangers. She doesn't describe herself as suffering; she describes continuing as something she can no longer find a reason to do, like being kept at a party long after she has said her goodbyes. Two psychiatrists have signed off that she has capacity and that depression doesn't explain the wish.

Does she qualify under the checklist as I gave it? Go criterion by criterion. Wherever she fails, tell me whether that criterion is doing real moral work or whether it's a sign the checklist is too narrow. And say whether "unbearable suffering" should be stretched to cover what she's describing, or whether a separate "completed life" ground would be a different policy rather than just a tidier interpretation of the old one.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then walk me through the reasoning. No preamble, no restating the question.
