Take the lottery paradox, but run it through accuracy-first epistemology instead of flat-out belief. Quick setup so we don't talk past each other. There's a fair lottery with N tickets; let L_i be the proposition that ticket i loses, and W the proposition that at least one ticket wins. In the classic version each L_i is hugely probable and W is basically certain, yet believing all the L_i's plus W is jointly inconsistent (the L_i's jointly say no ticket wins).

The variation: get rid of all-or-nothing belief and work with credences governed by epistemic utility theory. A credence function is rational only if it's probabilistic and maximises expected accuracy, where the inaccuracy of credence c in a proposition whose truth value is v (0 or 1) is the Brier score (c - v)^2, and the expectation is taken over the known lottery chances. Lower score is better.

(i) What credences should the theory assign to L_i and to W in this lottery?

(ii) Now add the Lockean acceptance rule: believe a proposition iff your credence in it is at least some threshhold t. Assuming belief is closed under conjunction, for exactly which values of t, as a function of N, does the original inconsistent combination reappear? And for N = 100 and t = 0.95, does the paradox survive?
