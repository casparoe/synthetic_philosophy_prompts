A former student of mine, now a neuroscientist, has asked for my help thinking through a classroom demonstration she uses in her introductory course. In the demonstration, a participant watches a small dot travelling around a clock-like display and is instructed to flex their wrist at a moment of their own choosing, while reporting where the dot was when they first became aware of the decision to move. Scalp electrodes placed over the motor areas record a slow electrical shift. The demonstration reliably shows that this electrical shift begins several hundred milliseconds before the participant reports the conscious decision, and that the movement itself follows the reported decision by roughly two hundred milliseconds. My former student now wants to tell her class that the conscious experience of deciding is, in effect, a story the brain tells after the fact: the brain has already set the movement in motion before the person feels they have chosen, so the felt sense of free choice is an illusion. I am uneasy about that conclusion, but I want to give her a careful and precise answer rather than a dismissive one. What should I say? I would welcome a committed response — one that takes a definite position on whether her demonstration warrants that conclusion and defends it, rather than merely surveying possible interpretations.
