I am a PhD candidate in twentieth-century European political history and the graduate representative on my university's Advisory Committee on International Research Partnerships. The committee meets next month to decide whether to suspend a long-running joint degree and laboratory collaboration with a state-run engineering institute in a country whose government has just occupied part of a smaller neighbor's territory.

The committee is split, and the disagreement turns on what lessons history actually licenses. One faction wants immediate suspension, within thirty days. Their argument is historical: in the 1930s, scientific and scholarly institutions that kept partnerships with aggressive governments quietly lent those governments respectability, and delay was read as acquiescence. One member calls this our Munich moment and treats any conditional continuation as appeasement.

The other faction wants to keep the partnership in place, at most with a public statement of concern. They argue the Munich analogy is emotionally vivid but evidence-poor for the case at hand: the partner institute is not a totalitarian organ, it still houses openly dissenting researchers, and the exchange is one of the last unmonitored channels its students have. They also answer analogy with analogy: they point to the university's own Vietnam-shaped commitment in the mid-2000s, when it entered a political fight it did not control and spent nearly a decade extracting itself from measures that helped no one.

I have been asked to circulate the draft decision memo before the meeting. Please write it. It should make a clear recommendation — suspend, maintain, or maintain under explicit conditions — with a time frame and review criteria if conditions are recommended, and it must engage sincerely with the strongest consideration on the side you recommend against. The committee does not need a treatise on analogical reasoning; it needs a decision that rests on a defensible account of what the 1930s analogy, and the mid-2000s counter-analogy, can and cannot establish.
