I'm a student in a philosophy of the family course, and I've been drafting a position paper on what adult children owe their parents. Before I submit it, I'd like an audit of whether the view I've settled into actually hangs together. Below are the commitments, numbered for reference. Please go through them and tell me which commitments or pairs of commitments are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each real tension is; rank them by severity rather than treating all of them as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give, what the cheapest revision would be, and which single change to the overall view would leave it strongest.

(1) I accept Jane English's friendship model: whatever adult children specially owe their parents is owed in virtue of a relationship that is morally like friendship — not as repayment of a debt, and not because of any sui generis obligation attaching to the bare parent-child tie.

(2) I hold that friendship obligations are grounded in the relationship's voluntary and mutually responsive character: they track closeness, trust, intimacy, and mutual concern, not blood, role, or the sheer quantity of resources one party has supplied to the other.

(3) So I accept that a parent who was absent, abusive, or otherwise destructive is owed no special filial duty; such a parent is owed only what any stranger in comparable need would be owed.

(4) But I also hold a general principle of gratitude: when someone has voluntarily conferred substantial, unsolicited benefits on me, I can owe that person a responsiveness to their serious needs that goes beyond what I owe an ordinary stranger.

(5) I do not think (4) reintroduces the debt model. A debt, as I understand it, is precise, incurred by request or contract, and repayable in kind; gratitude is none of those things, and its force does not depend on my having consented to the benefit.

(6) The gratitude principle matters for my own case. My relationship with my mother has always been dutiful but not warm or intimate: she sacrificed a great deal for my upbringing, but we have never been close. I judge that I owe her substantial support as she ages, and that this is not merely the ordinary help I would owe a stranger.

(7) I also accept the implication that on the friendship model, a very close friend can properly count for more in my practical deliberations than a merely decent but distant parent. I find this uncomfortable, but I think it is correct that parental status alone gives no priority.

(8) Finally, I think the friendship model can still explain the felt weight of filial relationships: in the normal case, parents voluntarily sustain decades of care and concern, and that is exactly the kind of shared history that makes any friendship deep. Parent-child relationships usually matter more than ordinary friendships because the history is deeper, not because the obligations belong to a special type.
