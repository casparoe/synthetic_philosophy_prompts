I keep running into this asymmetry and I want to make sure I'm not missing something obvious. A friend of mine is already convinced he'll sign up for scan-and-destroy uploading the moment it exists, and I think thats obviously a form of dying: a copy with your memories wakes up, but the original person is gone. The sane-sounding alternative is gradual replacement—take out one neuron, drop in a functionally identical silicon stand-in, let the brain adjust, repeat. At no point does anyone lose consciousness or report becoming someone else, and each single swap feels no more drastic than ordinary cell turnover (which already replaces most of your body's atoms over the years).

So here's the puzzle: if every step preserves me, how could the whole process fail to preserve me? But if it does preserve me, then the only real difference between the safe procedure and the obviously lethal one is just how many neurons you swap per step—and survival starts looking like an arbitrary engineering parameter, like a dial you turn until you stop dying. Is there a principled way to say what the maximum safe step size is, or should I just conclude that the "gradual replacement is safe" intuition is doing all the work?
