I have a small theory about how languages live with homonymy, and I want it checked before I keep relying on it. I call the central notion a word's semantic carrying capacity.

The claim is that a lexical item can carry only as many live senses as its typical contexts can cheaply disambiguate. A word like set or run supports dozens of senses not because of anything special about the word but because it nearly always occurs inside enough redundant co-text that the alternatives never surface; capacity is set, so to speak, by the average context the word has to survive in, not by the word itself. From this I draw a diachronic prediction: when a word exceeds its carrying capacity, one of three things gives. A sense dies; the form splits, through spelling, stress, pronunciation, or register; or the contexts specialize until the senses no longer compete for the same slots.

The example that convinced me is pitch. It carries at least the tar, throw, field, musical-note, slope, and sales-proposal senses, and it survives because those senses occupy nearly disjoint contexts; it is surprisingly hard to construct an ordinary sentence in which two of them are live rivals for the same occurrence. The contrast that matters to me is the compressed register of a newspaper headline: withdraw the redundant context and a word with even two senses threatens to exceed its budget, which is why headline style reaches for narrower near-synonyms where ordinary prose would not.

I want an honest assessment. Is this a real construct or only an ecology metaphor? Has it already been worked out under another name, and if so, what is the standard formulation I am circling? Where does it break: can you give me a clear case in which a word exceeds what should be its carrying capacity and nothing gives, or in which the account predicts the wrong sense dying or the wrong kind of split? And say plainly whether the concept earns its keep, or whether it collapses into something more familiar once the metaphor is stripped off.
