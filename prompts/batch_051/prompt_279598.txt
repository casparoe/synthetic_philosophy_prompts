I am a PhD student in philosophy and a graduate representative on my university's Rationality Across Disciplines seed-funding committee, which gives small grants to student projects at the boundary of philosophy, cognitive science, and computer science. We are currently deciding on a proposal from a computer science student who wants to build an experimental platform for studying how human reasoners track the logical consequences of what they already believe. Her motivating claim is that our standard normative models — Bayesian decision theory and epistemic logic — idealize thinkers as logically omniscient: they assign probability one to every logical truth and close belief sets under entailment, so that a reasoner counts as irrational whenever they fail to notice some distant consequence of their own commitments. She argues that this is not a harmless simplification but a systematic distortion, and that her platform will produce the data needed to build a resource-bounded alternative.

The committee is divided. Several members regard the proposal as an attack on a modeling idealization that nobody takes literally, in the way frictionless planes function in physics, and suspect it is ultimately a philosophical polemic dressed up as empirical research; others think this is exactly the kind of foundational challenge the fund exists to support. I have been asked to draft the committee's decision memo recommending whether to fund, reject, or fund with conditions, with a clear bottom line and a fair treatment of the strongest consideration on whichever side loses.
