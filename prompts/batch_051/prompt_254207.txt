I am preparing a paper on the philosophy of humour and would like a consistency audit of the position I find myself committed to before I present it. My commitments are these: (a) the benign-violation account is the best general explanation of what makes something humorous; (b) relief accounts identify a real phenomenon, but they explain a function of laughter—the discharge of strain—rather than what makes a joke funny, and so are not rivals to the other theories; (c) puns are humorous purely in virtue of the incongruity between their senses, and I would have to stretch the notion of a violation unilluminatingly to say that they involve one; (d) what makes a violation benign depends on the audience's values and psychological distance from the event, and we have no independent way to identify benignness in advance of finding something funny. Please audit the set rather than survey the literature. Reply as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: first the two genuine tensions ranked by severity, then an apparent tension that dissolves on inspection, then the least costly revision and what that revision would cost.
