For my advanced history class I am writing a paper on whether historical writing can be objective. What first drew me into the question is that two histories of the same period often agree on all the basic facts yet remain very different histories, while at the same time some histories are simply wrong about the evidence — and I want a standard that respects both sides of that before I take a position. We have read short selections from Leopold von Ranke, E. H. Carr, and Hayden White. Ranke holds that the historian's task is to show the past as it actually was; Carr argues that the facts do not select themselves and that every history is written from a standpoint in the present; White treats historical writing as narrative shaped by literary choices. I can restate each of these positions, but I suspect they can only be compared fairly if I first fix the complete list of things an adequate account of historical objectivity has to get right, rather than letting each camp's own definition of objectivity rule the comparison.

Please write that specification rather than a survey of the three authors, and structure it exactly as follows, in this order, with the approximate share of the answer marked:

1. The requirements (about 55%). A numbered list of criteria any adequate account of objectivity in history must meet or explain, each with the concrete case or consideration that makes it a requirement and not just a slogan. Cover at least these:
   - the status of ordinary factual claims (dates, deaths, signed documents) once the sceptical points above are granted;
   - the fact that two histories can agree on every individual fact and still be different histories, so fidelity to facts cannot by itself capture what is at stake;
   - the constraint the surviving evidence puts on even the most creative narrative, since not every story can be told truthfully;
   - the historian's own historical situation, which seems to shape the questions she asks even when she is not inserting opinions;
   - the distinction between a bias that should be corrected and a perspective she cannot be asked to shed.
2. What is negotiable (about 15%). Go through the list and mark which demands in this debate are genuine requirements and which are preferences of one camp, with what an account would lose by giving each up.
3. Tensions and incompatibilities (about 20%). State which pairs or sets of requirements pull against each other or cannot be fully satisfied together, and what an adequate account must say about the conflict. What I most want to know is whether the list forces a choice between the ideal of showing the past as it actually was and the claim that history is unavoidably constructed from a present standpoint, or whether those two can be made compatible once the requirements are stated carefully.
4. The single hardest test (about 10%). State which one requirement does the most to rule out or modify each of the three positions, and explain briefly what it rules out or modifies in Ranke's, Carr's, and White's accounts respectively.

In the first section, focus especially on whether objectivity should be treated as a single ideal or as a cluster of ideals (accuracy, fairness, completeness, openness about one's standpoint, fidelity to evidence). If it is a cluster, two histories could each be objective in different ways without either being more objective than the other; say whether the requirements should treat that clustering as one requirement or as several separate ones, and what that does to the possibility of saying that one history is more objective than another.
