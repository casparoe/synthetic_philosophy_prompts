I've been trying to work out what the badness of pain consists in, and my verdicts on particular cases pull me in opposite directions. I'd like you to determine whether some principle captures them, or nearly all of them, and to tell me plainly which of my verdicts it would force me to abandon or revise.

Here are my verdicts.

1. An ordinary throbbing toothache. This is clearly bad, and the badness seems to live in the hurt itself. If I could keep the hurt but remove my urge to avoid it, I suspect what was bad about the pain would still be there.

2. A postoperative patient on morphine who says, of pain whose intensity rating has not changed, that it is still there but no longer bothers them. I judge that the pain has ceased to be bad, or at least that its badness has collapsed while the intensity has not.

3. A person with pain asymbolia, a clinical condition in which patients report feeling pain, can locate it and rate its intensity, but do not withdraw from the stimulus and report no displeasure or distress about it. I find myself saying both that this person is in pain and that they are not in a bad state.

4. A person in severe agitated depression or grief, with no localized painful sensation anywhere. This is plainly bad, arguably worse than most pains, and yet there is no pain to which its badness attaches.

5. The intense pain of childbirth. I judge that the pain itself is bad the whole way through, even though the mother sincerely regards the whole event as worth it. The overall value of the event does not, I think, cancel the badness of the pain.

6. Wanted and enjoyed pain, as in some masochistic practices or the deep burn of a hard workout. The pain still hurts, and the wanting matters. I am torn here: sometimes I think the pain remains bad in itself and is merely outweighed; sometimes I think the badness has been transformed or absorbed.

The verdict that unsettles me is (1): it pushes me toward the view that the badness of pain just is its sensory hurt. But (2) and (3) push the other way, toward the view that badness lives in the affective side of pain, the felt unpleasantness or the aversion, since removing that side seems to remove the badness while the pain itself remains. Then (4) suggests that whatever makes pain bad can occur without pain, and (5) and (6) force me to say something about whether the will, the situation, or the person's projects have any bearing on how bad a pain is.

Please consider at least these candidate principles: (a) a pain's badness is its sensory intensity; (b) a pain's badness is its affective component, its felt unpleasantness or motivational force; (c) a pain's badness consists in the person's aversion to it; and (d) some hybrid or pluralist account. For each candidate, say which of my six verdicts it captures and which it cannot, then state the version of the best candidate precisely, with the clauses motivated by the cases. Finally, apply it to a case I did not list: a person who reports that a pain feels intensely bad yet denies any desire for it to stop and shows no tendency to avoid it. What does your principle say about that case, and would that help settle the choice between (b) and (c)?
