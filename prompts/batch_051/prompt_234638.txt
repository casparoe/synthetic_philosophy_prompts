Consider an election with three or more candidates in which a voter likes A best, B second, and C least. Suppose the polls indicate that A cannot win and that, if the voter ranks A first, C wins, whereas if she ranks B first, B wins. Most people would call it sensible—not dishonest—for her to put B first. We routinely advise friends to vote this way, and we rarely think a strategic voter owes anyone an apology. Yet many of the same people will say that a voting rule which invites such misrepresentation is flawed: it is manipulable, and manipulability is treated as a defect in the design of institutions. So the same behavior is excused, even praised, in the voter, while the condition that makes it possible is condemned in the rule.

This asymmetry is made more awkward by the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem: for any non-dictatorial voting rule whose range includes at least three alternatives, there will be preference profiles in which someone can obtain a better outcome by voting insincerely. In that sense the defect cannot be eliminated except by accepting a dictatorial rule or restricting the domain of admissible preferences.

Is the asymmetry in our judgments defensible? Identify the strongest principle that could distinguish the voter's misrepresentation from the system's manipulability—for example, a division of moral labor between individuals and institutions, an excuse of self-defense against a defective rule, or a difference in what each is required to optimize—and test it against nearby cases such as strategic candidacy, insincere voting that benefits others rather than the voter, and a committee chair who manipulates the agenda. Conclude with an explicit verdict: should we keep the asymmetry, abandon it by blaming voters and rules alike, or reverse it, and why?
