In my school philosophy club we have been arguing about two ways of treating concepts. The first side holds that philosophy should mostly try to analyze the concepts we already use—to say what knowledge, marriage, or race really amounts to by spelling out the conditions for applying them. The second side calls itself conceptual engineering, or ameliorative analysis, and it claims that a concept is best understood as a tool: something we adopt because it helps us classify, coordinate, and accomplish things, and which can therefore be sharpened, modified, or thrown away when it stops doing that work. Our club president has been defending the second side with a running analogy. A hammer, he says, is not something you should spend your time describing down to its rust spots and scratches; its value lies in what it lets you build, and when it is bent or too heavy for the job you simply get a better one. In the same way, he argues, we should stop treating inherited concepts like objects whose true nature we have to uncover, and should instead ask what we want a concept to do for us and then engineer one that does it.

Intuitively I find the analogy persuasive, but I suspect it is carrying more of the argument than it can support, so I want it audited carefully. Please give me an explicit table of correspondences: what plays the role of the tool, the user, the task, the designer, the wear, and the act of replacement. Then explain where the mapping breaks. I would especially like to know which disanalogies matter for the club president's conclusion and which are only superficial—for example, a hammer tends to have a single designer and a single purpose, while concepts arise and change across whole communities and typically serve several functions at once; and unlike a hammer, a concept is not something we can set down while we evaluate it, since the evaluation is itself done in concepts. Are these fatal to the analogy, or do they leave its main point intact? Finally, please say whether the conclusion—that we should engineer concepts rather than analyze them—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some other analogy would do that work better.
