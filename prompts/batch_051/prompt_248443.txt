I've been rereading some old historiography debates and keep tripping on a seeming contradiction. In the preface to his first book, Ranke says history has usually been assigned the job of judging the past and instructing the present for the benefit of the future, but he says his own aim is narrower: just to show how it actually was. Carr, in his 1961 lectures, turns this around and says facts are like fish on a fishmonger's slab: they don't speak at all until the historian decides which ones to use, and the historian calls on them. If I read both as statements about historical method, they look flatly incompatible. But maybe they're not. What are the best candidate reconciliations here (different senses of 'facts', methodological ideal vs. actual practice, level of narration vs. source criticism, a historicist attitude where Ranke himself selected facts but didn't notice)? Are any of them honest rather than special pleading? And where does Hayden White's point about narrative emplotment leave Ranke: is it a further refutation, or does it just make explicit a selection problem Ranke could acknowledge without giving up the ideal?
