I'm in a reading group that has been going through some of Aristotle's ethics alongside bits of modern moral psychology, and last week two of the members, Lena and Ravi, got into an argument I still can't referee. Lena said the virtue of temperance, as it usually gets preached, is mostly a repressive ideal: it treats strong desires as a problem to be managed, and the popular modern picture of self-control as a limited resource, a muscle that tires out, just reinforces that — the self becomes a tired cop keeping unruly impulses in line. Ravi said that's not actually the virtue tradition. Aristotle distinguishes the temperate person, whose appetites have been trained so the excessive desires aren't there in the first place, from the continent person, who still has the excessive desires and has to restrain them. Temperance, he said, is the first state, not the second, so it's not repression — it's the absence of anything that needs repressing; Lena's tired cop is describing the continent person, who Aristotle already treats as second best. Lena came back that this only moves the problem: the distinction makes the ideal a state where desires no longer conflict with judgment, but for most actual people the desires do conflict, and "become someone who doesn't want the extra drink" isn't something you can perform on demand; what you can practice right now is restraint. So the tradition praises a state most people can't reach while calling the usable capacity second best, which ends up moralizing everyone whose desires don't cooperate.

Ravi replied that second best isn't a put-down — Aristotle also praises continence, just as less complete — and that the tradition's cultivation story says repeated wise choices gradually reshape desire, so the continent person is on the way to being temperate, not repressing. He added that Lena's own appeal to depletion cuts against her: if willpower really is a limited resource, a life built on perpetual restraint is fragile, which is a reason to prefer the harmonized state rather than to dismiss it. Lena said that cultivation claim is an empirical one wearing a normative costume — whether repeated restraint actually reshapes desire or instead exhausts it and breeds resentment is an open question, and the recent failed replications and meta-analyses of ego depletion mean neither side can lean on the muscle metaphor; the tradition's optimism is as speculative as the repression worry Ravi brushed off.
