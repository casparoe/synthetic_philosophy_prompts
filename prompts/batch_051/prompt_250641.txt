Two weeks from now I'm giving a seminar paper defending a Nozick-style tracking theory of knowledge. The thesis I'm defending is roughly: knowing that p doesn't require being in a position to know everything p obviously entails; what it requires is that your belief tracks the truth, where the tracking is done by sensitivity — if p were false, you wouldn't believe p. On this picture closure under known entailment fails, and that's a feature, not a bug. My argument sketch is the standard one, and I'll be upfront that it is: I run the zebra/mule case and the lottery case as evidence that we already deny closure in ordinary life; I argue the tracking conditions explain both; I claim sensitivity does the real epistemic work and that safety- or justification-based views just smuggle closure back in; and I finish by saying the only alternative that saves closure is skepticism, which nobody in the room actually accepts.

The audience is a philosophy department epistemology reading group — three faculty, about eight grad students. Most of them were trained to treat closure as close to non-negotiable. One senior person works on safety and thinks Nozick is a historical curiosity. Two grad students are formal epistemology types who will want everything in possible-worlds talk and will pounce on how the closest not-p world gets fixed. And there's one contextualist who thinks the data is better handled by shifting standards than by killing closure.

Prepare me for the question period. I want a prep sheet, not a defense of the paper: the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way those people would actually say them, with how likely and how damaging each one is, what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. Include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me the one reply that has to land for the paper to survive.
