I have spent a long time reading rationalist blogs and forums, and I have picked up the guilt–shame distinction mainly in scattered discussions of cultural differences and how norms get enforced; I suspect my understanding of it is less careful than I would like. I recently came across a passage in a popular moral-psychology book that summarised the distinction as follows:

'In shame cultures, people are restrained by their sensitivity to how others see them; in guilt cultures, people are restrained by the voice of their own conscience. Shame is the emotion of being seen to fall short; guilt is the emotion of failing by one's own standards. Shame is directed at the whole self, is triggered by exposure, and needs an audience, whereas guilt is directed at a particular act and can arise in complete privacy. It follows that only guilt supplies a genuinely internal moral constraint, and that a shame-based ethic leaves people doing the right thing only where their conduct is observed. Guilt is therefore the more mature moral emotion, and societies that socialise their children primarily through shame are, on this score, less advanced than those that socialise primarily through guilt. The book added that the historical shift from shame to guilt was one of the achievements of Western modernity, and that any culture that wants responsible agents rather than obedient children ought to cultivate guilt and deliberately weaken shame.'

The passage's confidence made me wary, but I cannot tell how much of that wariness is justified and how much is contrarian instinct. I am inclined to think the book is unfair to shame, yet I cannot identify the precise step at which the unfairness enters.

Could you audit the passage for me? In particular: (a) which parts would a careful philosopher of emotion or anthropologist of morality accept as roughly accurate; (b) which parts are simplifications, conflations, or overstatements; (c) what a careful defender of shame would say in reply; (d) whether the conclusion that guilt is the more mature emotion, and that cultures should deliberately cultivate guilt and weaken shame, actually follows from the accurate parts; and (e) whether it even makes sense to say that a culture can choose between the two, as opposed to the two emotions being made more or less salient by its institutions. I am not looking for only a general verdict; I want a precise account of where the inferences hold and where they fail.
