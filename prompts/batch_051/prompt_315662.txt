I am a software engineer responsible for the data model of a question-answering system. The system builds a knowledge base by running an extraction pipeline over two corpora: a real-world corpus of news, biography, and encyclopedia text, and a fiction corpus that currently contains the original Sherlock Holmes stories. The pipeline extracts declarative sentences and stores them as ordinary facts when their confidence exceeds a threshold. The defect we keep shipping is that sentences about the detective and his world from the fiction corpus are stored as ordinary facts, so the system asserts that a particular man lives at 221B Baker Street with the same confidence it asserts the capital of a country. The reverse defect also occurs: a sentence such as that the detective is a fictional character, or that the detective never existed, is either dropped as contradictory or stored as an ordinary property of a person. This happens daily, and each miss is escalated by our QA team as a serious defect, but we cannot stop the pipeline and read theory. I have no formal philosophy background, and I am not asking for a history of the subject or for citations. I have been told that philosophy of language has long worked on names without referents, true negative statements about things that do not exist, and the difference between being true and being true according to a story, and I want that thinking converted into a repeatable engineering procedure.

Please write a decision procedure my team can run on one extracted sentence at a time to decide how it should be stored in the knowledge base. Assume the knowledge base has three layers: (a) the real world; (b) the internal world of a specified fiction, whose scope is the declared canon, by default the original Conan Doyle stories and not later adaptations, with the exact list kept in a config file; and (c) a meta layer for statements about a work of fiction or its elements as artifacts in the real world. The procedure should take as input the sentence, the source document, and the declared canon, and should output exactly one of a small fixed set of storage actions: store as real-world fact; store as fact internal to the fiction; store as meta-layer fact; store as undetermined within the fiction; reject as unrepresentable in the current scheme; or escalate to a human editor. The procedure must be expressible as a checklist or small decision tree that an operator with no philosophy background can run in under one minute per sentence, and it must say, for each question in the tree, what counts as a sufficient answer to move to the next step. Where the procedure relies on a philosophical distinction, translate it into operational terms rather than leaving it as a named doctrine. It must mark every step that is a judgment call rather than a mechanical test, and for each such step it must say what the editor receiving the escalation needs to know in order to make the call. It must also state honestly what the procedure cannot decide and where it is likely to misfire.

Then run the procedure on the following records and show the storage action each receives, with the reasoning compressed to the fewest steps the checklist allows:

1. "Sherlock Holmes lived at 221B Baker Street."
2. "Sherlock Holmes was a fictional detective created by Arthur Conan Doyle."
3. "Sherlock Holmes never existed."
4. "On an ordinary morning in 1888, Sherlock Holmes ate a poached egg for breakfast." (The stories are silent on this.)
5. "Sherlock Holmes was a better detective than any real officer ever employed by Scotland Yard."

At least one of the records should force the procedure to return a result the team would not have chosen by intuition, and you should say which result that is and why the procedure earns it. Finally, state what changes if the declared canon is set to include a modern television adaptation that updates the setting to present-day London.

Please write the answer in American English.
