I am taking a graduate seminar on the methodology of long-run environmental prediction, and I am trying to reverse-engineer my professor's implicit rules before she hands us a new case. From her lectures I have reconstructed the following track record. Malthus's 1798 claim that population must eventually outrun the food supply she refuses to call falsified; she says his timing and his preferred check were wrong, but the pressure of a growing population on a finite land base keeps reappearing, so she grants him a verdict she calls right but early. Jevons's mid-1860s warning that Britain's industrial power would decline as its domestic coal ran out she marks as falsified: the limiting resource turned out to be substitutable, and the actual sources of British decline were not the ones he identified. Ehrlich's late-1960s prediction of mass famine within a decade or two she scores as falsified in both timing and scale, and she will not rescue it, because what failed was not merely the date but his assumed dynamics — fertility and agricultural productivity moved in ways his model did not admit. Hubbert's mid-1950s prediction that lower-48 United States oil production would peak around 1970 she treats as confirmed, though she says it succeeded because it modeled a bounded stock with a measurable extraction curve rather than because it got the year right. The standard run of the 1972 Limits to Growth study she refuses to score at all, on the grounds that its horizon has not arrived and its claims concern the shape of systemic interactions, not a single dated event.

Next session she will turn to a case she has never discussed: a systems ecologist's forecast, made in 1971, that the world's remaining tropical moist forests would be functionally eliminated by 2000. He stated it categorically, not as a conditional on unchanged policy. The year 2000 has come and gone; the forests are far from functionally eliminated, though clearing continues in many of the regions he highlighted and has slowed or stopped in only a few. Tell me what verdict she should give if she is to stay consistent with her track record. In particular: state the two or three candidate rules that fit her prior verdicts; say what each rule predicts about the tropical-forests case and which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog; give your best prediction of her verdict with reasons; and specify one further case that would cleanly separate whichever candidate rules remain live.
