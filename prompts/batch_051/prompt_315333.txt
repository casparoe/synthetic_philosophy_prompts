I manage a small product team, and about once a sprint I have to decide whether the team actually decided something together or whether a bunch of individuals just happened to nod along. The difference matters for postmortems—whether it's fair to say "we shipped it" or "the team owns this bug"—for holding the whole group responsible when a feature slips, and for whether a quiet "we agreed in the room" should count when people later say they never really signed on. I keep hearing that philosophers distinguish between a bunch of I-intentions that happen to line up and a genuine shared or joint intention, with things like meshing subplans (our individual plans interlocking so each person's part depends on the others'), mutual responsiveness, and joint commitment doing the work. That distinction sounds exactly like what I need, but everything I've seen is about two people going for a walk together, and I need something I can run on a messy team.

So: can you turn that distinction into a decision procedure I can actually use? I'd like a checklist of questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure should stop and say "not joint," where it should stop and say "joint," and which steps have to be handed to a human because its a values call rather than a facts question. Keep it to something I could run in about an hour before a retro, with no philosophy background, and that I could explain to my manager and HR afterwards.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) five people all independently prefer option A and all say yes in a meeting, but nobody really listens to anyone else and each person privately plans around their own constraints; (2) the whole team co-writes a plan over two days, including a compromise two of them hate, and afterwards everyone refers back to it as "our plan" and treats deviations as something others owe them an explanation for; (3) a boss announces a plan and everyone sits in silence looking at their feet, then afterwards nobody says they agreed but everyone acts like the plan is in force. Please make at least one of these come out in a way that would annoy me—like counting the silent room as joint, or refusing to count the unanimous vote as joint. And be explicit about what the procedure can't settle: where I'm going to have to make a management call, and where the practical question of who to blame is a separate decision from the metaphysics of whether an intention was shared.

Put a one-sentence summary of the procedure as the very last line, with nothing after it.
