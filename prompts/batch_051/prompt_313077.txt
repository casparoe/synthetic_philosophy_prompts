People defending the economic reading of history keep reaching for the same building metaphor: the economy is the base, and law, politics, religion, art are the superstructure sitting on top of it. They lean on it to justify a strong claim, namely that you explain a society by its relations of production first, and that when the base shifts the rest eventually gets dragged along — not the other way around, or at least not mainly. I want an audit of how far this metaphor can actually carry that conclusion.

Give me the correspondence table: what in a society is supposed to play the role of the foundation, the load-bearing walls, the upper floors, the roof, the pipes, the renovations, the architect. Then go through the disanalogies. A building is deliberately designed by someone; it doesn't rebuild itself; it stands or falls all at once; a foundation has no internal tensions that push toward a new building. Which of those dissanalogies actually matter for the intended use, and which are just irrelevant features of the source? In particular, I worry the metaphor quietly imports the very thing it's supposed to prove — that the base is what holds the rest up and not the reverse. After the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion (economic explanatory primacy) survive, or was the metaphor doing the work? And if the metaphor is bad for that job, is there a better one that keeps what's good in it?
