In the moral philosophy seminar I am taking this term, I find myself pulled in two directions by a single problem, and I would like help thinking through it rather than being told who wins. I am genuinely drawn to utilitarianism: it gives morality a single currency of value, an impartial standpoint, a definite procedure for hard cases, and an explanation of why the numbers matter. And yet I cannot shake Williams's examples, precisely because the theory handles them so smoothly. If George refuses the job in chemical-weapons research on grounds of conscience, the position goes to someone who will pursue it more zealously and produce more harm, so utilitarianism tells him to take the job. If Jim must shoot one of twenty captives in order to secure the release of the other nineteen, utilitarianism tells him to shoot. I can find no error in the calculation, but I am left with the sense that these acts would cut George and Jim off from the commitments that make them the particular people they are, and that a theory which asks a person to treat her deepest loyalties as one more input to the aggregate has already changed the subject. The sharpest version of the worry for me is the drowning case, in which a man saves his wife and does not need to complete his thought with an impartial justification, such as the thought that in situations of this kind it is permissible to prefer one's spouse. That justifying thought, however correct, would be one thought too many.

I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a reading on which both survive. On one side, the intuition might be an artefact of parochial attachment: the unreflective rescue is exactly the kind of spontaneous partiality a sophisticated utilitarian should want people to develop, and the objection confuses the criterion of rightness with the decision procedure. On the other side, the intuition might be tracking an agent-relative reason, a reason the man has because she is his wife and not because the impartial value of the outcome picks her out, and no theory built out of solely impartial value can accommodate the integrity such reasons make possible. There is also, perhaps, a negotiated position: keep the impartial standard for evaluating practices or dispositions, but do not require the agent to consult it at the moment of action. Please give me the strongest version of each of these three moves, say what would justify settling on each, and offer a reasoned recommendation about which is most defensible as a reading of the drowning case.
