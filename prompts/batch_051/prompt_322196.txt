A friend and I have just finished a long, going-nowhere argument about a version of the stranded hitchhiker case, and I am still not sure whether he proved his point or simply moved too quickly at one step. The case, as we formulated it, runs as follows. A man is stranded on an empty road and will not be rescued unless the next motorist helps him. A motorist stops and offers a lift on one condition: the man must sincerely promise to pay a substantial sum once he is safely home. The motorist is an excellent judge of sincerity, and there is no time or means for a legally enforceable contract. The man knows that, once he is home, he will have no self-interested reason to keep the promise. We agreed to set aside any independent moral obligation arising from the promise itself, so that the case tests a point about rationality alone: whether a rational agent can make himself trustworthy by acquiring a settled disposition to pay, even though the payment will by that point be a pure cost. My friend's view is that the case cannot establish any such thing as rational irrationality, and he distilled his reasoning into the following numbered argument.

I have a persistent feeling that something goes wrong somewhere in the middle, but I could not make the objection precise. Could you assess the argument?

(1) The man will receive the lift only if he has a settled disposition to keep the promise once he is rescued; a merely insincere or half-hearted intention would be detected.

(2) Judged at the moment of performance and in terms of self-interest alone, keeping the promise is irrational: it costs him a substantial sum and brings no compensating benefit once the lift is behind him.

(3) So the disposition that wins him the lift is a disposition to perform an act that is irrational at the time the act is performed.

(4) A disposition that produces an irrational act when the time comes is itself an irrational disposition to have.

(5) If the man's only route to rescue is to possess an irrational disposition, then the fact that this disposition secures the lift does not make possessing it rational; it merely shows that an irrational disposition can, in this situation, be rewarded.

(6) Therefore the case does not show that it can be rational to make oneself irrational. At most, it shows that irrationality can sometimes pay.

Is this argument sound? If it is not, specify exactly which step, if any, fails, explain why it fails, and state what the correct formulation of that step would have to be.
