I'm a PhD student working on ideals of self-sufficiency and autarky, and there's an asymmetry I keep getting stuck on. The neighbour who grows most of her own food, keeps hens, fixes her own roof, and treats asking for help as a last resort gets called self-sufficient, and it's almost always meant as a compliment—there's a whiff of moral approval in it, like she isn't freeloading. But when a politician says the nation should be self-sufficient—grow its own food, make its own goods, stop depending on foreign suppliers—the same word flips into a diagnsis. A country like that gets called autarkic, and people treat it as economically irrational, backward, maybe dangerous. Same root idea, not depending on outsiders for what you need, but praised in a person and treated as a pathology in a polity. Now I know the cases don't line up perfectly: the neighbour is self-sufficient in only a few domains and still relies on the grid and the supermarket, while national autarky is usually imagined as near-total closure across sectors. So one answer is that the asymmetry is just an equivocation. But I'm not convinced that does all the work, because we'd also admire a person who push self-sufficiency as far as it can go, and we'd still be suspicious of a government that pushed even partial self-sufficiency in food or energy. Is there a real principle that justifies treating personal and national self-sufficiency differently, or should one of the two judgments go? Give me the strongest candidate justification you can build, test it against nearby cases (a self-sufficient firm, household, or village), and deliver an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, dropped, or reversed. Also tell me what would most change your mind about that verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. I want exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion.
