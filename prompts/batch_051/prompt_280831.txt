I'm trying to figure out whether abundance has value over and above what you eventually buy with it, and I want a principle that fits my verdicts on cases—or tells me which verdicts to drop. My aunt has a paid-off house and a $30,000 emergency fund she's never spent and probably never will; she knows its there, and I'd say her life is better for having it even though the money itself just sits. A lottery winner gets a check, never opens it, never transfers the money, and carries on exactly as before; I don't think he's better off. Two people with identical jobs and spending habits, where one has a year of wages saved and the other has nothing: I'd say the saver is better off even if no emergency ever happens. A country doubles its output and everyone spends the gain on bigger houses, but reported life satisfaction is flat because everyone's neighbours did the same; I want to say the extra floor space still counts for something, yet I also half-believe that if well-being didn't move, nothing was gained. A country that refuses to trade stockpiles grain it will never eat and it rots every year; I don't think that surplus had any value at all. And a man has a trust fund he doesn't know exists whose existence affects none of his choices; I'd say it adds nothing to his life. Is there a coherent principle that captures all of this, or close? What does the principle you'd defend say about the case I keep flip-flopping on: someone who hates having savings, feels burdened by the mental work of managing it, never spends it, but is plainly freer because of it? Also, say how confident you are in the principle and single out the point you're least sure of.
