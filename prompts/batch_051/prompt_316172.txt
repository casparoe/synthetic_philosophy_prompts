I've been trying to think through whether Chile's Project Cybersyn counts as evidence for or against the Hayek side of the socialist calculation debate, and I honestly cant decide. Here's how I've got the pieces laid out.

On the theory side: Mises said a planned economy can't rationally allocate capital goods because without real market prices there's no common unit for choosing among tractors, steel, coal, and so on. Lange answered that a central board doesn't need actual markets—it can set prices by trial and error, treat them as parametric, tell state managers to produce where marginal cost equals price, and raise prices when goods pile up or lower them when goods run short. Hayek then pushed back that Lange solved the wrong problem. The real issue isn't solving the equations, it's that the needed knowledge is dispersed across millions of people, is often local and tacit, and goes stale fast. Prices are a way of transmitting only the relevant sliver without anyone having to collect the rest. A board doing trial and error still has to somehow get all that information to the center, and that's exactly what can't be done.

Now the episode. Under Allende in the early 1970s, Stafford Beer built a cybernetic management system for the socialized part of the Chilean economy. Telex machines in factories fed production data to a central operations room in Santiago, statistical software flagged deviations, and there was a simulator of the whole economy for testing interventions. The system was actually used during the October 1972 truckers strike to keep supplies moving, so it saw some real operations, but it never got close to full rollout, and after the 1973 coup the ops room was taken apart.

What I want is a two-way analysis.

First, map the case onto the debate. Is Cybersyn best read as an attempt at Lange-style trial-and-error planning, or is it something neither Mises nor Lange had in mind? What plays the role of the auctioneer, what plays the role of the price, and where in the system would the two Lange rules (marginal-cost pricing and inventory-based price adjustment) actually live? Beer's whole design emphasized local autonomy and real-time feedback rather than top-down commands—does that make it more Hayekian, or is it still central planning with nicer sensors?

Second, say what each side would predict. What would Mises, Lange, and Hayek each have expected from Cybersyn, and what would they each make of the strike episode? Be specific about whether the system as built answers their objection or sidesteps it.

Third, tell me what the episode can actually establish. It was tested in a logistics emergency, not in setting long-run prices or planning investment, and it was ended by a coup, not by economic failure. So is it an unfair test, a partial vindication of the cybernetic approach, or evidence that the knowledge problem kept it from ever becoming more than a crisis tool?

Finally, what would the system have had to do, and for how long, to genuinely decide between Lange and Hayek? And does the case alter how the original debate should be framed, or just restate it in a shinier idiom?
