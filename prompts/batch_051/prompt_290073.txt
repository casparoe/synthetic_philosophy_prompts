I spent thirty years in bench science, mostly pharmacology, so maybe I'm biased toward thinking of moral failure as a dosing problem. Lately I've been reading the argument that our moral psychology evolved for small hunter-gatherer bands and isnt up to problems like nuclear proliferation or climate change, and that we therefore have an urgent collective obligation to develop and use moral bioenhancement—drugs, gene editing, brain stimulation, whatever works—to make people more altruistic, fair-minded, and patient. I'm half convinced. As I read it, the argument has an empirical premise (our evolved moral capacities are dangerously mismatched to the modern world) and a normative premise (if we can cheaply reduce the risk of catastrophe by changing our own psychology, we must). I'd like you to take the view seriously and tell me, as concretely as you can, what would have to be true for it to be wrong, or at least badly overstated. Dont just list objections; sort them. What experimental or clinical results would be merely awkward, and what would be fatal? For example, would it be fatal if oxytocin made people more generous toward their in-group but harsher toward outsiders? If enhancers changed people's scores on moral cognition tasks but not their actual behavior? If a drug improved fairness but blunted empathy, so some moral capacities went up while others went down? If the only realistic delivery was coercive or state-run, would that refute the normative argument or just change its politics? And should we be suspicious that the view can always be saved by saying we just havent found the right enhancer yet? I want an honest map of the position's exposure—what it genuinely risks, and whether its defenders can fairly shrug off the worst cases.
