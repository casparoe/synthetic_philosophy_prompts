For the past several months I have been reporting a feature on the way mathematical limit results get invoked in debates about artificial intelligence, and one argument has followed me through nearly every interview. In its strong form, the claim is that Gödel's incompleteness theorems impose a ceiling on any mechanical system. Any consistent formal system strong enough to express elementary arithmetic contains a sentence it can neither prove nor refute, and an observer standing outside the system can recognize that the sentence is true. Moreover, no such system can prove its own consistency. The argument then concludes that a machine, because it instantiates some formal system, is trapped inside a system it cannot step outside of, while the human mind is not trapped in this way, since we can see the truth of the system's Gödel sentence from the outside.

I have heard this argument endorsed by engineers building AI systems and dismissed by logicians who say it proves only something about particular formal systems, not about minds. What struck me during these interviews is that the two sides do not disagree about the mathematics, and yet they also do not seem to agree about what the disagreement is even about. Some treat it as a technical question about what a machine could do in principle; others treat it as a question about what the word "understanding" requires; still others treat it as a confusion about whether a theorem about formal systems can constrain a physical organ such as a brain. The standoff has that familiar frozen quality: each side can predict the other's replies, and neither has shifted ground in years.

I do not need a survey of the positions and I do not want a verdict on whether minds are machines. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement: is it verbal or substantive, formal or conceptual, one dispute or several sharing a name, and what would count as genuine progress given the diagnosis?
