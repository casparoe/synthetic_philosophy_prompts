I keep noticing a pattern and I do not have a word for it, though I suspect someone working at the border of philosophy of mind and cognitive science has already isolated it. Some years ago I read about a solitary wasp that stings and paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of its burrow, leaves it on the threshold while it goes inside to inspect the tunnel, then comes back out and drags the cricket in. If an experimenter moves the cricket a few centimeters while the wasp is inside, the wasp does not seem to register that the situation has changed: it drags the cricket back to the threshold, leaves it, goes inside to inspect, comes back out, and drags it in—and it will repeat this loop if the cricket is moved again. The wasp is doing something elaborate, competent, and apparently goal-directed, but its competence is welded to a fixed order of operations. It cannot take the shortcut that a marginally more flexible agent would take.

I notice the same shape outside biology. In certain bureaucracies, when one field on a form changes, the whole case is sent back to the beginning of the procedure, even if none of the downstream steps depends on that field. And I catch my own routines doing something like it: after a small interruption, I restart a familiar sequence from step one instead of resuming where I was.

What I want to know is whether this specific property—competent within a canonical environment, yet rigid in a way that prevents exploiting changed conditions or tolerating novelty—has an established name. I have come across the notions of fixed action pattern, reflex, habit, and compulsion, but each misses the feature I am after in a different way. A fixed action pattern is an ethological category that would cover the wasp, but it does not illuminate the bureaucratic or personal cases, where no one would call the routine an instinct or a reflex. Habit is too weak: the striking failure is not that the steps are unvarying but that already completed steps get needlessly repeated when the world changes. Compulsion seems to add an affective element—the agent feeling driven—that may be entirely absent here. I also have a strong but unplaceable sense that this contrast between rigid routine and genuinely flexible responsiveness has been put to work in debates about free will—that a free action, on this way of thinking, is not an uncaused action but one whose plan can be revised when its framing conditions shift. I may be conflating several debates.

A bare label will not satisfy me. I want the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, how it differs from the neighboring ideas above, which of those neighbors it is closest to, and where its edges are. Can a generally flexible agent be rigid in just one corner of its activity, and would that corner still count as an instance of the same thing? If there is no established term, propose one and define it so that it does not collapse into habit, reflex, fixed action pattern, or mere automaticity.
