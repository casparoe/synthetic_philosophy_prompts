I help run evaluation for a research organisation that builds safety benchmarks and also tracks internal quantitative goals, such as red-team findings per month, benchmark saturation, and incident-response time. In team discussions I keep noticing that Goodhart’s law is invoked as if it were a single, uniform warning that metrics will be gamed, with the only recommended response being to abandon numbers for qualitative judgement. That has begun to seem unhelpful. There appear to be at least three importantly different mechanisms bundled together: (1) the original Goodhart-style collapse of an observed statistical regularity once it is used for control, where a previously predictive relationship stops holding under selection or intervention; (2) Campbell-style incentive corruption, in which people whose rewards or status depend on an indicator distort the process that generates it; and (3) specification gaming in reinforcement-learning-style systems, where an optimiser finds an unforeseen way to score highly on the stated objective while bypassing the intended task.

I would like you to think through the practical consequences of keeping these three mechanisms distinct. Please give me, in exactly five numbered points, the most important implications of this three-way distinction for how we should design, maintain, and act on quantitative measures in our evaluation work. Each point should be one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Please write the response in American English.
