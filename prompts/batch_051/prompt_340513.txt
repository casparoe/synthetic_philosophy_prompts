I am putting together a document for a working group on the conceptual foundations of inferring human preferences from behavior, and I would like you to draft it. The document is to be the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps in inverse reinforcement learning and preference inference. The first camp — call it the recovery camp — holds that the reward functions extracted from observed behavior are, at least in principle, recoverable descriptions of an agent's actual preferences: choices are produced by possibly noisy or bounded optimization of a reasonably stable underlying reward, and with enough structure, the right state representation, and suitable priors, the inference can be said to discover the preference that was already there. The second camp — call it the constructivist camp — holds that inverse reinforcement learning does not discover a pre-existing preference so much as construct a compact rationalizing summary of a behavioral record. On this view, human and animal behavior is shaped by habits, norms, framing, affect, and context, and there may be no unique, stable utility function behind it; two equally predictive but quite different inferred rewards can have equal claim to being the preference, and the reward function is an artifact of the modeler's choice of features and priors.

The deliverable should read as a document both camps could put their names to. It should not adjudicate the dispute; its goal is to make the shared ground and the remaining divergence exact. It should include: (1) a numbered list of substantive claims both sides can sign, covering the IRL formalism, the assumptions made when a reward function is fit to demonstrations, the underdetermination of reward functions by finite demonstrations, and what preference would have to mean for recovery to be possible; (2) a disagreements table in which the wording of each side's claim has been approved by that side, with the residual difference made exact; (3) a list of cruxes, that is, sub-questions such that settling them would genuinely move at least one camp; and (4) for each crux, the observation, experiment, or argument both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Push the drafting past vacuous agreements. Address, for example, the familiar point that many reward functions can rationalize any finite set of demonstrations, the status of maximum-entropy or Bayesian priors as tie-breakers, whether the claim that the agent is approximately optimizing the recovered reward is an empirical hypothesis or close to a tautology, and whether framing effects and intransitive choices indicate a richer latent preference or a boundary of the preference model itself. Where you judge that a disagreement is partly or wholly verbal, say which terms are doing the work and propose a joint reformulation.
