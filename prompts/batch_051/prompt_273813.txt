A friend in my jurisprudence class laid out an argument that seems to show either every law is in force or none of them are, and I can't find the exit. He starts from an innocent-sounding premise about desuetude — the idea that a statute so long unused that no one enforces or invokes it anymore ceases to be law. In numbered form:

(1) By desuetude, a law that has fallen into complete disuse is no longer part of the law in force.
(2) Enforcement is a matter of degree: a statute can be enforced in every violation, in half of them, once a decade, once a century, never.
(3) So if zero enforcement makes a law no longer law, there must be some lowest level of enforcement that keeps a law in force.
(4) But any such threshold is arbitrary — why would one prosecution per century keep a statute alive while one per two centuries wouldn't?
(5) And the rule of law demands that whether you stand under a law not depend on an arbitrary line that someone just stipulates.
(6) So for any law enforced less than perfectly, there is no fact of the matter about whether it is in force. But every law is enforced less than perfectly: violations get missed, prosecutors decline, some never come to light.
(7) Therefore no law is in force. But plainly some are — the tax code and the murder statute are not dead letters.

He says the only escape is to reject desuetude outright. But that seems to leave us with a 200-year-old statute no judge would dream of applying still being law, which is it's own absurdity. I'd rather keep desuetude and the rule-of-law point in (5), and I want to know what I'm paying for that. Which premise do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
