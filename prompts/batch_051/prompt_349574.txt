I am writing an essay on the alignment tax, but I want to get the concept right: not as a flat safety cost that a well-intentioned lab could simply pay, but as a cost whose payment is governed by a competitiveness constraint — roughly, any actor who unilaterally pays it is outcompeted by an actor who does not, so the cost is deferred or externalized even when nearly everyone would prefer the safer equilibrium. Ursula K. Le Guin's short story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" keeps coming to mind, and I would like a serious reading of the story in these terms rather than the usual utilitarian one. Read it as a dramatization of the alignment tax, with the suffering child as the unpaid cost that stabilizes the system and the citizens' acquiescence as something closer to a structural constraint than to simple callousness. But I want the reading to be honest where it strains: Omelas has no rival city, no market, no race, so the story may lack exactly the competitive mechanism that makes the tax binding in the contexts where the concept is used. Where, if anywhere, does the competitiveness constraint actually appear in the story — in the citizens' choices, in the walkers' exit, in the child's fixed role, in the narrator's handling of the reader? Which scene or choice does the lens illuminate most, and where does an honest critic have to say the story is not about the alignment tax at all but about something adjacent, such as a sacrificial equilibrium, a commitment device, or a problem of complicity? Do not reduce the story to an illustration of the economics or stretch the economics to force a fit, and end by saying what the story, taken on its own terms, adds to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone leave out.
