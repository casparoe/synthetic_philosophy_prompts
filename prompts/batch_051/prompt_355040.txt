I am working through an introductory ethics chapter on global poverty, and it summarizes Peter Singer’s argument in “Famine, Affluence, and Morality” (1972) as follows:

Singer’s central principle is that if it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought, morally, to do it. His shallow pond example shows that physical distance is morally irrelevant: a drowning child in front of you and a starving child abroad both make the same kind of demand on you. Since need is need wherever it occurs, anyone in an affluent country who spends money on non-essentials is acting wrongly unless they give that money to famine relief instead. The only limiting condition Singer accepts is that you need not make a sacrifice that is comparably bad. So private donors should give until further giving would impose a comparable sacrifice on themselves. Governments are too self-interested for this duty to rest with them; the real obligation falls on individuals.

The chapter then quotes this passage from Singer to support its reading:

By “without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance” I mean without causing anything else comparably bad to happen, or doing something that is wrong in itself, or failing to promote some moral good, comparable in significance to the bad thing that we can prevent.

I suspect the summary is mostly right but too compressed at several points. Please give me an accuracy audit. Go sentence by sentence, separating what a careful reader of Singer should accept, what is a common simplification, and what Singer explicitly denies. In particular, does the chapter’s conclusion about private donors and governments actually follow from the quoted passage?
