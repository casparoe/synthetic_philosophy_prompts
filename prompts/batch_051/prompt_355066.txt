During a seminar on religion and social cohesion, a fellow student made a bold claim: religion makes people more moral, and this shows up in the fact that more religious communities have lower crime rates, more charitable giving, and stronger trust. I have not been able to get the claim into focus. Depending on what one means, 'more moral' could refer to following one's own professed code more consistently, conforming to a shared and community-enforced code, behaving prosocially toward strangers at genuine cost to oneself, acting honestly when detection is unlikely, or simply avoiding the kinds of rule-breaking that make it into crime statistics. The causal story is just as murky: perhaps people who are already self-controlled and cooperative are more likely to remain religious, or perhaps any dense community with strong social monitoring produces these patterns regardless of belief. Still, I suspect some version of the claim is testable, and I would rather test it precisely than dismiss it vaguely.

Do not engage with whether any particular religion is true. Instead, (a) pick the strongest operationalization of 'more moral' you can defend and design a concrete empirical test, or small battery of tests, for whether religion in that sense makes people more moral; (b) specify what pattern of results would count for and against the claim, distinguishing religious belief from religious practice and membership, and addressing selection effects; and (c) extend the analysis to the social level by asking whether the mechanisms you identify could plausibly explain how a society might sustain trust even as religious involvement declines, and what that would imply about whether religion is needed to sustain morality. Conclude by saying what even a strong positive result would leave unsettled—for instance, questions about private character, about counterfactuals, or about whether actions should be credited as moral when they are done from religious motives rather than concern for others.
