Last night a friend and I argued late into the night about the nonidentity problem. She took the line that a policy cannot wrong someone if that person would never have existed without the policy, so a great deal of long-run harm is not really harm to anyone; I kept replying that this lets a choice dodge its own worst consequences simply by changing which people come to exist. We parted without either of us being able to say what a working planner should do with the distinction come Monday morning.

I work in the long-term impacts office of a national infrastructure planning body. I screen dams, transport corridors, waste repositories, land remediation, and similar projects whose effects extend a century or more. A few times a month I must recommend whether expected harms to not-yet-existing people should block, condition, or allow a proposal. Many of these projects alter local economies, migration, and family formation, so they change which particular people are born later; the people who would suffer a project's later harms often owe their existence to that project, and the no-project alternative would have produced a different set of people. The question I need answered is not whether the nonidentity problem is philosophically real, but how to treat it procedurally in a public agency's decisions.

Please write a reusable decision procedure for this issue. It should be grounded in the best available philosophical analysis of the nonidentity problem, but usable by an analyst with no formal philosophy training in two working days, using information already in a project file or obtainable through routine demographic and exposure modeling. It should ask its questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and mark clearly where it stops with one of three outcomes: treat the expected harm as a harm to particular future people whose interests can be counted; refuse to count it as a person-affecting harm; or hand the decision to a human because the issue is one of values rather than facts or concepts. It should also flag which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say what the procedure cannot do.

Then run the procedure on three worked cases. (a) A spent-fuel repository expected to leak after roughly two hundred years and cause elevated childhood illness in a community that exists only because the repository workforce and its supply chains settled the region and raised families there. (b) A flood-control scheme that changes settlement and marriage patterns upstream; under the scheme, ten thousand children per generation are born who would not otherwise have existed, and some of them suffer occasional flood losses, while the no-scheme baseline would produce a different ten thousand children with a different distribution of losses. (c) A third case of your choosing from transport or energy planning, deliberately constructed so that following the procedure yields an answer that an analyst as attached as I am to the idea that a bad outcome must be bad for some particular person will find unwelcome. For each case, show why the procedure takes the turn it does and where, if anywhere, it leaves the final judgment to a person.
