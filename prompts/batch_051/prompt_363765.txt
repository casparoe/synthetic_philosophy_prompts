For some years I have occupied myself with a demanding and, by now, almost entirely private skill: I translate the speeches of the Attic orators into English verse. No one reads the results, and I have reached the point where I need to know whether it is still good to be excellent at something from which no one benefits. I have a friend whose verdicts on this I have been collecting, because she is firmer and more consistent than I am. Her rulings so far, stated as cleanly as I can put them:

1. A pianist who has spent three decades learning the late Beethoven sonatas and plays them alone in her apartment, to no audience and with no recordings, and who says this private playing is one of the best things in her life. My friend says: a genuine good, not a waste.

2. A chess player who reached master strength without ever playing publicly, teaching, or writing about his chess, and who says the climb gave him satisfaction though he now rarely plays. My friend says: the achievement was worth having.

3. A calculating savant who sets himself arithmetical problems, solves them instantly and without effort, and throws the answers away without caring whether any survive. My friend says: not good — an ability, but not an achievement.

4. A man who trains himself to lie flawlessly, uses the skill on no one, and takes pride in it. My friend says: not good — the kind of excellence matters.

5. A woman who makes herself an excellent player of an entirely trivial game, is wholly absorbed by it, and shares it with no one. My friend says: not good — absorption is not enough.

The new case is my own, put in the same terms. I work hard at a genuinely worthwhile object, and the product is, by any ordinary standard, excellent. But I have never much enjoyed the work, I enjoy it less now, no one else has ever benefited from it, and if the notebooks vanished I would feel little more than relief. Would she say that the excellence or the achievement was good for me, or that it was not good at all? Please give me: the two or three general principles that could reconcile all five of her earlier verdicts; where they diverge about my case; which prediction about my case is best supported and why; and the one further case I could put to her that would most cleanly settle which principle she is really applying. Finally, tell me what consideration would change your mind about that prediction, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
