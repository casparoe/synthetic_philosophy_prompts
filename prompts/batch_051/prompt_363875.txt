I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether individuals have any reason to change their behavior when the harm in question is produced only by millions of small acts taken together. His view was stark: since my car trip, my flight, my chicken purchase, and my vote will each make no discoverable difference to the outcome, none of them can be wrong or required, and moral energy aimed at private choices is misdirected away from governments and corporations. I kept objecting with particular cases, but every time I tried to say what principle lay behind my objections, I found myself either contradicting what I had said ten minutes earlier or retreating to something vague about decency.

So I wrote down my verdicts, and I want to know whether one principle can capture them, or which of them I should give up. Here they are. If one thousand people must each push once on a jammed gate to free a trapped person, and fewer than one thousand pushes will move nothing, and I do not know how many others will push, I think I should push: I might be the thousandth, and the stakes are high. I also think I should vote in a general election even though I am nearly certain my ballot will not decide it, partly because the stakes are enormous and partly because voting seems like something one simply does. But I do not think I am obligated to forgo a once-a-year flight to visit my family, and I do not feel that driving to buy groceries is wrong, even though I accept that the aggregate of such journeys is altering the climate and harming people. And yet I will not buy a chicken from a supplier whose factory farming I take to be brutal, even though I am nearly certain the purchase will not change how many chickens are raised next season; it feels wrong to participate at all, though my friend calls this nothing but squeamishness. Dropping a plastic cup on a hiking trail is wrong, I think, but it would be bizarre to locate the wrong in the measurable environmental damage done by that particular cup.

The nearest I got to a principle was this: an act is required if it stands a real chance of making a difference to a serious outcome, and if it cannot make such a difference its wrongness, if any, must come from a weaker source such as participation or symbolic meaning. But that principle seems to require pushing the gate and voting, excuse the flight and the car trip, and condemn the chicken and the litter only through the participation clause; and then I owe an account of why participating in factory farming through one purchase is worse than participating in climate change through one flight, which I cannot currently give. I would like to know whether there is a coherent principle of individual contribution to collective harms that yields my verdicts, or at least one worth adopting and the specific verdict it forces me to overturn. Please state the principle precisely, show how it handles each case and whether any of my verdicts must go, and tell me what it says about a case I keep avoiding: running the air conditioning all night during a heat wave when my individual use will not trigger a blackout but the aggregate of such uses strains the grid into rolling blackouts. I want continuous prose, as in a letter to me, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
