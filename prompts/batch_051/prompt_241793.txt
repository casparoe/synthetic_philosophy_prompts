I'm a phd student in argumentation theory, and I keep getting stuck on an asymmetry in how we judge games versus arguments. Consider a basketball defender who deliberately fouls a fast break near the end of a close game. We don't just accept that: we call it smart, professionals budget for it, and a coach who doesn't drill it is sort of negligent. Now consider a competitive debater doing the same kind of thing inside their format: reframing the opponent's position just enough to be unanswerable in the time left, or dumping seven weak objections into one speech so the other side can answer two. Even when no rule is broken, we call it sophistry and treat the debater as if they've violated something deeper then the rules. Both cases are strategic uses of the rules rather than attempts to do the central thing well, and both trade a bit of that central thing (clean basketball, actually tracking the other side's view) for a better chance of winning. So: is there a justification for the asymmetry, or are we just sentimental about argument while we've made our peace with sport? Give me the strongest defense you can, run it through some nearby cases (a lawyer objecting to rattle a witness, a chess player who plays for a draw from move five, a soccer team wasting time to protect a one-goal lead), and say whether you would keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or abandon it. Then tell me what your answer implies for the idea that formal competitive debate could be a sport and whether making it one would actually improve argumentation.
