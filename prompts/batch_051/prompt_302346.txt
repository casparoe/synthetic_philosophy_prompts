Take the following five abilities as a working analysis of what it is to understand a proof, rather than merely to reproduce or verify one.

(1) The ability to check that each step is a valid consequence of earlier steps, definitions, or already established results.
(2) The ability to state the main idea of the proof in compressed form, distinguishing the step that carries the argument from the surrounding bookkeeping.
(3) The ability to explain why the theorem is true, not merely that it follows: to say what features of the setup make the conclusion close to inevitable.
(4) The ability to adapt the proof to neighboring statements—weakening a hypothesis, strengthening the conclusion, asking for a quantitative version—and to predict in advance where the argument will break or require new ideas.
(5) The ability to identify which hypotheses are essential to the theorem and which are mere artifacts of a particular route to it.

Now consider a case that sits on the boundary. A combinatorialist proves a theorem of the form: every finite configuration of a certain type satisfies a certain local bound. The proof has two layers. First, by hand, the combinatorialist proves a reduction lemma that any counterexample must contain one of several thousand minimal obstructions; they can explain exactly why the reduction is complete, why it is a natural way to attack the problem, and how the obstructions were selected. Second, an exhaustive computer search shows that none of these minimal obstructions can actually exist. For each obstruction, a generated certificate is checked by an independent certificate checker, which the combinatorialist also wrote. The combinatorialist can explain why the checker is sound—why, if it accepts a certificate for an obstruction, that obstruction really is impossible—but no one, including the combinatorialist, has inspected more than a sample of the certificates by hand.

When asked for the main idea, the combinatorialist says only that the idea is to reduce every possible counterexample to a finite list and then eliminate the list; beyond that, they say, the proof is casework. They cannot give a unified explanation of why the obstructions all fail, cannot say which features of the configurations are doing the work, and cannot predict what would happen if the bound were tightened except by rerunning the search. A colleague who studies the proof for a semester learns to operate the machinery but cannot afterwards explain, in general terms, why the theorem is true.

Does the combinatorialist understand the proof? Go criterion by criterion. Where the answer is no or unclear, say whether the difficulty lies in the criteria, in this style of proof, or in the case itself. Finally, say whether the case gives reason to revise the working analysis.
