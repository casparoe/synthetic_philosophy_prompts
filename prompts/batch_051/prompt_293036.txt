I'm in a comparative privacy course right now and we keep going in circles about whether a country's privacy regime actually changes the kind of people it produces, or just changes what they can be fined for. The professor frames it through three cases: Germany, where data protection is built around informational self-determination and treated like a personality right; the US, where the default is pretty open information flows and privacy tends to win only when there's a concrete harm; and China's social credit system, which is supposedly designed to make people police themselves before the state has to step in. The readings keep making a strong conceptual claim: these regimes don't just produce different behavior under enforcement—they produce different citizens, with different internalized norms about being observed and about what you owe someone else's data. I can picture how you'd test behavior (compliance rates, disclosure rates, maybe what people do when penalties change), but I don't see how you'd test the internalization part.

I want you to design the best test you can of the claim that a surveillance/privacy regime changes how far citizens have internalized norms about observation and data-sharing, rather than merely changing the incentives they face. Be concrete about populations, conditions, and measures. Say what result would push you toward "same citizens, different calculators" and what would push you toward "different citizens." Then be honest about what even a positive result wouldn't settle—where the design stops testing and starts interpreting, and whether the gap is just a data problem or a sign that internalization has no clean behavioral footprint. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the test you propose and which point you're least sure about. Oh, and write it in American English.
