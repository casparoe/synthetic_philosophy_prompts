I'm prepping a graduate seminar on political philosophy and transitional justice, and before I assign the usual stuff on official apologies I want to get clear on what I think a theory of them actually has to do. The phenomenon I have in mind is the familiar one: a sitting government or head of state apologizes for a wrong committed by an earlier government or by a longer historical process—colonial land theft, enslavement, the forced removal of children, wartime atrocities—and the apology is supposed to do something a mere historical statement or museum plaque wouldn't do. But every time I read one of these apologies I'm struck by how unclear the success conditions are. Does the state have to feel remorse? Can a corporate agent even feel remorse, or is that category mistake? If today's officials weren't alive when the wrong was done, in what sense is it 'their' apology? Does an apology have to be accepted by the descendants of the victims to count, or is making it publicly enough? And what's the relation between the words and the money/land/legal reform—is a well-worded apology without material repair just cheap symbolism, while a reparations package without an apology misses the thing that restores standing?

So here's what I want: write the desiderata—the list of requirements a theory of official apology for historical wrongs has to meet to count as adequate, rather than just private apology blown up to state size. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, ideally from the messy real cases instead of toy examples. Then flag which requirements you think are non-negotiable and which are really just preferences of particular camps—say, the camp that thinks the point is collective moral repair vs. the camp that thinks it's political acknowledgment and guarantees of non-repetition. I'd also like you to say whether any subset of the desiderata is in tension or might be jointly unsatisfiable (that's the part I'm most unsure about), and which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories. Don't hand me an encyclopedia entry or a stack of options; I want a committed statement of your criteria, so take a definite position on, e.g., whether collective remorse is a genuine requirement or a red herring, and defend it. If it helps you make the criteria concrete, briefly score two or three rough families of views against your list—an agent-moral view that treats the state as a person, a forward-looking reconciliation view, and a reparative-justice view that treats apology as secondary to material repair—but keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard.
