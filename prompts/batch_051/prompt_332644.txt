I'm a software engineer and I keep getting into this argument with a coworker who says P ≠ NP means AGI is impossible. I tried to make his argument precise and instead it seems to prove too much — it would show working mathematicians can't be doing what they obviously do. I cant find the bad step.

(1) A mind is a physical process, so anything it does can be simulated by a Turing machine.

(2) The problem "does this formula have a proof of length ≤ n?" is NP-complete, so if P ≠ NP there is no algorithm that solves every instance in polynomial time.

(3) A working mathematician is basically an algorithm that takes a mathematical sentence and returns a proof; she does this reliably and fast enough to actually do mathematics.

(4) If P ≠ NP, then no algorithm solves every instance of that proof problem in polynomial time, and by (1) no mind is an exception.

(5) So either mathematicians solve a complete NP-hard problem in polynomial time (which is impossible by (4) unless P = NP), or they aren't really Turing-simulable, or they don't actually solve the proof problem.

(6) I don't like any of these exits: denying (3) feels like denying that mathematicians prove things, P = NP would be a bigger deal than anything, and denying (1) means giving up on minds being machines.

I'd like to keep (1) and (2) if I can. Which premise do I actually have to reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Answer in American English.
