For a graduate seminar I teach on the philosophy of emotion, I would like you to construct a diagnostic instrument for the first meeting: a ten-item "which account of emotion do you actually hold?" quiz with an explicit scoring key. The instrument should sort respondents among four positions: (i) the Neo-Stoic judgment view, on which an emotion is, or essentially contains, a full evaluative judgment or belief (fear is judging oneself endangered); (ii) the perceptual view, on which an emotion is a non-doxastic perception or appraisal of value (fear is seeing danger without judging it); (iii) the somatic feeling view, on which an emotion is the feeling of characteristic bodily changes; and (iv) the attitudinal view, on which emotions are felt evaluative attitudes directed at objects, neither judgments nor perceptions nor bare bodily feelings.

The central constraint is that no item may ask respondents which view they endorse or use any of that theoretical vocabulary. Each item should present a concrete case or a claim about a case, with two or three answer options that correspond to distinctive positions without naming them. Use at least the following cases: a recalcitrant fear of a dog the person firmly judges harmless; being moved by the death of a fictional character; fear or distress in a creature incapable of judgment; an episode of cold anger with no felt bodily change; a state of free-floating anxiety without an object; and a case in which the same bodily arousal is interpreted as anger in one context and excitement in another. Add further cases as needed, but the items must target at least these fault lines: whether emotion requires a judgment, requires a non-doxastic appraisal, requires an intentional object, or requires a felt body; and whether an emotion is identified by its evaluative content or by its feeling.

Construct the scoring key from the logical space of answers rather than by reverse-engineering the four labels. State the underlying distinctions, list the combinations of answers the instrument can distinguish, and locate each of the four positions in that space. Identify any answer combinations that correspond to no live position, and briefly diagnose each empty cell: is the view incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Flag any two positions the ten items cannot reliably separate, and specify what additional case or item would be needed to separate them.

Audience: graduate students, some with no prior philosophy of emotion. The entire instrument—respondent instructions, ten items, and scoring key with diagnostic notes—must not exceed 1,400 words, with each item no longer than 80 words. Do not include references or citations; the instrument must be self-contained.
