I co-run a small film discussion group, and last month a member objected to a planned screening because the director has been credibly accused of serious abuse. The ensuing argument forced me to organize, more systematically than I had before, the standard moves made in defence of the position that a work of art can be assessed on its own terms without reference to the artist’s moral character. Here are the arguments I have collected. First, aesthetic autonomy: the features that make a film, novel, or painting good—structure, rhythm, formal inventiveness, expressive depth—are internal to the work, and the creator’s private conduct is not among those features. Second, the public-object argument: once a work is released or exhibited, it is a public artefact whose meaning is constituted in the encounter between audience and work, not by the artist’s biography. Third, the inconsistency charge: if we refused engagement with works by morally bad artists, we would have to give up much of the canon—Caravaggio, Wagner, Gauguin, and others—and almost no one applies the principle that broadly; selective outrage tracks scandal visibility, not principle. Fourth, the wrong-target argument: refusing the work punishes audiences, collaborators, and cultural memory while the artist may be deceased or insulated; moral sanction should aim at the person, not the object. Fifth, the insight-from-imperfection argument: a work can embody moral insight or help us understand capacities for cruelty and self-deception even if the artist did not live by that insight. Sixth, denial of endorsement: engaging with a work need not certify the artist’s character, just as appreciating a painting by a wrongdoer is not an act of moral friendship.

I suspect this list is close to exhaustive; these are the moves I keep encountering in essays and in conversation, and they seem to cover the available space. Is there another argument for separating the work from the artist that I am missing? It should be genuinely different from these six, not a restatement, a special case, or a combination of them. Please also say how the new argument relates to the ones on my list, and, at the end, say what would change your mind about whether it is a distinct and successful argument—which consideration or counterexample would lead you to abandon it, or where the reasoning is most likely to fail.
