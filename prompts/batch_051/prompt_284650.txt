I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and a foundation has invited me to submit a one-page concept note for a six-month project on direct democracy. I have a half-formed angle: James Madison's Federalist No. 10 is usually read as a defense of representative government, but the contemporary debate over referendums and ballot initiatives often treats his worry as an empirical prediction about outcomes rather than as a claim about what kind of majority is entitled to rule. The line I keep returning to is this: "Complaints are everywhere heard from our most considerate and virtuous citizens, equally the friends of public and private faith, and of public and personal liberty, that our Governments are too unstable; that the public good is disregarded in the conflicts of rival parties; and that measures are too often decided, not according to the rules of justice, and the rights of the minor party, but by the superior force of an interested and overbearing majority." I want the proposal to ask whether the Madisonian objection applies differently when the decision is made directly by referendum rather than by a legislature. Some argue direct votes allow citizens to override factional legislatures; others argue they let an emotional majority bypass deliberation and minority safeguards. I don't want a proposal that simply picks a side; I want it to isolate the structural variable that could explain the difference — agenda control, question wording, the absence of a repeat-play representative, or something else — and to show why a philosophical analysis of "the rules of justice" and "the rights of the minor party" is needed before the empirical question can even be formulated. Please write the one-page proposal: the precise question; why the existing positions leave it open; the method, and why it is preferable to the obvious alternatives; a realistic six-month deliverable useful to journalists and referendum designers; and what would count as failure. End with a short paragraph saying what argument or consideration would change your mind about this framing, or where the proposal's reasoning is most likely to fail.
