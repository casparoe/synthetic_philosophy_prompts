I teach an upper-level undergraduate metaethics seminar, and the next session is on the Frege–Geach problem. The students have done the assigned reading, so they can state the standard moves: expressivists hold that ethical sentences express non-cognitive attitudes rather than beliefs; the Frege–Geach problem is that the same sentence seems to occur unasserted inside conditionals, negations, and disjunctions, where the relevant attitude is not being expressed, which makes it unclear how expressivists can explain the meaning of such embedded occurrences or the validity of ordinary moral reasoning like modus ponens. They can say all of that, and I'm fairly sure they don't yet feel why it is a serious problem rather than a terminological fuss. What I'd like is a 75-minute in-class activity for about 14 students that makes them run into the problem themselves: something where they have to build and use a little language whose atomic sentences only express attitudes, and then discover, by trying to combine those sentences with negation and conditionals, that something goes badly wrong. The structure should make the difficulty arise from their own attempts to make the language work, not from my explaining it afterward.

Please act as an expert metaethicist with experience designing classroom simulations. Start by brainstorming two or three candidate game mechanics (for example—but not necessarily—a card-language game, a role-play in which speakers exchange attitude tokens and must draw inferences according to rules, or a small constructed language with a restricted vocabulary), and settle on the one that most reliably produces the breakdown. Then give me the full design: materials, rules, a realistic timeline, the exact moment at which the activity should be allowed to stall, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto the components of the Frege–Geach problem—unasserted contexts, attitude-expression versus content, compositional meaning, and validity-preserving inference. Participants should come away seeing why the problem is a problem about meaning and logic rather than about tone, sincerity, or the psychology of making up one's mind. The idea should emerge from what happens during the game, not from the debrief lecture. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong—for instance, students may invent ad hoc workarounds that quietly smuggle in descriptive content, or may treat the difficulty as merely psychological—and how to respond in the moment. Finally, be explicit about which feature of the Frege–Geach problem the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator, so I don't leave the class with a misleading picture of the dialectic.
