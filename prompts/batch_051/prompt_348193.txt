I am the appointed astrologer-astronomer to a German city in the early 1620s. Each year I am required to produce a public prognostication covering weather, harvests, disease, and civil affairs, and I also take private clients who ask for nativities. I want to follow Johannes Kepler's reformed astrology as I understand it: the zodiac signs and houses are arbitrary divisions with no physical basis; the real celestial causes are aspects, that is, the angular configurations between planets, such as conjunction, opposition, square, trine, and sextile; these configurations act on the Earth through natural, geometrical-optical processes, including the stirring of the earth's soul and atmosphere; astrological influence produces inclinations rather than fates, and it preserves both divine freedom and human freedom. But I need to apply this under pressure from a council that expects traditional predictions and clients who expect sign-and-house readings.

Give me a reusable decision procedure for deciding whether a proposed astrological claim is acceptable under Kepler's reformed astrology. The procedure should be an ordered list of tests, with for each test: the question to ask, the evidence or calculation that would satisfy it, the point at which the procedure returns a no, and the point at which it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of natural philosophy, theology, or prudence rather than technique. Keep the procedure usable by someone with only tables, an ephemeris, and the ordinary mathematical training of an astrologer.

Then apply your procedure to these three claims and show how each reaches its verdict:

(a) The approaching Mars-Saturn opposition disposes the air to cold and dryness, so the coming winter will likely be hard and the harvest poor, though as a tendency rather than a determined decree.

(b) Because a notable citizen's newborn has Jupiter in Aries and in the third house, the child will grow up eloquent and successful in trade.

(c) At the next new moon the Moon will return to the same degree of the ecliptic it occupied at the city's founding, and therefore an epidemic will strike within a month.

Tell me which claims survive, which fail, and where the procedure gives an answer my paymasters or clients will not like. If, in your view, I have stated Kepler's position incorrectly or drawn a distinction he would not draw, correct me and adjust the procedure accordingly.
