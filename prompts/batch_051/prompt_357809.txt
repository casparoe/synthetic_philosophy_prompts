I retired from a career in physics a few years back and have been working through the philosophy of psychiatry. I've hit a snag that I suspect is more basic than it looks. The two-factor theory of delusions — roughly, the view that a delusion requires both a strange experience and a second failure in the machinery that evaluates that experience — spends a lot of time assuming we already know what a belief is. But every paper I read seems to mean something different by it, and the dispute over whether delusions are beliefs just slides around on top of that.

So I'd like a working definition of 'belief' that actually does some work. I want it to get the obvious cases right and give a defensible verdict on the borderline ones. Here is my list:

1. An engineer who has checked the load calculations and is willing to drive his own lorry across the bridge. (Clearly a belief, in my book.)
2. A man who insists, with every sign of sincerity, that his wife has been replaced by a near-identical impostor, yet keeps living with her, eats her cooking, and signs whatever she puts in front of him. (The sort of case the delusions literature worries over.)
3. Me looking at the Müller-Lyer figure: the lines look unequal, and no amount of measuring stops the illusion. But I don't believe they're unequal. I'd want your definition to say this isn't a belief.
4. A climate modeller who says 'I believe a drought is more likely than not; my models put it at 0.65.' Is 0.65 a belief in the same sense as case 1, or is the word doing something different under uncertainty?
5. A member of a religious congregation who says her commitment is a matter of choice and discipline, not evidence: 'I choose to believe.' Is that belief or something else?
6. An assumption I have never actually entertained — that the floor of my study won't give way this morning — but that plainly guides my behaviour. Do I believe it?
7. A man given every sign of believing his failing restaurant will turn round: he tells everyone so, secures another loan, and works eighteen-hour days — but he will not open the bank statements.
8. A chess engine whose evaluation function says the position is +0.7. It represents the position as favourable; does it believe it?

I susspect the ordinary word 'belief' is doing at least two jobs: one descriptive, about what is actually in the person's head, and one normative, about when we credit somebody's word and hold them to it. If you agree, give me the two definitions and say which of my cases move between them. Whichever way you go, run the definition through all eight cases, tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or tell me why I should accept the surprising verdict. Then state plainly whether, by your definition, the man in case 2 counts as believing his impostor claim, and what that does to the two-factor debate.

Oh, and British English throughout, please — I've gone native on that score.
