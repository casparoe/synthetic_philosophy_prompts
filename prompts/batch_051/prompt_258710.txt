Section 9(b) of the Data Stewardship Act reads: "An individual owns any information collected about that individual, and no person may sell, share, or use such information for any purpose other than a purpose authorized at the time it was collected." The Act dates from the late 1990s, when its authors understood "information collected about" a person to mean records: fields in a database, names, dates, attributes attached to a file.

Now take a case its drafters did not foresee. A music-streaming service collects users' listening histories under a consent form stating that the data will be used solely to operate and improve its recommendation feature. The service then trains a recommender model on those histories and sells the trained model itself, with no user records attached, to a life insurer. The insurer has lawfully obtained from a broker some behavioral facts about one user — listening times, skip rates, session lengths — which are not by themselves health data. It feeds those facts into the purchased model and receives inferences about that user's sleep disruption, mood, and likely health conditions, which it uses in underwriting. The model's parameters contain no identifiable records and are shaped jointly by the data of millions of users.

How does section 9(b) apply, if at all, to (i) the sale of the model and (ii) the insurer's use of it to draw inferences about this user? Work through the reading that follows from the words of the clause, the reading that follows from its purpose, the reading the drafters would have given had they been asked today, and the reading a reasonable reader would take from the clause; identify where these readings diverge; and say which should control and why. Conclude with a sentence on what the case reveals about whether information about you can still be owned once it no longer sits in a record but is distributed across a model.
