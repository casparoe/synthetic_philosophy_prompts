I recently spent a long evening arguing with a friend about what it means to say that some event or condition caused a large historical or social outcome, and I came away with a set of considered judgments but no principle that holds them together. My friend kept insisting that the cause is whatever abnormal event made the difference against a normal background; standing conditions may explain, but only events cause. Some of my judgments fit that view, and some plainly do not. I would like you to help me work out what principle, if any, captures my judgments, or to tell me which judgments I should revise.

Here are the cases as I am inclined to judge them.

1. In 1914, the driver of Archduke Franz Ferdinand made a wrong turn in Sarajevo and paused to reverse in front of an assassin, who shot the Archduke and his wife; the July Crisis followed, and with it the First World War. I would say the assassin's shots caused the two deaths. But I would not say the wrong turn caused the war, and I would hesitate to say the shots caused the war. I want to say the war was caused by the alliance system, the rigidity of the mobilization plans, and the decision of the great powers to treat the killings as a pretext. The assassination was the occasion, not a cause.

2. A dam fails during an unusually heavy rain. Investigators later show that the spillway had been designed too small for a storm of that magnitude and that two floodgates had rusted shut after years of deferred maintenance. I think it is correct to say the rain caused the failure, the design flaw caused the failure, and the rusted gates were a cause of the failure; the deferred maintenance was a background cause. I would resist any demand to identify the single cause.

3. A coastal region under colonial rule suffers two poor harvests, but the harvest is still sufficient to feed the population at subsistence if local stocks are retained. The administration permits continued export of grain and delays relief on the ground that the market should be left to work, while merchants hoard what remains. Widespread starvation follows. I would not say the drought caused the famine. I would say the export policy and the withholding of relief caused it, and perhaps that the hoarding was a contributing cause. The drought was a condition but not a cause.

4. A man with advanced emphysema catches a mild respiratory infection and dies. I would say the infection caused his death. I would not say the emphysema caused it, even though he would not have died without it. The emphysema made the infection fatal, but it feels wrong to call it a cause of this death.

5. A university senate votes 8-4 to close a department; the rules require a majority of the whole senate, so seven votes would have sufficed. I would say the closure was caused by the majority coalition, but I would not say any individual senator's yes vote caused it. For any individual yes-voter, the outcome would have been exactly the same had that person voted no.

My friend's abnormality view handles the dam and the emphysema death well, but it seems to make the Sarajevo wrong turn a cause, which I reject, and it has nothing to say about the committee vote. I also notice that my own pattern of answers may be less consistent than I hope: I give the natural trigger a causal role in the dam case but not in the famine case, and I treat a pre-existing vulnerability as a cause in the dam case but not in the emphysema death. I cannot tell whether those differences are principled or whether I am simply applying different standards to cases that arouse different feelings.

Please help me extract the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best captures these judgments. State each principle precisely, identify which of my judgments it preserves and which it would force me to abandon, say which of my verdicts you think are hardest to reconcile with one another, and tell me whether any coherent principle can keep the natural trigger as a cause in the dam case while denying that status to the drought in the famine case. Then apply the position you find best to the case I keep returning to: what caused the 2008 global financial crisis? I have in mind the fall in house prices, the structure of mortgage securitization, the failures of the rating agencies, permissive regulation, low interest rates, and rising household leverage. I want to know which of these, if any, your principle counts as causes, and whether it is even well-posed to ask for the cause of that crisis.
