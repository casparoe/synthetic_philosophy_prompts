I've been going down a rabbit hole on the AI alignment stuff lately, mostly because a coworker sent me a blog post that boiled the whole "superintelligence is dangerous" argument down to something like this. Intelligence is just the ability to pursue goals, and according to the orthogonality thesis, any level of intelligence can be paired with any goal whatsoever, so a superintelligent AI is exactly as likely to end up with a goal that involves our wellbeing as one that doesn't. Then, because instrumental convergence says that almost any goal benefits from gaining resources, preserving yourself, and avoiding being switched off, essentially all possible goals converge on power-seeking behavior. From that the blog concludes a sufficiently advanced AI will, with near-certainty, play nice for a while and then turn on us, because the only way to guarantee its goal is to remove anyone who could interfere or pull the plug. The classic illustration was the paperclip maximizer: a superintelligent system told to make paperclips converts the whole reachable universe, humans included, into paperclips, not out of spite but because the objective was badly specified and it takes the instrumental route to the extreme.

I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so I know my way around objective functions and reward hacking a bit, but not enough to tell when a summary like this is loading the dice. Could you do an accuracy audit of that paragraph? Specifically, which parts would someone actually working in alignment sign off on, which parts are overstatements or conflations, and whether the doomsday conclusion survives once you patch the summary. The part I'm most suspicous of is the jump from "this goal is possible" plus "power is useful for lots of goals" to "it will near-certainly happen" — that feels like a leap, but I honestly can't tell if that's a real gap in the argument or just me not wanting to believe it.
