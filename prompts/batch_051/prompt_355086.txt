I spent most of my career as a systematist, sorting organisms into taxa and arguing about where the boundaries of a species or a population should fall. Since retiring I have been reading the philosophy of the social sciences, and I keep returning to a question that strikes me as taxonomy in different clothing: are the generations — the Silent Generation, the Baby Boomers, Generation X, the Millennials, Generation Z, and whatever is now being named after them — real kinds, and if so, real in what sense?

I am putting together a working document for a small reading group of scientists and social scientists. The piece I have in mind is a decision tree that sorts the positions on this question. The tree should be built from the logical space rather than from a list of received names: begin with the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then place the actual positions in that space. Each internal node should be a question a person could answer without knowing any philosophical vocabulary — a concrete case or claim, not an invitation to declare allegiance to an “-ism.” Each leaf should carry the name of the position, or “unnamed” if nobody defends it, followed by one sentence on the position's most awkward commitment.

Because I came to this from systematics, I want a particular set of distinctions to do the real work. The tree should separate at least these four positions, plus the skeptic's answer that apparent generational differences are mostly age, period, or life-stage effects: (a) generations as natural kinds whose members share intrinsic properties that license induction; (b) generations as real but historical or population-like kinds, with fuzzy boundaries and relational properties, the way a biological population is real without any member being essential to it; (c) generations as social or performative kinds, made real partly because people classify themselves and others under the labels and alter their behavior in response; and (d) generations as merely nominal or conventional bins, cut for marketing, administration, or convenience. Wherever a concrete case is needed, use one of these three: the Silent Generation, the Millennials, and Generation Z. I choose them because their canonical defining experiences differ in how rupture-like they are, and I want to see whether the tree tracks the underlying ontology or merely the vividness of the story told about a cohort.

I would like between eight and twelve leaves, with no more than four internal questions on any path from root to leaf, so that it stays usable in a seminar. Where a combination of answers is logically possible but unoccupied, say so and diagnose briefly whether it is empty because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. If two named positions cannot be separated by the questions you have chosen, flag that rather than papering it over, and either add a further question that does separate them or say honestly that the tree cannot do it at this size.

Do not give me a menu. Commit to one definite tree, with definite questions and a definite placement of every position among the leaves, and give the reason for each choice: why this question rather than a nearby one, why the split falls here, why each position lands where it does. Where you are least confident, say what the weakest load-bearing joint is and what would change it.
