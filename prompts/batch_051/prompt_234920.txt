I am on my school's debate team, and we are researching a case about whether citizens have a genuine moral duty to vote, as opposed to a merely civic one. One teammate offered an argument that initially seemed to concern only voting, but on reflection appears to prove far too much: it would dissolve nearly every ordinary duty to contribute to large collective projects, including duties we are not willing to surrender. We cannot find the error ourselves.

Her argument runs as follows:

(1) An act is morally required of me only if my doing it would make a morally relevant difference to how things go.

(2) In a large election, my single vote does not change who wins, and a one-vote change in the margin of victory carries no further morally relevant effect.

(3) So my voting makes no morally relevant difference.

(4) Therefore voting is not morally required.

(5) The same test applies to a single household's sorting of its recyclables, one fewer short car trip taken to reduce emissions, or one small donation added to a large relief fund: no such act by itself changes the relevant aggregate result.

(6) Therefore, by (1), none of these acts is morally required either. The argument seems to leave no duty to vote against even a seriously dangerous candidate, and no individual duty to reduce emissions or relieve suffering.

We most want to preserve the ordinary view that at least some of these small contributions are required, especially the duty to vote against a candidate whose platform would harm vulnerable people. We are willing to give up one of the abstract premises rather than accept step (6), but every time we try to identify the wrong step, we find ourselves quietly assuming the answer.

Please diagnose the argument. Tell us which premise or step should be rejected, why that step looked so obviously right, and what the main ways of blocking the argument are — for instance, shifting from an actual-difference test to an expected-difference test, moving from individual acts to groups whose joint action makes the difference, or treating voting as an expressive or role-based duty rather than a consequence-based one. For each option, say what we would then have to give up that we might have wanted to keep. Keep your answer to no more than 600 words.
