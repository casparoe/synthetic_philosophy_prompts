In my work I keep running into a tension I can't articulate. Before my team or a standards body will bless a pseudorandom number generator, we run its output through a battery of statistical tests — looking for runs, correlations, and other patterns presumed to indicate non-randomness. If the finite outputs we inspect pass the tests in the battery, we pronounce the generator good and ship it. But the generator is a deterministic algorithm whose entire output is fixed by its seed, and the battery checks only a finite list of patterns out of infinitely many possible ones; nothing stops a generator from passing the battery while remaining predictable in a way no test in the battery happens to look for. At the same time, for cryptographic keys we do not trust this procedure at all: there we demand an argument that no computationally bounded adversary can predict the next bit from the previous bits, even knowing the algorithm. The two criteria feel aimed at different things, and I cannot state precisely what either asserts about the output itself. What theory of randomness does this two-track practice presuppose? Does it treat randomness as a property a finite output sequence can have, as a property of the generating process, or as something relative to the tests and purposes at hand? Reconstruct the implicit notion or notions at work, say which commitments the practice strictly requires and which it merely fits well with, whether the two tracks are consistent, and how the certification practice would have to change if we took seriously the view that randomness is incompressibility — roughly, that a string is random to the extent that no program much shorter than the string can generate it — and that the relevant quantity is not computable exactly, so no finite battery of tests can fully certify a generator.
