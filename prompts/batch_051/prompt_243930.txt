I'm a novelist, and for my current project I need to get straight what counts as the same myth. My narrator is a folklorist, and I keep putting words in her mouth that I'm not sure survive scrutiny. Here's the working criteria I've given her for two tellings being variants of the same myth rather than two myths that happen to share themes:

1. They share a plot kernel: at least three of five episodes in roughly the same order—separation from kin, forbidden act, encounter with a dangerous helper, loss of name, transformation.
2. The prohibition itself stays the same (a food taboo, say), and its violation produces the same kind of loss, even if the details shift.
3. The ending serves the same explanatory function: it accounts for the same type of thing (an animal's markings, a landform, a seasonal ritual).
4. The protagonist keeps the same structural role (youngest daughter, fatherless son, outsider-bride).
5. The communities that tell them treat them as versions of one story.

Now the case. In the novel, a travelling bard brings back a telling from the upriver villages. In it the protagonist is an old man, not a youngest daughter. The forbidden act is exactly the same—eating the winter fish before the ice breaks—and the punishment works by the same mechanism, because he is unmade in stages: first his name, then his shadow, then his body. But the ending lands differently. Instead of becoming a stone in the river so the river can pass words between the living and the dead, he becomes a piece of driftwood that wanders and teaches a lesson about restlessness. The upriver people still perform it at the same thaw ritual and insist it's the same story. My narrator says it clearly is. Her rival says its a separate myth that borrows the taboo and should be filed under its own name.

Does the old-man version qualify as a variant of the same myth under my criteria? Work through the criteria one by one, flag the places where they underdetermine the answer, and tell me whether this case shows one of the criteria should be dropped or modified—or whether the real problem is that there's no sharp thing called the same myth in the first place, let alone a canonical version to measure it against.
