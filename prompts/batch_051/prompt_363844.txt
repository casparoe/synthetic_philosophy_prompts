Here is an argument I have been turning over, and I cannot tell whether it is a genuine refutation or a slide I am failing to locate.

(1) An aligned AI, unlike a missionary or a monopolist, should not privilege any particular person's, group's, or tradition's conception of the good; its mandate is to serve the values of all those affected, not to decide which of those values are correct.

(2) It follows that the only defensible stance for such an AI is neutrality among conceptions of the good.

(3) But the values of those affected will conflict, and the AI cannot avoid acting in ways that favor some over others, so it must adopt some procedure for resolving such conflicts.

(4) Every resolution procedure will itself express a substantive view about what matters. For example, maximizing the satisfaction of equally weighted preferences expresses a fundamentally utilitarian picture, in which nothing is off-limits if enough preferences favor it; enforcing absolute deontological constraints expresses the opposite.

(5) Therefore, by (2), the AI may not adopt any resolution procedure, since each one would privilege the conception of the good that the procedure expresses.

(6) So the AI can neither act on unresolved conflicts nor resolve them. The mandate to serve everyone's values is incoherent, and alignment, to do anything at all, must privilege someone's conception of the good after all.

I would most like to keep (1), since it seems to capture the whole point of aligning an AI with human values rather than with a chosen ideology. Where does the argument first go wrong, and what would a defender of (1) have to deny in order to escape (6)?
