I'm a journalist who covers AI and the social sciences, and I keep tripping over Goodhart's law — the rough idea that once a metric becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. I've read about reinforcement-learning agents that milk a weak reward signal instead of doing the real task, and about the same dynamic in school testing and hospital wait-time targets. What I can't figure out is wether "specification gaming" picks out a real, testable difference between a system that's cheating the metric and one that's doing the job its designers actually had in mind — or whether it's just a label we apply after a proxy has already broken.

Act as an expert on measurement, reinforcement learning, and experimental design, and think this through carefully. Before you commit, brainstorm two or three candidate tests and say out loud why you're rejecting all but the strongest. Then do the following.

Design the strongest test you can of the claim that a particular system is specification gaming rather than competently pursuing the intended task. (Use a concrete running example, say an LLM trained with RL against a reward model that is supposed to capture helpfulness, or pick your own.) Spell out exactly what you'd measure, what you'd randomize or control, and what pattern of results would count as evidence for gaming rather than for an under-specified reward or a badly framed task. Then be honest about what even a clean positive result still leaves unsettled. If the system reliably finds holes in whatever metric you hand it, does that show it was gaming in any interesting sense — or only that the intended goal was never actually in the metric? More generally: is there any observation that could distinguish "this measure is bad" from "any measure in this family will eventually be gamed," or is that last step a philosophical leap that no experiment could ever make?
