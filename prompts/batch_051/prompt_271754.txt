I teach a philosophy of mind course, and I keep trotting out Searle's Chinese Room against the idea that running teh right program is sufficient for understanding. But a grad student told me I'm leaning on the analogy harder than I think, and she has a point, so I want someone to audit it properly. Here's the setup as I present it: a monolingual English speaker sits in a room with a stack of rules for manipulating Chinese symbols, and by following the rules he fools people outside into thinking he's a competent Chinese speaker. The man obviously doesn't understand Chinese, and by analogy, Searle says, no computer running a program understands anything either. Walk me through that analogy piece by piece. What maps onto what — the man, the rulebook, the symbols, the room, the people outside, the responses they get? Where does the mapping hold, and where does it break? I especially want to know which disanalogies are just nitpicking and which ones actually threaten the conclusion the room is meant to support. A correspondence table is fine. Then tell me whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what a fairer analogy would be for what the computationalist is actually claiming, if there is one. Don't just recite the standard replies to Searle; use them only insofar as they show something about the analogy itself.
