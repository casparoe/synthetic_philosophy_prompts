So I'm in a philosophy of mind and cognitive science class and we've spent the last few weeks on the extended mind thesis. I have to write a term paper (4000 words) and I really dont want it to be yet another summary of Clark and Chalmers. I've been going back and forth between a few candidate angles and I need to commit to one. Here they are:

1. Whether the whole debate is downstream of fixing the mark of the cognitive. The parity principle seems to just assume we already know what counts as cognitive — if something outside the skull does what we'd call cognitive inside, call it cognitive. The coupling-constitution objection kind of assumes the opposite without defending a criterion either. So maybe the live question is whether we should stop treating cognitive as a natural-kind-ish term at all, and whether the extended mind debate survives if we go deflationary about that.

2. The parity versus complementarity split. The second-wave people say extension isn't really about functional parity, it's about how inner and outer resources integrate and complement each other. Is that a real disagreement with the original thesis or just a friendlier reframing? And if the second-wave version is right, has the original claim quietly turned into something weaker or just less interesting?

3. The glue question. What has to hold between a person and an external resource for it to count as cognitive rather than merely useful: reliability, automaticity, trust, accessibility, how failures get handled? Nobody seems to have a principled line here, and it feels upstream of all the notebook and smartphone examples.

4. The normative and legal implications. Suppose something like the thesis is true — does it actually matter? Should privacy law treat your phone's notes as part of your memory? Should education or dementia care change? I can't tell whether this is the most important line or a red herring because the thesis itself is too amorphous to generate policy.

5. The predictive processing angle. There's a live dispute about whether predictive processing supports extension or actually privileges the brain boundary as an inference boundary. It could give the extended mind view a naturalistic backbone or undercut it, and I'm not sure which case is stronger.

I want to pick one of these for the paper, maybe with a second as a background thread. Rank them for someone with a month, no empirical training, and a page limit. For each one say how upstream it is relative to the others, whether it's tractable with just conceptual argument and the existing literature, and which one you'd deprioritize even if it's philosophically interesting. Defend the ordering rather than just listing pros and cons.
