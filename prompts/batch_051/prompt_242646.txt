Bit of background on me: I'm a retired chemist — forty years in the lab — and I've only recently started reading philosophy, so please don't drown me in jargon. I'm on the board of a small conservation trust, and we're trying to decide whether to release a new biological control (a weevil) against an invasive weed on one of our reserves. If it works, it saves a rare orchid. If it goes wrong, the weevil could jump to native plants and do real damage. The trouble is we have no base rates for this weevil in our climate: it's never been trialled here, the literature is thin, and the experts we asked wont put numbers on it.

One board member says the only non-arbitrary thing is to list the main scenarios and give each an equal probability, then go with the option that does best on average. Another says no, we should prepare for the worst and pick the option whose worst outcome is least bad. A third says we should pick the option we'd regret least once we find out which scenario was true. And I've noticed something odd: the third rule seems to change its answer depending on how finely we slice the possibilities — split 'it fails' into two different failure modes and the least-regretted option shifts. That feels wrong for a supposedly rational rule, and I suspect the equal-probability rule has the same disease.

I don't want a survey of these rules. I want you to commit: which of them (if any) should govern a one-off decision like this, and why? In your reply, give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. And write in British English, please.
