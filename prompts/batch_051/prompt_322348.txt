I work at a mid-size SaaS company, and we're about to pilot a new way of writing design docs. I'd like you to actually work through how it would go in practice, not just whether it sounds like a good idea.

Right now each project has one owner and a single canonical doc. Our CTO keeps saying this is a "Cartesian theater" — a made-up central place where the design pretends to be finished and where the "real thinking" supposedly happens, when actually the thinking is scattered across comments, side conversations, rejected alternatives, and half-remembered Slack threads. So he's pushing what he calls a "multiple drafts" process, borrowed loosely from Dennett. The pilot works like this:

- Once a project is greenlit, there is no canonical doc and no owner.
- Anyone on the team can create or fork a draft of any section. Every draft is versioned and publicly visible.
- You don't comment on a doc; you edit a copy. All edits across all drafts show up in a live global diff feed.
- Nobody has approval power before the "release compile." There are no meetings to reconcile drafts.
- At the deadline, a tool builds the release doc by taking, section by section, the draft version with the most cumulative edit-minutes (we log these), with recent edits weighted more heavily than old ones.
- Then a rotating "compiler" — deliberately not the same person twice in a row — gets 48 hours to fix contradictions. They can delete material, choose between existing alternatives, and add short connective sentences, but they cannot introduce new substantive content.

So the idea is that the design, like a stream of consciousness, is never anywhere all at once until the moment someone probes it by compiling it.

How would this actually play out? In particular: where does power quietly move to, what kind of documents would this reward, and how would people game the edit-minutes metric? Does the final human pass reintroduce exactly the theater we're trying to get rid of? And where does the analogy with Dennett's model hold up or break down — isn't a design doc more teleological than consciousness, and doesn't that matter? Be as concrete as you can about the incentives and second-order effects.
