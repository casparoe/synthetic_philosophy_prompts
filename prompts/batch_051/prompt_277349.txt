I recently read a popular science article that summarized chaos theory roughly as follows. Classical mechanics is deterministic, but chaos theory showed that determinism does not buy you predictability: in a chaotic system, two trajectories that start arbitrarily close together separate at an exponential rate, so any measurement error, however small, is amplified until a detailed forecast becomes useless. That, the article said, is the real content of the butterfly effect—the flapping of a butterfly's wings in Brazil can, through this amplification, set off a tornado in Texas weeks later. The geometry underneath all this, the article continued, is the strange attractor, a fractal object of non-integer dimension on which a chaotic system's long-term motion settles, and it is this fractal structure that makes the system's behavior genuinely random. The article concluded that chaos theory therefore shows that determinism is compatible with randomness and that long-range prediction of a chaotic system is impossible in principle.

The early part struck me as plausible, but I grew suspicious of the last few steps, though I cannot quite locate where the reasoning goes wrong. Is this a fair summary of what chaos theory actually shows? Please go through it roughly claim by claim and separate what a dynamical-systems theorist would accept, what they would qualify, and what they would reject; in particular, address whether the conclusion about randomness and the impossibility of prediction in principle follows from anything true in the summary.
