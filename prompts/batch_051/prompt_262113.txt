For a novel I am writing about an emptying house, I have been thinking about how space is described, and I keep meeting the same picture in two different places. In craft discussions among novelists, the standard advice is to treat a scene's space as a container: the room is a vessel, its objects are contents, and the space would remain if those objects were moved or removed. In the philosophy of physics, the same picture appears as the substantivalist reading of Newton's bucket. Substantivalists take spacetime to be a substance that exists independently of the matter in it, and they take the bucket experiment to show that there must be a background 'container' against which true rotation and acceleration are defined. Relationists reply that all we ever observe are relations among bodies, and they explain the concave surface of the water by rotation relative to distant matter rather than to any background space.

I would like an audit of the 'space is a container' analogy as it operates in that debate. First give the correspondence table for Newton's bucket case: what maps to the vessel, the contents, the stirring or spinning, and the observable effect; and be explicit about what is being claimed when space is called a container. Then identify the disanalogies. Some will matter for what the analogy is being used to prove and some will not—a literal container has walls and a floor and can survive empty, whereas space has none of those features, but I want to know which of those differences actually damage the argument and which are incidental. Say what the analogy quietly imports that space may lack: a fixed set of locations, identity of a place over time, a privileged rest frame, the possibility of a truly empty region, and whatever else you find. Then address whether the conclusion licensed by the analogy—that the bucket shows we need absolute or substantival space after all—survives once the disanalogies are laid out. In particular, consider whether the bucket argument needs the container analogy at all, or whether it only needs to show that relations between the water and the bucket are insufficient to explain the concave surface. If the analogy is doing more persuasive than logical work, say what a better analogy would be for what the experiment actually establishes, if it establishes anything.
