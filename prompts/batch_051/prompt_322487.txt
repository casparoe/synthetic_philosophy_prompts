Here are my verdicts on several claims that invoke Gödel's incompleteness theorems outside mathematics. I should like to know what principle, if any, captures them. Please state the principle precisely, say which clause is motivated by which case, and say what it predicts about cases I have not settled. If no single principle captures all of my verdicts, tell me which verdicts have to go and why.

1. A legal philosopher maintains that no constitutional order can be both consistent and complete, because every sufficiently strong consistent formal system is incomplete, and a constitution with amendment and jurisdiction rules is, in effect, a formal system. I find this unconvincing: a constitution is not a recursively specified deductive system, and legal completeness is not closure under proof. This seems to me an equivocation on 'complete'.

2. A cognitive scientist claims that Gödel shows no fixed, rule-following machine can match a human mathematician. He argues that, for any specified consistent machine that proves arithmetic, we can construct a sentence it cannot decide, but we can see that the sentence is true. I reject his conclusion, because the argument moves from 'for any specified machine there is some sentence that machine cannot decide' to 'our mathematical abilities cannot be captured by any specified machine'. The human 'we' in the argument is not itself specified, and the step in which we accept the Gödel sentence as true depends on accepting the machine's consistency, which is not itself an output of the machine. Still, I think the argument successfully establishes something narrower: no fixed, specified, consistent procedure is complete for arithmetic.

3. A technology company announces that its proof assistant, running a precisely specified decision procedure, is guaranteed to settle every arithmetical statement. I think Gödel's theorem straightforwardly refutes this, unless the system is inconsistent or 'settle' means something weaker than proof. This is, for me, a clean case.

4. A political scientist argues that a committee governed by a finite written rulebook will always face cases its rules cannot decide, by analogy with Gödel, provided its procedures can count votes and calculate deadlines and budgets. I am torn. On the one hand, a committee is not a formal system, and its rules are applied with judgement rather than deductive closure. On the other, if the rulebook really did mechanise both the arithmetic of its proceedings and the application of its own standards, I am not sure why incompleteness would not arise. I lean towards rejecting the argument, but I cannot locate the line.

5. A philosopher of religion claims that Gödel refutes the ideal of a complete systematic theology, because a theological system rich enough to state truths about numbers will contain undecidable propositions. I think this is misguided: being able to mention numbers is not the same as having enough arithmetic among its theorems, and a theology is not an axiomatised formal system unless someone makes it one.

6. A logician cautions that none of this establishes any limit on artificial mathematicians, because a machine can extend its own axioms indefinitely in a Gödelian progression. I think the caution is right as far as it goes, but the claim is too strong as stated: it shows the interesting comparison is not with a fixed formal system, but it does not guarantee that any such progression captures exactly human mathematical knowledge.

I have been toying with this principle: a Gödelian limit may legitimately be attributed to a practice, procedure, or institution only when that thing (i) embodies a formal system whose theorems are generated from finitely many axioms by mechanical rules, (ii) is being treated as closed under deductive consequence, and (iii) proves, rather than merely mentions, enough arithmetic to encode the Gödel sentence. But I can already see that this principle may be too restrictive, too permissive, or both. Does it deliver my verdicts above? If not, how should it be repaired, and which of my verdicts would have to be revised?

Please also say what the repaired principle predicts about these further cases, which I have not yet made up my mind about:

(a) An ethics agency issues a finite, precise 'complete safety code' for autonomous systems, where the code contains rules for aggregating numerical risk scores but is not presented as a proof system. Does Gödel give any reason to expect a case the code cannot classify?

(b) A professional body adopts a rule that its disciplinary committee must decide every complaint by applying a published matrix of offences, with points assigned for aggravating and mitigating factors and thresholds computed by addition. Does Gödel imply that some possible complaint will fall outside the matrix?

(c) A philosopher proposes to replace an open-ended, revisable body of scientific theory with a single axiomatised theory of everything in which all laws of nature are deductive consequences. Should Gödel make us pessimistic about this proposal, and if so, precisely which feature does the pessimism depend on?

I am less interested in being told that Gödel applies only to mathematics than in being given a principle with enough structure to sort these cases.
