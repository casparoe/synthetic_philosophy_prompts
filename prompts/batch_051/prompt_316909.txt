I'm on the board of a small annual prize for verse translation, and this year the two finalists translated the same short poem, so we can't just give one prize to each and move on. The original (you don't need to know the language) is four lines, each with four strong beats, rhyming ABAB, and it's built almost entirely around a single repeated word — call it "still" — that hovers between "calm/unmoving," "nevertheless," and "even now." A rough gloss of the poem would run: still water, still the mill, still the bell that called the miners up from underground; still here, still here; and then farewell.

The two finalists:

A keeps a strict four-beat line and a neat ABAB rhyme, but it has to spell out the different senses and add connective tissue:
The water's calm, the mill is stilled;
the bell that called the miners out
is silent now — and yet we've filled
our ears with its remembered shout.

B keeps every one of the five repetitions of still and the bare farewell, but gives up on meter and rhyme:
Still water. Still the mill.
Still the bell that called the miners up from underground.
Still here, still here.
And then farewell.

The prize's charter says the award goes to "the translation that best carries the poem over as a poem," which is doing a lot of work. The board is genuinely split, and its fallen to me to draft the decision memo. Splitting the prize isn't allowed, so it's A or B. Draft the memo: a clear recommendation up top, the reasoning, and an honest treatment of the strongest reason to pick the other one.
