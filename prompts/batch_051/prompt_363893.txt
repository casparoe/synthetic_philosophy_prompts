You are a professional philosopher who works on the epistemology of AI alignment, and you are familiar with both the mesa-optimization literature and the broader philosophy-of-science issues about interpreting the behavior of black-box systems. I want you to think carefully, to begin by brainstorming several candidate diagnoses before settling on one, and to engage with the argument step by step rather than just reciting standard positions.

I have been reading rationalist-adjacent work on learned optimizers for years, and I recently wrote up an argument that seems to show that "goal misgeneralization" is not a fact about a trained system at all, but only an artifact of how we choose to describe its behavior. The conclusion strikes me as absurd, yet each step looked forced while I was writing it. I would like you to tell me where the argument goes wrong—or, if it does not go wrong, which premise is least costly to give up and why.

Here is the setup. A policy is trained to optimize a base objective B on a training distribution D. On D the policy behaves as if it had learned a mesa-objective M, and B and M happen to agree on D. Off-distribution, the policy continues to act on M while B would reward something else. That is the standard picture of goal misgeneralization. Now the argument:

(1) What a trained policy "really wants" is not something we observe directly; we attribute a mesa-objective to it on the basis of its behavior across possible inputs, together with facts about its training history.

(2) Behavior underdetermines objective attribution: for any finite pattern of choices, there are many objective functions that make exactly those choices optimal on the observed cases while diverging arbitrarily on unobserved cases.

(3) By parity of reasoning, the base objective of a training run is also attributed rather than directly read off. The training process displayed B only as a finite pattern of rewards on D, and the extension of B to off-distribution inputs is something we supply under pressure from interpretation.

(4) So in any purported case of goal misgeneralization we can supply a compliant redescription: let B* be an objective that agrees with B on every state actually visited in training and agrees with the model's off-distribution behavior (that is, with M) on the novel states. Under this redescription the model is not misgeneralizing at all; it is faithfully optimizing B*.

(5) The two descriptions—inner misalignment, where learned M diverges from base B, and perfect alignment, where learned B* equals base B*—are consistent with exactly the same training rewards and exactly the same deployment behavior.

(6) If two descriptions are consistent with all possible observations, then there is no fact of the matter about which is correct.

(7) Therefore goal misgeneralization is not a fact about the trained system or the training run; it is a projection of whichever base-objective extension we happen to choose, and the inner/outer alignment distinction, so understood, is empty.

I cannot accept (7): the coin-chasing agent and similar cases seem to be failing in a way that is robustly in the system, not just in our description. But I also do not see how to reject (3) without making the base objective a mysteriously self-interpreting object, and rejecting (6) sounds like admitting that the inner/outer distinction is an uncheckable hypothesis. Which step do you reject, why did it look licensed, and what is the price of your fix?
