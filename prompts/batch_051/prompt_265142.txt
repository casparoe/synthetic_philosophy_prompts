I'm an applied mathematician, and I've noticed I run an unofficial policy: I only fully trust a mathematical object if there's a finite, explicit recipe for producing it. A constructible regular polygon, like the 17-gon, counts. An algebraic curve given by a polynomial equation counts, because I can grind out its points to any accuracy from a finite rule. But an object whose existence is guaranteed only by a nonconstructive theorem — a fixed point I can't display, a weird function delivered by the axiom of choice, a root promised by the intermediate value theorem with no closed form — I treat as a useful fiction rather than a real object. Same for things I only know numerically: a PDE solution I've approximated to 15 decimal places but can't actually write down.

I know this roughly echoes the old fight between Descartes (algebraic curves in, mechanical and transcendental curves out) and Leibniz (let in the cycloid, the catenary, the tractrix if they're generated by simple motions). The problem is I find myself siding with Descartes almost every time, even though the catenary is literally the shape of a hanging chain. A colleague says I'm just smuggling 17th-century aesthetics into ontology and that modern mathematics correctly admits whatever the definitions license. Whos actually right here? Is my policy defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as a rule?
