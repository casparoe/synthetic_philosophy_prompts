I am a novelist — three books published, a fourth in revisions — and I keep running into a question that my working life ought to equip me to answer but somehow does not: what, if anything, does a writer's intention have to do with what a text means? In my own practice the relation is unstable. I begin a novel with an intention I could state in a paragraph; two years later the finished book means something that paragraph never named. I cut a scene because its effect contradicts my intention, only to have a reviewer praise that same scene for a meaning I never put there — and I cannot honestly say the reviewer is wrong. When an interviewer asks what I meant by a chapter, I answer with a story about drafting and revision, not with anything a philosopher would recognize as a theory of meaning. I know, in a blurred way, that literary theory fought exactly this battle in the twentieth century: the New Critics' charge of the intentional fallacy, E. D. Hirsch's defense of the author's determinate meaning, Barthes's claim that the author's death is the birth of the reader, and more recent positions such as hypothetical intentionalism, on which the right interpretation is the best hypothesis about what an ideally informed reader would take the author to have intended.

Act as a professional philosopher of literature who can work as a careful translator between disciplines, and restate the main positions in the authorial-intention debate using the working vocabulary of a novelist rather than the vocabulary of academic hermeneutics. The vocabulary I actually live in includes: the stated intention before drafting; what the prose discovers during revision; the gap between what I planned and what the text actually does; cutting a passage one loves because it belongs to a different book; the implied author a reader assembles from the sentences; point of view and the unreliable narrator; the workshop response that ignores one's intention and addresses only the text; the editorial note; the published book as an artifact that has left the desk. I am fully fluent in that language and only distantly acquainted with the theoretical positions. Begin by laying out the main positions and the craft-cognate you will map each onto, then translate them one by one, as faithfully as possible.

Finally, give me the residue report. For each position, tell me what translates cleanly, what must be bent to fit, and what has no counterpart at all in a novelist's vocabulary. Then the question I actually care about: does the residue reveal something the philosophical debate is blind to — something a working novelist knows from the inside that the theories cannot see — or does it reveal the reverse, that my craft vocabulary quietly presupposes an account of intention and meaning I have never had to defend? I suspect the most instructive position will be the one whose translation is least flattering to it, and I want you to be precise about that rather than rewarding whichever position flatters a writer's self-image. Think through each translation before judging it, and flag every point where you had to fudge.
