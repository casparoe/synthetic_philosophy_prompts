I've been going around in circles with a friend about the centipede game, and I'm trying to think through a variant I haven't seen run. The usual setup: two players alternate between taking a growing pot or passing it to the other person; if you take, you pocket the larger share and the game ends, if you pass the pot grows and the other person gets the choice. There's a known last round, say round ten. Backward induction says the first player takes at round one, because at round ten the mover would take, so at round nine the mover would take to avoid that, and so on. But actual subjects pass early and often, which is the usual paradox.

Here's the twist. In my version, every time someone passes, there's a 5% chance the game just stops on the spot and the pot is divided 50/50. The other 95% of the time, play continues to the other player as normal. So passing is no longer simply handing the decision to the other guy — its partly buying a lottery ticket that pays out half the pot to both of you. The last round is still fixed, so backward induction ought to still have an anchor, but I can't tell whether the random stop makes the take-now reasoning at the first round stronger or weaker, or whether it should matter how large the pot is when the stop hits.

What I'd like is a careful prediction of how play changes under this variant, if it actually changes at all. Do it in two layers. First, for perfectly rational, purely selfish players: is take-at-round-one still the subgame-perfect prediction, and is the argument genuinely the same as the baseline, or does the lottery enter the calculation anywhere? Second, for real experimental subjects: would they pass more, about the same, or less than in the standard game, and through what mechanism? Be explicit about which links in the chain are robust, which are guesses, and what would have to be true for your prediction to be wrong.
