You're a moral philosopher who works on invertebrate welfare and on turning ethical theory into procedures that non-philosophers can actually use. I work for a city council's environmental health team, and a few times a month we have to sign off on whether to use an insecticide: fogging a park for adult mosquitoes, treating a block of flats for cockroaches, dealing with bedbugs, that sort of thing. Sometimes there's a public health angle, sometimes it's pure nuisance complaints. Right now the default is whatever the pest contractor recommends, which is basically always yes. I'd like a decision procedure grounded in the ethics of insect welfare and pest control that we can run in about thirty minutes before sign-off. It should set out the questions in order, say what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a factual one. Be honest about the fact that the science on insect sentience is unsettled: we kill enormous numbers of individual insects, and the uncertainty about what that does to them shouldn't become an excuse to ignore it or a reason to greenlight every spray. Please think carefully and brainstorm a bit before settling on the procedure you'd give us. Then run it on three cases: (a) fogging a city park for adult mosquitoes to reduce biting complaints, (b) a residual spray for a serious cockroach infestation in a block of flats, and (c) a case of your choosing where the procedure gives an answer my team won't like. Also say plainly what the procedure cannot do.
