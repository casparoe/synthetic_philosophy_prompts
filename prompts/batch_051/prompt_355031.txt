I have been returning to a dispute with colleagues that I would like to see turned into a question about what evidence could possibly settle. Consider two patients in an under-resourced clinic with one dose of an effective analgesic left, where the triage decision seems to require saying whether Amara's pain is genuinely worse than Ben's. We can ask each of them for a numerical rating, observe how much each would trade or pay to avoid the pain, or image both of their brains; we still seem to be choosing a scale rather than reading off a fact. The economists I know insist that interpersonal utility comparisons are meaningless; the clinicians behave as though some comparisons, such as a kidney stone against a stubbed toe, are plainly true; I suspect the two groups are partly talking past each other by confusing a measurement problem with a normative one.

The precise claim I want tested is this: for at least some pairs of people and felt states, there is a fact of the matter about whose state is more intense, and it is in principle possible to build instruments that track that fact rather than merely stipulate a convention for comparing people. Please design a concrete empirical research program aimed at that claim. What exactly would we measure—self-report, choice behavior, willingness to pay, just-noticeable differences, cross-modal psychophysical matching, neural signatures of pain or affect, or something else—and what pattern of results would count in favour of a determinate-fact reading as against a merely-conventional-calibration reading? Then make the residue explicit: even after an unambiguous positive result, which part of the question would no test have settled because it is a choice about a zero point, a shared unit, or how heavily one person's interests may weigh against another's? I am less interested in a verdict than in a precise map of where measuring can end and stipulating must begin.
