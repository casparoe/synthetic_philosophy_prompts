I keep noticing a structural parallel between a puzzle in probability and a demand that shows up in the natural sciences, and I want to know whether the parallel is deep or superficial.

On the probability side, Bertrand's paradox asks for the probability that a random chord of a circle is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle. Depending on how one parameterizes the space of chords—by the angular position of the endpoints on the circumference, by the radial distance of the chord's midpoint, by the angle between the chord and a radius, and so on—the principle of indifference gives 1/2, 1/3, or 1/4. The same symmetric ignorance, expressed in different coordinates, yields incompatible numbers.

On the science side, a well-functioning theory is often required to be invariant under changes in representation or coordinate system: the same physical situation described in different coordinate charts should not yield different predictions. Quantities that change under reparameterization while the underlying state does not are usually treated as artifacts of the description, not features of the world.

The parallel is that the principle of indifference seems to violate exactly this invariance constraint. It treats equal probability over the cells of a partition as the natural expression of ignorance, but the partition depends on the parameterization. A measure over possibilities that changes with the coordinate system is behaving more like a gauge artifact than like a degree of belief.

I suspect the parallel is more than verbal, because in both cases the trouble comes from the absence of a canonical or group-invariant measure. But I can also see a disanalogy: probability is supposed to be an epistemic state, not a physical quantity, so requiring coordinate-invariance may be an imported norm that does not belong. Maybe the correct response to Bertrand is to accept that ambiguous evidence does not pick out a unique prior at all, whereas in physics the aim is to factor out the representation.
