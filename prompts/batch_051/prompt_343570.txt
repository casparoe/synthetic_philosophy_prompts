In debates about why promises bind, I keep meeting the analogy that making a promise is like voluntarily incurring a debt: the promisor owes the promisee the promised performance in roughly the way a debtor owes a creditor a sum, and breaking the promise is a kind of default. The analogy is usually used to support the conclusion that promising gives the promisee something akin to a right to performance, so that the wrong of breaking a promise is measured mainly by what the promisee is deprived of rather than by the promisor's bad faith. I would like you to audit how far this analogy holds. Lay out the correspondences explicitly (what plays the role of creditor, debtor, sum owed, discharge, default, and so on), then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular, say which features of debt the analogy quietly imports that promising may lack (such as fungibility, discharge through payment of an equivalent, transferability, or a clean separation between the obligation and the person bound), and which disanalogies matter for the conclusion the analogy is being used to support rather than being merely superficial. Finally, say whether that conclusion survives the audit or was being carried largely by the analogy, and whether some other comparison would serve better. Please separate what is settled among philosophers of promising from what is genuinely contested — for example, the roles of expectation, reliance, and the promisor's intentions in grounding the obligation — and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
