I've been arguing with a colleague about procreative beneficence and I want to see where the disagreement actually lives. Take the cleanest case I can make: a couple is already doing IVF with PGT, they have two viable embryos, one carries a fully penetrant single-gene disorder that will cause severe chronic pain through childhood and death by fifteen, the other doesn't, and choosing the other costs them nothing except the choice itself. The affected child's life would still, on balance, be worth living. I put my credence at about 0.8 that they'd be morally required—not just permitted, not just being nice—to transfer the healthy embryo. My colleague is at about 0.3. Give me your own number for that exact claim (where "required" means it would be wrong not to), then decompose it: (i) how much rides on whether "required" versus merely "better" is doing real deontic work; (ii) how much on whether the non-identity problem means the affected possible child is never wronged because it's only alternative was not existing; (iii) how much on whether selecting against a disability expresses disrespect or is the thin end of liberal eugenics, and whether that counts independently of consequences; (iv) how much on whether the disease/enhancement distinction can bear this moral weight, or whether once you accept selection against disease you're pushed toward selecting for non-disease traits too. Then tell me which of those sub-judgments would move your number most, and which one my 0.8 and my colleague's 0.3 most likely diverge on. Don't just tell me it depends on your ethical theory—give me the number you'd actually defend.
