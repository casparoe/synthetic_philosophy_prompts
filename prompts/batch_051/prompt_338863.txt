At my school's ethics club, two members argued about a classmate who, as student council treasurer, refused to bend a funding rule to help a club that had made an innocent mistake on its application, even though the amount was small. Priya said the praise for her "incorruptibility" was misplaced: refusing to make exceptions isn't by itself integrity; it can just be rigidity. An incorruptible person can't be bought, but she still has to judge whether her principles are worth keeping. Tom said that makes incorruptibility too convenient: if you may revise your principles whenever they seem wrong, then "I can't be bought" collapses into "no offer is high enough," and everyone has a price. Priya replied that revising a rule because you've been persuaded it is unjust is different in kind from breaking it because you've been paid, and that Tom was treating every reason for changing one's mind as a bribe. Tom answered that from the inside the two can feel the same—a person being paid can always tell herself the rule was unjust—which is exactly why incorruptibility matters. They asked me to referee and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, identify who is right about each specific point, and say what both are missing about the difference between praising a person's character and praising her particular decisions.
