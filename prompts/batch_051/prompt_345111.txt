I am a novelist, and I am currently drafting a book in which two characters exchange promises under radically one-sided conditions: one party can simply walk away after the other has performed, and both of them know this in advance. While trying to make the scene psychologically and philosophically exact, I keep returning to two cases that lie just beneath my plot, and I would like a rigorous comparison of them rather than a conventional summary. The first is Derek Parfit's hitchhiker, in which a stranded man promises a passing driver a generous reward for a ride out of the desert; the driver declines, since he knows that once the man is safe, paying will no longer serve the man's self-interest, and the man is known to be guided exclusively by self-interest. The hitchhiker would, it seems, be better off if he could make himself disposed to keep promises even when keeping them no longer serves his interests. The second is Gregory Kavka's toxin puzzle, in which an agent is offered a large reward, payable in advance, on the condition that at an earlier specified moment he genuinely intends to drink a mildly unpleasant but harmless toxin the next day; whether he actually drinks does not affect the payment, and he knows he will not drink when the appointed time arrives. Both cases appear to ask whether a person can rationally acquire or maintain a state of mind that would later issue in an action he will then have no reason to perform. Yet they seem to run on different principles: one turns on being believed by another person, the other on a purely private intention; one concerns a durable disposition, the other a dated intention; one makes rational self-modification look like a sensible investment, while the other can make the entire notion look incoherent.

I would like you to take on the role of a professional philosopher working in practical rationality and decision theory, and to treat this as a technical question rather than a request for potted definitions. Before committing to a conclusion, begin by brainstorming the dimensions along which the two cases might differ: what the benefit attaches to, whether the agent can bring about the relevant state of mind by a direct act of will, whether another person's trust and evidence are in play, how much time elapses between intending and acting, and whether the case pressures us to evaluate dispositions rather than isolated acts. Then compare Parfit's hitchhiker with Kavka's toxin puzzle along those dimensions, and give a reasoned verdict on which case is the more illuminating test of the thesis that it can be rational to make oneself irrational — or whether that thesis must be reformulated once the differences are made explicit.

I am less interested in the standard textbook readings than in what the side-by-side comparison reveals, and I would welcome enough precision that I could borrow the distinction as a structural principle in the novel as well as a philosophical one.
