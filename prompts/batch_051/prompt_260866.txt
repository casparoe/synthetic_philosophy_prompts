I have been trying to give a precise account of what happens when a constitutional order runs out of rules. My proposal is a mechanism I call the residual operator. Definition: when the written rules are silent, contradictory, or point to an office or procedure that no longer exists, the last official act performed under a still-intact rule becomes the template for the next official act — not because any norm authorizes this, but because no available rule lets anyone stop it. My central claim is that an interregnum is not a rare interval between two legal orders; it is the permanent, low-grade condition of a constitutional order, visible only when the usually seamless continuation is interrupted by an open dispute. Example: a constitution says the outgoing head of state must swear in the successor, the outgoing head of state dies before the ceremony, and someone else administers the oath; the system proceeds as if that were authorized, though no rule authorized it. I recognize this sounds close to a state of exception, to customary constitutional law, and to de facto authority. I would like an honest assessment. Is the residual operator a real mechanism or just a label for the fact that someone always acts? Is it actually distinct from those neighboring ideas? Give me the three strongest objections, and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable — in particular, can you construct a case where governance during a gap could not be described as the last authorized act continuing under inertia?
