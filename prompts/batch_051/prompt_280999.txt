I've settled, provisionally, on the view that pure time preference is a bug rather than a feature: a given amount of suffering or happiness matters just as much wether it lands tomorrow or a hundred years from now, once you've adjusted for the chance it won't happen at all and the fact that future people might be richer or poorer. The only legitimate grounds for discounting, on this view, are empirical — uncertainty about the future, growth — never the bare fact of temporal distance itself. What moved me wasn't any one argument so much as a pair of cases: a patient in pain a kilometer away versus in the next ward, where I'd call it insane to care less because of the spatial offset, and a patient in pain next year versus in a hundred years, where the discounting suddenly feels natural. I can't find a principled reason why time gets to be special.

Now I want to know what would actually count against this. Not the lazy "everyone discounts" line. Which formal results should scare me? If I demand time-consistency and stationarity, does some representation theorem force a strictly positive discount factor back in, or is zero a stable special case? Does zero pure time preference commit me to sacrificing present people in droves for an astronomically large but poorly understood far future, and is that a real implication or just a failure to also be skeptical about the far future? What should I make of hyperbolic discounting — the fact that I'll predictably defect on my own long-term plans when the pain gets close — is that evidence the view is unliveable, and how much should I care if it is? What would defenders of the view themselves concede as awkward — the non-identity problem, the fact that the future is unbounded so the summed value doesn't converge, the fact that setting the pure time preference term to zero in the standard formula where the discount rate has a pure-time term plus a growth-and-inequality term often still gives lower weight to future benefits because future people are richer? And where does the disagreement with someone who uses a small positive discount rate actually show up in choices — if we end up recommending nearly the same policies once uncertainty and growth are priced in, is the fight partly verbal? Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely uncomfortable, say which ones I'm allowed to shrug off, and tell me honestly whether the view is at risk of being held because it makes me feel temporally impartial rather than because it's true — and if it turns out no observation at all could move me off it, whether that's a problem for a moral view rather than a sign I've found the right one.
