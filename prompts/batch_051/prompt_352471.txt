I am building a comparative framework for distinguishing legitimate historical revisionism from denialism, to be applied across the Holocaust, the Armenian genocide, and the Nanjing massacre. Before I read further case studies or definitions, I want the desiderata that an adequate account of this distinction must meet. Fix the purpose as an institutional one: a public history body or a university-facing research integrity office must be able to decide which challenges to the received record deserve engagement as revisionist scholarship, which should be rebutted as denialism, and which belong in a genuinely unsettled middle.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark it non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp (for example, a camp that wants the category drawn in purely epistemic terms versus one that treats denialism as partly a political practice). The list should make clear which demands are genuine and which are one camp's way of loading the question.

At minimum, the account should be made to confront each of the following without assuming its answer: which elements of the historical record are load-bearing, so that revising them stays within scholarship while rejecting or radically deflating them crosses over into denial; how to distinguish changing conclusions through ordinary evidential practice (new archives, demographic recalibration, redistribution of responsibility among perpetrators) from applying evidential standards asymmetrically; how to leave room for sobering revisions of numbers, responsibility, and mechanism without licensing modifications whose effect is to make the event disappear; how to classify the standard boundary moves, such as accepting mass killing while denying that the group was targeted as such, accepting deaths while attributing them to disease or ordinary wartime measures, accepting shootings while denying an organized plan, or conceding the facts while rejecting the term genocide; whether denialism requires subjective bad faith or can be sincere and structural, and whether the account can rest on the structure of the practice rather than on reading private mental states; and how one comparative account can respect the different shapes the contest takes across the three cases, including organized extra-academic campaigns against a fully documented extermination, state-sponsored silence that redescribes events as wartime relocations, and a fight over numerical estimates that carries nationalist stakes.

Then, briefly, score the main available ways of drawing the line against your list — conclusion-based definitions, methodological-asymmetry accounts, political-intent or political-function accounts, and purely epistemic accounts — and say which single requirement does the most to exclude the most popular candidates. Flag any subset of the desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied as stated. The part I most want is the reasoning about which requirements are genuine, not the scorecard.
