Ok so I'm reading Kant's Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, and early on he says:

"Everyone must admit that if a law is to have moral force, i.e., to be the basis of an obligation, it must carry with it absolute necessity; that, for example, the precept, 'Thou shalt not lie,' is not valid for men alone, as if other rational beings had no need to observe it; and so with all the other moral laws properly so called; that, therefore, the basis of obligation must not be sought in the nature of man, or in the circumstances in the world in which he is placed, but a priori simply in the conception of pure reason"

And it really bugs me because I think he means that the test for a moral law shouldn't depend on human nature or the particular situation, but when I go through my actual gut verdicts on cases they seem super situation-dependent. I want to know whether there's a principle behind my verdicts, and whether it has anything to do with Kant's universalizability test or whether my verdicts are just inconsistent. Here they are, roughly in my own voice:

- Lying to the murderer at the door to keep my friend safe: fine.
- Lying on a job resume about why I left my old job: not fine.
- Using a friend's Netflix password without asking, when I'm pretty sure they wouldn't mind but haven't said so: I keep going back and forth, but lean fine.
- Downloading an expensive required textbook from a pirate site because the course is unaffordable and the publisher's price is outrageous: fine, but I feel a bit gross.
- Taking a few items from a large supermarket self-checkout without scanning because I'm broke and hungry: not fine, even though I understand it.
- Not reporting a couple hundred bucks of cash income on taxes: not fine.
- Telling a friend I'm "almost there" when I've just left the house: fine.
- Breaking a lunch date because a close friend called me mid-crisis and needed someone: fine.
- Keeping a borrowed book because the owner forgot about it and I love it: not fine.

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures all or most of these? Please say which of my verdicts it can't fit, and what it predicts about the case I keep going back and forth on: telling a dying patient that their estranged son called to say he forgives them, when he actually never called.

Also, do any of the candidate principles actually look like Kant's universal law of nature test, or do they end up having to build in the kind of circumstances he says shouldn't matter? If so, which of my verdicts or which clause of Kant do I have to give up?
