I've been working out a view I call the sunk-cost contract. The idea is that investing in a project — years in a discipline, money in a venture, practice at an instrument — is not just a transfer of resources; it is an implicit promise to the earlier self who did the investing that the investment will not be abandoned merely because continuing has become painful, dull, or expensive. Honoring a sunk cost is therefore sometimes not an economic mistake but the keeping of an intrapersonal obligation. This seems to explain several things: why finishing a book one now hates can feel like repaying a debt once past selves are taken seriously, why people finish degrees whose career value has collapsed, and why a blanket policy of abandoning projects the moment they turn bad would make it impossible to trust one's own resolutions. The view also predicts that the pull of sunk costs should weaken when the original investment was explicitly framed as a gamble, or when the earlier self lacked the information on which a promise could knowingly rest. I want an honest assessment. Is the sunk-cost contract a genuine addition, or merely a dressed-up version of existing accounts such as commitment signaling, regret avoidance, or self-narrative protection? Reconstruct the view in its strongest form, then identify exactly where it breaks: give the clearest case of a sunk cost it wrongly tells us to honor, and the clearest case it wrongly lets us abandon.
