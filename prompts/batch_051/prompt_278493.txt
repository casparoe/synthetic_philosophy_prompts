I want a "which theory of guilt and shame do you actually hold?" quiz for the blog, in the spirit of a political compass but for moral psychology, and I want the gear-level version, not the intro-lecture version. I've been reading the forums since the Sequences were new, and the standard "shame cultures vs. guilt cultures" post feels like it's been done to death; I want something that sorts people by what they actually say about hard cases, not by which label they like. So no question should be a disguised way of asking "do you think shame is about the whole self?" — every item has to be a concrete case.

Here's the debate as I understand it, with three fault lines I want the quiz to track:

(a) Is shame's target the self-as-a-whole versus the act (the psychologist line), or is it your standing in someone's eyes — real, imagined, or your own eyes as a spectator (the audience/esteem line)?

(b) Does guilt require a victim you could repair toward, or just a violated standard you accept?

(c) Is the difference between the two emotions ultimately about content (self vs. act, rule vs. ideal), or about what they dispose you to do (approach/repair vs. hide/submit)?

Plus I want one skeptical leaf: people for whom the whole distinction isn't doing real work — they only feel "bad about a norm violation" in varying intensities, and the labels mostly track how public the event was, not two natural kinds.

Cases I definitely want included, because I think they're the cruxes:

1. A private transgression nobody will ever discover (you quietly skim a small amount from an estate you administer; it's undetectable).
2. Public exposure for something embarrassing but not wrong (your porn history leaks at work).
3. Failing an identity-defining goal with no victim and no audience (you drop out of a marathon you trained a year for; nobody else is affected).
4. Survivor's guilt/shame: your patrolmate is killed and you live; you did nothing wrong and couldn't have swapped places, but you still feel awful. What is the awful about, exactly?
5. Vicarious shame: your employer or national team is caught in fraud; you had no part in it, yet you cringe and want to hide. The audience view should handle this easily; the act-vs-self view has to contort.
6. Harming a friend while drunk when repair is possible (you can apologize and make it right) vs. the same harm when repair is impossible (he's gone). I want this to separate repair-first people from self-condemnation people.

Deliverable, with sections in this order:

1. "The Questions" (about 60% of the piece): nine questions, each a one-to-three-sentence case plus three options. Options should correspond to different positions without ever naming them. Use my six cases plus three more of your choosing that cleanly split the remaining fault lines.

2. "Scoring Key" (about 20%): show how each option maps onto the positions, give an explicit rule for what pattern means which leaf (scores and tie-breaks), and flag where two positions are compatible enough that a high score in both isn't a contradiction — don't force mutual exclusivity if it isn't there.

3. "The Leaves" (about 10%): name each position, give a one-sentence gloss, then one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing a defender of that position has to eat.

4. "Where the Quiz Breaks" (about 10%): which pairs of leaves these cases can't seperate, which combinations of answers look logically possible but no known position occupies, and a one-sentence diagnosis of each empty cell (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored).

No bibliography or reading list, and don't quote anyone from memory — paraphrase whatever distinctions you're drawing on. I'll add links and citations myself later.
