Last month two of my housemates got into a long argument about a thought experiment neither of them could resolve, and I would like a referee's verdict. The case is this. A person has a device attached to his body that will deliver electric shocks for the rest of his life, controlled by a dial that can be set to any whole number from 0 to 1,000. Setting 0 delivers no shock at all; setting 1,000 delivers constant, unrelenting agony. The settings are so finely spaced that the wearer cannot detect any difference between one setting and the one just below it: moving from n to n+1 feels exactly the same. Each week he is offered a choice—turn the dial up by one step and receive $10,000, or leave the dial where it is forever, with no chance to turn it back. Starting at 0, he climbs one step at a time all the way to 1,000, ending with $10,000,000 and a life of pain. At every single step the choice felt reasonable; at the end he would happily return all the money to be back at 0.

Karim argued that the example shows rational preference need not be transitive. The wearer cannot feel any difference between adjacent settings, so at each step the extra $10,000 is, from his point of view, a gain bought at a cost he cannot register; insisting that he stop would demand that he act as though he feels a difference he does not feel. But indifference between neighboring settings does not, Karim insisted, add up to indifference between 0 and 1,000—those two the wearer ranks clearly, and prefers 0. So the wearer is rational at every step even though the steps land him somewhere he disprefers, and any theory that forbids this has smuggled in an artificial rule of "transitivity of indifference" that real agents have no reason to obey. Diana replied that Karim had misdescribed the choice as a series of isolated pairwise comparisons. The wearer knows the entire structure of the dial from the beginning, so the relevant question is not "does n+1 feel worse than n?" but "is the policy of always taking the next step acceptable?" Since that policy ends in agony, it is straightforwardly not acceptable. The imperceptibility of the boundary, she said, shows only that the wearer lacks a sensory threshold, not that the underlying differences in suffering lack value; a rational agent can draw a line well below 1,000 and stop there, even if no particular line is forced by felt difference.

Karim answered that her policy test proves too much: if the foreseeable end of a chain of individually harmless steps makes each step irrational, then nobody may rationally eat one chip, or stay in bed one extra minute, because a sufficiently long chain of such choices leads somewhere clearly worse. Since rationality cannot require perfect self-governance over arbitrarily extended sequences, he concluded that the single steps remain rational and the bad outcome is simply the cost of living with fine-grained, compounding choices. Diana answered that the chip cases are disanalogous precisely because, in them, the agent can stop later without having lost the option to continue, whereas in the device case a single refusal permanently fixes the dial at its current setting. The wearer has an obvious off-ramp and fails to take it only because ten thousand dollars looms larger than a difference he cannot feel; that is weakness of will, not a proof that rational preference may cycle. She added that even if no particular stopping point is uniquely required, there may still be a range of acceptable stopping points with 1,000 outside it, so the arbitrariness of drawing a line does not license drawing it nowhere. I was unable to referee them, so please adjudicate carefully: which specific claims is each person right about, which are wrong, what are they both missing, and who is more right overall?
