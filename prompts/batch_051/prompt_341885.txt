In my government class, we have been studying the origins of sovereign immunity, and I keep noticing an asymmetry I cannot find a convincing justification for. If a private person damages someone else's property, we say the person has done a legal wrong and can be sued. But when a state agency does the same thing—say, a public works crew destroys a fence or a child welfare agency wrongfully removes a child—the state often cannot be taken to court unless it has passed a law allowing itself to be sued. The state, unlike a person, seems to have a built-in shield against being called a wrongdoer. Our teacher assigned this passage from William Blackstone's Commentaries on the Laws of England, where he quotes Sir Henry Finch: "the king's prerogative stretcheth not to the doing of any wrong." This seems to say that the sovereign's power does not include a permission to do wrong. But if that is true, why does the law also give the sovereign special protection when wrongs happen anyway? Is there a defensible reason for the asymmetry between ordinary legal wrongdoers and the state, or is this just a leftover fiction we should drop? Evaluate the asymmetry directly: what principle, if any, could justify it, and does the Blackstone passage help or make it harder?
