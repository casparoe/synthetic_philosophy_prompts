Last year my dad was declared brain-dead in an ICU and we had to make decisions about stopping treatment really fast; it was confusing enough that I ended up taking a job as a transplant coordinator afterwards, and now I read hospital death policies all day. Our hospital's policy says: "A patient is dead when either (a) their heartbeat and breathing have stopped permanently, or (b) their entire brain, including the brainstem, has permanently stopped working."

Last month we had a donation case I can't stop thinking about. The donor had a very severe brain injury but did not meet the brain-death criteria. His heart stopped, the team waited five minutes, confirmed he wasn't breathing, and declared him dead under (a). The family had already consented to donation, so the surgical team then clamped the big vessels so no blood could reach his brain, and hooked him up to a machine that restarted circulation to his abdominal organs only — and his heart started beating again inside his body while they recovered the organs. I wasnt in the room, but the way the nurse described it, it sounds like under a literal reading of our own policy his heartbeat had not "stopped permanently" at all. I have to write a short memo for our ethics committee walking through how the policy should be read: the plain words, the purpose of the rule, what the people who wrote it would have said if they'd been asked about this exact setup, and what a reasonable ICU nurse reading the policy would expect. Which reading should control? And if the policy just doesn't cover the case, what's the smallest change that would make it cover it without wrecking organ donation entirely?
