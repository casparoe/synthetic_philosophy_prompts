One of my friends has been an arbitrator for consumer disputes for years, and I've been collecting her rulings on these "I agree" cases because they all seem to hang together even though she never states a general rule. A guy signed a two-page gym contract without reading it and then had to give thirty days' written notice to cancel; she held him to the cancellation clause. A woman clicked "I agree" under a scroll box on a monthly subscription app, never scrolled, and got held to an auto-renew clause she said she'd never seen. A website advertised a free trial that you could cancel anytime, hid the $99-a-month charge in a terms link at the the bottom, and never made you click anything; she said the guy wasn't bound. Someone bought software in a box, and the terms inside said opening it meant you accept, including a cap on damages, but you could return it if you refused; she held the buyer to the cap. And once a man clicked "I agree" on a sixty-page terms document that had, on page fifty-two, a clause sending any dispute to arbitration in a foreign country, and she said he wasn't bound to that clause even though he was bound to the rest.

Now she has a case against a photo-storage app. The app's whole pitch, in the ads and during onboarding, is that they never use your photos for anything but showing you your library. During signup there's a scrollable terms box, and the first line is in bold: you grant the company a worldwide, royalty-free license to use your photos to train AI. The user clicked "I agree" without reading past the opening screens. I can see it going either way, because the clause is about as conspicuous as a clause can be, but it's also exactly the promise the company spent the whole signup flow making you believe it wasn't making.
