You are a philosopher of science specializing in confirmation theory. I am a graduate student working through Hempel’s 1945 article *Studies in the Logic of Confirmation*, and I have reconstructed an argument from it that now looks as if it proves too much. Please think through the diagnosis step by step rather than just signaling a preferred solution.

In the article, Hempel considers the familiar hypothesis that all ravens are black and points out that it is logically equivalent to the hypothesis that all nonblack things are nonravens. A white shoe is a nonblack nonraven, so it is a positive instance of the second hypothesis. By a principle Hempel calls the equivalence condition, if a piece of evidence confirms a hypothesis, it confirms any logically equivalent hypothesis. Therefore the white shoe confirms the hypothesis that all ravens are black. Hempel accepts this consequence, but I want to know whether the reasoning generalizes too far and whether the price of avoiding it is worse than the paradox itself.

Let me lay the argument out with numbered steps.

(1) A positive instance of a universal generalization confirms that generalization: a black raven confirms all ravens are black, and a white shoe confirms all nonblack things are nonravens.

(2) If a piece of evidence confirms a hypothesis, it confirms any logically equivalent hypothesis.

(3) All ravens are black is logically equivalent to all nonblack things are nonravens.

(4) A white shoe is a positive instance of all nonblack things are nonravens.

(5) Therefore a white shoe confirms all nonblack things are nonravens. (from 1 and 4)

(6) Therefore a white shoe confirms all ravens are black. (from 2, 3, and 5)

(7) By exactly parallel reasoning, a green leaf confirms all crows are black; a red chair confirms all emeralds are green; a blue shirt confirms all swans are white; and a piece of quartz confirms all non-chalk things are ravens.

(8) But this makes confirmation far too cheap and indiscriminate: almost any observation confirms almost any universal generalization, including generalizations we take to be false or pathological.

(9) So at least one of (1) or (2) must be false, or the notion of a positive instance in (1) and (4) must be doing illicit work.

I would most like to keep (2), because equivalence seems like a nonnegotiable feature of confirmation. But I also don’t see how to modify (1) without giving up the idea that observing black ravens confirms all ravens are black, which would gut ordinary confirmation. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If it doesn’t go wrong, which premise is least costly to reject, and what exactly would rejecting it commit me to about confirmation, induction, or the relation between evidence and hypothesis?

Please start by separating the logical core of the argument from the intuitive discomfort in step (8), then evaluate the candidate responses. I want a menu of fixes with their costs, not just the one you think is correct.
