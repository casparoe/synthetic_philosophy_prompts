Assume for the sake of argument that the gender-equality paradox is a robust finding rather than a measurement artifact: across countries, higher scores on standard indices of gender equality are associated with larger, not smaller, sex differences in occupational and educational choices, and in the preference measures that track them. For example, on this stipulated finding, more gender-equal countries have a lower proportion of women among STEM graduates than less gender-equal countries do.

I'm planning a long-form piece for a scientifically literate general audience about what, if anything, actually follows from this pattern for three debates: the nature/culture question (do such aggregate preference differences trace to biology or to socialization?); the explanation question (which theories of gendered choice, such as social-role or stereotype-based accounts, are confirmed or disconfirmed?); and the justice question (what should gender-egalitarian policy aim at, and is parity across fields a sensible success metric?).

Commentators draw wildly different morals from the same pattern. Some treat it as close to decisive evidence for an evolved basis of occupational interests. Some treat it as refuting social-role or stereotype explanations of gendered choices. Some treat it as showing that egalitarian policies backfire or fight an unchangeable nature. Some treat it as uninformative because national wealth and economic security confound the gender-equality measures, or because country-level correlations don't license claims about individuals.

I want the piece to be organized around the two or three implications that are actually best supported by the finding, genuinely revisionary for a thoughtful reader, and not already obvious. Please rank the candidate implications—including any important ones I haven't listed—by how much they matter for that purpose, and defend the ordering.

In your ranking, I'd like you to do the following. Separate carefully what follows about preferences, about choices, about abilities, and about policy, marking the places where the pattern licenses no inference at all. Distinguish the claim that the paradox favors a biological explanation from the weaker claim that it merely defeats one simple socialization-based prediction—the prediction that making environments less sexist will make aggregate choices converge—and say how revisionary each of those two claims actually is. Take the confound interpretations seriously and say whether they make the strongest biological and anti-socialization readings overrated. And tell me which candidate implications you would leave out because they don't follow, are banal, or would actively mislead readers. What I want in the end is not a survey of the literature on the paradox but a defended agenda: which two or three morals deserve the space, and why those rather than the others.
