I teach a graduate seminar on collective responsibility, and a recurring case has resisted the vocabulary I have for moral and legal complicity. A senior official takes no part in planning a wrongful policy, signs nothing, and is never asked for approval. The planners nevertheless go forward because they are confident that, once the harm is public, this official will speak about it in the institution's preferred terms — calling the outcome unfortunate but unavoidable, absorbing it into routine business, and declining to criticize it. That confidence is based on the official's long record, not on any promise or prior communication. Had the planners expected the official to denounce and resist such decisions after the fact, they would likely have shelved the policy. The official's only act occurs after the wrong is complete, and yet the anticipation of that later act appears to be among the wrongdoing's enabling conditions. A neighboring case turned up in discussion of a critic whose predictable favorable review of whatever work emerged emboldened the authors, though the critic was never consulted. The common structure seems to be that a wrong is undertaken partly because the wrongdoers can rely on a third party's anticipated post hoc normalization, even though the third party contributes no instruction, approval, or help at the time. Is there an established name for this configuration, and what is the best analysis of it? I can see that it is not clearly accessory liability after the fact, because the wrong is already complete when the response occurs, and not clearly encouragement, because nothing is communicated in advance. But I am dissatisfied with a bare label; I want the boundaries drawn — how it differs from each of those neighboring categories, whether it is best classified as a form of complicity despite the absence of contemporaneous participation, or whether it is merely a case of ex post rationalization that the wrongdoers happen to exploit. If no established term fits, I would ask for a coined term with a precise definition and an account of the boundary cases.
