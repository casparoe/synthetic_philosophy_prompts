Define algorithmic fairness in a way that yields defensible verdicts in the following cases:

1. A credit model approves men and women at equal rates, but among applicants who would repay, it rejects more women, while among applicants who would default, it accepts more men.
2. A pretrial risk-assessment instrument assigns identical risk scores to Black and white defendants with identical charge and prior-record combinations, yet because arrest rates differ across neighborhoods, the high-risk category contains a larger share of Black defendants.
3. A university admissions model includes distance from the campus as a predictive factor because remote students historically graduate at lower rates; this lowers offers for a particular ethnic group that is geographically concentrated far away.
4. A healthcare allocation algorithm targets patients with the highest predicted future cost, but because cost data reflect unequal access, equally sick Black patients are much less likely to be selected than white patients.
5. A hiring tool maximizes predicted performance and has the same false-negative rate for male and female applicants, but since there are many more male applicants, it rejects a much larger number of qualified women.
6. A school assignment lottery selects randomly among students above a common minimum test threshold; a group with lower average scores has fewer students above the threshold and therefore receives fewer seats.

I think 4 is clearly unfair and 6 is clearly fair; 1, 2, 3, and 5 are hard for me. A satisfactory answer should not simply say “satisfy all the standard statistical criteria,” since impossibility results show that several of those criteria conflict. Propose a definition, apply it to all six, say which standard fairness criteria your definition refuses to prioritize, and identify where the definition gives the verdict you find least comfortable.
