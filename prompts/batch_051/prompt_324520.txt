Before I settle on any defense of petitionary prayer, I want the specification that an adequate defense must meet, not the defense itself. Take the God addressed in petitionary prayer to be omniscient, perfectly good, and immutable: God already knows what will be asked, already wills what is best, and cannot be changed by anything outside God. The account I am looking for must explain what petitionary prayer could actually accomplish or be for relative to such a being, and it must do so without quietly changing the subject to meditation, ritual, or social bonding.

Write the desiderata. Include at least the following, with the case or consideration behind each: the concrete situations an account must handle (praying for another person's recovery, for one's own moral change, for forgiveness, for outcomes that arrive and do not arrive, and for things the person praying suspects are impossible or trivial); the distinctions it may not collapse (between petition and self-persuasion, between an answer and a mere subsequent event, between God responding to a prayer and God being causally determined by it); the theological commitments it must respect (omniscience, immutability, perfect goodness, and, if you include it, divine aseity or simplicity, with each flagged as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular theological camp); and the failure it must avoid, namely an account on which every possible sequence of events counts as the prayer being answered. Then tell me which single requirement is most damaging to the stock defenses (prayer changes the person praying; prayer is a divinely ordained means; God eternally hears all prayers in willing what occurs), and say whether any pair or subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. That last judgment is the part I want most.
