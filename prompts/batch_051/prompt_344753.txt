My game-theory friend has a knack for calling what people will actually do in coordination puzzles, and over the years I've written down a bunch of her snap verdicts. The setup she uses in all of them is: a room of kids, some with mud on their foreheads, each kid can see everyone else's forehead but not their own, everyone knows the rules, and a teacher asks once a minute for anyone who knows whether their own forehead is muddy to step forward. She stands by these calls: with three kids and one muddy, right after a public announcement that at least one has mud, the muddy kid steps at minute one; with three kids and two muddy, same public announcement, both step at minute two; with four kids and three muddy, same announcement, all three step at minute three. But she also says that if you keep the three kids, two muddy and swap the public announcement for private one-on-one whispers (each kid is told at least one has mud, but no kid knows what the others were told), then nobody ever steps. Now someone asks her a new one: five kids, four muddy, public announcement as usual. Does she say they step, and at which minute, or does she say their stuck? I'm less interested in the answer itself than the rule. To me it looks like she's choosing between "the kids run the induction all the way up, so publicness gives them common knowledge with no depth limit" and "people top out around three levels of she-knows-that-he-knows, so anything that needs a fourth level stalls." Do those two rules really cover all her old calls? Are there other rules that fit just as well? And which extra case would cleanly settle which rule she's actually running?
