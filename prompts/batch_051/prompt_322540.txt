Consider the dollar auction as a model of escalation of commitment. In the classic version, an auctioneer sells a one-dollar bill under two unusual rules: the highest bidder pays their bid and receives the dollar, but the second-highest bidder must also pay their bid and receives nothing. Once two people are locked into a bidding contest, each one faces a recurring choice between raising the bid by a small increment or accepting a certain loss, and the result is that the dollar often sells for far more than a dollar. The game is meant to capture the dynamics of throwing good money after bad, but real situations differ from it in important ways: future costs and benefits are uncertain, abandoning a project can damage a reputation, and decision-makers can sometimes change the rules, call in outside help, or renegotiate commitments.

I am the treasurer of a small choral society, and I am currently in the middle of something that feels like a live dollar auction. A year ago the society agreed to host a modest regional singing festival. The original budget was tight but manageable; since then, the venue deposit, printed materials, an extra insurance rider, and a hired sound engineer have all come in above estimate, and each time the board has paid the additional amount because we were already financially committed and did not want to forfeit what we had spent. We have now reached a point where the next unavoidable payment would nearly empty our reserves, and even a generously optimistic projection suggests the festival will at best break even. I suspect the dollar auction has something to teach us here, but I want to know exactly what it clarifies, where the analogy might mislead us, and what a wiser decision procedure would have looked like from the start.

Please describe the most important implications of the dollar auction for how I should think about this decision. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
