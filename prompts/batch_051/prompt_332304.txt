I run the intake desk for the county ag extension, and a big part of my job is fielding calls from farmers and landscapers who think they've spotted a disease or an invasive pest. It's several calls a day, I get about fifteen minutes per call, and I have to decide whether to bump it up to an inspector or let it go. If I bump too many, inspectors waste whole days driving out to look at overwatering and leaf miners that where always there; if I let one go, an outbreak could spread and I'm the one who missed it. My supervisor has to sign off on whatever procedure I use, so it needs to be in plain language, not theory.

I recently read a dumbed-down thing about an old Indian school of philosophy called Nyaya, and it said there are basically four pramanas, or ways you can know something: perception, inference, word (testimony), and comparison. The way I understood it, perception is what you see or smell or touch yourself; inference is what you figure out from a sign, like smoke means fire; word is what you accept because a trustworthy source told you; and comparison is recognizing something by its likeness to something you already know, like "these spots match the extension photos so it's early blight." I've been using that as a rough mental checklist on calls, but I don't think I'm doing it right, and I don't know how to combine them when they disagree. Say a farmer I know to be reliable tells me his tomato plants look exactly like the early blight photos, but I can't see them myself. Is that testimony, comparison, neither, both? And does it matter?

What I want is a genuine step-by-step procedure I can put in front of my supervisor and run on every call: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, where the procedure should stop with a no, where it should bump to an inspector, and where it should hand the call to a human because the step is a judgment call and no checklist can settle it. Keep it usable by someone with no philosophy background, and keep the four pramanas doing real work in it rather than just relabeling my current gut calls. Then run it on two or three made-up calls, including one where the procedure tells me to do something I won't like, and say what it can't handle. Also be honest about how confident you are in the procedure overall and single out the one point you're least sure about.
