You are grading a final examination for an upper-level course on twentieth-century analytic philosophy. The student was given the following question:

Question. In Language, Truth, and Logic, A. J. Ayer discusses the sentence that one will survive the death of one’s body. Reconstruct his argument that this sentence lacks factual significance. Be careful to distinguish the claim that it is self-contradictory from the claim that it is unverifiable, and explain what Ayer thinks someone who asserts or denies personal immortality is doing. Finally, say whether Ayer should be classified as an agnostic about life after death.

Student’s answer:

Ayer’s verification principle says that a non-analytic sentence is factually meaningful only if it can be verified or falsified by possible sense-experience. Applied to the sentence that I will survive the death of my body, he argues first that it is not self-contradictory. After all, we can conceive of a stream of experiences continuing even though the body has stopped functioning. The problem is rather that the sentence is unverifiable in principle: no experience I can have while alive can confirm or disconfirm what happens after death, and a dead person cannot report anything. Since both the sentence and its negation fail the test, both are pseudo-propositions. Ayer therefore concludes that the reasonable philosophical attitude is agnosticism: we should suspend judgment because there is no evidence either way.
