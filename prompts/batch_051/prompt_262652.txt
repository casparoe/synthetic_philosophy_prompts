I spent my career as a geologist and, since retiring, have been taking seminars in philosophy to keep myself occupied; this term it is family ethics. We read Jane English's friendship view of filial obligation—that a grown child does not owe her parents repayment of a debt for past care, but that whatever she owes is owed in virtue of the relationship she actually has with them now, so that a close parent–child relationship carries the duties of a close friendship and an estranged one carries considerably less. I am sufficiently persuaded of it that I have started keeping track of the verdicts of a fellow seminar member, M, who describes the view more sharply than I would: on M's telling, blood relation by itself does almost no normative work. M's recorded verdicts are these. (1) A mother and her adult daughter are genuinely close—they holiday together, confide in one another—and the mother, a devoted though not unusually self-sacrificing parent, needs substantial help during cancer treatment; M says the daughter owes help on the scale one would owe a closest friend. (2) A father paid for his daughter's degree and still sends her gifts, but she finds him controlling and they speak only on holidays; he asks her to co-sign a car loan he could obtain without her; M says she has no obligation to co-sign and may decline politely. (3) A neglectful parent with whom the adult child has no relationship asks for regular visits in old age, insisting the child owes it as a matter of duty; M says no such visits are owed, at most the common courtesy due to an acquaintance. (4) A close and mutually supportive parent and child face the parent's move into a care facility, which the child could ease at real inconvenience; M says the help is owed. (5) A father who left in the child's infancy reappears destitute after a house fire and asks his well-off child for a spare room for two weeks; M says the child owes the two weeks as a matter of easy rescue, but owes nothing further. (6) An unrelated family friend, close to the daughter for years, needs help of the same kind as the mother in case (1); M says the daughter owes the friend the same as she would owe her mother, because what matters is the friendship, not the parentage.

The new case is this. A father worked overtime for years to pay for his daughter's music lessons and education. The daughter, now in her forties and financially comfortable, has never been close to him; she finds him overbearing, and their contact has settled into holiday telephone calls and occasional visits performed out of propriety. A pension shortfall now means he cannot keep the house he has lived in for decades; he could sell it, but in his town rentals are scarce, he has little in savings, and his health is poor, so the sale would leave him without stable housing for an uncertain period. He asks her for either monthly support equal to about five percent of her disposable income for as long as it takes, or a smaller one-off sum to tide him over while he sells. M has not yet pronounced.

I would like a prediction. What are the candidate rules that fit M's six verdicts nearly equally well, and what does each predict about the new case? Give your best guess as to M's verdict, with the reasons for preferring one rule over the others, and say which further case—however artificial—would most cleanly separate whatever contenders remain.
