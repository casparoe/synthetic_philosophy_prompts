I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a name for. Someone analyzes what a person really wants as what that person would want under idealized conditions: fully informed, calm, not under social pressure, thinking clearly. Then a concrete stated preference is offered as a counterexample, say a friend who has reflected for years and still wants to stay in a career that looks to the theorist like a mistake. The defender says the friend must lack some relevant fact, or must be under pressure. When evidence shows otherwise, the defender does not retreat from the conclusion; they add a further idealizing condition: the friend has not truly imagined the alternative, has not lived through both outcomes, or is not being sufficiently rational. The hypothetical ideal chooser is quietly respecified each time so that it comes out endorsing the defender's answer, while the defender claims to be applying one fixed standard. I've seen the same move in arguments about whether someone genuinely wants to have children.

It resembles no-true-Scotsman, but the retreat is not about reclassifying who counts as a real case; it is about adjusting the counterfactual chooser. It also resembles ordinary ad hoc patching, but the patches are all in the description of the ideal agent rather than the substantive claim. Is there an established name, and perhaps a literature, for this specific pattern of moving-target idealization of the choosing self? I already know the labels no true Scotsman and ad hoc rescue, so do not stop there. I want the carving: the nearest established concepts, how this phenomenon differs from each of them, which fits best, and, if none fits, a proposed name and definition with at least one case where the pattern is genuinely present and one where it is not. I am not after a bibliography; I want the conceptual distinctions. Structure the answer in sections, in order: (1) Bottom line (1–2 sentences); (2) The phenomenon stated sharply (one short paragraph); (3) Nearest established concepts and where they fall short (one short paragraph per concept); (4) Proposed name and definition, with boundary cases (short); (5) Why this matters for idealized-preference theories generally (two or three sentences).
