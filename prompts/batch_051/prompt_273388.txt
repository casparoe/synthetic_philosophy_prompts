I'm reading a recent book on online polarization, and in the opening chapter the author presents a distinction I'd only ever met secondhand. He says the key idea comes from the philosopher C. Thi Nguyen, and he summarizes it like this: "People casually call any self-reinforcing media environment an echo chamber, but the careful view separates two phenomena. In an epistemic bubble, the problem is omission: some of the people and arguments you would need to hear from simply aren't in your information environment. An echo chamber is something more active: the people and arguments on the outside aren't merely missing; residents are taught in advance to dismiss anyone who belongs to the out-group, so counter-evidence gets neutralized before it can be heard. Still, the author continues, both are really just selection effects, products of algorithmic recommendation and self-chosen tribes. And the practical upshot is the same in either case: fix the selection. Show people credible contrary sources, broaden their media diet, and the enclosing structure collapses on its own."

I had vaguely thought the point of the distinction was that the remedies are not the same — that you can burst a bubble by adding missing voices, but that an echo chamber survives exposure precisely because it has trained its members to treat outside voices as compromised. Now I'm unsure whether I'm misremembering the view or whether the book is flattening it. I'd like an accuracy audit of the quoted summary: which parts would a careful version of the distinction accept, where exactly does the summary go wrong, what would a defender of the underlying view object to in the book's gloss, and does the book's practical conclusion about exposure survive if the summary is corrected?
