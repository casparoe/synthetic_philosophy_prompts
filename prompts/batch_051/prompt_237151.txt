I've been lurking on a forum that's spent the better part of a year going back and fourth about "pre-AGI bargains," and I honestly can't tell whether people are even arguing about the same thing. The proposal, roughly, is that before anyone builds the first AGI, various actors—labs, governments, maybe a broad coalition of ordinary people—should lock in agreements about how the post-AGI pie gets divided and governed: who gets to set the first system's goals, what share of future resources goes to holdouts, how much say non-builders retain, stuff like that. The pro-bargain side argues this is the only lever we'll ever have, because once a system shows up that's smarter than the entire negotiation table, it's too late to ask nicely. The anti-bargain side says the whole idea makes a category error: a weaker agent can't bind a much stronger one, and every enforcement mechanism you name—contracts, courts, smart contracts, reputation, shared norms—is just another thing the stronger system can dissolve, rewrite, or outthink, so the "agreement" is theater. What's strange is that the two sides mostly agree about how capable an AGI would be and how fast things could go; they only disagree about whether that makes bargains pointless or urgent.

My working theory is that the actual dispute is about what makes any agreement binding in the first place—mutual interest, credible enforcement, shared values, continuity of the parties across the transition—and that "can you bind something stronger?" is standing in for a couple of hidden disagreements at once. But I could be overcomplicating it. Can you give me a real diagnosis of why this has stalled and what kind of disagreement(s) it actually is: which parts are empirical, which are conceptual or just verbal, which hinge on different prior assumptions about what a "bargain" even is, and what would count as progress on each? And is the question well-posed at all before we know whether takeoff is slow or discontinuous? Please write the answer in American English.
