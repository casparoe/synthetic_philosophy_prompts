The debate over whether consciousness is essentially global availability, of the sort described by global workspace theory, or whether there is a further purely phenomenal aspect that could come apart from any functional property, has been running for decades without much movement. The access side points out that unattended or unbroadcast stimuli are denied in report; that change blindness and inattentional blindness suggest surprisingly little conscious detail outside the focus of attention; and that every experimental claim about conscious experience must pass through report, which is exactly what global availability is supposed to provide. The phenomenal side replies that these are facts about access and report rather than about experience itself; that partial-report experiments and the accompanying claims about phenomenal overflow suggest a brief rich store of available detail even though only a fraction of it could be broadcast or reported; and that no account of global processing, broadcasting, or reportability seems to answer the question of why there is something it is like for the subject at all. The moves on both sides are familiar, and I almost never see anyone shift.

I would like a diagnosis of this impasse rather than a verdict. My working hypothesis, offered as such, is that the two sides are running two different explanatory projects under a single label. The first is functional: characterize the computational role by which some contents become available for reasoning, planning, report, and memory, and explain why only some representations get that role. The second is constitutive: explain the existence of a first-person point of view in the first place. The appearance of a substantive dispute arises because both projects treat verbal report as decisive evidence, even though the first treats report as part of the phenomenon to be explained while the second treats it merely as a fallible external index of the phenomenon.

I want to know how far that diagnosis can be pushed. Is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or a disagreement about what would count as an explanation at all? Does it bottom out in starting intuitions — for example about whether the conceivability of a system that behaves and reports exactly as you do while lacking phenomenal consciousness should count as a datum — such that no empirical result is likely to move both sides? Or is there a hidden empirical question that would, if settled, dissolve most of the dispute? If the two projects are indeed different, trace concretely where each side's favorite arguments become irrelevant to the other side's target, and identify what each side would have to give up in order to engage the other. Then say what progress would look like under your diagnosis: a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a revised question — or, if you conclude none is possible, argue for why the participants are stuck by design. In doing so, engage the actual data each side cites, in particular change blindness and inattentional blindness on one side and partial-report or overflow results on the other, since each side tends to treat the other side's favorite data as evidence for something it never denied.
