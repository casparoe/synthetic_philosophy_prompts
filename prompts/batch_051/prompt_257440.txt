ok so I read Peter Singer's Famine, Affluence, and Morality, the one with the child drowning in a shallow pond. I know Singer's main principle is something like if it is in your power to prevent something bad, without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, you ought to do it. But I keep getting stuck on why the pond example does such heavy lifting. I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, and my diagnosis is concurrency and latency. The pond case is a single-threaded, low-latency, one-to-one transaction: the victim is present, the action is atomic, the cost is a pair of ruined shoes. Singer then scales that up to millions of distant victims, but he never accounts for the fact that moral attention is bounded. So I came up with two categories. Hot rescue: victim salient, action simple, agent unique. Cold rescue: victim statistical, action ongoing, agent replaceable. My small theory is that Singer's argument assumes something like zero propagation delay between a fact about the world and an agent's duty to respond to it. But duties are more like event-driven callbacks with queue depth limits. If you subscribe to every possible tragedy, the event loop hangs and you do nothing. I think hot and cold rescue are not just different psychological states; they are different moral kinds. I know about effective altruism, and I know people talk about moral distance and emotional salience, but I mean something stronger: that the duty in cold rescue is structurally different, not just harder to feel. Please tell me if this distinction already exists, give me the three strongest objections to the claim that cold duties are structurally different from hot ones, and say whether any objection is fatal. I don't need citations, just honest assessment. Also tell me if the whole analogy is doing more work than it deserves.
