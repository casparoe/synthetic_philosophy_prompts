I'm a novelist, and in the book I'm outlining there's a dinner scene where two characters — a Bayesian economist and a philosopher who's fallen down the imprecise-probability rabbit hole — argue about whether rationality really requires your degrees of belief to obey the probability axioms. I want the philosopher to have a real point and the economist to have a real comeback, so I need the underlying dispute worked out before I write any dialogue.

The classic Dutch book argument, as I understand it: if your degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, there's a set of bets, each of which you'd take on its own given your credences, that together guarantee a loss no matter what. So rationality demands probabilistic coherence. The changed assumption my philosopher wants to lean on is this: suppose agents have imprecise credences — a set of probability functions rather than one number (her credence that it rains tomorrow is the whole interval [0.3, 0.7], not a point value) — and suppose such an agent only accepts a bet if it's favorable under every function in the set. Then add a second twist: she refuses to evaluate the bets one at a time when the bookie can bundle them; she looks at the whole package and walks away from anything that's a sure loss. Does the Dutch book argument survive that?

Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion: (1) which premise of the Dutch book argument this development actually bites, (2) whether the argument is dead, repairable, or fine as is, (3) what, if anything, it still shows once precision isnt forced, (4) what the Bayesian should concede, and (5) what the imprecise-credence philosopher still has to explain.
