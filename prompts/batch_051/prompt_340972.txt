Last year my mom, who was on heavy painkillers after surgery, got signed up for something called a longevity study by a chiropractor—really just a bunch of expensive tests and supplements—and signed on an iPad without understanding any of it. She later said she only did it because he was wearing a white coat and she didn't want to be rude. Fast forward to now: I'm in a weirdly fitting new job as a research coordinator, and I'm the one whose supposed to get legitimate informed consent for a ketamine trial for treatment-resistant depression. The people I see are often desperate, some have been through ten failed meds, and relatives frequently hover and answer for them. I get about forty minutes per person, and the form is eighteen pages.

I want a decision procedure I can actually run in the room, not the standard make-sure-they-understand speech. I need the questions to ask in order, what counts as good enough understanding for this kind of trial, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to someone else—the PI, an ethicist, a clinician—because it's a values call rather than a facts call. Specifically the procedure has to check: the difference between ordinary hope and believing the trial will treat you; family pressure that shades into coercion, especially when the patient depends on the relative; how to test whether someone is competent enough on that particular day when they're depressed but not confused; and what to do with a patient who asks me to leave out the scary details because knowing them would make them too anxious to participate. Is that a valid waiver of information, or am I just deceiving them with extra steps?

Then run the procedure on three people: a man who can recite the risks back perfectly but says he'll do anything, including permanent side effects, because he has a plan to kill himself if this doesn't work; a woman whose husband answers every question for her and she just agrees with whatever he says while looking at the floor; and a retired nurse who understands the trial better than I do but is enrolling for the stipend because her disability got cut and she's about to lose her apartment. Make sure at least one of the three comes out as a no, even though I'll probably want it to be a yes, and tell me honestly where this procedure will still misfire.
