I spent my career in the natural sciences and have only recently begun reading philosophy; the issue that keeps bothering me is the form of the central question in the Great Divergence literature. Historians standardly frame that question as why sustained industrial growth began in Britain rather than in the Yangzi Delta, or why modern science arose in Europe rather than in China, and they treat these as well-posed requests for an explanation. The routine method is contrastive: catalogue the salient antecedent differences, hold the shared background fixed, and ask which difference made the difference. I would like to know what philosophical commitments that practice presupposes. Does it presuppose that the two cases are comparable units, that both sides of the contrast were live historical possibilities, and that explanation works by isolating a difference-maker rather than by tracing a path? Which rival views of causation, contingency, and historical explanation does the practice quietly rule out, and where is it underdetermined — that is, where would several different philosophical theories license the same contrastive question, and which theories would reject it as ill posed?
