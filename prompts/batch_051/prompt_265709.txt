I've been trying to settle on criteria for when a planetary-scale geoengineering project counts as legitimately decided, rather than one group imposing its will on everyone else. Here's what I have so far: (1) it's authorized by a body that represents, in some real way, the people who will be affected—not necessarily literally everyone, but something close to it; (2) the people most vulnerable to climate change and to the side effects of the intervention get a meaningful say, or at least a credible channel to object; (3) there's a credible off-ramp, meaning the intervention can be halted without a termination shock that leaves people worse off than before; and (4) the reasoning and evidence behind the choice are out in the open, not hashed out in private by a small group of scientific advisors.

Now the borderline case. A coalition of nineteen low-lying island states, together about a quarter of a percent of the world's population, faces being largely submerged within decades. After countless rounds of UN negotiations that go nowhere, they secretly fund a marine cloud brightening program: ships spray a fine mist of seawater to brighten low clouds over their region, aimed at cooling the ocean enough to save coral reefs and slow sea-level rise. They argue they're acting in self-defense, they are exactly the stakeholders my second criterion was built for, the program would stop within days if they turned ships of, so there's no sulfate-style termination shock, and they went secret only because they knew big emitters would veto any public proposal. Two years later they're caught, after downwind rainfall has shifted slightly and hurt farms in two neighboring countries they hadn't consulted.

Does this count as a legitimate decision under my criteria? Or does the case show one of the criteria is doing too much work—maybe transparency is a luxury the drowning can't afford—or that some criterion is doing too little?
