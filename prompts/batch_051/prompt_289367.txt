"State the Condorcet jury theorem and assess whether it gives a good epistemic justification for majority rule in large electorates, as opposed to delegating decisions to a single well-informed ruler. State the theorem's main assumptions and say whether real elections satisfy them."

The student wrote:

"The Condorcet jury theorem says that if n people vote independently on a two-option question and each has a probability p > 0.5 of being right, the probability that the majority is right is at least p, and this probability tends to 1 as n grows. So the majority is always more reliable than any individual voter, which means a large electorate should beat even a knowledgeable single ruler. The key assumption is independence, and in the theorem independence just means the voters don't talk to each other or pressure each other before voting. The theorem also doesn't require equal competence: as long as the average voter is better than chance, the majority is more reliable than each individual voter and its reliability still goes to 1 as the group grows. Real elections fit this well enough: they are binary choices, voters use their own private information from daily life, and the average voter is more likely right than wrong about which party will govern better, so the theorem does justify majority rule over rule by one expert."
