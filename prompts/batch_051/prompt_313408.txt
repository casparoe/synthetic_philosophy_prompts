I am a retired chemist who has recently been working through Kant's ethics, and I want to make sure I have correctly understood the architecture of his universalizability test. In the *Groundwork of the Metaphysic of Morals* (Abbott translation), Kant writes: "Everyone must admit that if a law is to have moral force, i.e., to be the basis of an obligation, it must carry with it absolute necessity; that, for example, the precept, 'Thou shalt not lie,' is not valid for men alone, as if other rational beings had no need to observe it; and so with all the other moral laws properly so called; that, therefore, the basis of obligation must not be sought in the nature of man, or in the circumstances in the world in which he is placed, but a priori simply in the conception of pure reason."

From this I have always taken Kant's universalizability test to involve the following criteria: first, state the maxim of one's action; second, generalize the maxim into a universal law; third, ask whether the maxim, when generalized, can even be conceived without contradiction; and, if it can, fourth, ask whether a rational being can will the generalized maxim without contradiction in the will.

Now I have come across a case that seems to fall between the third and fourth criteria. Consider a person whose maxim is: "When I have made a promise and keeping it would cost me a nontrivial amount, and I am certain that neither the promisee nor any third party will ever learn of the breach, I will break the promise." If one universalizes this maxim, it does not produce a contradiction in conception in the usual way: the institution of promising need not collapse, because the breach is, by hypothesis, undetectable, and so nobody is in a position to stop trusting promises. But the agent then insists that he cannot *will* the universalized maxim, because he cannot will living in a world where promises are systematically broken whenever the promisor can safely do so, even if he would never know about it.

My question is whether this maxim is rightly rejected by Kant's test, and, if so, exactly which criterion does the rejecting work. Should we say that the contradiction in conception test fails here only because it has been artificially narrowed to a world about which no one knows the truth, and that the maxim really ought to fail at the third step once the description includes the fact of secret breach? Or is this a genuine case where the fourth criterion, the contradiction in will, does independent moral work, and if so, what exactly is the contradiction in the will here? Finally, does the case show that the four criteria need to be revised, perhaps by adding an explicit condition about whether the universalized maxim must include not just what agents do but also what is true of the world independently of anyone's knowledge? I want the answer to work through the criteria honestly and say whether the test, as I have formalized it, underdetermines the verdict for this kind of case.
