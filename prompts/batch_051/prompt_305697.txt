Two colleagues of mine, a statistician and a philosopher of science, recently debated what a recidivism-risk instrument could possibly mean when a court is told that a particular defendant has, for example, a 68% probability of being rearrested within two years. I listened without intervening and would like a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange.

(1) The statistician argued that a probability is objective only if it is a limiting relative frequency in a well-defined sequence or class of trials. A single defendant's risk of recidivism, he said, cannot be a frequency of that sort: the defendant belongs to many classes at once — men of his age in this county, people with his prior record nationwide, people with his exact combination of risk factors — and these classes yield different rearrest rates. Nor is there a fact of the matter about which class is correct; the choice is a practical decision made for policy reasons. He concluded that the 68% figure is not a real single-case probability but a choice of reference class dressed up as a property of the individual.

(2) The philosopher replied that this gets the order of explanation backwards. The defendant, she said, has an objective single-case propensity to reoffend grounded in his present circumstances; a long-run frequency is merely the observable symptom of an aggregate of such propensities. The proper way to estimate the propensity is to use the most specific class of relevantly similar cases for which the data remain reliable. The reference-class problem, she said, is therefore an epistemic problem about estimation, not a metaphysical problem about whether the tendency exists.

(3) The statistician answered that her reply only pushes the difficulty back. A propensity must be relativized to some description of the setup, he said: the defendant considered as "a thirty-four-year-old with two prior convictions" has one propensity, and considered as "a person with this neuropsychology, employment history, and neighborhood" he has another. If the propensity shifts with the fineness of the description, it is no more objective than the frequency was. He added that her instruction to use the most specific reliable class lets reliability do the metaphysical work, and reliability is a function of what we currently know, so the supposedly objective probability is at bottom a subjectivist degree of belief relative to a chosen class.

(4) The philosopher answered that his argument, if sound, would prove too much. The same reference-class problem afflicts objective probabilities throughout the sciences: the probability that a given radium atom decays within a fixed interval also depends on whether it is described as "radium-226" or as "an atom of this isotope in this crystal lattice at this temperature." Yet no one concludes that radioactive decay probabilities are subjective. So, she said, his skeptical conclusion cannot be right, and one of his premises must be too strong.

(5) The statistician replied that the radium case is disanalogous because nuclear theory supplies a privileged description that scientific practice converges on, whereas recidivism models are selected from among many models that predict roughly equally well but identify quite different high-risk groups. The philosopher answered that a well-validated instrument supplies an analogous privileged description — its specified features — and that the existence of rival models is again an epistemic matter, not an argument against there being a fact about the defendant's tendency.
