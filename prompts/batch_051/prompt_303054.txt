I'm in a ML fairness reading group and everyone treats the Kleinberg–Mullainathan–Raghavan impossibility result as the end of every argument about competing fairness criteria. I finally sat down with the paper and want to pressure-test it rather than just cite it. The authors write:

"Recent discussion in the public sphere about algorithmic classification has involved tension between competing notions of what it means for a probabilistic classification to be fair to different groups. We formalize three fairness conditions that lie at the heart of these debates, and we prove that except in highly constrained special cases, there is no method that can satisfy these three conditions simultaneously."

I know the formal result is fine: roughly calibration, balance for the positive class, and balance for the negative class cant all hold at once outside degenerate cases. My question is: as a position about the world and about the design of risk scores, what would actually count against it? I dont mean mathematical counterexamples to the theorem. I mean the uses people put it to—the claim that fairness tradeoffs are inherent, that satisfying multiple intuitive fairness conditions is hopeless, that we should stop looking for ways around this. What assumptions of the setup are doing the real work, what would the decision problem or the world have to look like for each to be a bad model, and which of those escape routes are live rather than merely logically possible? Are there kinds of empirical distributions, label-generating processes, or intervention strategies whose existence would make the impossibility conclusion less important in practice, or show that a decision system could still be fair in a thicker sense? I want a map of the position's exposure: from fatal defeater to awkward result, including what a committed defender would have to concede counts against them and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. And if it has, is that a problem for this kind of formal impossibility result?
