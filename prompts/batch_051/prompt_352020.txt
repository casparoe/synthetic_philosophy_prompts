My colleague in an art-school seminar defends what she calls the decisional-texture thesis: a video game is aesthetically good or bad, as art rather than as a pastime, chiefly in virtue of the structure of choices it presents, and not in virtue of its visuals, audio, story, or atmosphere. On her view, a game with crude graphics and no plot could be a serious aesthetic achievement if its decision space is finely shaped, while a visually magnificent game whose choices are trivial, merely cosmetic, or illusory would be aesthetically second-rate. I find the thesis half credible and half metaphysically overweening, and I want to know whether any observation could adjudicate it rather than merely illustrate it.

How would one test the decisional-texture thesis? I would like a concrete experimental design — ideally one that holds audiovisual and narrative materials as fixed as possible while systematically enriching or degrading the decision structure, with judgments collected from both players and passive viewers of the same recorded session. Please specify which pattern of results would count in favor of the thesis and which would count against it, and then say exactly what even a favorable result would leave unsettled: for instance, whether subjects are tracking aesthetic value or only enjoyment, mastery, or investment in their own choices, and whether a spectator's judgment can ever be evidence about the aesthetics of agency at all.
