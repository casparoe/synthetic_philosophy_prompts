I am a novelist, and I have been in a long-running disagreement with a friend who lectures in philosophy about what, if anything, fictional names refer to. He recently put to me what he called the received philosophical position, roughly as follows. Because no such person as Sherlock Holmes has ever existed, the name "Sherlock Holmes" does not refer to anything, and sentences such as "Sherlock Holmes was a detective" are either outright false or true only inside a pretence. What is true in the stories is fixed by what the author intended the text to convey, or at best by what the text explicitly states or formally implies. Consequently, claims about a character made outside the canonical texts — that Holmes is the most famous detective in fiction, or that he is a kinder man than Conan Doyle intended — are at bottom either false or loose talk, kept respectable only by our shared make-believe. By the same reasoning, he said, there can be no genuine disagreement over matters the stories leave unsettled, such as whether Holmes was happy in his Sussex retirement.

I found myself resisting this characterisation at several points I cannot yet articulate technically. As a novelist, "Holmes is a fictional character" does not feel like an error explained away; it feels exactly as true as any sentence about me. I also doubt that a story's content is fixed by authorial intention, since I know how far a written text can outrun its author's plans, and how much readers, adaptors and later writers legitimately add to a character's history. My friend has asked me to say where I think he has gone wrong, so the help I need is an accuracy audit of his summary. Which parts are accurate, and which parts run together positions that careful philosophers keep apart — for example, the question of how an empty name manages to mean anything and the question of how a fiction acquires its content? Must everyone in the field accept that the name is empty, or is there a live view on which characters are created, artefact-like entities with a real-world career? And do his concluding claims — that metafictional remarks such as "Holmes is more famous than any real Victorian detective" can only be loose talk, and that questions the stories do not settle have no genuine answers — survive a corrected statement of the view? I want to know what the fair version of the position commits me to, and which parts of my instinctive resistance are worth keeping.
