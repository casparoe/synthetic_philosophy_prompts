So I sit on my university's academic integrity board and we have to rule on an appeal from a student who got caught using her phone during a closed-book physiology exam. She has a documented working-memory deficit and already gets extra time as an accomodation. What she was doing on the phone was scrolling a notes app where she keeps all the pathways and mechanisms she can't reliably hold in her head; she's used the same system for years, updates it daily, and treats it as her memory. Her appeal is basically the extended mind thesis. She says the phone is coupled to her routines tightly enough that it's doing the same job internal recall does for other students, and the only reason the exam counts it as cheating is that the storage happens to be outside her skull. She even ran the parity argument: if she'd memorized the notes, nobody would blink, so treating the phone differently is just skin-and-skull prejudice about where cognition has to end. A friend and I went back and forth about this for hours last night. He kept saying the committee's job is to enforce the rules, not adjudicate metaphysics. I kept saying you can't decide whether she 'cheated' without some view about what counts as the student's own knowledge in the first place. So I need to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's vote. Draft the memo for me. It should recommend a concrete decision — uphold the cheating finding, overturn it, or some middle path, with whatever penalty or accomodation changes follow — and it should engage the strongest version of her position head-on rather than just citing the no-electronics rule. If you think the extended mind parity argument does real work here, say exactly where it bites and where it stops; if you think it's a red herring, say why. Keep the bottom line crisp, and dont make it sound like a philosophy seminar or the rest of the board will tune out.
