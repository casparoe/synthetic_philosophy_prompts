Two classmates of mine got deep into Montaigne's essay Of Cannibals after we'd read a paragraph near the start where he says, roughly, that we have no other test of truth and reason than the example and pattern of the opinions and customs of the country where we live, and that everyone calls barbarous whatever is not his own practice. Leah said that's the key: barbarism is just the name a culture gives to difference, so there is no cross-cultural standard by which to condemn the Tupinambá. Dan said that can't be the whole story, because Montaigne goes on to judge them anyway — he says they roast and eat their prisoners for revenge, not nourishment, he admires their courage, and he treats their social arrangements as in some ways healthier than Europe's with its inequality and hypocrisy. Leah replied that praising them is still part of a relativistic critique: he's flipping European self-assurance on its head, not building a positive moral standard. Dan said her reading makes him incoherent, since he denies any test of truth and reason but then makes evaluative comparisons throughout. Leah said he isn't denying that we make evaluations, only denying that our local custom supplies a neutral test; the evaluations are real but situated. Dan asked how a situated evaluation can still criticize his own culture as worse without smuggling in some standard. I'm supposed to referee but I couldn't. Please go through their moves one by one and rate each as mistaken, weak, fair, or strong, then say who's more right about the passage and about the essay as a whole, and whether Dan's charge of incoherence actually lands.
