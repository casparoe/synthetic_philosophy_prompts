I'm designing a unit on sequential decision-making and want to teach Christopher Nolan's Memento alongside the absent-minded driver problem, and I'd like a careful reading of the film through that lens — one that says where the analogy genuinely illuminates the film, where it strains or misleads, and what the film adds to the decision-theoretic puzzle.

As I understand the puzzle: a driver must take the second of two identical exits to get home; exiting at the first, or driving past both, leaves him worse off than exiting at the second. Because he is absent-minded, his information at the first exit and at the second exit is exactly the same, so he cannot make his choice depend on which exit he has reached. He must therefore settle in advance on one policy — say, a fixed probability of exiting that he applies each time he reaches an exit. The central question is whether the policy it is rational to choose before the trip is also the policy it is rational to execute once he is standing at an exit he cannot identify. Some read the problem as showing that a rational agent should bind himself to an ex ante plan and stop re-deliberating, while others read it as revealing an inconsistency in that advice, because from inside the trip he might reason differently about where he is and what to do.

Memento seems to literalize this structure. Leonard cannot form new memories, so he writes instructions to himself — Polaroids, handwritten notes, tattoos — and at each moment he is effectively at an information set he cannot distinguish from earlier ones: he does not know whether he has already found the man he is hunting, which people his own earlier self decided to trust, or which of his standing instructions were left by a clear-sighted plan rather than by a deceived or impulsive moment. His only workable policy is to keep executing what his past self wrote down. But the film seems to complicate the puzzle in ways the formal model does not: the absent-minded driver can trust his ex ante preferences and his own plan, whereas Leonard inherits policies from a past self whose evidence, goals, and sobriety he cannot reconstruct, and other agents — Natalie, Teddy — can deliberately shape what that past self records.

I'd like you to work through three questions. First, where does the absent-minded-driver lens genuinely illuminate the film — which choices, scenes, or mechanisms become visible under it that a discussion framed only around memory and personal identity would miss? Second, where does the analogy mislead if pressed? Is "can't tell which exit this is" actually the same predicament as "can't verify the provenance of my own instructions," and what does the film show that the model omits? Third, does the film take or dramatize a position on the puzzle's question — binding versus re-deliberating — and what does it contribute to the philosophical debate that the thought experiment alone cannot? I want a reading that is fair in both directions: it should not reduce the film to an illustration of a decision theory result, and it should not distort the puzzle to fit the film. Please avoid relying on verbatim quotations; work from the film's overall structure and well-known scenes.
