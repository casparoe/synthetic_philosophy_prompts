I've been reading the self-deception debate the way I read compatibilism for the first time: each side is using different acceptance conditions and mostly talking past the other. I want the full specification before I update. Write the desiderata that a theory of self-deception must meet if it is to count as an adequate account of the target phenomenon—an account of it as a distinctive cognitive and ethical failure rather than a special case of ordinary error. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that earns it a place on the list, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a camp-specific preference (intentionalist, deflationary, divided-mind, and so on).

Build the list around at least these cases, and add others only if they force a requirement into view: the jealous spouse who somehow both knows and does not know; the student who arranges never to see the answer key until she has settled on her preferred answer; the patient who misreads a diagnosis letter; the person who hands the letter to a friend and asks to be told only the good parts; and the person whose sincere self-report sits in stable conflict with their own behavior. I also want the empirical side handled explicitly rather than quietly assumed: any theory that requires a literal simultaneous pair of contradictory standing beliefs should have to say what architecture permits that, and any theory that denies genuine inner conflict should have to say why self-deception is still a distinct failure worth a distinct name, rather than merely bad reasoning or self-serving bias.

Then address the two high-risk parts. First, flag any subset of the desiderata which you think cannot be jointly satisfied. The pairs I can already see pulling against each other are: the requirement that self-deception be something a person does, enough to ground blame, against the requirement that it be a subpersonal, largely automatic bias, enough to be real and widespread; and the requirement of genuine inner conflict against the requirement of psychological realism. Say whether these are genuine trade-offs a theory must navigate, and which side, if either, should bear the burden of proof. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and explain why.

Close, briefly, by scoring the three main families—intentionalist, deflationary motivated-cognition, and divided-mind—against the list. Keep that part short; the criteria are the deliverable, not the scorecard. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not let a contested view pass as the consensus.
