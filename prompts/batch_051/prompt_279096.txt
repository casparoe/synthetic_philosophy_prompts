I have been working through the literature around Aumann's agreement theorem and keep hitting the same puzzle. The formal result is not in dispute: if two agents share a common prior, form posterior probabilities for some event by conditionalizing on their private information, and those posterior probabilities are common knowledge, then the posteriors must be equal. Yet the philosophical argument about what this shows for real disagreement—religious, political, scientific—has not moved in decades, and the same moves keep recurring.

One camp reads the theorem normatively: agents who satisfy the conditions cannot rationally agree to disagree, so the persistence of disagreement among people who have fully exchanged their views is evidence that someone is irrational or is not integrating private evidence. A second camp treats the theorem as a reductio of the common-prior assumption: evidently rational people do agree to disagree, so the shared prior must be the idealization doing all the work. A third camp argues that the theorem is nearly silent about actual disputes because common knowledge of posteriors almost never obtains outside constructed cases.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse has a specific structure. The camps are not disagreeing about the mathematics, and not ultimately about the correct theory of rationality. They are disagreeing about what would make the theorem's assumptions satisfied and about what kind of work an idealization is allowed to do in social epistemology. In particular, I suspect the notion of a common prior is doing double duty: some participants hear it as identical starting information, others as identical starting beliefs, and these come apart once agents can differ in how they weight the same evidence. If that is right, the dispute is partly verbal and partly a clash of standards for applying formal results to real agents.

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a survey of the positions and not another adjudication of the theorem. Is the dispute verbal, substantive, or a clash over standards of idealization? Are the camps answering different questions under one name? Is a hidden empirical question—such as how often real disagreement survives a genuine exchange of beliefs—or a hidden formal question doing the work? Given the diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal extension, an experiment, a changed question, or an argument that no progress should be expected?
