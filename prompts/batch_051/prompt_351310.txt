I am currently taking a seminar in the philosophy of the social sciences, and a claim that keeps recurring in discussion is that fields whose practitioners get fast, unambiguous feedback about error—engineering, surgery, molecular biology, machine learning—accumulate reliable knowledge faster than fields where error signals are slow, noisy, or absent, such as macroeconomics, educational policy, and much of the social sciences. My instructor often treats this as nearly self-evident, but it strikes me as asserted far more often than it is tested. I want to know how much of it can be brought under empirical control. Suppose the claim is this: the speed and clarity with which a field corrects its errors is a major, perhaps the major, determinant of how quickly that field makes genuine progress. Design the strongest feasible empirical test of that claim. Be concrete about the comparison—across fields, across time within a field, across matched problems, or some combination—and about what you would measure as feedback quality and as progress; say what pattern of results would count for and against the claim. You might, for instance, track a set of open problems from a fixed year and measure time-to-consensus, or exploit a natural experiment in which a single field's feedback became markedly clearer; choose the strongest approach and defend it. Then say what the test would still leave open: whether progress can be measured without importing the standards of the field under study, how to handle selection effects and differences in intrinsic problem difficulty, and whether a correlation plus a plausible mechanism amounts to a causal explanation or only a redescription. I am not after a yes-or-no verdict on the claim; I want a design that makes visible exactly where the empirical evidence ends and the conceptual or causal assumptions begin.
