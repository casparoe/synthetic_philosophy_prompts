I've been turning over a chain of reasoning about decision-making under total ignorance, and it feels like I've proven something too strong, which means I've probably cheated somewhere. Can you tell me exactly where it breaks?

Setup: by genuine ignorance I mean you face a choice between actions, there are possible states of the world that determine the outcomes, and you have no probability distribution over those states — not even a rough subjective one. Think choosing a caching strategy when you know literally nothing about the request distribution.

Here's the argument:

(1) Expected utility maximization needs a probability distribution over states; without one the expectation is undefined.
(2) Under genuine ignorance, you have no probability distribution over states.
(3) So expected utility maximization just isn't available to a decision maker under genuine ignorance.

(4) Maximin — pick the action whose worst possible outcome is best — requires nothing probabilistic; it only looks at each action's minimum payoff.
(5) Maximin is the only decision rule that works without a probability distribution. Anything else either smuggles probabilities in (like assigning equal probability to every state) or turns out to be maximin once you work it out.
(6) So under genuine ignorance the only coherent choice rule is maximin.

(7) But then in the original position behind the veil of ignorance — the setup where you're supposed to pick principles of justice without knowing which person in society you'll be — rational people would have to use maximin. Rawls wins outright, and Harsanyi's expected-utility approach (treat every position as equally likely) is flat-out irrational, not just a different tie-breaker.

The conclusion in (7) is suspiciously clean to me, and I'm told its exactly the kind of move Harsanyi would have called a mistake, but I can't pinpoint the flaw. I've also heard there's a rule called minimax regret where you pick the action whose worst regret is smallest, and it supposedly doesn't need probabilities and isn't the same as maximin.

So: is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what is the strongest corrected version of that step? If it is valid, does that mean Harsanyi-style equal-probability reasoning is just a disguised probability assignment and therefore off the table?
