I keep finding myself thinking there's a deep parallel between the socialist calculation debate and the Bitter Lesson in machine learning, and I can't tell whether I've found something real or am just pattern-matching. Here's the analogy. Hayek and Mises argued that central planning fails because the knowledge needed to run an economy—the fine-grained, local, often tacit stuff about particular circumstances of time and place—is scattered across millions of people and cannot be assembled in one place. Prices, by contrast, are a way of getting that dispersed knowledge to flow into a single usable signal through the dumb, massively decentralized process of people exchanging things. Lange's reply was basically: fine, keep prices, but let a central board set them and adjust them in response to shortages and surpluses, so you get market-like calculation without private ownership. Now the Bitter Lesson (the Rich Sutton essay—I'm paraphrasing, not quoting) says something structurally similar: over and over, approaches that lean on massive general computation and learning from data beat approaches that try to build human knowledge into the system by hand. The knowledge is in the data, and you extract it by running a dumb algorithm at scale, not by sitting a smart designer at the center who writes down what we know.

So the mapping I see is: Hayek's dispersed knowledge is the thing the hand-coded system can't represent; market prices are the learned, massively decentralized representation of it; Lange's price-setting board is the expert-system, good-old-fashioned-AI approach that keeps trying to do explicitly and centrally what should be left to an implicit, distributed process. My question: is this a genuine identity of structure, or does it fall apart as soon as you push? I know one obvious disanalogy is that Lange's trial-and-error price adjustments are themselves a kind of learning, so maybe Lange is closer to the scaling side than I'm making him. And markets don't optimize a single scalar objective the way training loss does. So give me a commited verdict rather than a survey of options: say whether the analogy is deep or superficial, state exactly what would have to be true on each side for results to transfer, and tell me the one or two places where it most cleanly breaks.
