I write literary fiction, and I keep coming back to a line of thought that flatters my whole profession: that no amount of scientific explanation can get inside what an experience is like, and that's what fiction is for. So when a book on consciousness I was reading gave the Mary's room argument, I wanted it to be true. Its summary ran something like: 'Frank Jackson's thought experiment proves physicalism about the mind is false. Mary is a neuroscientist who knows every physical fact about color vision but has never seen color. When she leaves her black-and-white room and sees red, she learns what red looks like. Since she knew every physical fact already, this new bit of knowledge can't be physical—so there is at least one non-physical fact about the mind. This means no description of brain processes, however complete, could ever explain consciousness.' I can't shake the feeling that the last sentence especially is doing more work than the thought experiment can support, but I cant trace the slip. Could you audit that paragraph for me? Which claims would a careful defender of the knowledge argument accept, which ones would a physicalist reject, and where does the summary slide from what Mary learns to what science can and can't do? I'm less interested in a textbook verdict than in knowing whether my novelist's intuition really is licensed by the argument or whether I've been borrowing philosophical authority I don't have.
