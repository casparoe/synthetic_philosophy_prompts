I got into a late-night argument about the doomsday argument, and now I'm stuck on the difference between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption, and I'm in a wierd spot. SSA says I should reason as if I'm randomly selected from all actual observers in my reference class. That feels basically right in small anthropic cases, but it also seems to be what gives the doomsday argument its teeth. SIA says instead that, all else equal, the fact that I exist counts as evidence for hypotheses on which more observers exist. It stops the doomsday argument dead, and if I treat my own existence as just another observation, it seems to fall out of ordinary Bayesian reasoning. So SIA is the one I'm drawn to.

But then I run through the presumptuous philosopher and I can't shake the feeling that something has gone badly wrong. Suppose there are two cosmologies, A and B, that make identical predictions about everything we can measure. A says there will have been a billion observers total; B says a trillion. Ordinarily I'd say I have exactly no reason to favor B over A. On SIA, though, my own existence is a thousand-to-one update in favor of B, because I'm a thousand times more likely to exist under B than under A. So I should end up 99.9% confident in the trillion-observer world without having made a single new measurement. But my existence is something I could never have failed to observe; there is no version of me who looks around and notices she doesn't exist. Can a fact like that count as evidence for a cosmological theory? So either SIA has to be restricted or abandoned, or I have to accept that I should be basically certain B is true for no empirical reason, or there's some reading on which both survive. Give me the strongest version of each option and tell me which one you'd give up.
