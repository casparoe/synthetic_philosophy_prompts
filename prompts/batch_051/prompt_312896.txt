I spent forty years in a plant-biology lab and have only recently started reading philosophy, so please keep jargon minimal. These days I volunteer two evenings a week at a university listening service: short-term work, usually six sessions, with students who come in with anxiety, grief, relationship break-ups, academic panic. The stakes are modest but real — I can waste one of a student's six sessions, or send them off feeling their emotion was talked out of rather than taken seriously. And I keep noticing that the philosophy-of-emotion arguments I've been reading map onto a choice I make several times a shift without any rule. One camp says an emotion is at bottom a judgement or appraisal: my anger is the thought that I've been wronged, and it can be argued with. Another says it's a perception of value, like seeing that something is unfair or dangerous, which can be wrong but can also reveal something. The third, which I half-remember from my undergraduate days, says the feeling is mostly the perception of the body's response and the thought is a story told after the fact. I find myself flipping between these: sometimes I treat what a client says as a belief to be examined, sometimes as a perception to be checked, sometimes as a bodily state to be sat with untill it passes — and the same client in the same week can get a different treatment depending on my mood.

What I want is a decision procedure the service could actually use to train new volunteers, with no philosophy background assumed. For a given emotional episode a client reports, give the questions to ask in order to decide whether to treat it as judgement, perception, or feeling; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it hands the decision to a supervisor because the question is one of values rather than facts. The conceptual grounding should come through in the questions themselves, but a volunteer should be able to run it in about ten minutes after a session without knowing the theory. Since most real episodes are probably a muddle of all three, the procedure should also say what to do when the questions don't pick a clean bucket. Then run it on three cases: a student whose anger at their flatmate they describe as the rational conclusion from a pattern of disrespect; a student with a vague, wordless dread about their new partner; and a student in exam-term panic who reports only bodily symptoms. Include at least one case where the procedure tells me to do something I will be reluctant to do, and say plainly which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure and what the procedure cannot do.
