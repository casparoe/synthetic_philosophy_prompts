For a reading group I've been asked to write down my philosophical profile on moral uncertainty so the other members can audit it before I present. Here is the position I currently hold. (1) Uncertainty among rival moral theories is a genuine practical problem of its own kind, not reducible to uncertainty about ordinary empirical facts. (2) The right way to choose under such uncertainty is to maximize expected choiceworthiness: for each option, weight how choiceworthy it is according to each theory by my credence in that theory, and choose the option with the highest total. (3) Choiceworthiness is not intertheoretically comparable in any precise way; there is no neutral unit in which to compare how much better an action is on a deontological theory than on a consequentialist one. (4) I am more moved by avoiding serious moral wrongs than by maximizing expected choiceworthiness: if a course of action would be gravely wrong according to a theory I assign even moderate credence, that should count disproportionately in the decision, more than a linear expected-choiceworthiness calculation would give it. (5) None of this requires a special epistemology or logic, because deciding under moral uncertainty is formally the same kind of problem as deciding under ordinary uncertainty, and the standard decision-theoretic apparatus applies. Please audit this package. Go through the five claims and say which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. Rank the genuine tensions by severity; for each, say what the least costly revision would be and what it would give up; then say which single change would do the most to restore coherence. I want precision about where the conflicts really are, not a list that flags every pair.
