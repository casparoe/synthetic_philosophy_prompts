I am a graduate teaching assistant for an upper-level course in the philosophy of history, and I am marking a short take-home assignment. The assignment followed a unit on explanatory virtues, counterfactuals, and the ways retrospective knowledge can distort historical accounts. The question asked students to compare two diagnoses of distortion in historical explanation — hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy — and to say what a historian could do about each. The course has stressed that these two diagnoses are often run together in casual discussion, so the assignment was designed to test whether students can keep them apart while still explaining how they interact.

Below are the question and one student's answer. Please mark the answer out of 20, and then give the point-by-point comments that justify the mark. The comments should identify the specific claims that earn credit and the specific claims that should be penalized, and should separate a correct definition from an inference that merely sounds correct. I want a criterion-referenced marking, not a general impression of the essay.

Question: Psychologists and philosophers sometimes claim that historical explanation is systematically distorted by hindsight bias and by the narrative fallacy. Are these two diagnoses ultimately the same diagnosis under different names, or are they distinct? Answer with reference to at least one concrete historical example, and explain what, if anything, a historian could do to counteract each.

Student answer: Hindsight bias is the tendency, once an outcome is known, to believe that the outcome was more predictable than it actually was: one retroactively raises the estimated probability of what happened and forgets that other outcomes looked live at the time. The narrative fallacy is the tendency to impose a coherent story on events — selecting a few salient causes, compressing the timeline, and making the sequence look as if it moved toward its conclusion. In historical writing the two clearly travel together: the historian knows the ending before beginning to write, and that knowledge shapes both the probabilities assigned and the plot imposed.

But on reflection the two are really the same defect. Both begin from the known outcome and work backward, and both make the past look more inevitable than it was. The only difference is stylistic: hindsight bias is a distortion of probability judgments, while the narrative fallacy is a distortion of prose. A narrative is just hindsight bias written out in sentences. Consider the outbreak of the First World War. After 1914, observers treated the war as though it had been foreseeable all along; historians then assembled the alliance system, the mobilization timetables, and the July Crisis into a single chain that made the war seem unavoidable. That is hindsight bias (the war was not nearly so predictable before the fact) and the narrative fallacy (a messy sequence compressed into one story) operating as one.

It follows that the remedy is the same for both: historians should refuse narrative. They should report events chronologically, without selecting causes or arranging the material toward an outcome. Any attempt to tell a story reintroduces the bias; the only honest history is a chronicle.
