I've been stuck on a reconstructed version of the argument Popper gives against laws of history, and I need to know whether it honestly works. Here's how I'd lay it out:

(1) The future course of human history depends in large part on the future growth of scientific and technological knowledge. (Try describing the twentieth century without nuclear physics, antibiotics, or the computer; you basically can't.)

(2) The future growth of knowledge cannot be predicted. If you could formulate today the content of tomorrow's discoveries, those discoveries would already be known now; and if you can't formulate that content, you haven't predicted it.

(3) So any law of historical development would have to be a law covering events that are partly shaped by an unpredictable process—the growth of knowledge.

(4) A genuine scientific law has to be able to support predictions of the events it covers; a "law" that is in principle barred from predicting those events isn't a law at all.

(5) Therefore, there can be no scientific laws of historical development; the whole project of historicism is impossible.

Step 2 seems undenyable to me, but people I've shown this to say the whole thing is too slick, and I can't tell whether they're right. Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and what a defensible version of it would say. If it is valid, tell me whether the conclusion is actually as strong as it sounds, or whether "prediction" is doing sneaky double duty somewhere. Don't talk about what Popper "really meant"—just evaluate the argument as written.
