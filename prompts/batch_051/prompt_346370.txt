I run a little discussion group — about twelve people, mostly LessWrong/ACX-reading types — and we've been going in circles on whether thought experiments in philosophy are doing evidential work or just giving a vivid costume to whatever intuition we already had. I've been rereading Mach's chapter on thought experiments in Knowledge and Error (1905), and what stuck with me is his claim that thought experiments are continuous with real experiments: the method is the same variation of circumstances, only carried out in imagination, and the main advantage is that it lets us test a possibility at a fraction of the cost. But I'm not sure I buy it — a real experiment can surprise me, whereas a thought experiment seems to just unfold what I packed into the setup. So I've been trying to separate three possible roles: thought experiments as evidence, as illustration, and as intuition pumps. The group has already argued about Thomson's violinist, Mary the color scientist, and Searle's Chinese room a few times, but we've never examined how the thought experiment itself is doing whatever it's doing.

I want an activity that makes participants feel the difference between those three roles, not just hear a lecture about it. Twelve people, two hours, a room with a whiteboard. They should come away able to look at an unfamiliar thought experiment and ask: what would have to be true for this to count as evidence? and what is the intuition doing here? Ideally, by the end they should be able to reconstruct Mach's continuity claim and either defend it or say precisely where it breaks. I'm open to small-group games, forced choices, role-play, or a structured debate, but the distinction should emerge from what they do before it's named. Include rules or materials, a timeline, a debrief that maps moments in the activity onto the three roles and Mach's continuity claim, what will predictably go wrong the first time, and an honest note on which feature of the distinction the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself. At the very end, make the last line a one-sentence summary, like "Summary: ...".
