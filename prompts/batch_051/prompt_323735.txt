Last night I ended up having a long, slightly beery argument with my flatmate about the reference class problem, and it's still rattling round my head. The question we kept butting against was whether there's any principled way of choosing the reference class for a one-off probability judgement — say, the probability that my uncle, who's 64, has high blood pressure, and hasn't excercised since about 2019, will have a heart attack in the next ten years. You can put him in lots of different classes — men over 60, men with hypertension, men with hypertension who don't exercise, men with hypertension who don't exercise and eat badly — and the numbers from reliable data will differ depending on which one you pick. He reckons there's a sensible rule: pick the narrowest reference class with reliable statistics. I kept objecting that 'narrowest' and 'reliable' often pull in opposite directions, and that there can be several equally narrow, equally well-evidenced classes that give conflicting numbers, and then what? We also kept circling the question of whether the right answer depends on what the probability is for — an actuary pricing insurance, my uncle deciding whether to bother with statins, a doctor giving a rough prognosis.
