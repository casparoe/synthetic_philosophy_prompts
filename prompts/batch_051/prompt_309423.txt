I am a novelist working on a historical novel about a small rural community during the enclosure of common lands in eighteenth-century England. The novel must stay faithful to the documentary record, but I find that arranging the same verified events into a story inevitably imposes an arc—tragic, ironic, or comic—and I cannot tell whether that imposition is merely presentational or whether it changes the historical claim itself. I keep thinking about Leopold von Ranke's old insistence that history's aim is to present the past as it really occurred, without the historian's own passions or inventions, and about the later scepticism of E. H. Carr and Hayden White, who held that a bare record of events is not yet history and that narrative form itself carries interpretive, even fictive, force. I want a thought experiment that isolates exactly where these two positions part company, and I want it built to my needs as a novelist.

Construct a thought experiment in which two historical accounts of the same event are factually identical—every dated occurrence, every attributed statement, every economic detail stipulated to be the same—but differ only in emplotment: one casts the event as a tragedy, the other as a comedy of errors. The event should be ordinary and small, something like a failed local petition, a short-lived strike, or a disputed inheritance; no battles, no famous figures, no world-historical turning points. The two accounts must not differ in any causal assertion unless that causal assertion is already inferable from the stipulated facts; the difference should lie entirely in what White would call the narrative mode.

The case should be engineered so that, on the first version, a strict Rankean and a Whitean agree about which account is the better history, though for different reasons. Then add a third account that changes only one narrative feature—perhaps the ordering of episodes, the assignment of a central protagonist, the placement of a turning point, or the tone of the ending—while leaving the factual record untouched. Make this third account the point at which the two views diverge: the Rankean should say the change is historically significant and the account is now worse, while the Whitean should say the change makes no difference to the account's historical adequacy because the facts are unchanged and the new emplotment is just another legitimate way of configuring the same record. Or make them diverge in the opposite direction, if that is more faithful to their positions.

After you have constructed the scenario, walk through the reasoning each view would give at each step: what the Rankean treats as the constraint that narrative form must respect—a real causal hierarchy among the events, a real sequence of choices and consequences, or the avoidance of anachronistic emotional colouring—and what the Whitean treats as the unavoidable work of emplotment that no mere chronicle escapes. Tell me which feature of the third account does the work: what exactly the Rankean is tracking and the Whitean is discounting. Then, because I need this for the novel, state how a defender of the losing view would attack the case, and tell me whether the feature you identified really marks a boundary between objective history and narrative invention, or whether it simply reveals that the two views are answering different questions. I want to know, in the end, whether my choice of tragic rather than comic framing for the enclosure story is a decision about truth or only about craft—and whether that distinction can survive the thought experiment you build.
