I recently joined the governing board of an old but small institution, and we are weighing whether to retire a long-standing rule that nearly everyone agrees is now costly and obscure in its purpose. The strongest voice for keeping the rule keeps returning to an image: a fence in the middle of a field should not be taken down until you understand why it was put there in the first place, because its persistence is itself some evidence that someone once had a reason for it. He gives this a Burkean gloss, treating the institution's accumulated arrangements as a repository of wisdom we should not casually discard. I am trying to determine how much actual weight this analogy can bear. Could you work through it systematically — what maps onto the fence, the field, the builder, and the act of demolition — and then show where the analogy diverges from the situation of an inherited institutional rule? In particular, when no one can reconstruct the original rationale, does the fence image license treating that ignorance as itself a reason to keep the rule, or does it quietly import an assumption that survives only while the analogy is doing the work? Please indicate which disanalogies matter for the conservative conclusion being drawn and whether that conclusion stands once the disanalogies are on the table.
