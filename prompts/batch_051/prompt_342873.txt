Here are the candidate criteria I have collected for answering the question of when Latin became Italian — that is, for saying what makes a late stage of Latin a different language rather than merely a changed version of the same language. (1) Mutual intelligibility across time: Latin ends when a speaker from an earlier stage could no longer understand the speech of a later stage. (2) Speakers' own identification: Latin ends when people cease to regard their speech as Latin and begin to regard it as the vernacular, volgare or italiano. (3) The rise of a written vernacular tradition: Latin ends when people begin deliberately composing legal, administrative, or literary texts in the vernacular rather than in Latin. (4) Accumulated structural divergence: Latin ends when enough grammatical changes — loss of case endings, development of articles, reorganization of vowels and word order — have crossed some threshold. (5) An external political or institutional rupture: Latin ends with the collapse of the Western Empire or some later break that dissolved the institutions maintaining the written standard.

Give me a sixth criterion that is genuinely different from these five — not a rewording, special case, or combination of them. Then give your honest assessment: is the new criterion any good, or does it merely relocate the problem? And take a definite stand on which of the six, on your own view, does the best job, with reasons rather than a survey of tradeoffs.
