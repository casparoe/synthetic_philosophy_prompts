In a working group on diachronic rationality, an interlocutor has been defending the following line. The money pump, he says, stands to preferences as the Dutch book stands to degrees of belief. A Dutch book argument shows that a non-probabilistic credence function is rationally defective because it licenses a package of bets that yields a sure loss no matter how the world turns out; likewise, a money pump shows that a preference ordering containing a strict-preference cycle is rationally defective because it licenses a sequence of trades that yields a sure loss no matter how the agent's preferences evolve. He concludes that vulnerability to a money pump is as good a reason to reject a cyclic preference ordering as vulnerability to a Dutch book is to reject a non-probabilistic credence function, and that a rational agent's strict preferences must therefore be acyclic.

I am not convinced that the analogy carries its argumentative load, in part because the Dutch book's bets can be offered as a single simultaneous package, whereas the money pump's trades have to be accepted one after another, with the agent in a position to see where the sequence is heading. I would like a careful audit of the analogy and of the argument built on it.

Please do the following.

First, give the mapping explicitly: what corresponds to the bookie, the agent's acceptable betting odds, the sure loss, the incoherence being diagnosed, and the norm the argument is supposed to vindicate. A table of correspondences is fine.

Second, separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended argument from the ones that do not. In particular, address at least these points: how the simultaneous character of the Dutch book contrasts with the sequential character of the money pump, and whether the agent's ability to foresee the trap and to adopt a sophisticated or resolute choice policy undermines the inference; whether the money pump targets the preference profile itself or rather a myopic choice policy or a standing disposition to accept each pairwise trade; whether bare ordinal preferences fix a willingness to pay the small amount each trade requires, or whether running the pump needs a further assumption about how preferences extend over goods and money; and whether the argument requires a strict-preference cycle rather than some weaker structure such as intransitive indifference, with what consequences for its conclusion. For each disanalogy you flag as consequential, say exactly which premise of the Dutch-book-style argument it threatens.

Third, say whether the conclusion that a rational agent's strict preferences must be acyclic survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and in what form. If it survives only in a weakened version, state the weakened principle precisely and identify the extra premise needed to restore a stronger conclusion. Also say whether exploitability itself, as opposed to incoherence or accuracy-dominance, is doing any real normative work once the disanalogies are acknowledged.

Finally, if there is a better analogy for making the same argument, present it and explain whether it supports the intended conclusion more cleanly than the Dutch book analogy does.
