Please take on the role of a professional philosopher who works on measurement, conceptual engineering, and the design of standards. I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I need your help before I write a date/time library.

The library must represent years and eras across ordinary application use, historical dating, astronomical calculation, archaeology, and long-span computations. I keep circling on three related decisions: (1) whether there ought to be a year zero at all; (2) what epoch the year-numbering scheme should count from; and (3) how the scheme should behave outside its intended range. The candidate approaches I keep seeing are:

- the historical BC/AD style, in which 1 BC is followed immediately by AD 1 with no year zero;
- astronomical year numbering, in which 1 BC is year 0, 2 BC is year −1, and ordinary integer arithmetic and divisibility rules are preserved;
- ISO 8601's proleptic Gregorian approach, which, as I understand it, also includes a year zero (0000 = 1 BC) — though I have also run into XML Schema 1.0 having no year zero and later realigning with ISO 8601;
- the Unix epoch of 1970-01-01, convenient for seconds-based computation but apparently arbitrary as a human-facing epoch;
- the Holocene/Human Era proposal, which simply adds 10,000 to AD/CE years; I have since learned that the geological start of the Holocene is now formally dated to roughly 9701 BC (11,700 years before 2000), so this epoch is about 300 years off from the event it is named after.

Before I evaluate any of these, I want the specification first. Please write the desiderata that an adequate choice of epoch and era-numbering scheme has to meet: the cases it must handle, the distinctions it cannot collapse, the arithmetic and sorting properties it should preserve, the compatibility constraints with existing data and standards, and any intended-use ambiguity it must resolve. For each requirement, give the motivating case or consideration. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp (historians, astronomers, database engineers, geologists and archaeologists, and so on), and flag any pairs of requirements that you think cannot be fully satisfied at once. Then tell me which single requirement does the most work to knock out the candidate approaches listed above, and why.

To keep it concrete, make sure the requirements cover at least: computing the elapsed time between 2 BC and AD 2; determining which BC years are leap years; sorting a mixed list of BC and AD years; handling radiocarbon-style "before present" dates where the "present" is anchored at 1950; representing dates far outside any chosen epoch; the fact that other calendar systems such as the Hebrew, Islamic, and Buddhist calendars carry their own epochs and rules, so any supposedly universal year-numbering scheme has to say how much interoperability it promises; and distinguishing "the epoch is merely an arbitrary origin" from "the epoch itself announces a world-historical or geological event." Since I have no formal philosophical training, begin with a short brainstorm of what kind of thing choosing an epoch even is — a factual claim, an arbitrary convention, a measurement standard, or some mix — and then build the requirements from there. Think carefully about edge cases rather than settling for the familiar defaults.
