Please make the case for the following deflationary view of van Fraassen's Reflection principle — the principle that a rational agent's current credence in a proposition, conditional on her later credence in that proposition being r, should itself be r: for example, if you are now certain that tomorrow you will assign probability 0.8 to rain, your present credence in rain should be 0.8. Argue that Reflection is an unstable fusion of two distinct commitments: (1) a diachronic claim which, under the standard idealization in which one's future evidence is a known partition and one updates by conditionalization on whatever is learned, is simply a consequence of that update rule; and (2) a synchronic claim that one should defer to one's future self, which is plausible only when one expects that future self to be at least as informed and reliable as one's present self. Once these are separated, make the case that the standard counterexamples to Reflection — anticipated drunkenness, expected memory loss, foreseen bias — refute only the unrestricted reading of (2), so that the surviving principle is no longer a freestanding norm of rationality. Address the natural objection that the diachronic Dutch Book argument often invoked in favor of Reflection shows it must hold even in the problem cases, and defend the deflationary view against the further objection that separating the two commitments merely changes the subject. Please answer in American English.
