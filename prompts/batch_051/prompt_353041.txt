At a recent meetup of the local rationality group, the room split cleanly between the view that rationality is just applied statistics and the view that rationality is a martial art you drill, and I noticed that neither side could actually state the standard that would settle the argument — both gestured at the same couple of transfer findings and declared victory. I am not asking you to settle the empirical dispute. I want the thing that ought to come first: the desiderata that a theory, or a training program, must meet before it can claim to be training rationality as a skill rather than teaching people facts about reasoning. Fix the purpose at the level of a serious amateur — someone who wants measurably better real-world judgment under pressure, not a professional forecaster and not an armchair epistemologist. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark clearly which are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preference. Useful places to start, if you find them compelling: tight feedback loops, ecological validity of the drills, durability over months rather than minutes, generality across domains, measurable outcomes, and whatever captures the difference between performing in the dojo and performing in the street.

Treat the transfer problem as the central constraint rather than a side note: a kata matters only if the skill survives contact with unpracticed situations. So say precisely what should count as sufficient evidence of transfer, and address whether rationality training is being held to a stricter transfer standard than scales in music or footwork drills in boxing — because if it is, that itself needs a justification. Once the list is built, tell me which single requirement would knock out the greatest share of the currently popular proposals I keep seeing recommended (one-shot debiasing workshops, pure curriculum reading, calibration and forecasting practice, mindfulness), and whether any two of the requirements are jointly unsatisfiable in the sense that no program could meet both. If the conclusion is that no existing program passes the bar, that is fine and probably expected; the bar itself is the deliverable. Keep it under 900 words; a table is fine if it helps, but the reasoning behind each criterion should be in prose.
