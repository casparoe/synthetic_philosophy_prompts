In most North American and European jurisdictions, a human being can be declared dead in either of two ways. Under the circulatory-respiratory standard, death is the irreversible cessation of heart and lung function; a patient whose heart has stopped and will not restart is dead. Under the neurological standard, death is the irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem; a patient on a ventilator whose heart is still beating, whose skin is warm, and whose body still metabolizes nutrients and heals wounds can nonetheless be declared dead and, once declared, treated as a corpse for legal purposes. Each standard has its own difficult boundary. On the neurological standard, the body is in many biological senses unmistakably alive at the moment of declaration; the claim is that the person has died even though the organism has not. On the circulatory standard, death is declared in some organ-donation protocols only a few minutes after the heart stops, before the brain's loss of function has been demonstrated to be irreversible, and in circumstances where the heart might be restarted by intervention but deliberately is not. A hospital can, in the same day, classify one body with a beating heart and a dead brain as a corpse and another with a stopped heart and a still-viable brain as a corpse, and it is pressed toward both verdicts by the dead donor rule, which permits vital organs to be removed only from patients who are already dead.

What theory of death does this legal and clinical practice presuppose? Is there a single concept of death lying behind the two standards, with the standards serving as two operational tests for one underlying state, or do the two standards commit the practice to different and possibly incompatible theories — roughly, a view on which the person is the functioning brain or mind, and a view on which the person is the integrated whole organism whose circulation must have ceased? Reconstruct the implicit theory or theories as precisely as the practice warrants, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely tolerates, and say what would have to change, in the hospital and in the law, if one standard were applied consistently.
