I've been trying to work out when trying helps and when it backfires, especially for states like being natural, calm, happy, or asleep. These cases all feel like instances of the Daoist problem of wu-wei — the paradox of trying not to try — but I don't want an account of the tradition; I want a principle that fits my own verdicts on the following cases.

1. Insomnia. When I lie down and try hard to fall asleep, I stay awake; but when I deliberately try to stay awake — the old paradoxical-intention trick — I often fall asleep quickly. Verdict: trying to sleep fails, trying to stay awake works.

2. Spontaneity. Being urged at a party to just be yourself and then trying to be natural makes me stiff and self-conscious; but if I give myself the concrete task of asking people questions and genuinely listening, naturalness tends to return on its own. Verdict: aiming at naturalness fails; aiming at absorbing activity works.

3. Anxiety. Before a talk, telling myself to calm down and not be nervous makes me more nervous; but deliberately accepting that I am anxious and trying to notice exactly where I feel it in my body tends to reduce it. Verdict: trying to suppress anxiety fails; trying to observe it works.

4. Happiness. Telling myself to be happy right now does nothing; but scheduling a run or calling a friend, which I do partly because I want to feel better, often improves my mood. Verdict: commanding the state fails; acting on its conditions works.

5. Performance. A golfer who tells himself not to hit the ball into the water tightens up and hits it into the water; focusing on one simple mechanical cue about his grip works better. Verdict: a negative self-instruction fails; a narrow positive focus works.

6. Forgetting. Trying not to think about a painful memory makes it intrude more; deliberately thinking about it with a therapist over time reduces its grip; distracting myself with work gives short-term relief but the thought returns. Verdict: suppression fails; deliberate exposure works eventually; distraction is mixed.

7. Grace. On the dance floor, trying to be graceful makes me self-conscious and stiff; practicing the steps until they are automatic eventually produces grace, as long as mid-performance I am not aiming at being graceful. Verdict: practice works; aiming at grace during the performance fails.

Please state the best principle you can find that captures the relation between trying and these states, say which of my verdicts it forces you to reject or modify if any, and tell me what it predicts about the case I genuinely cannot settle: a writer who claims he can only write well when he is not trying to write well. Is he reporting a real regularity about his writing, and if trying to write well does interfere with writing well, what kind of trying, if any, would help him?

I'd like the response in American English.
