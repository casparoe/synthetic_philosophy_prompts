Take the post-Katrina price-gouging fights — gas stations and hotels charging multiples of their normal rates, bottled water and ice selling for several times the usual price, and the outrage and anti-gouging enforcement that followed — and analyze them through the lens of repugnant markets and commodification. I want the analysis to run in both directions. First: what does that framework say the actual wrong was — brute repugnance at trades people won't accept, a commodification worry about pricing something whose meaning changes once it is sold, an extension of the logic used against kidney sales, or some mix — and, by its lights, were the anti-gouging laws aimed at the right thing? Which of the real complaints about gouging does it handle cleanly, and which does it have to strain to capture (like the sense that something was desecrated, not merely overpriced)? Then reverse it: does Katrina make repugnance and commodification look like the right framework, or does it mostly show that repugnance-talk was doing vague work and the real issue was something the theory has no name for? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating of the question.
