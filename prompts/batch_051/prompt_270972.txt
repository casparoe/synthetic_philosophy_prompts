I have been defending a principle I call "origin latitude." The idea is that origin essentialism is not all-or-nothing: what is essential to an individual's coming-to-be is not its origin described at maximum precision, but its origin described at a certain granularity, and that granularity is fixed by the kind of thing it is. For a cast bronze statue, the latitude is narrow: it could not have been cast from an entirely different ingot, even though it could survive small-scale replacement later. For an organism, the latitude is coarser: I could not have had different parents, but it seems wrong to me to say that if my parents had conceived at a slightly different moment, a different sperm from my father would have meant that I never existed — that alternative is still within my origin latitude, because what matters for a biological individual is lineage, not token gametes. The difficult case is identical twinning: one sperm and egg, yet two persons, which suggests token origins settle identity less than the Kripkean picture assumes. Does "origin latitude" name a real distinction or just a metaphor for vague intuitions? Does it conflict with the necessity of identity or with standard origin essentialism? Where does it break, and has something like it been worked out under a better name?
