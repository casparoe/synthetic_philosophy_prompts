I've been developing a view I call the Janus account of the Church–Turing thesis, and I want it assessed honestly rather than simply placed. The view starts from the observation that the thesis is usually stated as a single claim—roughly, that every function computable by an effective procedure is computable by a Turing machine—but in practice it does two different jobs. One face is mathematical or conceptual: it legislates what is to count as an effective procedure, so a proposed method that outruns Turing computation tends to be treated not as a counterexample but as not being an effective procedure after all. The other face is physical: it conjectures that no finite physical process, however exotic, can produce behavior beyond the Turing limit. My claim is that the thesis's famous longevity comes from these two faces being fused in a way that lets each absorb problems the other cannot handle. When someone offers an allegedly incomputable mathematical function, we appeal to the physical face and say such a function could not actually be computed in this world; when someone offers an allegedly super-Turing physical process, we appeal to the mathematical face and say the process, if genuinely effective and repeatable, is already captured by the standard notion. I want to know whether this Janus structure is already the standard epistemic diagnosis under another name, or whether it collapses a distinction the relevant literature keeps separate. Give me the strongest formulation of the view, the nearest existing position characterized in substance, and the precise point at which the two faces come apart or the retreat from one face to the other becomes question-begging. I am less interested in whether you agree than in whether the account carves the matter at a real joint or merely renames a familiar ambiguity.
