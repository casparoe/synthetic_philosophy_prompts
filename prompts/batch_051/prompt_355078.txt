For my advanced undergraduate course in the philosophy of art I have been developing a stock exercise about whether moral defects can count against a work's goodness as art, and in particular about whether the maker's life should be separated from the work. I put four provisional criteria of goodness-in-art on the board: (1) formal mastery—control of the medium, structural integrity, and originality within a tradition; (2) expressive depth—the work repays sustained attention and yields more on repeated encounters rather than exhausting itself in a single effect; (3) human significance—it engages durable human concerns rather than the fashions of a moment; and (4) what I call imaginative integrity—formal choices and content answer to one another so that the work's vision seems earned rather than imposed. Against these criteria I set the following case, which I have deliberately made fictional so that no student can fall back on an existing critical consensus.

Imagine a feature film—call it Sorrow's Arithmetic—made in a fictional country in the 1970s by a director who went on to be celebrated as a formal genius. It was commissioned by the state a few years after that state carried out a massacre of a minority population, and it dramatizes the massacre as tragic necessity, portraying the victims as insurrectionists and the killings as unavoidable; its material is thus abhorrent. But no one disputes that it satisfies the first criterion: the film is visually pathbreaking, includes a forty-minute sequence shot in near-darkness that is still studied for its editing and sound design, and its construction is tightly unified. It would be hard to deny that it satisfies the second and third criteria as well: audiences report being moved rather than merely stimulated, and the film evidently concerns loyalty, sacrifice, and collective survival, however monstrously it treats them. The fourth criterion is in dispute: some students read the film as a flawlessly realized apologia, others find subtle shots of the victims' faces that undercut the narration and suggest the director was working against his commission. To complicate the matter further, the director systematically abused members of the cast during production, and later said he regarded the film as his greatest achievement and would not alter a frame.

Take each of the four criteria in turn and say how Sorrow's Arithmetic fares. Then render an overall judgment: is it, on balance, good art? Where the criteria conflict or fail to decide, say explicitly whether that is a defect in the criteria or simply a genuinely hard case. Finally, address whether the director's abuse should enter the judgment at all, or whether the film must be evaluated apart from its maker—and whether this particular case shows that the moral character of a work and the moral character of its author cannot be cleanly separated in evaluation.
