I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I also run a short seminar for reporters on how to evaluate technical claims in debates they may have to cover. As part of that seminar, I set the following written exercise. One participant submitted the answer below. Please grade the answer as if you were the instructor: assign a grade and provide a point-by-point commentary that identifies what the answer gets right and exactly where its reasoning slips. The comments should be specific enough to hand back to the student, not a general impression.

**Exam question**

In AI safety, a distinction is often drawn between *aligning* an AI system and *controlling* it. Alignment concerns what the system is trying to do: making its objectives match the objectives its operators intend. Control concerns what the system is able to do: building safeguards—monitoring, restricted action channels, sandboxing—that constrain a system's behavior if its objectives turn out not to match. In a short answer, address the following: Should safety work treat control as a substitute for alignment, or as a hedge to be used alongside it? Defend your answer.

**Student answer**

Alignment and control are different things, and the difference matters. Alignment is a property of a system's goals: an aligned system pursues the goals its operators intended. Control is a property of a system's action space: a controlled system sits inside guardrails such as monitoring, limited output channels, or a sandbox, so that even if it pursues the wrong goals, the damage it can do is constrained. The two projects interact in a fairly straightforward way. If a system were perfectly aligned, control would be redundant, because the system would never select harmful actions. Since perfect alignment cannot be guaranteed, control is normally described as a second layer that limits the damage when alignment fails. That much is standard, and I accept it.

Where I part company with the usual view is the further claim that control and alignment are complements. Alignment is in practice verified behaviorally: a system passes alignment evaluations exactly when its outputs match the intended outputs across the test battery. So alignment is really a property of a system's behavior, not a property of its inner goals. Safety is likewise behavioral: what matters is which outputs occur, not which goals produced them. It follows that a sufficiently controlled misaligned system is just as safe as an aligned one, because the guardrails prevent disallowed outputs from ever occurring. For that reason, control and alignment are substitutes—two routes to the same behavioral endpoint. Any safety gain from one can be replaced by an equivalent gain from the other, and the distinction between them is ultimately an engineering convenience rather than a principled difference.
