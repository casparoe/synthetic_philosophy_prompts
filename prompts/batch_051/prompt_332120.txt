I have recently been asked to advise the finance ministry of a small open economy on whether to introduce a retail central-bank digital currency, and in preparing for that work I returned to David Hume’s essay “Of Money” (*Essays, Moral, Political, and Literary*, Part II, Essay III). The passage that now seems central to me is this:

“This has made me entertain a doubt concerning the benefit of banks and paper-credit, which are so generally esteemed advantageous to every nation. That provisions and labour should become dear by the encrease of trade and money, is, in many respects, an inconvenience; but an inconvenience that is unavoidable, and the effect of that public wealth and prosperity which are the end of all our wishes. It is compensated by the advantages, which we reap from the possession of these precious metals, and the weight, which they give the nation in all foreign wars and negociations. But there appears no reason for encreasing that inconvenience by a counterfeit money, which foreigners will not accept of in any payment, and which any great disorder in the state will reduce to nothing.”

I want to know how far Hume’s argument here actually reaches before I use it in our deliberations. Please rank the following six claims from most to least supported by the quoted passage alone, and give a sentence or two for your placement. Where two claims seem equally well supported, say so, and if you prefer you may group them into tiers.

The claims to rank are:

(a) A rise in the price of provisions and labour that comes with greater trade and money is an acceptable cost, since it accompanies public prosperity and power.

(b) Issuing paper credit is more objectionable than increasing the supply of gold and silver, because paper credit raises domestic prices while lacking the external advantages that specie gives a state in wars and negotiations.

(c) The main benefit of an abundant stock of money is not private wealth but the weight it gives a nation in its dealings with foreign states.

(d) Governments should therefore hinder the growth of paper credit even when private banks would otherwise supply it conveniently.

(e) Money, whether specie or paper, is neutral in its long-run effect on domestic prices and wealth.

(f) Banks and paper credit should be judged favourably because they render the wheels of trade more smooth and easy.

I am less interested in whether these claims are true by modern monetary theory than in which of them Hume’s own wording best licenses.
