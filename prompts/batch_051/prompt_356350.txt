For more than a decade, decision theory and economics have hosted a stalled dispute about how to average a risky prospect. The standard expected-utility and portfolio-theoretic frameworks evaluate a gamble by an ensemble average: an expectation over all parallel possible realizations at a fixed time, weighted by their probabilities, whether that expectation is formed over wealth itself or over a utility transformation of wealth. The ergodicity critique, most closely associated with Ole Peters, replies that a single agent who repeatedly faces a gamble does not experience the ensemble average; she experiences a time average, the long-run growth rate of one realized trajectory. For multiplicative dynamics with stationary and ergodic per-period growth factors, that long-run growth rate converges to the expected logarithm of the growth factor, and the corresponding per-period growth factor is the geometric mean. The arithmetic mean and the geometric mean of the same sequence coincide only when there is no dispersion in the outcomes; with dispersion, the arithmetic mean runs above the geometric mean by the familiar Jensen gap. The canonical illustration is a repeated favorable coin toss, positive in ensemble expectation but negative in time-average growth, so that an agent who always accepts it has wealth that almost surely tends toward zero. The mathematics of the divergence is not contested. Still, the two camps have not converged on a criterion of choice. Roughly, the orthodoxy treats the time-average approach as conflating a one-shot gamble with a repeated strategy, while the critics treat the ensemble approach as answering a question about parallel worlds that a single agent can never inhabit.

My working hypothesis is that the standoff is neither a live formal disagreement nor an empirical one, but a disagreement about how to individuate a decision problem and about which probability space is the proper object of choice: the ex ante spread of parallel states at a time versus the temporal path of a single realized world. I want a diagnosis, not a survey of the positions and not a verdict on who is right. Is the dispute verbal, substantive-formal, substantive-empirical, or a case of two camps answering different questions under one heading—one about rational preference over prospects at a time and the other about long-run growth and survival? Support the diagnosis from what each side actually says rather than from a caricature, and state which claims both sides should accept once the terms are made precise. Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a theorem isolating exactly where the two criteria coincide, an experiment on actual repeated choices, a conceptual distinction about the object of evaluation, or something else. Finally, give an honest answer about whether any such development could actually end the debate, or whether the two sides should instead be understood as doing different jobs that cannot be collapsed into a single rule.
