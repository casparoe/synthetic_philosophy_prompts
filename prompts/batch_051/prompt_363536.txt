**Resultant Luck Is an Information Problem, Not a Control Problem**

The standard opening move in discussions of moral luck is to describe two agents whose conduct is identical in every respect that lies within their control, but who come out differently because of factors outside their control. Two equally careless drivers take their eyes off the road at the same instant; one happens to pass a pedestrian crossing just as a child steps out; the other drives on through an empty junction. The first is blamed severely; the second is blamed mildly, if at all. The difference, it seems, can only be luck, and yet our judgements track it. Hence the puzzle.

I want to suggest that this puzzle has been mislocated. The familiar response is to say that the outcome functions as evidence of the agent’s carelessness, so that the difference in blame is really a difference in what we know. But that response collapses immediately in the standard example, because the example stipulates that we already know the two agents’ conduct to be identical. If the outcome were merely evidence, fully informed observers would blame the two drivers equally; they do not.

A better diagnosis is that outcome does not change what the agent deserves; it changes what the agent is answerable for. Blame, as we actually deploy it, is not a single scalar quantity that measures a fixed amount of inner badness. It is a cluster of practices: reproach, resentment, demands for acknowledgement, demands for repair, expressed distrust, and changes in the relationship between the blamer and the blamed. Some of these practices track desert; others track the fact that a harm has occurred and that this particular agent is the one causally entangled with it. A child is dead in one case and not in the other, and that difference alone changes what must be said, mended, explained, or borne. The unlucky driver is not being charged a higher tax on a fixed quantity of culpability; she is being asked different questions, and rightly so.

Thought of this way, resultant luck is not a counterexample to the control condition on responsibility. It is a prediction of any view that treats blame as socially situated. The driver who kills is answerable not merely for her quality of will but for the intelligible consequences of her act, because that is what the practice of holding people answerable is for. Constitutive and circumstantial luck raise harder, genuinely control-based problems: they go to the formation and availability of the will itself, before any causal entanglement with the world. Resultant luck, by contrast, is the least troubling kind, not because it does not exist, but because what it threatens is only the hope that blame could be a pure instrument for reading off desert from inner states.
