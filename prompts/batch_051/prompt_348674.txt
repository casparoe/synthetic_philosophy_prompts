Last week at my AI reading group, two friends got into a long argument about the frame problem and I genuinely could not tell who was winning. Rob said the frame problem is basically an engineering problem from the old AI literature: once you decide that a logic-based agent has to represent which facts change and which ones don't after an action, the only real issue is how to do that efficiently without writing infinitely many frame axioms. He said the philosophical version that Dennett later made famous — how a real agent in an actual world knows which of the indefinitely many possible downstream effects are relevant — is a different, much looser problem, and mixing the two together just makes both unhelpfully vague.

Mina pushed back that this gets it backwards. She said the original McCarthy–Hayes setup already contains the relevance problem: frame axioms are needed precisely because the planner shouldn't have to recompute the truth of every sentence after every action, but the logical machinery has no native notion of "these facts are still true because this action doesn't concern them." So any proposed solution either solves a narrower formal puzzle or quietly smuggles in a relevance relation. Rob replied that nonmonotonic logics handle that: you add a default rule that facts persist unless contradicted, and the technical work is just ordering defaults correctly. Mina then brought up the Yale shooting problem: the obvious persistence defaults give the intuitively wrong answer, and fixing it requires exactly the kind of world-knowledge prioritization Rob wants to exclude as "not the real frame problem." Rob answered that the Yale shooting problem just shows the original problem was under-specified, and that relevance is only a name for ranking defaults, which is still a formal issue in principle. I had to leave before they resolved whether these are really two separate problems or one problem seen from different angles.

Please tell me who is right about which specific points and who is more right overall, as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
