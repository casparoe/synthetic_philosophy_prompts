Exam question: "Explain the paradox of self-deception, and assess at least two standard responses to it — one that appeals to a divided mind and one that gives up the requirement that the self-deceiver intentionally deceive herself. What, if anything, do these responses show about whether self-deception is possible?"

Student's anwser:

"Self-deception is usually understood as coming to believe something false, against the evidence, because the belief is somehow motivated — for example, a father who refuses to accept that his son is using drugs despite clear signs. The paradox is that deceiving someone normally requires the deceiver to know or at least believe the truth and intentionally keep it from the deceived person; but in self-deception the deceiver and the deceived are the same person, so the person would have to both know the truth and be ignorant of it at the same time. That looks impossible.

One response is Davidson's partition view. Davidson argues that the mind is not a single unified agent; it contains quasi-independent substructures, each with its own beliefs and desires, and one subsystem can keep the truth from another. On this view the person can both know and not know the incriminating facts because different parts of the mind hold the conflicting states. This solves the paradox by rejecting the assumption that there is a single self doing both jobs.

The other response is Mele's deflationary account. Mele denies that genuine self-deception requires either an intention to deceive oneself or contradictory beliefs. Instead, a desire that p biases the person's treatment of the evidence — she spends more time on evidence for p, finds it more convincing, and fails to consider alternatives — and in this way she arrives at the false belief that p without ever intending to form it. On Mele's view, then, what we call self-deception is really just wishful thinking plus motivated reasoning, not deception strictly speaking.

I think the deflationary account is right about the mechanism but it changes the subject. Both accounts leave an explanatory gap: because neither requires the person to intend to deceive herself, neither can explain the sense in which the self-deceiver is responsible for the lie she lives. What the paradox shows is that self-deception is possible only if we accept something like a divided mind; once we go deflationary, we are no longer talking about self-deception at all."
