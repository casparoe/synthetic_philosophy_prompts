I sit on the nomenclature committee of a clinical specialty society — the group that tries to keep the official names of newly described findings and procedures from spiraling into total chaos. We've been fighting for two years about a proposal pushed by a vocal minority. The proposal is to make formal a rule we've half-followed informally: any newly coined term in our specialty's official vocabulary has to be built from Latin or Greek roots (some purists on the commitee want to ban even mixed Greco-Latin hybrids), and eponyms, acronyms, loanwords from living languages, and repurposed everyday English words are all out. The stated rationale is the usual one: a term coined from dead languages is semantically transparent to specialists everywhere, stable, and doesn't smuggle in the legacy of whatever person happened to describe it first. Enforcement would run through the society's journals and conference abstracts, which would return submissions that use nonconforming new terms, plus a required terminology refresh in training programs every few years.

I've been asked to write the committee a short analysis — not about whether the rule is noble, but about how it would actually behave if adopted. Who gets to coin these terms, and what do they end up incentivized to coin? How do people game it, the true believers as much as the cynics? What happens when a ten-syllable Greek compound has to be said aloud in an emergency, or translated into languages that don't stack classical roots the way English does, or when a root picked for transparency turns out to be a misnomer the day after it's minted? And is the rule really serving neutrality and clarity, or just laundering a different kind of prestige? I'm less interested in a verdict than in the concrete failure modes and whether any of them can be patched without gutting the rule.
