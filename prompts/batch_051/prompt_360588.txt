Historians and textbook authors commonly do two things that appear to be in tension. On the one hand, when explaining a large event—the outbreak of a war, a revolution, the collapse of a government—they routinely warn that the event was overdetermined: several distinct factors were each, by themselves, enough to bring it about, so no single factor was necessary for it. On the other hand, in the very same narrative they single out one factor as the cause. It gets the chapter title; the others are described as background conditions that merely made the event possible, or as underlying structures, while the favored factor is said to have made the event happen. They may even say the war would probably have happened anyway without the triggering incident, and yet the narrative still turns on that incident as the decisive event.

What theory of causal explanation in history could make both moves defensible at once? Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, distinguishing what this practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. In particular, does selecting one cause from among several individually sufficient causes require an implicit distinction—trigger versus underlying cause, abnormal versus normal condition, or something else—and is the practice coherent only under that distinction? Finally, say what historical writing would have to look like if historians instead adopted a strictly counterfactual view of causation, on which a cause is simply a condition without which the event would not have occurred.
