I'm a PhD student in economics, mostly doing mechanism design, and I keep seeing the same gloss of Goodhart's law in ML-adjacent explainers, so I want a sanity check on how fair it is. One widely shared article says, in effect: Goodhart's law is the rule that once you turn a measure into a target it stops being a good measure. Goodhart was a Bank of England economist who noticed this about monetary policy — stable statistical relationships in the data fell apart once policymakers started leaning on them for control. But the article says the principle is completly general, and it treats Goodhart's law as something close to a theorem about optimization: any proxy, optimized hard enough, will be gamed. It rolls together hospitals gaming mortality stats, schools teaching to the test, and RL agents reward hacking as the same phenomenon, and concludes that the only safe stance is to distrust quantified targets pretty much everywhere. I suspect this crams together a few claims the literature keeps apart, and that the 'theorem' part is overblown, but I couldn't say exactly where a careful person would draw the lines. Which parts of that summary would someone who actually works on Goodhart-type failures accept, which are conflations, and does the final anti-metrics conclusion follow from the accurate bits?
