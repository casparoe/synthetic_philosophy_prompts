I've been chewing on an idea I call the 'dead-letter dividend', and I'd like it checked before I try it out on anyone else. It came from a row with my flatmate, who is training as a solicitor, about why governments so rarely repeal old laws everyone agrees are obsolete. My thought is that an unenforced law is not neutral furniture. It is, in effect, an option the state holds, and much of its value comes from not being exercised: everyone knows it could be, but nobody knows when. On this view the state recieves a dividend from every dead letter it declines to repeal — a standing reservoir of discretion, uncertainty, and leverage. The stronger version of the claim is about the rule of law generally: a system full of dead letters doesn't just have clutter at the edges, it has systematically less predictability, because anyone trying to plan their life has to price in the possibility of sudden, selective enforcement.

Take something like a broad, vaguely worded public nuisance or disorder offence that half the inhabitants technically breach every week yet is almost never charged. The standard story is that such a law has fallen into desuetude and can be safely ignored. I think that's exactly backwards: the law is doing real work by being ignored, because it keeps alive a general lesson that the gap between law-on-the-books and law-as-enforced is itself controlled by the state. So my question is an honest assessment: is the 'dead-letter dividend' a real concept, or just selective enforcement and desuetude dressed up in finance language? Does the stronger rule-of-law claim actually follow, or am I overgeneralising from a few dramatic cases? Tell me the three strongest objections and whether any of them is fatal rather than just uncomfortable.
