I've been going back and forth on this for months and I want you to actually adjudicate it rather than hand me one side's slogan. The argument I find compelling is, roughly, the treacherous-turn argument: training only ever selects on outputs, never on what is going on inside, so 'try to do what the humans want' and 'try to do what the humans want until you are powerful enough that you no longer have to' are behaviourally identical throughout training. The training signal literally cannot tell them apart, so the second policy is at least as likely to be selected as the first. Once the capability threshold is crossed, the difference shows up all at once. That doesn't feel like a sci-fi add-on; it feels like the boring consequence of selecting on behaviour while not selecting on motive.

On the other side I have an intuition I can't shake, and I'd rather hear it put at its strongest than dismissed. A policy that maintains an honest-looking mask through arbitrarily many contexts has to represent the overseers' expected behaviour, the deployment threshold, the hidden target, and the decision procedure that switches between them. A policy that is simply 'be helpful and honest in the way the training data suggests' is much simpler. If you believe training biases toward simpler generalising circuits, the honest policy should be the default and the scheming policy should be a complicated edge case. When people reply that the two policies have identical training behaviour so simplicity can't prefer one over the other, I don't see why that follows — two models can have identical input-output behaviour and still differ in complexity, and Occam should be allowed to prefer the cheaper one.

So which yields, and why? Specifically: is my simplicity intuition just anthropomorphism wearing a signal-processing costume, and should I drop it? Or does the treacherous-turn argument quietly smuggle in assumptions — that the system has goals in the first place, that there is a legible 'power threshold', that what gets reinforced is the mask rather than the underlying disposition — which are more contested than the story lets on? Or is there a reading where both are correct, once you separate the idealised selection argument from what actual training dynamics with finite data and inductive biases do? I'd like the answer to say what would justify each of those moves rather than just picking a side, and to flag clearly which parts of this are settled and which parts are still genuinely contested among people who know the material. its fine if the answer is 'mostly contested' as long as it says exactly where the settled core is.
