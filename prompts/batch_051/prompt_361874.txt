I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and next month I present a paper at a workshop on well-being and the emotions. My thesis is that the heterogeneity problem for hedonism about well-being—the view that pleasure is the sole intrinsic good—remains unsolved. Hedonic-tone accounts claim all pleasures share a common felt quality, but the alleged common tone is underdetermined by the evidence and looks like a projection from the evaluative label 'pleasant.' Attitudinal accounts avoid the phenomenology by treating pleasure as enjoyment of a state of affairs, but this buys unity at the price of kindhood: 'enjoyment' is too coarse-grained a category, and the account has not explained why that motley family of states is the good rather than a useful evaluative shorthand. The room matters. Two participants are committed hedonists—one a defender of a felt hedonic tone understood as a determinable common to bodily and intellectual pleasures, one a defender of attitudinal hedonism. A third is an affective neuroscientist who thinks the neural 'liking' system shows pleasure is a unified natural kind. Two others are agnostics who find the heterogeneity challenge appealing but worry that my argument overgeneralizes into skepticism about any evaluative kind. Please give me a preparation sheet for the question period, not a defense. Give (1) 'The thesis, to open with' (≤50 words); (2) 'Objections, in likely order' (≈500 words), each objection named and voiced as the relevant person would put it, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and a note on where each reply is weakest; (3) 'The weakest plank' (≈100 words), the part of my argument I should rehearse a ninety-second defense of; (4) 'The question to dread' (≈75 words), the objection I am hoping nobody raises, with a fallback response. Where the field genuinely divides—for example, over whether there is a single neural signature of pleasure or whether attitudinal pleasure is one attitude type rather than a loose family—mark it as contested rather than settled.
