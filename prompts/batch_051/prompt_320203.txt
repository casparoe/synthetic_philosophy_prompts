Okay so my friend and I spent a whole evening going in circles about what it takes to be complicit in someone else's wrongdoing, and I still haven't landed on an answer. The picture we loosely settled on had four requirements. One: there has to be an actual wrong done by someone else—a primary wrongdoer, not you. Two: your action has to help, embolden, enable, or in some way legitimate what they're doing. Three: you know, or are being willfully blind, that your action plays that role. Four—and this is the part he fought hardest for—you don't actually have to cause the wrong, or even change the odds on the margin. A lookout who never has to raise the alarm still counts, and so does the tenth shooter in a firing squad who deliberately misses, because they're still part of an arrangement that produces the wrong. So causation is off the table as a requirement.

Then there's my uncle. He's got a chunk of his retirement in a total-market index fund, and he knows perfectly well that the fund includes companies that, say, run private prisons with a terrible abuse record or make cluster munitions that end up used on civilians. He doesn't pick the stocks; the index just holds basically everything. When he bought in, he bought on the secondary market, so the companies never saw a cent of his money, and if he sold tomorrow their plans wouldn't shift one bit. On the other hand, he does collect dividends that trace back to exactly those profits, and he's formally a part-owner—if only through the fund. Under the four criteria, is my uncle complicit in what those companies do? Work through each criterion honestly, say where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, and then tell me whether this case shows the definition is missing something—a fifth criterion about profiting from wrongdoing, say, or about bearing ownership of it—or whether it's just unpleasant without being complicity.
