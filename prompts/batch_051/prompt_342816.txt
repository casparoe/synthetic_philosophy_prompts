I am a philosophy professor and chair of my university's Academic Integrity Board. We are deadlocked on a case that turns on the difference between lying and misleading, and I need to circulate a decision memo.

The facts are settled. A graduating senior applied for a university-administered fellowship for doctoral study. In response to a question asking him to describe his research experience and any presentations, he wrote: "I have presented my research at a national conference." The sentence is literally true. Last year he presented a poster at the annual meeting of a national professional association, and the meeting is national in every ordinary sense. But the poster session in which he participated is open to all registered attendees. There is no peer review, no acceptance decision, and no competition; the student paid a registration fee and hung a poster summarizing a term paper. In the Board's hearing he conceded that he understood the selection committee would take the phrase to refer to a peer-reviewed or selective presentation, that an accurate description of the session would have weakened his application, and that he chose the wording for that reason.

The fellowship rules provide that any applicant who knowingly supplies false information in connection with an application shall be disqualified and referred to the Academic Integrity Board. They say nothing about misleading information, misleading impressions, or omissions.

Some members of the Board argue that the student did not violate the rule: every word he wrote is true, and the rule reaches false information, not true statements that a reader happens to over-interpret. They add that no applicant should be expected to disparage his own credentials when the wording is accurate. Others argue that this is exactly the kind of dishonesty the rule exists to prevent, and that acquitting him would reward a deliberately engineered loophole. A third group is genuinely uncertain.

Draft the Board's decision memo. It should do three things. First, recommend a specific disposition: whether the student is responsible under the rule and, if so, what should follow. Second, give the reasoning, and in doing so take a clear position on the underlying question: is misleading through a literally true statement the same moral wrong as lying, a lesser wrong, or a different kind of wrong, and why should that answer govern the Board's interpretation of the rule? Third, state and answer honestly the strongest consideration favoring the disposition you reject. End by proposing language to close the gap in the rules, if you think there is one.
