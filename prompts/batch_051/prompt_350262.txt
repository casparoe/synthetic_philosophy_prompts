I am a novelist, and the book I have been failing to write for two years concerns displaced worship: the ways a person can take something finite — a child, a cause, an archive, a landscape, a talent — and make it carry the weight only a final meaning can carry, with consequences that are often tender and often ruinous. Because my characters keep doing this, and because I cannot yet tell whether my narrator should condemn them or forgive them, I have tried to set down my own commitments on the matter. I would like that set audited for consistency: which commitments pull against one another, which pairs only appear to conflict, and what the least costly repair would be. I would rather be shown where I am smuggling in a distinction than be given another survey of the topic.

Here is the set, in the order in which I arrived at it.

(1) Structural, not objectual. The wrong in a misplaced worship is not a property of the object's worth. A genuinely excellent thing — a just cause, a beloved person, a body of work — can be worshiped in the wrong way, and a mediocre thing can be loved sanely. The defect is in the structure of the valuing.

(2) Qualitative, not quantitative. The defect is not a matter of intensity or amount. A person can love with enormous heat and not be an idolater; a cool, exact, even arid dedication can be idolatrous. The decisive feature is the role the valued thing plays in the agent's wider economy of meaning, not the temperature of the feeling.

(3) A source-of-meaning test. A valued thing becomes an idol when the agent, in her practical reasoning rather than in any explicit creed, treats it as the final ground of justification: the thing by virtue of which her other activities are worth doing, the thing by reference to which losses and gains are counted as losses and gains, and the thing that is not itself held answerable to any standard independent of it.

(4) Some centers are legitimate. It cannot be wrong simply to let a finite thing occupy the center of a life. Love, vocation, and close friendship do exactly that: they dominate attention, impose priorities, and alter what else one is able to care about. If only marginal attachments were permissible, the novel I am writing would have no subject; the book depends on there being a difference between devotion and displacement, not on devotion itself being a pathology.

(5) Self-sealing is the distinguishing form. The genuinely idolatrous center is closed against the question of its own worth. It does not merely sit in the place of an ultimate standard; it becomes the standard by which every objection to it is judged. The further such a devotion goes wrong, the more the evidence against it is read as a test of constancy or as a defect in the world rather than in the object.

(6) Lucid idolatry. Self-deception is not essential. A person may know, without any ordinary self-deceit, that the object is finite and inadequate to the role she gives it, and may even see that her center is arbitrary or answerable, while continuing in the same practical posture. I want my view to count that person as an idolater, not merely as a weak-willed or confused evaluator.

(7) An asymmetry by cases. If a mother lets her child become the thing by virtue of which her sacrifices make sense, I would not call her an idolater on that ground alone. If a revolutionary lets the Cause occupy the same place in his understanding of what his actions are for, I would call him an idolater — even when the Cause is just.

Please deliver the audit in the following sections, in this order:

1. Reconstruction (about 300 words). Restate the seven commitments in your own terms, flagging any ambiguities that must be settled before the set can be tested.

2. Tensions, ranked (about 600 words). Identify the genuine conflicts among (1)–(7) in descending order of severity, and for each say which commitments cannot stand together as written and what the cheapest repair would be.

3. Apparent tensions that dissolve (about 250 words). Identify the pairs that look inconsistent but are not, and state the distinction that removes the appearance.

4. Cheapest coherent package (about 200 words). Give the single revision that would leave the largest coherent remainder, and say what it costs in terms of the cases or commitments I would have to surrender.
