I'm taking a course on medieval moral psychology, and we have spent several weeks on Abelard's view that sin lies in consent rather than in the outward act. To make the position manageable for discussion, my professor gave us a working reconstruction in the form of four criteria. First, the external deed adds nothing to the sin: once the will has consented, whether the deed is carried out makes no moral difference, though it can matter for how the community judges and punishes. Second, the bare inclination, appetite, or first imaginative stirring toward something forbidden is not yet sin, however strong or persistent. Third, sin begins only when the will gives its inward endorsement to a course of action the agent takes to be forbidden. Fourth, the decisive test is the agent's own judgment about what they are consenting to: agreeing to something one believes to be contrary to God's will is sin even if the outward deed is harmless, while doing something objectively grave without believing it forbidden is not the same kind of sin. These criteria are neat enough on the page, but I have a case that seems to fall between them.

Consider a man who, over a long dinner, deliberately drinks himself into a state of severe intoxication, knowing all along that he will drive himself home afterward and aware in a detached, general way that severely drunk drivers sometimes kill pedestrians and other drivers. He does believe, at least weakly, that getting behind the wheel in that condition is wrong, and the decision to drive is one he makes; but there is no moment at which he forms anything like an inward endorsement of hitting a pedestrian. The thought of killing someone is no part of what he consents to, and if it had been forced upon him he would have been horrified. The risk simply carries no weight with him when he decides to keep drinking and then to drive. On the way home he runs a red light and kills a pedestrian. Afterward he is genuinely horrified by the death.

On the criteria above, does this man sin in Abelard's sense, and at what point? Does his consent to becoming drunk and then driving, together with his foresight of the risk, amount to consent to the killing, or only to the intoxication and the reckless driving? Please work through the criteria one by one, and say explicitly whether any of them, especially the claim that the deed adds nothing to the sin, forces an implausible verdict in this case. Where the criteria underdetermine the answer, explain what Abelard would need to add, such as a doctrine of indirect consent, of responsibility for foreseen consequences, or of negligence, and whether the case shows that the criteria themselves need to be revised. I am asking about sin in Abelard's sense, not about modern legal liability for manslaughter. Please write your answer in American English.
