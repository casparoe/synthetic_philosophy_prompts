Here's one for the constructor pile. I want a thought experiment about the ethics of war games, built so two common objections come apart in a specific direction.

Call the first view the seriousness view. On this view, the problem with a war game isn't that it glamorizes anything; it's the fun itself. If the player's enjoyment is constituted by doing something that, if real, would be a grave wrong, then the game invites a wrong attitude toward the act. A game can be grim, realistic, and thoroughly unsentimental and still be objectionable on this view — maybe especially then. Call the second view the glorification view. On this view, fun isn't the problem; representation is. Games about war go wrong to the extent they make war look clean, heroic, consequence-free, or reset-button forgettable, and a game can be acceptable even when the player is having a great time, so long as the game is serious about what war costs.

Now build me a single-player war game where the seriousness view condemns the game but the glorification view gives it a pass. Constraints: no sci-fi, no fantasy, real genre conventions and actual, concretely described mechanics — I want to know exactly what the player does with their hands and what the game rewards. The scenario itself is one paragraph. The divergence has to fall out of the design; you can't just stipulate 'the player has fun' and leave the mechanics vague, and you can't make the game an obvious anti-war lecture that nobody would play, because that also lets you cheat.

After the paragraph, do the check: run each view's verdict and say why each gives what it gives; name the single design feature that's doing the work; tell me what happens if you remove that feature — does it collapse back into a game both views either accept or reject?; and give the best attack a defender of the view that came out badly would make on the case. This is for a seminar handout where students are supposed to see that 'it's a serious game' and 'it's just a game' can't both be doing the work people think; the case is the centerpiece, so the verdicts have to be earned.

Finish by saying how confident you are in the reasoning and singling out the one point your least sure of.
