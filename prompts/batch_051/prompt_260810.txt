For a long-form feature aimed at readers who follow science and ideas, analyze the nuclear arms race between the United States and the Soviet Union during the Cold War as a case study in the multipolar trap: the dynamic, often grouped with races to the bottom and sometimes called Moloch, in which several actors each pursue their own perceived interest yet collectively produce an outcome all of them regard as worse than a feasible coordinated alternative, and in which no single actor can unilaterally opt out without being penalized by the logic of the competition itself. The analysis should run in both directions: the theory should illuminate the episode, and the episode should test the theory.

Begin by making the mapping explicit and disciplined. Who are the relevant players, what were the concrete options at the main choice points—procure another warhead, develop a new delivery system, deploy to a new theater, negotiate, stand still, or draw down—and what were the relevant payoffs, including security, relative standing, domestic political cost, and economic burden? Explain precisely in what sense the accumulating arsenals formed an equilibrium even though both governments publicly deplored the arms race and the risks it created.

Then assess the fit. Identify which features of the episode the trap model explains well—for example, why public denunciations of the race could coexist with continued procurement, and why unilateral restraint looked self-defeating—and which features it leaves out or misdescribes—for instance, ideological hostility, domestic industrial and bureaucratic interests, intelligence failures, misperception, or the pursuit of capabilities meant to do more than merely keep pace. Say explicitly where the model is silent rather than forcing it to explain everything.

Finally, let the episode speak back to the theory. Arms-control negotiations eventually produced partial, verifiable limits on the competition. If that is the right way to read the record, what does it show about the conditions under which agents caught in a multipolar trap can credibly coordinate their way out, and what kind of trap would remain genuinely inescapable even in principle?
