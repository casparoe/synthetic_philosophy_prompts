Why do we treat the outside world as bottomless and our own experience as all sewn up? Here's what I mean. Tell someone that a single street corner is inexhaustible — that no finite mind could list every truth about it and you could stand there for years and keep noticing new things — and they nod along. But tell them the same thing about a person's experience of standing on that corner for five minutes, that the experience itself can't be summed up and there's more in it than the person reports or even notices at the time, and they resist: "no, I was just standing there, a bit cold, looking at the cars." So the world outruns every description of it, but an experience is treated as finite, surveyable, something the haver can exhaust from the inside. Is there a principle that justifies this asymmetry? The obvious candidate is that the corner has millions of independent details while the experience is just whatever it's like and is already fully had. But that feels like it might just be a bias toward treating our own states as transparent. I want the strongest version of the case that the two verdicts are consistent, tested against a hard case: the experience of staring at a blank wall. Does the experience inherit the wall's inexhaustibility, or not? And at the end give me an honest verdict: keep the asymmetry, flip it, or give it up?
