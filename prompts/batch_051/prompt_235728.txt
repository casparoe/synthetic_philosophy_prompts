Exam question: Evaluate the claim that a legal order which recognizes sovereign immunity is thereby committed to the proposition that the state cannot do wrong. Distinguish immunity from suit from immunity from liability, and legal wrongdoing from moral wrongdoing.

Student answer: "I do not think sovereign immunity commits a legal order to saying the state can do no wrong. Immunity from suit is a rule about access to court: a plaintiff is barred from proceeding unless the state has consented, and that says nothing about the merits. Immunity from liability is more substantive — it says that even where a court can hear the case, the state is not answerable in damages — but it is still a rule about remedies rather than about whether conduct was wrong. The maxim that the king can do no wrong should be read in this spirit. It was never a claim of royal moral infallibility; it recorded the idea that wrongs done in the sovereign's name are imputed to the ministers and officers through whom the sovereign acts, so the king is not personally answerable. Once we see this, the claim under evaluation clearly fails: the state can commit legal wrongs through its officers, and modern statutes waive immunity precisely because governments recognize that they can harm citizens unlawfully. If the state could not do wrong, such waivers would be pointless. The only truth in the maxim is that the state need not answer in its own courts without consent, and that is a procedural point. Therefore a legal order with sovereign immunity is not committed to the proposition that the state cannot do wrong."
