A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about whether mathematical talk of infinite objects is a literal description of a completed totality or merely an extremely useful shorthand for patterns of finite construction. By the end, I suspected that the distinction matters less as a question of fundamental ontology than as a question of how mathematical institutions reward different kinds of proof, and the argument left me turning over the following proposal.

Suppose a prestigious pure-mathematics journal announced an experimental editorial policy, intended to test how much of contemporary mathematics could be made finitistically respectable without collapsing its scope. Under the policy, every paper in the main section would have to satisfy two conditions. First, no step in the proof may treat an infinite domain — the natural numbers as a completed whole, the set of all subsets of a given set, a completed infinite series — as an object over which one quantifies freely. Infinite language may appear only as shorthand for an open-ended finite schema, in the manner of what is traditionally called potential infinity, and every existence claim must come with an explicit construction or algorithm. Second, each paper must include something called a finitary core: a plainly stated assertion about finite structures or finite computations that the theory actually secures, together with a proof of that assertion using only finitistically acceptable means. Papers that cannot meet these conditions may still be published, but only in a separate and visibly distinct annex labelled as instrumental, with a note that no finitary certificate is currently known. Annex papers remain citable, but they count for less in the journal's own contribution index, which several hiring and promotion committees have agreed to adopt.

Please treat this as a mechanism rather than as a position on the philosophy of mathematics. I want an analysis of how it would actually behave: what it would incentivise among researchers at different career stages and in different subfields, such as proof mining, constructive algebra, set-theoretic topology, or heavily infinitary areas like parts of algebraic geometry; how a clever author or referee would game it; what second-order effects it would have on the journal's prestige, on graduate training, and on the wider publication ecosystem; and where the scheme would break down in ways its designers could not easily undo. Where possible, distinguish failure modes that could be patched without gutting the policy from failure modes that are intrinsic enough that they would likely kill it. Please give your answer in British English.
