I'm a novelist, working on a book about a military drone operator and a war correspondent, and I keep coming back to Aristotle. In the Nicomachean Ethics, Book III, chapter 1 (W. D. Ross translation), after discussing mixed actions and the man who throws goods overboard in a storm, Aristotle says:

On some actions praise indeed is not bestowed, but pardon is, when one does what he ought not under pressure which overstrains human nature and which no one could withstand. But some acts, perhaps, we cannot be forced to do, but ought rather to face death after the most fearful sufferings; for the things that 'forced' Euripides Alcmaeon to slay his mother seem absurd.

I'm trying to figure out whether this idea—that there are some acts you can't be forced into, and you ought to face death rather than do them—still makes sense now that martial virtue mostly shows up in a peaceful society where people aren't actually facing death on a battlefield. If courage is no longer about a soldier on the ground choosing death over a shameful act, but about, say, a drone operator who kills from safety, or a bureaucrat who blows the whistle and looses their career but not their life, does Aristotle's argument survive? Does it still pick out the same kind of virtue, or does it collapse into something else? I'm not after policy takes; I want to know what happens to the argument itself.
