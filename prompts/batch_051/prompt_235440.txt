I'm a PhD student in cognitive neuroscience, and I've ended up as the token "consciousness person" in our lab, which means people keep trying to settle arguments by saying "consciousness is an illusion" in my direction. The more I hear it, the less I know what it's supposed to mean. Last week a postdoc used it to brush off a question I asked about whether a painful stimulus is literally felt as bad or merely detected and then misread by the part of the brain that reports on it. When I asked if she meant the pain wasn't real or just that our introspective model of it is wrong, she said something like "both, but also neither," and moved on. That worries me, because it seems to run together a bunch of claims I'd been keeping separate: that a state is misdescribed by the person in it, that a state doesn't exist at all, that a state exists but lacks private qualitative character, that introspection says something false about an otherwise real state, and that "there is something it is like" is itself a bad theoretical way of talking.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses someone might be asserting with that slogan, ordered from the trivially true or merely terminological to the strongly eliminativist? For each, tell me whether it is compatable with there being something it is like to be in pain, and which readings would actually license dismissing a question like mine about felt pain, as opposed to just refusing to answer it. I know this general area gets called illusionism, but the label is doing no work for me unless the readings are separated.
