I'm a retired geochemist who's spent the last couple years reading philosophy of science and, through that, a fair bit of AI safety. The idea that won't leave me alone is the competitiveness constraint: even if every frontier lab would rather live in a world where everyone spends real money on pre-deployment safety, any one lab that actually does so is paying an alignment tax it's competitors don't pay, so the careful labs get outcompeted and the market grinds toward the floor. I've been toying with a scheme to fix that and I'd like you to analyze it as a mechanism, not tell me if it's morally nice.

The scheme. A consortium covering most frontier labs and their main cloud providers agrees that any training run above a certain compute threshold has to post a bond, say 10% of training cost, into a shared trust. The bond comes back only if a pre-deployment safety certification board signs off on the lab's safety case; if the lab skips or fails certification, the bond goes into a fund that pays third-party auditors and red-team competitions. Board members are drawn by lot from a pool of accredited auditors, paid the same regardless of how they rule, but if a later outside audit overturns one of their certifications they're dropped and forfeit some of their accumulated fees. The threshold is set so only the top few dozen runs a year trip it.

Walk me through what actually happens. What do labs do — split a big run into sub-threshold pieces, call it a research preview instead of a product, train in a country outside the consortium, or game the checklist? Do the certifiers become the real power center, and what do they do with that? Does the bond just become a cost passed on to customers, and does that even matter if it hits everyone equally? Where does the whole thing fail first, and can you patch that without turning it into a global regulator nobody would join?
