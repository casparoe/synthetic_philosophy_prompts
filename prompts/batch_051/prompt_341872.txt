I have been in a running argument with two friends whose instincts point in opposite directions, and I keep noticing that our object-level disagreements bottom out in a methodological one. One of them treats intuitive verdicts about concrete cases as the only real evidence in philosophy: principles are compressed summaries of those verdicts, and when a principle and a case judgment conflict, the principle is what gets revised. The other treats principles as having independent evidential force: she takes formal constraints like transitivity (if A stands in a graded relation to B, and B to C, then A should stand in it to C) and theoretical virtues like simplicity to be reasons in their own right, so a case judgment that would force us into an intransitive or gerrymandered concept is itself a candidate for dismissal. I can steelman both positions, and I do not think the disagreement is a simple mistake. The obvious reflective-equilibrium answer is that both sides get adjusted until coherence, but that just relocates the problem: when something has to give first, what determines the asymmetry?

I would like to see this argued through a concrete worked example rather than in the abstract. Set up a conceptual issue — from ethics, epistemology, or semantics is fine — in which a compact, simple principle that makes a graded or ordering relation transitive conflicts with a cluster of intuitive verdicts about particular cases. Then compare the two methodologies as accounts of how to resolve that conflict. I am less interested in the generic answer that both adjust until coherence than in the weighting question: which side should give way first, what exactly is serving as evidence in the dispute, and what would have to be true for you to reverse your ruling? Justify your answer.
