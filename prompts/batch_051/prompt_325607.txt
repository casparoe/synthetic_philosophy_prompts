I'm a PhD student in computational photography, working mostly on view synthesis and neural rendering, so I tend to think of an ordinary photograph as a two-dimensional sample of the plenoptic function taken by a particular optical system at a particular pose and time, then turned into a viewable image by a pipeline of demosaicing, denoising, white balancing, tone mapping, and display. Lately I've been reading philosophy of photography, and I keep circling around the transparency thesis — the view, associated with Kendall Walton, that photographs are transparent in that we literally see the photographed object through them, in something like the way we see through a telescope or a mirror, rather than merely seeing a depiction of it. The usual arguments I have encountered rest on the causal provenance of the photograph and its relative insensitivity to the photographer's beliefs about what was before the lens; the usual objections concern the loss of egocentric information (the photograph does not let me locate the object relative to my own body), the time gap between exposure and viewing, and the possibility of digital manipulation.

I'd like you to act as a philosopher of perception who is also fluent in computational photography and image processing. Begin by laying out a tentative mapping between the central terms of the debate and my technical vocabulary. Then restate the transparency thesis and the strongest version of the objections to it in those terms: image formation as optics plus sensor plus processing; the photo as a fixed, pose-locked sample of the plenoptic function; the distinction between measurement and reconstruction; tone mapping and dynamic range; compression and display; and the modern blur between capturing and synthesizing an image in a neural rendering pipeline. Please keep the translation honest — do not let words like "reconstruction" or "sample" quietly settle the philosophical question, and flag every place where my vocabulary tries to smuggle in an answer. Then give me a residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which ones survive only with distortion, and which parts of the debate have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Finally, tell me whether the residue reflects a genuine blind spot in the computational-imaging picture or a place where the philosophical position is less clear than its proponents think. My working hypothesis is that the transparency debate is, at bottom, a dispute about when an imaging pipeline stops being a sensor and starts being a renderer — and I want to know whether that hypothesis is right or whether something is lost in forcing the issue into those terms.
