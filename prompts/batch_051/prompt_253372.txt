A coworker and I have been going in circles about multi-agent coordination — we're building a system where a bunch of agents have to complete a task together, and we keep hitting the question of whether they're ever genuinely doing it jointly or just each running their own subtask while the system-level behavior looks joint from the outside. He came up with an argument that's aimed at humans, but we couldn't stop applying it to our agents, and it seems to prove that no two people ever literally do anything together. That cant be right, but I can't find the bad step.

His argument went like this:

(1) If two people genuinely do something jointly — carry a couch, play a duet, work on a team — each person has to have what I've seen people call a we-intention, something like "we do X," not just "I'll do my part."

(2) So the content of each person's intention includes a plural subject, a "we."

(3) But an intention is a mental state, and mental states live in individual heads. When we carry the couch, there isn't some extra group-shaped mind hovering above us that has the intention.

(4) He then said: in a we-intention, the "we" in the content isn't just decorative—it's supposed to be the subject of the intention. So if I intend "we carry the couch," then the thing doing the intending is the group.

(5) But groups don't have mental states. There's no group consciousness, no single place where the group's intention lives.

(6) So a we-intention would be a mental state whose subject isn't the kind of thing that can have mental states, which is incoherent.

(7) Therefore I can't really intend "we carry the couch." At most, I can intend "I do my part on the assumption that you do yours," and you intend the same, and we each have beliefs about what the other will do.

(8) But that's just a bunch of coordinated individual actions with mutual expectations bolted on, not a single joint action.

(9) So genuine joint action never happens; what we call "doing something together" is really synchronized individual behavior.

I find the conclusion absurd — I'm pretty sure my band isnt just four people accidentally playing the same song at the same tempo — but every step sounded plausible when he laid it out. I want to know which step to reject, why that step looked right, and what rejecting it commits you to. If the argument is actually valid, tell me which premise is the least costly to give up, and what the main ways out of this each have to sacrifice. I don't need a reading list; I just need to know what the actual options are.
