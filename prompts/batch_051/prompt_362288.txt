In my social epistemology seminar we spent a week on reputation and karma systems and the way they function as miniature economies — the kind used on discussion forums and content platforms — and afterwards two of my classmates, Mara and Dev, kept arguing about what a high score actually shows. I wrote down as much of their exchange as I could remember, and I would be grateful for a careful referee.

Mara opened with a point about aggregation. If thousands of independent readers have judged a post worthwhile, she said, that is a large body of distributed evidence, and the resulting karma score is not merely a popularity number; it is a genuine signal of reliability. Unless you think the crowd is systematically wrong, a high score should raise your estimate that an account's contributions are useful.

Dev replied that the independence assumption is doing all the work. Voters see the existing score, follow early ratings, and are selected from people who already engage with that community, so the individual votes are not independent tests of quality; they are partly copies of one another. A post that takes a slight early lead can snowball, and then a high score reflects cascade dynamics rather than accumulated testimony.

Mara granted that cascades exist but said the systems contain corrections — downvotes, moderation, time-decay, and the fact that inflated posts still get read and criticized. Even a noisy and biased signal carries information if it remains correlated with quality. The fair question, she insisted, is not whether karma is infallible but whether it beats the baseline of no aggregation at all.

Dev's response was that the scoring function is not neutral. What gets coded as karma-worthy is a set of design choices: one system rewards agreement, another rewards wit, another rewards effort or persistence. Since those choices encode a community's norms, treating the resulting score as evidence of objective quality smuggles in the very standard the argument was supposed to establish.

Mara answered that this is a feature, not a bug. Users choose communities whose norms they broadly accept; relative to those norms, a score does measure something real. So trust in a reputation system is trust in a community's revealed standards, not a claim about context-free quality. That is still a legitimate epistemic position, she said.

Dev shifted ground. Once reputation functions as a currency, he argued, it changes the behavior it was meant to measure. Contributors learn to produce what earns karma, and that incentive makes the score track the reward structure, not pre-existing reliability. The more weight people put on karma, the more the score becomes a measure of itself.

Mara countered that the incentive effect cuts both ways. If reputation really shapes behavior, then it is not a mere popularity contest; it is doing epistemic work by rewarding the kinds of contributions the community wants to see and penalizing the kinds it does not. Revealed preferences are, at minimum, information about what that community finds valuable.

Dev replied that revealed preferences are not the same as reliability. A system can manufacture the very consensus it claims to measure: people upvote what high-reputation users have already blessed, so the aggregate looks like independent endorsement when it is partly an artifact of the scores themselves.

At that point they turned to me, and I found I could not cleanly separate the claims they were making, let alone say who was more right overall. Could you adjudicate the exchange point by point? In particular, I would like to know which of Dev's objections genuinely damage Mara's initial position, which of Mara's replies genuinely answer those objections, and whether either of them is conflating distinct questions — such as whether karma predicts quality, whether it justifies individual deference, and whether it improves the community over time.
