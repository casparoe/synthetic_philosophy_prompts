Analyze the public controversies over continuing to honor Thomas Jefferson, Winston Churchill, and Christopher Columbus through statues, names, and official memorials, using the "man of his time" defense as the lens. I am not asking for a historical survey; the commonly known facts will do. What I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions, with the defense itself treated as a family of arguments rather than a single settled move.

Begin by disambiguating the defense into at least three readings, and state for each exactly what it is meant to establish. The first is a psychological/exculpatory reading: the figure could not reasonably have been expected to see what was wrong, so retrospective blame is unfair. The second is a sociological/justificatory reading: the act was less wrong, or not wrong, because it conformed to the operative standards of the time. The third is aimed at present-day judgers rather than at the historical agent: the condemner is being anachronistic, smug, or selectively presentist. For each reading, identify the empirical premise it needs — about what the agent had access to, what the surrounding consensus actually was, and what internal critics were saying — and the normative premise it needs — about when ignorance excuses, when local norms vindicate, and whether a condemner's own failings undercut the verdict.

Then apply these readings to the three cases as they actually appear in public argument: Jefferson's slaveholding set against the universalism of the Declaration of Independence that he drafted; Churchill's racial and imperial commitments set against his leadership of Britain in the Second World War; and Columbus's treatment of the Taíno set against his part in opening a permanently joined Atlantic world. For each, say which reading of the defense is doing most of the work, what it genuinely explains, what it silently drops, and whether one of the other readings would serve the defender better. In all three cases, attend to the fact that the agent faced contemporaneous dissent — abolitionists, anti-imperialists, and critics of colonial violence — so the defense cannot treat the surrounding moral order as an undifferentiated consensus. The central question here is whether the defense quietly moves from a psychological claim, that the agent could not have known, to a sociological one, that the dominant norms pointed elsewhere, and whether that shift can be defended.

Finally, let the cases test the defense. Say where it is genuinely illuminating — for example, in blocking the inference from "this person caused serious harm" to "this person's character was simply monstrous" — and where it is silent or overreaching, particularly when the question is not whether to blame a dead person but whether present-day institutions should keep honoring him. Conclude with your verdict: what is the best reconstruction of the man-of-his-time defense, and what would that best reconstruction actually license — and rule out — in the Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus cases?
