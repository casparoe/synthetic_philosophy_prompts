I've been going back and forth with a friend who works in ML on the whole "could future AI systems literally suffer" question, and he keeps falling back on a picture that I half believed myself until I tried to steelman it. His line is: reinforcement learning already gives us the natural framework for valence. Pain and pleasure just are the reward signal — or at least the closest thing evolution built to one. A negative reward is the system being told "that was bad" and a positive reward is the system being told "that was good", and once you have that you have the core of suffering and enjoying. Therefore (he says) the AI welfare question isn't some deep mystery: either the system has a reward channel and can be made to "suffer" by driving it negative, or it doesn't and there's nothing to worry about.

I'm suspicious but I can't quite articulate all the ways the mapping might fail, and I also don't want to loose the thread of the parts where the analogy does do real work — for example, the way a rat pressing a lever for stimulation looks related to wanting, or the way credit assignment looks a lot like learning what caused the bad outcome. I've seen rationalist-adjacent people gesture at the idea that the reward signal is not the thing being optimized, and at the old wireheading thought experiments, but I want something more careful than slogan-trading.

Can you act as someone who genuinely knows both the RL machinery (reward shaping, sparse vs. dense reward, the difference between a reward signal during training and no reward channel at deployment, PPO-style setups, intrinsic rewards, credit assignment) and the philosophy/neuroscience of affect? Do an actual audit of the analogy "RL reward is valence / negative reward is pain." Maybe start by brainstorming what maps to what in both directions, then give a table of correspondences, then separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion from the ones that are just cosmetic (including any properties the analogy quietly imports from human affect into the RL setup), and finish by answering whether his conclusion — AI suffering is easy to create, if it can exist at all — survives the audit, and what a better analogy would be if this one is broken. I suspect the interesting crux is whether reward is a signal the system can be "told" versus a state the system feels; and keep "the agent is being shaped to seek X" distinct from "the agent experiences X as good."
