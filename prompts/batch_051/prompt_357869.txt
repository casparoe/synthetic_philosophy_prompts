I have been working through a question in the philosophy of grammar, and I have arrived — tentatively — at a position I want to test by asking what would count against it. The position is this: the core grammatical categories, first and foremost the parts of speech such as noun, verb, and adjective, but also the major case distinctions and the category of adpositions, are not merely labels imposed on language-specific facts for descriptive convenience. They are genuine natural kinds. They are grammatically real classes that are cross-linguistically robust, that children and adults acquire and process as though they were built into the architecture of language, and that any humanly learnable language must realize in some form, however mediated by the language's own history and surface machinery.

What I want is not a defense of the position but a map of what would count against it. In particular, how much trouble should each of the following be: languages in which a single open class of roots appears to do the work that English splits among nouns, verbs, and adjectives, with category assigned only by syntactic slot, or left underdetermined; categories that leak into one another, such as participles, gerunds, and nominalized verbs; and categories caught mid-grammaticalization, such as adpositions still behaving partly like nouns or verbs and case affixes still behaving partly like independent words. I want you to separate the phenomena that should genuinely unseat a natural-kind realist from those that only push toward a weaker or more nuanced version of the view, and to sort the counterevidence from fatal to merely awkward. I also want an honest assessment of whether the position, as I have stated it, is at risk of being insulated against all counterevidence by quietly redefining the categories whenever a problem appears — and whether that kind of insulation is a vice here or merely the expected behavior of a claim about the architecture of language. I am not asking for a survey of the literature; I want the structure of the case against the view.
