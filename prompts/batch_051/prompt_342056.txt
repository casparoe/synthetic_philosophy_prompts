I'm a PhD student in moral psychology, and there's a tic in my reasoning about animals that I can't tell wether I should get rid of or keep. My habit is this: when an argument tells me that something I do every week, like eating eggs from caged hens, is morally on a par with something I'd walk through fire to stop, like torturing a puppy, and I fail to feel anything like the same urgency, I take that missing feeling as evidence that the argument's analogy has gone wrong somewhere. I then go hunting for a disanalogy. If I find one that seems plausible, great; if I don't, I still don't update, because I treat my own reluctance as information about a moral difference I can sense but haven't managed to articulate yet. My labmate says this is speciesism and status quo bias wearing an epistemic costume, and that an argument's moral force doesn't depend on whether it feels the same as a more familiar wrong.

I can see both sides: in other parts of my life I do something similar with hunches, testimonies, and arguments that feel too slick, and I trust my calibrated sense of when a move is suspicious. But in this particular case there's an obvious social interest in not updating, and I might be rationalizing. Is there a defensible version of the rule, something like 'treat lack of felt conviction as a reason to look harder, but set a deadline after which the argument wins by default if no disanalogy survives'? Or is that just procrastination disguised as due diligence? I'd like your answer in continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, the way you'd write an email to a colleague.
