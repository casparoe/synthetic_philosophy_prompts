In my philosophy of biology seminar, a colleague defended the gene's-eye view associated with Dawkins and Williams against objections from developmental systems theory and multi-level selection by leaning on an analogy I keep hearing in different forms: we may treat genes as if they were rational agents whose goal is to maximize their representation in future generations, because any allele whose aggregate effects failed to promote its own transmission would be replaced by one whose effects did. On this picture the organism is a temporary vehicle or instrument through which the gene executes a kind of long-run investment policy, and a phenotypic trait is explained by specifying the policy an allele has, in effect, adopted. The analogy was put to work to license a strong conclusion — that the gene, rather than the organism or the group, is the entity for whose benefit adaptations exist, and that developmental and niche-constructing activities are just the extended execution of gene-level strategies. I want an audit of how far this analogy actually holds. Give me an explicit table of correspondences: what maps to the agent, the goal, the strategy, the investment horizon, the portfolio, the profit, and the constraints. Then identify the points where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that matter for the conclusion being licensed from the ones that are cosmetic. In particular, what does the rational-agent or investor picture quietly import — forward-looking optimization, a single utility function, fungibility, diversification, limited liability, unconditional ownership of outcomes — that is not respected by recombination, linkage, pleiotropy, epigenetic inheritance, and phenotypic plasticity? Once those disanalogies are on the table, how much of the licensed conclusion survives, and is there an available analogy that serves the gene's-eye view better, or does the view essentially depend on the agentic picture to do its explanatory work?
