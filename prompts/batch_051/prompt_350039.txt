I'm teaching a small seminar next term on what wisdom is, and I've been trying to compress my actual view into something I won't be embarrassed to have read back to me. I keep arriving at a set of commitments that feel right individually, but I suspect they might not all fit together, and I'd rather find out now than in front of students.

Here's where I've landed. (1) Wisdom isn't a stock of propositions or maxims; it's closer to a kind of skill—knowing how to read a situation, when to act, what to weight. (2) It can't be taught the way algebra is taught, but people do seem to acquire it, and some settings seem to accelerate that acquisition. (3) The main engine of the acquisition, as far as I can tell, is failure: specifically, having your own judgment fail on you in a way you can't disown and then having to live with the fallout. (4) All the same, I think there are fairly general traits of wise people—calibrated confidence, a sense of when to defer, a resistance to treating every setback as a catastrophe—that show up across very different domains of life. (5) I'm suspicious of any actual list of wise principles, because almost any candidate (say, wait and see; or act decisively) is wise in some contexts and foolish in others. (6) And I want to hold onto the old-fashioned view that wisdom is still a proper goal of education, even though it can't be listed as a learning outcome on a syllabus.

My question is whether this set hangs together. I can feel two or three places where commitments rub against each other, but I'm not sure which are real tensions and which are just me sliding between senses of 'taught' or 'learned' or 'principle.' Could you rank the tensions by severity, flag which apparent tensions actually dissolve on inspection, and say what the cheapest repair would be if one of these has to go? I'd rather not be told everything is simultaneously true in a fuzzy way—I want to know which specific claims are doing the damage.
