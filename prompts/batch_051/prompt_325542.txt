I teach a fourth-year seminar on happiness in the early modern period, and we spent three weeks on Émilie du Châtelet's Discours sur le bonheur. I'm writing the take-home exam and I want one essay question that seperates the students who actually read her carefully from the ones who can only say she was Voltaire's girlfriend and liked studying. The question should zero in on what I think is the real crux: she says happiness requires being susceptible to illusions, and also that it requires being delivered from prejudice; and she singles out the passion for study as the most reliable route to happiness because it's a resource that depends only on ourselves, even though her whole account seems to require desires that reach outside ourselves. I'd like the question to force them to say what an illusion actually is for her — is it a false belief she knowingly keeps, or something closer to keeping one's attention and hope fixed on what makes life vivid — and whether the love of study can really serve as both a passion and a form of independence, or whether that's a tension she never quite resolves. The question should also make them take seriously her naturalism about happiness (pleasant sensations are the raw material) and her worry that boredom, not pain, is the real enemy that the passions have to keep at bay.

Give me the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with for each point the marks it's worth and a one-line note on how candidates usually get that bit wrong. Aim the question so its unanswerable by summarizing the Discourse; the student has to argue. 90 minutes, upper-level seminar, and the marking scheme shouldn't reward anyone for just slotting her into Epicurean or Stoic and stopping there.
