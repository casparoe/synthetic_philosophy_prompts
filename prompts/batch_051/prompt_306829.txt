I chair the discipline and credentials committee for a midsize professional association of physical therapists, about 4,000 members. Every member swears an oath on joining — partly to uphold our code of ethics, partly some specific commitments (don't poach clients during disputes, do report gross incompetence, don't go public about internal matters in a way that hurts patient trust, and so on). We handle maybe a dozen oath disputes a year: a member petitions to be released from some part of the oath, or they're acussed of breaking it and say it shouldn't bind them anymore. None of us on the committee are philosophers or lawyers, we meet monthly, and we need each case turned around in about twenty minutes before the board signs off. Sanctions run up to expulsion, so the stakes are real.

Build me a reusable decision procedure — a checklist, or a flowchart in words — for deciding whether an oath is still binding, whether it's been dissolved or voided, and whether someone who didn't do what they swore should be treated as an oath-breaker. Ground it in whatever philosopy has actually worked out about promising and oaths, including the old idea that swearing on the Styx gave an oath an extra layer of force beyond an ordinary promise, but spare us the theory. Keep the distinctions that actually matter — consent defects, being released by the person owed the promise, impossibility, terms that would be wrong to keep, changed circumstances — and flag the steps that are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical cases:
(a) a member who swore the non-solicitation clause and has been working with us to rewrite it wants to be released from the old clause before the new version is ratified;
(b) a member who took the oath only after two senior partners made clear that refusing would end their career is now accused of breaking it;
(c) a member who swore not to discuss internal association matters publicly says she can only stop a serious patient-safety problem by going public, and argues the oath would be wrong to keep.
Make sure at least one of these lands on a recommendation the board won't like.

Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do and where it'll reliably misfire.

Start with the procedure itself — the actual steps in order — and then give the reasoning and the three applications. No preamble, no restating the question.
