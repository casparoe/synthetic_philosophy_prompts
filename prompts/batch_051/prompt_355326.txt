Last night I had a long argument with a friend about what a writing system is supposed to encode, and specifically about whether English orthography is best understood as a defective phonemic alphabet or as something else entirely. My friend took the familiar view that an alphabet's job is to map letters to sounds, that a script is better the more regular that mapping is, and that by this standard English spelling is a historical mess while a language with a highly regular orthography is close to ideal. I think that conclusion is too fast, and I have drafted below the notes for an essay I want to write against it. I would be grateful for comments on the notes.

The claim I want to defend is that a script is not fundamentally a record of surface sound; it is a theory of what is invariant in the speech stream. Even a maximally regular alphabet already collapses a great deal of phonetic detail. It treats the p of pin and the p of spin as the same unit whenever the language does not use aspiration contrastively, and it ignores coarticulatory variation that an acoustic record would show. Every alphabet therefore is already an abstract, theory-shaped object rather than a transparent record of sound. The interesting question is not how phonemic an alphabet is, but which invariances it chooses to make visible.

The real difference between what linguists call shallow and deep orthographies, I want to argue, is that they make different choices about where to recover information that surface pronunciation has neutralized. German is a useful test case. Word-final obstruent devoicing makes Rat and Rad sound alike in isolation; but the spelling Rad keeps the d, which surfaces again in Räder. That is a deliberate departure from surface phonemicity in favor of a morphophonemic invariant. English does the same thing more thoroughly: sign keeps a g that reappears in signal, and electric and electricity keep the same written form of the root even though the relevant consonant changes pronunciation. In each case the script declines to encode what the mouth does at the surface and instead encodes what the lexical item is across its forms.

If that is right, then the contrast between a regular phonemic orthography and a deep morphophonemic one is not a contrast between a good alphabet and a failed one. It is a difference in what the system treats as the unit that persists across contexts: the surface phoneme, the underlying segment, or the morpheme. Each choice plausibly has different costs for different users. A strictly surface-phonemic system may be easier for a beginning reader who knows the spoken language but has not yet learned its morphology, while a morphophonemic system may be easier for a reader who already has the morphological generalizations and wants the spelling to make them explicit. A system that preserves still older historical forms may serve yet other purposes.

Where I am less confident is how far this idea can be pushed. I am tempted to say that a script is like a compression scheme whose objective function differs from language to language, and that no script can be optimal on every criterion at once, so that at some point the same symbol is forced to stand for different sounds or different underlying units. I am also tempted to say that the question of what a script encodes is badly posed until one says for whom and for what task. I want to know whether these moves commit me to an untenable relativism about orthographies, or whether they amount only to taking historical systems seriously as ways of representing a language rather than as failed attempts at phonemic transcription.

I would especially appreciate comments on four points. First, whether the move from every alphabet ignores phonetic detail to deep orthographies are just a different choice of invariant is valid, or whether it silently slides from suppressing allophonic variation to suppressing neutralizing alternations, which may not be the same phenomenon. Second, whether the German and English examples carry the weight I place on them, or whether a defender of phonemic regularity can reasonably dismiss them as marginal exceptions. Third, whether the compression-scheme analogy illuminates the issue or obscures it. Fourth, whether the essay currently collapses into the weak conclusion that every orthography lies somewhere on a continuum, and if so, what the sharper thesis should be.
