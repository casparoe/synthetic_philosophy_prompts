I teach an introductory practical-ethics seminar for adults, and next week's unit is on the moral weight of small commitments — being on time, returning calls, keeping low-stakes promises such as sending an article or watering a plant. I would like a diagnostic instrument in the form of a decision tree, not a prose survey, that sorts a reader into one of seven or eight positions on the question: what, if anything, makes a broken small promise or a modest lateness wrong?

The instrument must be usable by students who have never read moral philosophy. Every branch question should therefore be either a concrete case or a plainly worded yes-or-no claim, and no branch question may mention a philosopher, a school, a view's name, or theoretical vocabulary such as "deontology" or "promissory obligation." The instrument should sort people by how they answer the cases, not by which label they like, so no question should ask readers which position they hold or make obvious which leaf a given answer leads to. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of candidate views: first determine which distinctions genuinely separate the positions, set out the combinations of answers those distinctions allow, then locate the defensible views among those combinations as leaves. For any combination that remains empty, add a one-line diagnosis of whether it is empty because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of positions the tree fails to separate and specify the further question that would split them. Present the final tree as a nested list in which each node is a question with two or three answer options, and each option leads either to another node or to a leaf.

The following distinctions must appear as nodes, though you may add others if they earn their place.

(a) Whether a bare assurance — an "I'll do it" — gives the speaker a reason to perform even when the other party has not relied on it, formed no settled expectation, and would lose nothing from nonperformance.

(b) Whether the wrong-making feature of a broken small promise lies in the costs or disappointments actually caused, in what the breach expresses about the promisor's regard for the other person, or in what it does to the promisor's standing as a reliable agent.

(c) Whether strict punctuality is a moral requirement in its own right, or whether it is a local social convention whose moral content comes only from the expectations and costs that local customs attach to it.

(d) Whether small promises differ from large ones merely in degree of stringency, or in kind — for example, because small promises are governed by etiquette or by local norms of tolerable lateness rather than by obligation.

Use at least the following cases among the questions:

- You arrange to meet a friend at a café at 3:00, arrive at 3:07, and find them happily absorbed in their phone.
- Same meeting and same seven-minute delay, but the friend has waited outside in the rain because the café was unexpectedly closed.
- You tell a colleague "I'll send you that article this afternoon," forget, and send it after midnight, by which time they have already found it themselves.
- You promise a neighbor to water their plant over a long weekend, skip one day, and the plant is fine.
- You are consistently five minutes late to a standing weekly commitment whose other participants have begun treating the later start time as the real one.
- You make the same low-stakes promise to a close friend and to a distant acquaintance, and break both for the same trivial reason.

Each leaf should carry a plain-language name — or be marked as an unnamed position if no one has defended it — along with one sentence on the most uncomfortable commitment the view imposes on its holder, and one sentence on whether the consistently-five-minutes-late person is morally at fault on that view or merely has a social habit. Keep the whole instrument to seven or eight leaves; do not pad beyond eight.
