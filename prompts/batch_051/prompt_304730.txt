I’m a journalist working on a peice about overlooked rationalists, which is why I’ve been picking through Zera Yacob’s Hatata, the seventeenth-century Ethiopian inquiry. I want to get past the usual “African Descartes” hook and actually pin down what his ethical method is. One passage I keep returning to is where he rejects the Mosaic purity rules. He reasons that God made blood flow monthly from women’s bodies so they could bear children, and then he says the law that treats menstruating women as impure is against nature and the Creator because it “impedes marriage and the entire life of a woman, and it spoils the law of mutual help, prevents the bringing up of children and destroys love.”

I have a thesis I’d like you to argue for, or rip apart if it can’t be defended. The thesis is: Yacob is doing something more interesting than a local critique of Moses. He’s implicitly proposing a test for acceptable moral rules: a rule is suspect if it treats a naturally occurring, function-serving process as a source of impurity and, in doing so, predictably damages ordinary human goods like marriage, mutual help, child-raising, and love. Please make the strongest available argument that this is a genuine, general normative principle in the Hatata, and explain whether it can stand without his theism. Also say what you think the sharpest objection is.
