I've been turning over an argument about dispositions and it seems to prove way to much, but I can't figure out where the right way out is. Here's the setup, using "fragile" as a stand-in.

(1) A glass is fragile iff it would break if dropped onto a hard floor from about a meter up.

That's the natural conditional analysis. But take a mask case: I wrap the glass in bubble wrap, drop it, and it doesn't break. Obviously it's still fragile — the packaging is just preventing the disposition from manifesting. So (1) has to be weakened, to something like:

(2) x is fragile iff, were x dropped from a meter onto a hard floor in the absence of masks, it would break.

But then there's the fink case. Suppose a bare glass sits on the table, and a demon (or a hidden gadget, doesn't matter) is poised to zap the glass's microstructure and make it non-fragile the instant before it hits. Drop the glass: it doesn't break. There's no mask; the glass itself was altered right before impact. But before the zap it sure seems fragile. So the conditional in (2) is false, and we have to exclude finks too:

(3) x is fragile iff, were x dropped from a meter onto a hard floor in the absence of masks and finks, it would break.

Now here's where it starts to bite. What does "in the absence of masks and finks" mean? It seems to mean: in the absence of anything that would stop the glass from breaking when dropped. So (3) collapses into:

(4) x is fragile iff, in the absence of things that would prevent its fragility from manifesting, its fragility would manifest.

That's circular, or at least empty — it doesn't tell us what fragility consists in. And the bite gets worse: for any object at all, you can cook up some possible mask or fink for any proposed manifestation. So whether a thing counts as fragile seems to depend entirely on what background factors we choose to hold fixed, not on the thing itself. Conclusion: either nothing is determinately fragile, or fragility isn't really a property of the glass — talk of dispositions would just be an artifact of our ceteris paribus stipulations.

That conclusion seems way stronger than the setup deserves, so something is off. Which premise or move should I reject, and why did it look fine? What do the main possible replies have to pay for rejecting it? If I'm allowed to be picky, I'd like to keep two things: that a bubble-wrapped glass is still fragile, and that whether the glass is fragile is settled by how the glass itself is, not by what else happens to be lurking.

If you think I've set this up badly, or that the fink/mask considerations aren't doing what I think they're doing, just say that instead of playing along. And I'd appreciate it if you flagged how confident you are at each step instead of writing as if it's all settled. Oh, and could you write in British English?
