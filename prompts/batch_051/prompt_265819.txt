I keep a small apiary, and after a season of watching bees draw comb I have become preoccupied by a problem that I would previously have dismissed as scholastic. Darwin, in *On the Origin of Species* (1859), chapter VII, treats the cell-making instinct of the hive-bee as a standing difficulty for his theory. He writes:

"especially as so wonderful an instinct as that of the hive-bee making its cells will probably have occurred to many readers, as a difficulty sufficient to overthrow my whole theory."

And he adds, more generally:

"No complex instinct can possibly be produced through natural selection, except by the slow and gradual accumulation of numerous, slight, yet profitable, variations."

What unsettles me is not the evolutionary origin of the instinct but the explanatory role of mathematics in the case. The honeycomb's hexagonal structure is, in some precise sense, a mathematical optimum: it is the tiling of the plane into equal cells with least total wall length. That mathematical fact seems to enter the biological explanation of why the comb takes the form it does. Is this a genuine case of mathematical explanation in science, or is the mathematics merely a concise way of describing physical constraints that do the real explanatory work?

I want you to construct a thought experiment to specification for a paper I am drafting. The paper argues that the honeycomb case is a sharper test of the explanatory indispensability argument than the familiar cicada-period case, because the bees' construction problem has a known mechanistic solution. Please design a scenario satisfying all of the following:

1. The case involves an empirical, biological phenomenon in which a mathematical fact—ideally a structural or optimality fact of the kind exemplified by the hexagonal honeycomb—is genuinely indispensable to the best available explanation: removing or paraphras­ing away the mathematics leaves the explanation measurably weaker.
2. Two opposed views must deliver divergent ontological verdicts on the case. The first is the explanatory indispensability view: if a mathematical entity is indispensable to our best scientific explanation of an empirical fact, we ought to be ontologically committed to that entity. The second is a deflationary or structuralist view: the mathematics is explanatory only because it tracks a physical feature of the system itself—such as a local construction rule, a mechanical constraint, or a selection gradient—and the explanation therefore does not carry commitment to mathematical objects.
3. The divergence between the two views must be traceable to a single isolable structural feature: whether the mathematical fact itself, or the physical mechanism that happens to realize or approximate that fact, is what does the explaining. The scenario should be engineered so that this feature alone separates the two verdicts.
4. The case must be realistic in the following respects: no perfect optimizers, no ideal agents, no exotic physics, and no stipulation that the mathematics causes the physical process. The explanation should be one a working biologist could offer.
5. The scenario must not be a re-run of the periodical cicada prime-period example, nor a standard Quinean indispensability case where mathematics is merely used in formulating an empirical theory. It should be a case where the two specified views genuinely separate, and where the deflationist cannot simply deny that the mathematical fact is explanatory.

After presenting the scenario, show that it meets each specification: set out the verdict each of the two views gives and the reasoning behind it, identify the structural feature that does the work, and explain what happens if that feature is removed—does the case collapse into a standard indispensability case, or into a different debate? Finally, include one strong objection from the side that fares worse in your constructed case, and say whether that objection can be answered without abandoning the scenario's specifications.
