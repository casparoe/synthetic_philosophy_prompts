I'm a novelist, and in my current book there's a scene at a food-tech expo where a Kant scholar and a bioethicist get into it about cultivated meat—animal muscle cells grown in bioreactors, so no whole animal is killed. The Kant scholar keeps insisting Kant would have no problem with it because the only relevant issue is whether it coarsens human character, and this process doesn't involve cruelty to a sentient animal. But I'm not sure that's right, and I dont want him to be a straw Kant.

Here's the mental picture I'm working with. Kant says we have no direct duties to animals because they aren't rational ends in themselves, but we do have duties regarding them: hurting a dog isn't wrong because of the dog, but because it weakens our sympathetic tendencies toward other humans. He also seems to have allowed painful animal experiments when the pain was necessary for some humanly useful purpose, because animals are our instruments. So my question is, if I take that structure seriously, what would Kant actually say about three modern things—(a) lab-grown meat, (b) cloning a beloved family dog, and (c) genetically engineering pigs to grow organs for transplant into humans? Would he see these as morally different from factory farming, or do they all sit inside the same indirect-duty box? And where would he draw the line—would he say that because no animal suffers in lab-grown meat there's nothing to object to, or would he still worry about a human character that treats animal life as raw material? I want the dialogue to get into those details rather than just have the scholar repeat the standard line about animals not counting.
