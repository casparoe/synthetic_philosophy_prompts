A friend of mine is pitching a startup idea and I want to think it through before I tell him what I think. The product is a personal assistant built on inverse reinforcement learning. The key design rule is that it never asks you what you want. Instead it watches your behavior — clicks, purchases, how long you spend on things, what you flake on, what you say yes to under time pressure — and fits a reward function to that behavior. The pitch is that this is more honest than the usual "tell us your preferences" approach: people lie on surveys, they don't know what they want, and their stated values are mostly for show. Behavior, the founder says, is the only real preference data. The assistant then uses the inferred reward function to manage your email, plan your time, decide what to show you, and eventually make small purchases and negotiate on your behalf. On top of that, the system gets a running bonus for correctly predicting your next action, which is supposed to make its model of you converge faster.

I want to know how this actually plays out if it works as designed. What does it get right about revealed preference, and what does it miss about the gap between what people do and what they actually want? How do users start gaming it, consiously or not? What happens given that a lot of different reward functions can make the same behavior look optimal? And what does the prediction bonus do — is there a real difference between predicting someone and optimizing for them, and does the assistant end up with an incentive to make the user more predictable? Where does the whole thing break first, and can any of it be fixed without basically reintroducing asking people what they want?
