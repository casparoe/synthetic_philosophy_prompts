A colleague has pressed the following argument on our reading group, and I cannot find a way out of it. Each step seems forced, yet the conclusion is one I am unwilling to accept.

(1) A moral judgement is a bias rather than a preference when the best explanation of the agent's holding it cites factors other than sensitivity to the moral reasons that tell in its favour.
(2) For any moral judgement, there is a plausible possible history in which the agent holds that same judgement because of disgust, self-interest, cultural transmission, or social conformity, while still finding it self-evident and supported by reasons, had the agent's upbringing and circumstances been only modestly different.
(3) If such a possible history exists for a judgement, then the agent's actual introspective evidence, the judgement's seeming self-evident and well supported, does not discriminate between holding it out of sensitivity to reasons and holding it out of bias.
(4) If an agent's evidence does not discriminate between those two causes of their judgement, they are not entitled to rely on that judgement.
(5) Therefore no agent is entitled to rely on any moral judgement.
(6) But we are entitled to rely on at least some moral judgements, such as the judgement that torturing a child for amusement is wrong.
(7) Hence at least one of (1)–(4) is false.

The argument seems to show that the distinction between a moral preference and a moral bias cannot do the work we routinely ask of it: attempting to dismiss others' moral judgements as biased appears to collapse into scepticism about all moral judgement. I am unwilling to give up (6), but beyond that I want to know exactly what each option costs. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost for any project of distinguishing moral preferences from moral biases?
