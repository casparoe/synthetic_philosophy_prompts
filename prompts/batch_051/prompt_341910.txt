I run the weekly meeting for my school's psych club — about fifteen of us, mostly juniors and seniors, forty-five minutes. Its my turn next week and I want to do an activity on how people keep throwing more into something that's already gone wrong just because they already put something in (sunk cost, escalation of commitment). I've read about the dollar auction: you auction off a dollar or some prize, but the rule is that both the highest and the second-highest bidder have to pay their bid, and only the highest bidder actually gets the prize. So if I bid 80 cents and you bid 90, I should bid a dollar to avoid paying 80 cents for nothing, and then it spirals past the prize's value. I love that it shows the trap comes from the structure, not from people being dumb.

But my school has a rule against anything with real money (they count it as gambling), and I also don't want to just run the classic auction, because a club full of friends will either cooperate on purpose or one kid will bid weird just to troll. So: design a runnable 45-minute game for 15 high schoolers that gets them to feel themselves escalating before I name what happened. It can use points, fake money, tokens, roles, teams, rounds, whatever — but it has to make walking away actually cost something inside the game, or nobody's gonna care. Include the exact rules, what I should say up front (no lecture), a debrief where we map what they did onto the escalation idea, the ways it usually goes wrong when people actually run it and how I should react in the moment, and one thing about real-world escalation that the game can't capture no matter how well it goes. Also try to set it up so the one smart kid who solves it right away doesnt wreck the whole thing for everyone else.
