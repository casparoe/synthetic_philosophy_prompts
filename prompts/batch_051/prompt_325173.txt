Two people on my team were arguing at lunch about a friend's bet-sizing plan. The friend has a coin that wins 60% of the time and pays even money, and he can bet repeatedly with a big bankroll. I mostly just listened, and now I honestly can't sort out who's right. Can you adjudicate the argument move by move?

(1) Dana: The right fraction is full Kelly, so 20% of the bankroll each flip. That maximizes the expected log of wealth, which is the same as maximizing the geometric growth rate of the bankroll over many flips. Half-Kelly and everything else just compounds slower in the long run.

(2) Priya: Maximizing expected log wealth is just what you do if you have logarithmic utility. That's one taste among many. Kelly is only 'the' rational rule if your utility over final wealth happens to be log. For any other utility function, the right size is whatever maximizes that expected utility. So you can't call full Kelly rational without knowing the friend's preferences.

(3) Dana: You're conflating a utility function with a growth property. Kelly doesn't presuppose log utility. If you stick to fixed fractions, full Kelly has the highest time-average growth rate, and its terminal wealth will beat any other fixed fraction's terminal wealth almost surely as the number of flips grows. That's a mathematical property of the process, not a preference.

(4) Priya: 'Almost surely in the limit' is doing a lot of the work. It assumes an infinite horizon and probabilities known for certain. Full Kelly has brutal drawdowns on the way, and a real person would likely bail. Half Kelly gets you roughly three quarters of the growth with much less variance, and if the 60% is even slightly off, full Kelly puts you at risk of overbetting past the true peak. Besides, wanting the fastest asymptotic compounding is itself an objective — it doesn't come for free from the word 'rational.'
