I keep being pulled between two attitudes toward the consistency of elementary arithmetic, and I would like someone to force them against each other rather than merely side with one.

On one side, the metamathematics seems to push toward a principled, permanent sliver of agnosticism. Gödel's second incompleteness theorem shows that a consistent formal system strong enough to encode elementary arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency. Every proof of arithmetic's consistency I know of is therefore carried out in something stronger—a background set theory, transfinite induction over a large ordinal, or an appeal to the intended model of the natural numbers—and those resources are themselves at least as open to doubt as the arithmetic they are meant to secure. The claim that Peano arithmetic is consistent thus begins to look unprovable without circularity: we can never establish it except by presupposing something that already includes the basic arithmetic we trust. And because an inconsistency would not be an amorphous worry but a finite, mechanically checkable object—a derivation of "0 = 1"—the disciplined conclusion seems to be that we should carry a tiny, nonzero, ineliminable uncertainty even here. This is the conclusion I find hard to resist when I am doing the formal work.

On the other side, I find that I cannot make the doubt real. If I were presented with a fully checked derivation of a contradiction inside Peano arithmetic, my first thought would be that the checking procedure had been corrupted, that the formalization had gone wrong, or that I was being deceived—not that arithmetic is inconsistent. My confidence in elementary numerical reasoning is more secure than my confidence in any of the stronger systems offered as its warrant, so the proposal that arithmetic's consistency is an open question, to be assigned a probability like any other, feels less like intellectual honesty than like a misuse of the concept of doubt. I do not know how to weigh a conviction this basic against a limitative theorem that is itself believed only because elementary arithmetic can be believed.

Force the two together and give a negotiated verdict. Present the strongest version of each side, then say which should yield and what would justify that move: is the firm intuition a debunking target—something we are built or trained to feel even though it outruns what can be proved—or is the metamathematical result being overread when it is converted into grounds for doubt? Is there a reading of "consistent," "prove," or "doubt" on which both survive, and what would each side have to give up for that reconciliation to be more than verbal?
