Freud argues in *The Future of an Illusion* that religious ideas owe their hold on us to something other than evidence or reasoning: they fulfill, he says, the oldest, strongest, and most urgent wishes of mankind, above all the wish for a protecting father in the face of natural forces and fate, and that wishful origin is what makes them illusions even where they have not been shown false. Now consider a later development: research in the cognitive science of religion has produced a different genetic account, according to which religious concepts spread so widely because they fit ordinary evolved cognitive machinery—hyperactive agency detection, intuitive dualism, minimally counterintuitive concepts with memory advantages, and the like—rather than because they satisfy infantile wishes. This alternative explanation is also genetic, but it replaces Freud's motivational story with a more neutral, information-processing story. Does Freud's argument survive this development? In particular, does the conclusion that religious beliefs are illusions still follow if the relevant psychological source is a byproduct of normal cognition rather than wish-fulfillment, or must the argument be repaired—perhaps by restricting its force to certain kinds of religious belief—or is it undermined?
