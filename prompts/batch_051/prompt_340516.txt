Act as an expert in philosophical logic and metaphysics, and think through the strongest versions of both positions before you give a verdict.

I am a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and I have absorbed a certain style of thinking about bivalence and fatalism. I keep finding myself stuck on a version of the sea-battle problem that feels more serious than the usual "Aristotle thought there were truth-value gaps" summary suggests. Here is the clash as I experience it.

The theory I find compelling is classical bivalence: every well-formed declarative sentence is either true or false, and the law of excluded middle holds. This gives a clean, non-arbitrary semantics. If I say there will be a sea battle tomorrow, it is either true or false; if it is true, then the world is the way it is, and the future is just the later part of the way the world is. The theory is simple, it avoids ad hoc exceptions, and it keeps logic and truth pristinely straightforward.

But I also have a firm intuition that the future is open in a sense that bivalence seems to close off. Aristotle puts the difficulty in De Interpretatione 9 roughly like this: if every affirmation or negation is already true or false, then it was already true yesterday that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, and then it seems the sea battle must happen, and nothing anyone does can prevent it. But that cannot be right: whether there is a sea battle tomorrow is not already settled, and the ordinary practice of deliberation presupposes that both options are genuinely possible.

I am not asking for a history of interpretations of Aristotle. I am asking a systematic question. Present the strongest case for each side. Then walk me through what it would take for the bivalence/past-truth side to yield, what it would take for the open-future side to yield, and whether there is a reading of "true" or "settled" on which both survive without cheating. Which do you give up, and what exactly does the loser lose?

If I am framing the problem badly, or if the apparent clash rests on an equivocation, say that plainly instead of pretending there is a clean answer. I would rather have the question repaired than answered politely.
