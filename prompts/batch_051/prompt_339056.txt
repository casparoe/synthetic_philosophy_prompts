Ok heres a knot I've been chewing on. Suppose I add a column of numbers three times, pretty carefully, and I'm dead sure the total is 517. Then a friend who's just as quick at arithmetic as I am adds the same column and gets 518, shows me her work, and I can't see the mistake at a glance. What bugs me is this: if I genuinely know it's 517, then her work has to be wrong somewhere, so I could just shrug it off and not even check it. And even if I do check it and can't find the flaw, do I have to get less confident? My not finding the mistake doesn't mean there isn't one. But then again that sounds exactly like how a flat-earther treats counterevidence: if it goes against what I know, it must be flawed, and if I can't find the flaw, fine, I still know. I'd like a dialogue between two people who are both sharp and taking this seriously. One of them holds that real knowledge gives you a right to brush aside apparent counterevidence without grinding through it, and even after you've looked and failed to find the error, you still don't have to lower your confidence much. The other holds that you can't call evidence misleading until you've actually examined it, and if you examine it honestly and it holds up, that has to shake you at least some, or your so-called knowledge is just a wall. They should stick with that 517/518 example, and each one should respond to the specific thing the other just said rather than taking turns making little speeches. Write exactly five exchanges, numbered 1 through 5. In each exchange both characters speak, a sentence or two each. No introduction and no conclusion.
