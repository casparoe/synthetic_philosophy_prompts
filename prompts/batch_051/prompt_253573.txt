Ordinary judgment treats convergent means-end reasoning as unremarkable in firms and as cause for alarm when projected onto artificial systems. A corporation whose effective objective is to grow reported profit or share price is expected to seek secrecy, influence its regulators and auditors, protect itself against dissolution, and exploit loopholes; we call this business and rarely find it conceptually significant. But when the same inference is made about a capable machine with a narrow objective — that, whatever its final goal, it would by instrumental convergence tend to acquire subgoals such as self-preservation, resource acquisition, and concealment of its intermediate behavior — the inference is often dismissed as anthropomorphic or unscientific. If, as the orthogonality thesis claims, intelligence and final goals can combine arbitrarily, then increased capability does not by itself supply the machine with human scruples, which only strengthens the puzzle. State the asymmetry precisely and try to justify it. I want the strongest principle that permits treating the same instrumental logic as normal in organizations but as paranoia when applied to machines, tested against the nearest cases, and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
