I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I have worked myself into a knot while drafting a book. The book concerns a genetically engineered songbird, which I call the sentinel, bred to give a particular call when it detects a predator. The call sometimes goes off when a shadow moves, or when a branch falls. I want my narrator to say that those are false alarms—that the call means predator and is wrong when only a shadow is there. The thought seems plain. But when I tried to write the sentence, it stopped seeming plain at all, and I reconstructed the following argument. It seems to prove something stronger than I wanted, and I half suspect a term is sliding between two senses.

(1) If the sentinel's call means predator, then some fact must make predator, rather than predator-or-shadow-or-branch, the content of the call.

(2) For a purely physical signal, the natural candidate is the causal-informational relation: the call means what reliably causes it, or what it carries information about, under the relevant conditions.

(3) But the sentinel's call is caused by shadows and falling branches as well as by predators; looked at as a physical event alone, the call's regularities do not single out predator.

(4) So the causal-informational account is committed to assigning the call the disjunctive content predator-or-shadow-or-branch, rather than predator.

(5) A token of that disjunctive content is true whenever any disjunct obtains, so the call could not be false when a shadow sets it off.

(6) To count the shadow case as an error, we must privilege predators among the causes—we must say the call is supposed to indicate predators, not that it merely does indicate the disjunction.

(7) But being supposed to indicate something is function and design talk; bare causation and bare information do not mark one cause as correct and another as error.

(8) Therefore, if a physical signal can be false, nature must contain something like functions; if nature contains no such functions, then no physical signal is ever false.

My hunch is that the weak point is either the move from (3) to (4) or else step (7), but I cannot make the objection precise without begging the question. Is the argument valid exactly as written? For each premise, give the strongest reading on which it is true, and say whether the argument, with all premises on their strongest readings, still reaches (8). If a step fails, identify it exactly and give the strongest repaired version of it; then tell me whether the repaired argument still yields the conclusion that false alarms are possible only because the sentinel has a function.

Please structure your answer under these headings, in this order:

1. The target claim (about 100 words): what the argument is trying to establish and why a novelist would care.
2. Validity check (about 150 words): whether the argument as written is valid, and if not, the exact step at which the inference fails.
3. Step-by-step audit (about 500 words): go through each premise individually, giving its strongest reading and judging its truth.
4. The strongest repair (about 250 words): repair the faulty step or rebuild the argument in its most persuasive form, and state what it actually proves.
5. My verdict (about 150 words): whether, in good conscience, my narrator may say the sentinel's shadow-triggered call is false.
