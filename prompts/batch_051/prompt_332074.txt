I've been arguing with a friend about whether either of us has ever genuinely agreed to a terms of service, and she showed me a scheduling site she'd supposedly signed up for. When you hit the signup page, the clickwrap reads: "By clicking 'I agree,' you confirm that you have read and understood these Terms and that you agree to be bound by them; your click is your electronic signature." Except she never clicked it. She uses one of those agentic browser apps — she types "book me the next DMV appointment" and it fills in the forms. When we replayed the session, the app had opened the site, entered her name and email, and clicked "I agree" as just another step toward the appointment; she'd never seen the site before, let alone the Terms, and she'd only asked for an appointment, not to except anything. The site is take-it-or-leave-it — there's no way past the screen without a click — so it's a textbook adhesion contract, and the clause looks like it exists to manufacture proof that a person read and accepted.

So: is she bound by that clause, including the arbitration provision buried later in the Terms? I care less about the practical bottom line than about how the main interpretive approaches come out on the agent's click: the plain words, the purpose of the clause, what the drafters would have said if you'd asked them about a software agent clicking for its user, and what a reasonable person in her position would take an "I agree" button to be doing. Do these readings converge on treating the click as hers, or do they pull apart? And if they pull apart, is there any reading of "you" that handles this without either pretending she read the Terms or turning the agent into its own signatory?
