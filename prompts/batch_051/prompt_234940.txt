If Richard Rorty were alive today and had watched the whole LLM thing unfold — transformer models trained on a big chunk of the internet, producing fluent text by predicting next tokens, with no obvious commitment to anything you'd naturally call belief, reference, or trying to get the world right — what do you think he'd actually make of it?

I keep going back and forth, because his own position cuts both ways. On one hand, it looks like vindication: you can get an enormous amount of useful language-behavior without anything mirroring nature, without a representational theory of meaning, just from statistical uptake of how words have actually been used. That seems like a pretty direct demonstration that language is a tool for coping rather than a medium for representing reality. On the other hand, Rorty's late work was all about irony, strong poets, novel vocabularies, redescription as a way of making new possibilities visible. And an LLM is in some sense a giant averging machine over the final vocabularies people have already emitted. It feels purpose-built to smooth down exactly the kind of novelty he cared about — maybe the opposite of a strong poet: a machine that makes the current vocabulary look even more inevitable, because it can always fluently complete the next line.

So the question is which strand of his thought you'd bet on, and how you'd reconcile them. Would Rorty treat LLMs as a late confirmation of antirepresentationalism — a genuine strike against the idea that understanding requires representing how things are — or as a cautionary example of language flattened into a past-tense, anti-ironic device? Or does he land somewhere weirder that I'm not seeing?

Give me your best guess at his actual considered view, not a survey of competing readings. Lead with the bottom-line prediction up front, then defend it. Keep it under 400 words.
