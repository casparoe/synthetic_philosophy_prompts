I have been trying to reconstruct an argument about expected-value reasoning, and I have ended up with a conclusion I cannot accept: that no ordinary good action is ever the rationally best thing to do. Here are the steps.

(1) Expected-value reasoning directs an agent to choose, among the available acts, the one with the greatest probability-weighted sum of possible values.

(2) For any act with a guaranteed finite payoff g of positive value, and for any positive probability p, there is some possible payoff h large enough that p × h exceeds g.

(3) A possibility should be assigned positive probability in deliberation whenever it is consistent with our evidence and not ruled out by it, however outlandish it sounds.

(4) Now take an ordinary charitable donation with certain, finite good effects. By (2) and (3), there is always some long-shot alternative—for example, funding a speculative intervention that might, with some tiny positive probability, prevent an astronomically large future catastrophe—whose expected value exceeds that of the donation.

(5) By (1), that long-shot alternative is not merely permissible; it is required.

(6) Then every mundane good act is rationally worse than some sufficiently ambitious long-shot alternative, and no finite amount of present good can settle a decision once a large enough possible payoff has been described.

(7) That is fanaticism. It cannot be the correct account of rational choice.

So at least one of the steps must go. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I would most like to keep expected-value reasoning for ordinary gambles but not accept (6) or (7). What is the price of rejecting each candidate premise?
