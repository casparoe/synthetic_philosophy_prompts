I've been doing fieldwork with a small religious community whose constitution dates from the 1880s, and one clause keeps coming up whenever a new technology arrives. It reads: "No likeness of the Holy may be made by any craft, nor shall the Name be carried into unclean places." The precedents are a bit of a tangle. Painted icons were treated as forbidden likenesses; a cassette recording of the liturgy was eventually allowed because the elders reasoned that sound is not a likeness; and a member's landscape painting of the meeting-house was allowed because the building, though consecrated, is not itself the Holy. Now there's a fight over two new things. Some younger members use a prayer app that displays the sacred text and the divine Name on a phone, including in bars and public toilets, and a teenager in the community has trained a small language model on the hymnary so it can write new verses in the old style. The elders want both to fall under the clause; the younger members say the clause is about idolatry and public blasphemy, not apps and neural nets. Honestly I care less about who wins than about how the clause itself applies to cases its authors couldn't have pictured. Does a phone "carry the Name into an unclean place"? Does a model trained on sacred songs make "a likeness of the Holy by craft"? Walk me through what the plain words, the purpose, what the drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable member would take them to mean each give, and say whether those readings really diverge or just look like they do.
