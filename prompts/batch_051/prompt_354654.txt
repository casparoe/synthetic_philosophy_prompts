In my course on the faith-and-works controversy, we have mostly framed Paul and James as disagreeing about what makes a person righteous before God. I keep noticing a parallel with a completely different field, and I would like help deciding whether it is deep or superficial. James's insistence that faith must be shown in deeds looks to me like costly signaling in biology and economics: an unobservable quality (sincere trust) can be made credible only by actions that are expensive enough that someone without that quality would not bother performing them. In the standard model, the cost of the display is exactly what lets an observer separate the genuine from the counterfeit, and James seems to be doing something similar when he treats works as what makes an otherwise inert faith visible and, in some sense, complete. Paul, by contrast, could be read as denying that any display can establish the state in question: right standing is given, not generated by the works, so the costly act is not what produces the status it might appear to advertise. Is this a genuine structural parallel between James and costly-signaling theory, or a loose analogy? Where exactly do the mappings match and come apart? In particular, does the analogy predict something false — for example, that the largest or most publicly visible works should be the strongest evidence of faith — and what would have to be true of both sides for a result from costly-signaling models to transfer to the theological case?
