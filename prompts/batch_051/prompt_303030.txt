Okay so I'm an amateur but this has been bugging me for a while and I want an honest check. I've developed this little account I call the comparative-advantage theory of moral compromise. The core idea: two people or groups with deeply different value systems can make deals that are genuine moral improvements—not just a practical truce—without any shared moral scale. Each side internally ranks its own commitments by how painful it would be, by its own lights, to give them up. A trade is available when each side would rather have the other side's concession than hang onto the thing it would have to give up in return. So each side gives up the commitment it finds relatively cheap to abandon, and gets the concession it finds relatively expensive to do without. No common currency, and nobody has to admit their view was false before or after.

That's the principle. An example: a religious hospital and a secular reproductive-health clinic are both in a rural county. The hospital won't offer contraception and is applying for public money for a new wing. The clinic wants school-based sex ed and is angry that the hospital board lobbies against it. Suppose the hospital board cares far more about getting the public money than about the sex-ed fight, and the clinic cares far more about sex-ed than about blocking the hospital's funding. They agree: the hospital stops lobbying against sex-ed (which it still thinks is wrong, just less important), and the clinic stops opposing the hospital's funding request (which it still thinks is wrong, just less important). Each side gets the thing it cared about more by giving up the thing it cared about less. That looks to me like a moral gain on both sides, by their own standards, no conversion necessary.
