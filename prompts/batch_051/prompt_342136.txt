Novelists are in the business of legibility: in a novel, a character whose choices cannot be made intelligible by her motives, constraints, and circumstances is usually judged a failure of craft, and I spend much of my working life making inner lives more transparent to a reader than any actual person's inner life is to me. But the demand for legibility does not stay in fiction. In ordinary life, many of us treat a partner who will not give reasons, a supervisor whose criteria remain undisclosed, an institution that decides by opaque procedures, or a friend who will not say what she actually thinks as failing us, as though being interpretable to others were something they owed. Yet we do not hold ourselves to the same standard: the same opacity, when it is ours, goes by the names of privacy, autonomy, or mere reserve, and a demand that we render ourselves fully legible would strike us as intrusive. Is anything available to justify this asymmetry between the legibility we claim from others and the opacity we reserve for ourselves, or is it simply an inconsistency we live with? A strong answer should identify the best candidate justification, test it against nearby cases, and say whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned.
