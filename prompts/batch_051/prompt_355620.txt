I cover ideas and the sciences for a general-interest magazine, and at a recent workshop on decision theory I spent the dinner hour next to two participants who were arguing about what money-pump arguments actually show. To fix the case, the economist — call her Dana — proposed a person holding a voucher for restaurant C whose pairwise preferences run in a cycle: A over B, B over C, C over A. She added that such a cycle need not be silly; the three restaurants might differ along several dimensions at once, such as food, price, noise, and convenience, so each pairwise ranking reflects a different salient comparison. A trader then offers to swap C for B for a small fee. Since the person prefers B to C, she accepts. The trader offers to swap B for A for a small fee; accepted. Then A for C; accepted. The person holds the voucher she began with and has paid three fees. Dana said this shows the cyclic preferences themselves are rationally defective, because they make the person vulnerable to a sure loss.

Her interlocutor, Leo, a philosopher, replied that the argument goes through only because each trade is offered separately. Presented with the whole sequence at once, the person would decline the package; so the case does not indict the preferences — it indicts a chooser myopic enough never to look one step ahead. Dana countered that Leo's defense quietly abandons the preferences themselves: if B really is preferred to C, the first swap is an improvement no matter what comes after, and a person who declines a chain of individually improving steps is no longer letting her own preferences guide her but is managing herself — a different, unargued ideal. Leo answered that there is no abandonment here. A chain of trades is a single plan, he said, and plans are assessed as wholes; one can prefer every local step while rejecting the journey. The real burden, he insisted, is on Dana to explain why a norm about not courting sure losses across time should govern momentary pairwise judgments in the first place.

Please adjudicate the disagreement move by move. For each of the four moves — the pump and its lesson, the myopia reply, the self-management counter, and the plan-evaluation reply — say whether it is clearly right, defensible but incomplete, or clearly mistaken, and briefly justify each rating. Then say which distinctions both parties are sliding over and what a fuller resolution of the dispute would need to settle.
