I'm a novelist doing background reading for a book set in a garment factory outside Dhaka, and I keep running into an argument I don't have a name for. The factory pays wages that are awful by Western standards but genuinely better than the alternatives most workers have: going back to a village, day labor, or another factory with worse fire safety. Nobody was coerced — when there's an opening, people line up. The owner's side says: these workers are better off with the job than without it, they took it knowingly, and if you shut the factory or forced much higher wages the buyers would just move their orders to Vietnam and the workers would be worse off. As far as I can tell, all of that is actually true.

And yet I cant shake the sense that something morally wrong is still in the arrangement, and not just in a general the-world-is-unfair way. The owner's profit depends on the fact that the workers have almost no good options. If they had a decent alternative, he'd have to pay more or clean the place up. So I'm left with what feels like a puzzle: an exchange that makes both parties better off than they were before, fully consented to by the worse-off party, and nonetheless the better-off party seems to be doing something objectionable precisely by making the offer, or by profiting from the other side's bad options. I'm not asking for a verdict on whether sweatshops are good overall. I want to know if this specific configuration has a name — the mutually-improving-but-still-wrong-seeming exchange — and how philosophers carve it apart from ordinary exploitation where the victim ends up worse off. Also: is there a named principle for the idea that if you improve someone's position relative to how things would otherwise be for them, you haven't wronged them, and what are the standard objections to it? I'd rather get the distinctions right than just have a label.
