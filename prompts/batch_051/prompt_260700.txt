A colleague pressed the following dilemma on me, and I cannot find the step to reject. It concerns the old Muʿtazila–Ashʿarite dispute over whether moral obligation is grounded in God's command or in something reason can grasp, and it leads to a conclusion that seems too strong.

(1) On the Ashʿarite view, an act is right just in case God commands it and wrong just in case God forbids it; there is no good or evil prior to, or independent of, divine command. (2) If that is right, then God's commanding an act is the sole feature that makes it right. (3) So if God commanded us to harm the innocent and forbade us to relieve them, harming the innocent would be right and relieving them would be wrong. (4) And on that view 'God is just' can mean only 'God's commands are God's commands'; it cannot mean that God's commands answer to any standard of justice. (5) But a God whose justice is vacuous in this sense is not morally distinguishable from an omnipotent tyrant whose decrees are right simply because he decrees them. (6) So the Ashʿarite strategy of making command prior to justice leaves us unable to call God just in any contentful sense.

(7) The Muʿtazilite alternative avoids this by holding that reason can know certain acts to be good and evil independently of revelation, and that God's command answers to that prior standard. (8) But if the standard is already available to reason, then God's command adds nothing to the rightness of the acts it commands; revelation at most publicizes a law that already binds us. (9) Therefore the Ashʿarite empties God of justice, while the Muʿtazilite empties God of authority. Since the debate treated these as the two live accounts of obligation, it rests on a false dilemma, and no coherent account of divine command and moral obligation is available.

That conclusion is stronger than I am willing to accept, but I do not know which step I should give up. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it force you to give up?
