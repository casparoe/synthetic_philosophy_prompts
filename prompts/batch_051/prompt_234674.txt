I am a novelist at work on a historical novel set in the 1850s, in London and in British India around the end of East India Company rule. One of my central characters is an earnest young Englishman who admires John Stuart Mill, and I want to render Mill's attitude toward empire accurately rather than as a modern caricature. In the course of my research I read a recent intellectual biography that summarizes Mill's views on India roughly as follows.

Mill held that the liberty he defended in On Liberty was never intended to apply universally. The harm principle presupposes people capable of being improved by reasoned discussion, and Mill regarded whole societies, including India, as still in an earlier, childlike stage of development; for such peoples he thought despotic government legitimate, provided its purpose was their improvement. On this account, Britain's task in India was not immediately to extend freedom but to train Indians in the habits of industry, foresight, and self-command, after which they could safely be granted self-government. Mill was no outsider to empire: he worked at the East India Company for thirty-five years, and when Company rule came under attack he defended it against direct Crown control. His position was therefore not anti-imperial. He was, in effect, a liberal imperialist, convinced that a civilizing despotism abroad was compatible with his domestic liberalism. The biographer concludes from this that Mill's harm principle was a local doctrine for Europeans: the liberty he prized was a privilege of the already civilized, while for the rest of the world he prescribed the very paternal interference he refused to allow in England.

I want to know how much of this I can trust. Please assess the biographer's summary claim by claim: what is accurate, what is a common simplification or compression, and what would a careful reader of Mill object to? In particular, is the concluding characterization fair? Is it true that Mill reserved the harm principle for Europeans while endorsing paternalism for Indians, and how much of the gap between those two stances is Mill's own explicit position rather than the biographer's editorializing? I need to decide whether presenting Mill in the novel as simply a liberal imperialist would be defensible, or whether it would flatten a more conflicted position.
