I serve on a working group that reviews proposals for new and revised diagnostic categories in a psychiatric classification, the kind of process that produces manuals like the DSM or the ICD. The group meets once per revision cycle, which in practice means every few years; in a typical cycle we see forty or fifty proposals, and each proposal gets roughly ninety minutes of committee time before our recommendation goes up to the body that makes the final adoption decision. The stakes are high: the categories we adopt or reject go on to shape who is diagnosed, what treatment is reimbursed, what counts as a disability claim, how people describe themselves, and how clinicians, schools, insurers, courts, and families treat the people who receive the label. I have become convinced that our current review process has a specific blind spot. We tend to evaluate a proposed category as though it simply described a fixed population that exists independently of the classification. But classification can act on the people it classifies. Once a category is in circulation, people learn about it, take it up, reinterpret their own history through it, change their behavior or self-presentation, form communities and advocacy groups around it, and build institutions and incentives around it; clinicians, employers, insurers, and family members adjust too. Those changes can in turn alter the very phenomenon the category was meant to capture, and can create a new kind of person in the world rather than merely naming one that was already there. I gather this is close to what Ian Hacking called looping effects, but the group has no philosopher on it and the procedure must be usable by clinicians and methodologists without that vocabulary.

Please write a decision procedure we can run on each proposal before we vote, to anticipate these looping effects and let them count in our recommendation. It should take the form of an ordered checklist: the questions to ask, in order, about a proposed category; what would count as a sufficient answer to each question and what sort of evidence or expertise we would need to answer it; where the procedure stops with a red flag or a no; and where it hands the decision to the committee as an irreducible judgment of values rather than a factual question. It should also mark the cases the procedure cannot settle and the ways the checklist can be gamed or can misfire when the committee is under time pressure.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical proposals: (a) a new category for a subthreshold but impairing pattern of mood instability in adolescents, proposed to unlock early intervention; (b) a proposal to split an existing broad category so that one new subtype is defined partly by how intensely the person identifies with the diagnosis; and (c) a proposal to remove a long-standing category that patient groups now treat as central to their identity. At least one of the three applications should arrive at a recommendation the committee will not like, whether that is a red flag on a category we are inclined to approve or pressure to approve a category we would rather avoid, and I want you to state that recommendation plainly rather than softening it. Finally, say honestly what a checklist cannot capture about looping effects and what the person chairing the process will have to supply by judgment in the room.
