I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and last month I was reporting a piece on the doomsday argument. One of my sources, a physicist who works on existential risk, kept deflecting my questions with the same slogan: "you should assume you're a random sample." When I asked whether the argument still works if the relevant unit is civilizations rather than individual humans, or if a lot of the future population would be digital, he just said "look, you have to assume you're typical," as if the slogan settled both questions. I'm sure there's a real principle in there somewhere, but the more I think about it the more it seems like a whole cluster of different claims hiding in one sentence. Can you seperate out the precise things that slogan could mean? Specifically: what set is the draw from, what exactly is being sampled (a person, a life, a moment, a civilization?), what notion of "random" is doing the work, and why should I accept any of them? For each candidate reading, tell me whether its a platitude, a substantive and defensible assumption, or just false, and then say which reading actually gets you to the doomsday conclusion. And because I want to push back in the next interview: which readings do people quietly slide between when the slogan is challenged, and which reading would be needed to make the "civilizations" or "digital people" objections irrelevant?
