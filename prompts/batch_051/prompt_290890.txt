In several recent discussions about whether the aim of philosophical work should be to analyze our concepts or to improve them, I have encountered the same analogy in increasingly confident forms: concepts are tools. A concept is said to be like a hammer or a saw. It has a job to do; it can be more or less suited to that job; it can become blunt, obsolete, or dangerous; and when it fails, the only sensible response is to sharpen it, repair it, or reach for a better tool. The analogy is then used to carry a strong conclusion: that conceptual engineering, the deliberate revision or replacement of defective concepts, is just tool maintenance applied to thought, and that descriptive conceptual analysis, which merely records how a concept is in fact used, is as misdirected as cataloguing the defects of a broken hammer instead of fixing the shelf. On this view, asking whether our concept of race, or addiction, or statistical significance is defective is no different in kind from asking whether a particular wrench fits a particular bolt.

I want an audit of this analogy as an argument, not as a slogan. First, supply the correspondence table explicitly: what in the conceptual case is supposed to play the role of the tool's handle, material, blade, user, workshop, task, and the act of replacement, and flag any row where the mapping is forced or ambiguous. Second, identify the places where the mapping breaks down. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended normative conclusion from those that only show that concepts are not literally made of metal. Third, isolate the features of the source domain that the analogy quietly imports into the target domain, such as the idea that there is a stable, user-independent task by which fitness is judged, that an old tool can be discarded without loss, and that all users share the same purposes. Fourth, give a verdict: does the conclusion that conceptual engineering is continuous with tool maintenance survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is the analogy carrying the argument? If the analogy fails in important respects, state what a better analogy would be and exactly which features it captures that the tool analogy misses.
