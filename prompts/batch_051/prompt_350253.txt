I'm taking a grad seminar on Darwin and moral philosophy, but my actual training is in evolutionary biology, so I keep hitting on things that are probably old news to philosophers and not knowing whether I've found a real problem or just a bad reading. The passage in The Descent of Man that I keep circling is the one where Darwin says that any animal with social instincts and sufficiently developed intellectual powers would inevitably acquire a moral sense or conscience. His story, as I understand it, is that a social instinct is more persistent than a temporary urge like hunger or fear, and after the temporary urge passes, the social instinct reasserts itself in memory and produces a feeling of dissatisfaction. Morality, on this picture, is what it feels like when one instinct outlasts another inside reflection.

I've been calling this the "lagging instinct" theory of conscience. The idea is that a moral judgment is just the felt residue of a persistent social instinct after a conflicting impulse has subsided. Not the content of the norm, just the structure: any social species could have a conscience, but it would track whatever its social instincts happened to be. I know this sounds very deflationary, and maybe that's the point, but I want someone who knows the literature to tell me whether this is a coherent theory or just a metaphor. Is it already in Darwin under another name, or worked out later by someone like Michael Ruse or Patricia Churchland? Where does it break? Specifically, can the lagging-instinct story distinguish moral guilt from ordinary regret over any thwarted desire, or does it collapse into a purely descriptive account that has no room for normativity? Give me the strongest version of my view, the nearest existing view if there is one, and the two or three hardest counterexamples. Please write in American English.
