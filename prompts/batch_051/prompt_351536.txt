I am drafting a short statement of my positions on inheritance, dynastic wealth, and equal opportunity, and I want a consistency audit before I circulate it. I hold the following positions, and I genuinely cannot tell whether they form a coherent package or where the strain is.

1. Equality of opportunity matters in this sense: it is unjust if two children of equal talent and motivation have very different life chances solely because of the wealth of the family they were born into. Large inheritances are one of the central mechanisms by which this happens, so the state should impose a heavy tax on large estates at death, above a high exemption.

2. At the same time, the state should not try to prevent parents from giving their children substantial advantages while the parents are alive: paying private school fees, funding university and professional training, helping with a first home, making interest-free loans, bringing a child into a family business, or putting money in a trust. Family autonomy and legitimate parental partiality protect these transfers. I also think a lifetime gift is morally different from a bequest: a gift is embedded in an ongoing relationship and the giver can see what it does, whereas a bequest is simply a windfall left behind.

3. My support for estate taxation does not depend on the claim that all family-transmitted advantage could be equalized. Much advantage is transmitted through upbringing, education, networks, and genetic luck, and no tax regime could fully equalize those. I take an inheritance tax to be only a partial correction of starting positions, not a complete one.

4. As between an annual tax on wealth and a tax levied once at death, I prefer the latter. An annual wealth tax seems to me to punish saving, to penalize the same asset holdings again and again, and to raise serious valuation and liquidity problems. A tax at death is a one-off adjustment that falls at a natural point of transfer.

5. I do not find the standard double-taxation objection persuasive. There is no principle that a dollar may be taxed only once; income is taxed when it is earned and again when it is spent. So the fact that an estate consists of already-taxed savings is not, by itself, an objection to taxing it again at death.

Please audit this set. I want to know which of these commitments genuinely pull against which others, which pairs only look as though they conflict, and how the genuine tensions should be ranked by severity. For each real tension, state the least costly revision and what it would force me to give up.
