I'm preparing to teach Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day in a course on the ethics of emotion, and I want to use the novel to test the Stoic ideal of apatheia — understood not as modern apathy but as freedom from the disturbing passions (pathē), which the Stoics analyzed as judgments that something outside our control is good or evil. Stevens, the novel's narrator-butler, turns a life of emotional self-suppression into a professional ethic he calls dignity: he keeps serving while his own father dies upstairs, he refuses to acknowledge his feelings for Miss Kenton, and he extends the same scrupulous neutrality to Lord Darlington's political misjudgments, only asking near the end of his life whether the composure was worth what it cost him. Read the novel through this Stoic lens and tell me how far the lens holds. Does Stevens actually achieve anything like apatheia, or does he just repress feeling and mistake the numbness for self-mastery? Where does the novel dramatize something the Stoics would recognize, and where does it trade on a confusion between apatheia and insensibility? Is the book best read as a critique of the ideal itself, or as a portrait of someone who pursued it badly — and what difference would that distinction make to a defender of equanimity? I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the novel to a case study in Stoicism, and it should not flatten apatheia into mere calm. End by saying what the novel shows about the cost of freedom from disturbance that the standard philosophical defenses and criticisms of apatheia tend to leave out.
