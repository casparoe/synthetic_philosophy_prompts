I'm working through Kant's position that we have no direct duties to animals, only indirect duties regarding them: cruelty to an animal is wrong because it coarsens the agent's own character and makes mistreatment of human beings more likely, not because anything is owed to the animal itself. The standard objections I already know are these. First, the psychological link from animal cruelty to human cruelty is empirical and contingent, and the moral status of animal abuse should not rest on a shaky prediction about the agent's future conduct. Second, the criterion of direct moral standing, rational self-consciousness, would also exclude human beings who lack it, such as infants or people with severe cognitive disabilities. Third, the view assigns animals only instrumental value, whereas the wrongfulness of cruelty seems to lie in the harm done to the animal, not in its effect on the perpetrator's character. Fourth, in a case of secret cruelty that leaves the agent's dispositions unchanged, the view seems unable to say that anything wrong has happened at all. Fifth, the whole architecture of indirect duties cannot even state the conviction that we owe animals something in their own right. I want an objection that is not on this list and is not a variant, special case, or combination of anything on it. Give me the strongest candidate you can construct, and then assess it honestly: does it succeed against a careful defender of Kant's view, and what does it add that the listed objections do not?
