Ever since my dad announced he wants his skeleton articulated and stood in the front hall after he dies, with a little engraved plate that says go to work, I've been trying to predict how my wife will react: she's my tiebreaker, and she has a track record on this purity-and-sanctity stuff that seems crazy consistent but I can't pull out the rule. Here's what she's said over the years:

- When our old dog died, a friend joked about eating him, and she said that would be wrong, full stop—not just gross.
- When my brother went through a cricket-flour phase she said it was fine, even though she visibly gaged at the cookies; disgust by itself isn't a moral reason.
- I once used an old thrift-store Bible to prop open the garage door and she said wrong, and it wasn't about damaging a book.
- She says making a necklace out of your own pulled wisdom teeth is weird but not wrong.
- She thought our city museum shouldn't display a real human skeleton posed like a ballerina, even though it came from an old anatomy collection; the person never agreed and it felt like a circus act.
- But when her aunt wanted her ashes mixed into a concrete stepping stone for the garden and left it in writing, Sarah said that was sweet, not a violation.

Now my dad: he wants his bones cleaned and wired into a standing figure in the hallway, with a nameplate that says go to work. He genuinely thinks it honors his wishes, and he thinks it's hilarious. Do I predict she goes with the stepping stone and says okay, or with the museum skeleton and says wrong? Which earlier case is the closest analog? What underlying rules fit the whole track record, where exactly do they split on my dad, and what one further case would settle whether consent or dignity is doing the real work? Whatever she says, what principle is she committing herself to?
