I've been teaching Simone Weil's essay On the Abolition of All Political Parties and I've reconstructed what seems like the core argument for a handout, but now it looks like it proves too much, and I want to know where it goes wrong. She opens with a line that I think is doing more work than I noticed at first: "The word ‘party’ is taken here in the meaning it has in Continental Europe. In Anglo-Saxon countries, this same word designates an altogether different reality, which has its roots in English tradition and is therefore not easily transposable elsewhere." Here's the argument I built from the essay:

(1) A party, in the Continental sense Weil has in mind, is not merely an association for advancing a shared political program; it is an organization whose first imperative is its own growth and survival.
(2) Because of that, a party must impose on its members a collective line that they are expected to support even when their own judgment about truth or justice cuts against it.
(3) To belong to such a party is therefore to suspend the very faculty — independent judgment about justice and truth — that makes political action legitimate.
(4) But if that is what makes parties illegitimate, the same reasoning should make any organization with a collective line and membership discipline illegitimate: churches, unions, professional bodies, maybe even academic departments.
(5) That seems absurd; not all such organizations are corrupting.

So either Weil's argument proves too much, or one of these steps sneaks in something specific to parties that doesn't transfer to other bodies. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (3) than (4), but I'm not sure I can.
