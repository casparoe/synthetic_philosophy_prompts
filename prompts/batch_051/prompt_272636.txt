I teach ancient philosophy, and I keep getting stuck on a methodological aside in Herodotus, Histories 2.123 (Godley translation). He's right before relaying Egyptian tales about the underworld and the transmigration of souls, and he says: "These Egyptian stories are for the benefit of whoever believes such tales: my rule in this history is that I record what is said by all as I have heard it." I want to test whether the stance in that sentence can be restated in modern Bayesian terms without losing whatever makes it interesting.

I know enough Bayesian machinery to be dangerous—likelihood ratios, posterior odds, priors over witness reliability, conditional independence—but Im not a formal epistemologist, so be explicit about the models and dont let the translation fudge itself. Take Herodotus' rule as a proposal about how an inquirer should handle many reports of unknown reliability. Restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of Bayesian updating on testimony: what prior over informant reliability is implicit in "record what is said by all as I have heard it," what exactly gets updated when he records a report he does not endorse, whether the rule is best read as updating on the fact that so-and-so said p rather than on p itself, and whether iterating this leads to the posterior-convergence, priors-washing-out story that formal epistemology likes.

Then give me the residue report: what translates cleanly, what had to be distorted, and what has no Bayesian expression at all. I especially want a verdict on whether Herodotus' stance is just the correct Bayesian policy of conditioning on testimony while suspending judgment about the facts—in which case the ancients got there first—or whether there's a leftover about the duty to transmit reports you cannot vouch for, or the difference between inquiry, what he saw himself, and what he merely heard, that a Bayesian partition of evidence flattens. If there's a leftover, tell me whether that's a defect of the Bayesian framework or a sign that the Herodotean stance is less principled than it sounds.
