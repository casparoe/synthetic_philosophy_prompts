In Anglo-American courts, photographs are routinely admitted as evidence and treated as showing the event itself — a surveillance still is treated as showing the defendant at the till — while a photorealistic drawing of the same scene is treated as a mere illustration or reconstruction, admissible under different conditions and given different weight. At the same time, courts accept that photographs involve choices of framing, timing, and exposure, and they draw a firm line between acceptable adjustments such as cropping or brightening and unacceptable manipulation such as airbrushing a face or moving a figure. What view of the relation between a photograph and what it depicts does this body of practice presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose that photographs are in some sense transparent — that we see the scene through them — in a way drawings are not? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say whether the practice is coherent. Then tell me what would have to change in evidentiary practice if we instead held that photographs are just another kind of depiction, produced mechanically but no more transparent than a drawing. Finally, say which argument or consideration would most likely change your verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
