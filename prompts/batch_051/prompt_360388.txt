For a feature I was reporting on how debates about time and identity bear on the idea of a continuing self, I sat in on a working group about persistence through time. In the discussion afterward, two participants kept circling a disagreement: Amara defended endurantism, the view that objects are wholly present at each moment at which they exist; Julian defended perdurantism, the view that objects are extended through time and composed of distinct temporal parts. I reconstructed their exchange from my notes; it ran roughly as follows.

Amara: "Consider a brass trumpet that was shiny when it was new and is tarnished now. The natural thing to say is that one and the same trumpet was shiny and is now tarnished. That requires the whole trumpet to have existed then and to exist now, bearing different properties at different times. On a temporal-parts picture, by contrast, it is strictly one part that is shiny and a different part that is tarnished. Then no single thing has changed from the first state to the second."

Julian: "The single thing is the worm. The shiny and tarnished parts are both parts of it, just as a road can be straight in one stretch and winding in another without being two roads. Change is the worm's having different properties at different times by having different temporal parts. The trumpet that was shiny is the same trumpet that is tarnished — the whole four-dimensional trumpet."

Amara: "But the road's being straight here and winding there is not a change in the road; it is a variation between parts. If you analyze change as variation between parts, you replace becoming with a static spread of differences. Change requires a subject that was one way and now is another way, not one part that is one way and another part that is another way."

Julian: "Then tell me how one wholly present trumpet can be both shiny and tarnished without contradiction. It cannot be both simpliciter. You will have to say it is shiny-at-t1 and tarnished-at-t2, which turns being shiny into a relation to a time rather than an ordinary property. The temporal indexing is doing exactly the work my temporal parts are doing."

Amara: "The indexing is legitimate, not a dodge: properties are had at times. The trumpet is wholly present at each time, and it has incompatible properties at different times. Your view has its own, worse cost. If only temporal parts are present at a moment, then when I hold a trumpet I hold a slice of a trumpet, not the trumpet; and for a person, what deliberates or feels pain at a time is at best a stage that is not identical with the person. You owe an account of how the person itself is conscious or responsible if the person is a spread of momentary parts."

Julian: "Nothing thinks or feels at a durationless instant anyway. Experience and deliberation take time, so the subject of a mental state is an extended portion of the worm, not an instantaneous slice. The person just is the whole temporally extended worm, and its stages are parts of it, as hands are parts of a body. Responsibility attaches to that whole through the causal and psychological connections among its stages, not to an isolated slice."

Amara: "But now you owe an account of why these stages, located at different times, form one person rather than many. Endurantism does not face that problem: there is a single wholly present subject at each time, and its identity is not assembled out of parts. If you answer that the stages are unified by causal and psychological continuity, you are borrowing a notion of continuing identity that the endurance picture handles directly, whereas your picture still needs to explain how a collection of parts can be the kind of thing that is conscious or responsible as a whole."

Julian: "Every theory needs an account of unity. Ask what makes a nose, ears, and hands parts of one body; we do not find spatial composition mysterious, even though those parts exist all at once. Temporal composition looks strange only because we assume from the start that being wholly present at a time is the norm. What unifies a person's temporal parts is the same kind of spatiotemporal and causal continuity we track when we say it is the same person over time."

I could not tell where the weight of the argument fell, partly because each seemed right that the other was purchasing a solution at some theoretical cost. Could you go through the exchange claim by claim and say who is right about what, where each argument succeeds or goes wrong, what both of them are missing, and who is more right overall?
