In my history and philosophy of science seminar we keep having the same fight about Newton, and it never moves. It's over the opening definitions of Newton's Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy (the Principia). I'm reading the 1846 English edition, where Definition II reads:

"The quantity of motion is the measure of the same, arising from the velocity and quantity of matter conjunctly."

One camp reads this as Newton groping toward what we now call momentum: quantity of motion is mass times velocity, and the later vector treatment just makes explicit something Newton already had. The other camp says that's an anacronism: Newton's quantitas motus is a scalar, additive quantity, not a directed vector, and it sits inside a conceptual scheme of vis insita and impressed force that has no exact modern counterpart, so reading "momentum" back into Definition II imports structure Newton didn't have. Both sides quote the same sentence and then nobody moves, and the argument has looked like this for years. I'm less interested in a verdict on who's right than in the anatomy of the impasse. My working guess is that it's two different questions parading as one—one about what Newton thought, the other about whether his definition is mathematically equivalent to a modern notion—but I keep second-guessing myself because the two sides really do seem to disagree about what Newton meant. So: what kind of disagreement is this actually—a factual one, a verbal one about the word "momentum," or two groups answering different questions under one heading? And given the diagnosis, what would move it: a criterion for when two concepts are the same, a distinction between mathematical content and conceptual content, or just an agreement to stop treating it as one debate?
