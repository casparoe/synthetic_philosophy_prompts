I'm a math PhD student and this just got personal: in the same month I refereed two papers. One was a 250-page human proof that probably no one read start to finish; it was accepted after the author and two referees vouched for it, because the main idea was "clear enough." The other rested on a computational check of a million cases, code included and rerunnable, and it was rejected as unverifiable. I can't survey either one myself, so here's the asymmetry that's been bugging me: when a proof outruns my ability to check every step, I'm basically relaxed if it rests on a chain of other humans vouching for it, but I get suspicious if it rests on a deterministic program that won't get tired or fudge anything — it can only crash or have a bug. Is there actually a principle that justifies treating human-vouched unsurveyability different from machine-vouched unsurveyability? I want your strongest candidate principle and then an actual commitment: keep, reverse, or drop the asymmetry. If you keep it, defend it against the case where the human chain is every bit as opaque to me as the code and much less reproducible; if you reverse or drop it, tell me what that means for how mathematics should treat the four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture.
