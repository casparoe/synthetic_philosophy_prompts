A friend and I recently had a long argument about how far a country should go toward economic self-sufficiency: reshoring critical industries, keeping food and energy production within its own borders, and minimizing reliance on other states. My friend kept returning to a personal analogy to carry the load. A nation that depends on others is like a person who never becomes an adult; just as a mature person should stand on their own feet, meet their own needs, and not be at the mercy of someone else's choices, a mature nation should do the same, and dependence is a form of arrested development in both cases. He concluded that a country should pursue something close to autarky. I wanted to resist, but I could not tell how much of my resistance was really an objection to the analogy rather than to the conclusion.

Please audit this analogy for me. Give the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the person, the parents, the family household, the process of growing up, the child's vulnerability to a parent's will, and the adult's independence. Then mark where the mapping is faithful and where it breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for my friend's argument from the ones that are merely cosmetic. I have one suspicion I want you to test: the analogy seems to import a flattering picture of personal self-reliance. A self-reliant adult in a modern economy is not independent of other people; they depend constantly on farmers, engineers, judges, and counterparties they have never met. Their independence is really reliable, rule-governed access to others, not the absence of dependence. If that is right, the personal ideal, properly described, may not point toward autarky at all. I want to know whether this point transfers cleanly to the national case, or whether there is a relevant difference between a person's dependence on anonymous others within a shared legal order and a nation's dependence on other sovereign states, and whether that difference can bear the weight my friend puts on it. Finally, tell me what would have to be true of the world for the person-to-state transfer to license autarky, and whether the analogy, once its disanalogies are on the table, supports a strong, weak, or merely decorative generalization.
