I'm a journalist who covers politics and the science of how people reason, and I need a reusable decision procedure for a recurring judgment call: when is it fair to describe a politician's, pundit's, or movement's argument as arguing in bad faith, rather than as wrong, overconfident, or a good-faith position I reject? I make this call a few times a week, usually on deadline with about ten minutes to think, and the wording has to survive my editors and a corrections process. I have no formal training in philosophy, so the procedure must work without the theory.

There is a known asymmetry the procedure should correct for: people attribute their own side's excesses to legitimate grievance or pressure, and the other side's identical behavior to bad character or bad faith. I also mostly encounter the other side through my own feed and through clips chosen by their critics, so the procedure should include a step checking whether I have seen the argument in its strongest form before I call it bad faith.

Please give me:
- a working definition of arguing in bad faith that does not require reading minds;
- an ordered checklist, each test separating bad faith from what it is often confused with — sincere disagreement under different priors, motivated reasoning, echo-chamber distortion, incompetence, or a motte-and-bailey move in which someone retreats from a strong claim to a weak one and acts as if the strong claim was established;
- the hard cases the procedure must handle, including a speaker who is sincere but so epistemically sealed that the practical effect matches bad faith;
- worked applications to three short cases, one of which must be a case where the procedure tells me not to use the phrase even though I would like to;
- an honest note on which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and what the procedure cannot establish about a speaker's actual state of mind.

Write as an expert in political psychology and informal logic. Before drafting the checklist, think through the cases where the distinction is genuinely hardest, so the procedure does not just flatter my priors.

Keep the whole response under 1,200 words.
