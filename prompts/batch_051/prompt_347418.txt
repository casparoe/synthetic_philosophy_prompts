I am the lexical editor of an English learner's dictionary and its companion app at a small publisher. I recently took over the wordlist after the previous editor left, and I inherited an inconsistent policy: some formations are buried inside a base word's entry as inflected forms while formations of the same general kind get their own headwords, apparently decided by feel. The inconsistency is now blocking our next release. Every additional headword carries real per-entry costs — recorded audio, translations, example sentences, editorial review — while every wrongly hidden form makes a learner's lookup fail, so I need a defensible, reusable policy rather than my own case-by-case judgment.

Please write a decision procedure that a junior editor with no formal linguistics training can run in about fifteen minutes per candidate formation, to decide whether a formation should be (a) treated as an inflected form displayed within the paradigm of another headword, (b) given its own headword as a derived lexeme, or (c) escalated to me as a genuine judgment call. The procedure should be grounded in the actual inflection–derivation distinction and its standard diagnostics: obligatoriness in syntactic context, productivity and the significance of gaps, change of word class, semantic regularity against lexical drift, closed paradigms, and participation in agreement or other syntactic rules. It should face the fact that these diagnostics form a cline and can pull in opposite directions. Give me ordered questions, what counts as a decisive answer to each, and a short battery of test sentences or substitutions an editor can apply; say where the procedure stops with a decision and where it stops with "escalate"; and mark the steps that are judgment calls rather than mechanical tests. The rationale for any escalation should name the conflicting criteria.

Then apply the procedure to at least these four English formations, showing the reasoning for each disposition: (1) dogs versus doglike; (2) the suppletive comparative better, taken against good and well; (3) the adverb quickly, taken against quick; (4) the -ing form running as it occurs in I am running, Running is healthy, and a running joke. At least one application should reach a conclusion that the cost-conscious side of the team would rather avoid, for example that a highly productive formation still requires its own headword, and at least one should reach an escalation or a policy decision the procedure cannot make. End by stating clearly what the procedure cannot settle and where dictionary policy, rather than morphology, must decide.
