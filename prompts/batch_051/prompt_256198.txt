Two housemates of mine recently argued for an entire evening about a single thought experiment, and I'm still not confident either of them got it quite right. The thought experiment was a gradual-upload procedure: a clinic replaces each of your neurons, one small batch at a time, with an artificial unit engineered to reproduce that neuron's input–output behavior and preserve its connections to the surrounding tissue. You are awake and conversing throughout, and at every stage you report feeling fully like yourself; by the end, every neuron is artificial, and the patient walks out convinced that the procedure was safe. Here is the dispute, roughly as it went.

Arman argued that the person plainly survives. There is no step at which the original person dies; each artificial unit preserves the causal role of the neuron it replaces, memories survive in the connection weights, and the patient is continuously conscious. If you deny survival here, he said, you are committed to saying that ordinary metabolism kills us all the time, since our atoms already turn over constantly. Lena replied that this conflates levels of replacement: metabolism swaps atoms inside a living neuron while leaving the neuron intact, whereas the procedure replaces the neurons themselves with nonbiological hardware. Arman, she said, was assuming that a neuron's entire contribution to consciousness is exhausted by its input–output profile, which is the very thing at issue. Arman countered that if the silicon unit does the same causal work, then by any reasonable functionalist criterion the mental state is preserved. No single replacement, he insisted, can be the step that ends you; if you admit the patient survives after each individual step, denying that they survive the whole sequence is a sorites mistake. You cannot say when the person became someone else, so the boundary is an illusion.

Lena answered that she did not need a sharp boundary: survival might be partial, or identity might be vague. The deeper problem, she said, is that third-person continuity is not the same as first-person continuity. The patient's reports of feeling fine are exactly what a convincing imitation would produce, so Arman is using the thing in question—the continued existence of the same perspective—as evidence for itself. Arman shot back that this standard is unsatisfiable: dreamless sleep interrupts consciousness and no one can directly verify that they woke up as the same subject, so her skepticism would make every morning a problem. Lena replied that sleep is not analogous, because the same biological system is maintained continuously and the subject is merely unconscious, whereas in the upload the hardware that generates the subject is removed incrementally. She then offered the case she thought was decisive: if someone killed you in your sleep and created a perfect duplicate, the duplicate would insist it was you, but you would be dead. The gradual procedure, she said, is just that death-and-duplication scenario smeared across a year. Arman rejected the analogy because the kidnapper case contains a break in the causal process, while gradual replacement preserves a single overlapping causal chain. If the material matters even when that chain is preserved, he said, Lena owes us a reason rather than a prejudice in favor of carbon. Lena replied that everything he called continuity was really continuity of behavior and report; nothing in the procedure ruled out the possibility that the original stream of experience went dark somewhere in the middle and that the rest is a system that acts as though the lights are on. The burden, she concluded, falls on the person claiming the procedure is safe, not on her to prove that it is not. Arman's last move was that she was treating an undetectable difference as a real one: if nothing in the patient's behavior, memories, or testimony changes, he asked, what fact could be left over for "you did not survive" to be about?

I stopped them there, because I felt they were talking past each other but could not identify the exact fault lines. I want an adjudication of the exchange as it actually happened, not a general essay on personal identity. Who is right about which specific claims—the level-of-replacement point, the sorites argument, the sleep analogy, the death-and-duplicate analogy, the burden-of-proof point, and the charge that the dispute has become merely verbal—and what are both of them missing?
