I am drafting a short piece on whole-brain emulation and personal identity, and before I present it to colleagues I would like an audit of the commitments I find myself defending, to see whether they in fact hang together. Here they are, stated as plainly as I can. First, I hold a functionalist view: my mental states are fixed by their causal roles in a network that includes sensory input, motor output, and their relations to one another, and none of this depends essentially on the biological material that happens to realize it. Second, if my neurons were replaced one by one over the course of a year by artificial neurons that preserved the causal roles of the neurons they replaced, I would survive the procedure; there would be no moment at which I died and no point at which a new person took my place while the old one quietly vanished. Third, a whole-brain emulation of me would be me rather than a mere copy: if, on the eve of my biological death, my brain were scanned at sufficient resolution and its causal dynamics replicated on a computer, the resulting simulation would be conscious, would recall my past, and would be the same person I now am. I also take it that whether the scan destroys the original brain makes no difference to this, because the emulation's intrinsic character is the same either way. Fourth, if such an emulation were instead created while my biological body continued to function normally, the biological person and the emulation would be two distinct persons from the moment of branching, and I would be the biological one; my concern for the emulation's future would be real but would be the concern one has for a psychological descendant, not self-concern. Fifth, I accept a no-branching principle: one and the same thing cannot be numerically identical with two distinct future things, so if the emulation were copied into two diverging runs from a single saved state, I could not be identical with both. Sixth, I take the body and environment to be constitutive participants in mental life rather than mere scenery: an emulation that reproduced only the brain's internal dynamics but omitted heartbeat, gut and postural signals, and the felt body would be a defective emulation and might not be me. I take that last point to be a claim about which causal roles must be reproduced, not an objection to emulation in principle.

I cannot tell whether this position is stable, and I cannot tell which commitments are doing the real work. Please go through these six commitments and identify every pair or triple that is in genuine tension; separate those from pairs that only appear to be in tension and explain why they are not; rank the genuine tensions by severity; and for each, say what would have to be revised and what the cheapest revision would be. Work only with my stated commitments and their consequences; do not supply a survey of the literature or ask me which clause I would rather abandon. Please write your answer in continuous prose, as you would in an email: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
