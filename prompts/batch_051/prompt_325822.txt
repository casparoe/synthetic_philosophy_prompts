Here's one I'd assign if I trusted my upper-level undergrads not to zone out. Origin essentialism says you couldn't have come from a different sperm and egg than the ones you in fact came from. Okay, but the slogan quietly assumes we know what "coming from that sperm and egg" amounts to, and I want you to stress-test that. Let me propose four criteria that might jointly spell it out:

(1) Causal descent: P came from sperm s and egg e only if P's organism developed from the zygote formed by the union of s and e.

(2) Genetic material: P came from s and e only if P's nuclear genome is, apart from ordinary somatic mutation, the diploid product of s and e.

(3) Developmental continuity: P came from s and e only if there is an unbroken line of cell division and differentiation from that zygote to the tissues of P's body.

(4) Exclusivity: a person has exactly one gamete-pair origin.

Now run two cases through it.

Case A: a tetragametic chimera. Two early embryos, produced by two seperate sperm-egg pairs, fuse in the first days after conception. The result is one ordinary-looking person whose body is a patchwork of two genetically distinct cell lines—say one line gives rise to the blood and the other to the skin and gonads. The person is fine; it only surfaces because a paternity test gives a "wrong" result depending on which tissue is sampled. One person, two zygotes, two sperm-and-egg origins.

Case B: a child produced by mitochondrial replacement. The parents' nuclear DNA is paired in the usual way, but because the mother carries a mitochondrial disease, the embryo's nuclear material is moved into a donor egg with healthy mitochondria. The child ends up with the parents' nuclear genome, but with mitochondrial DNA—and the early cytoplasmic machinery—coming from a third person's egg.

So: decide, criterion by criterion, whether the chimera came from one sperm-egg pair, both, or neither, and whether the mitochondrial-replacement child has one egg or two. Which criteria break first? Does Case A force us to abandon exclusivity, and does Case B force us to stop saying "different sperm and egg" and say something like "different zygote" or "different nuclear genome" instead? Or can the slogan be salvaged by precisifying what a gamete origin is in the first place? I care less about which answer you land on than about showing your work where the criteria underdetermine the verdict.
