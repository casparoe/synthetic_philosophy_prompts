My old philosophy teacher is retiring and I've somehow ended up giving the toast. The dinner is a weird mix: a big chunk of the department will catch any Whitehead reference, but the rest of the room is her kids, her neighbors, and a bunch of former students who don't remember the terminology. She used to tell us that she deliberately inverted Whitehead's rhythm of education—that the precision had to come first, and the romance only arrived after you'd earned it by doing the unglamorous work. I'd like the toast itself to run through Whitehead's three stages in order—romance, precision, generalization—without ever naming them. So it should open on the confused, vivid excitement of sitting in her class for the first time, move into the grueling middle where she made us diagram arguments and rewrite paragraphs until they were actually clear, and then close on the freedom of being able to use what she taught without thinking about it. The trick is that her family should just hear a warm retirement story, but any colleague should be able to tell exactly where the stage changes happen and why the toast turns where it does. Please write it, around five minutes when spoken.
