I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, trying to figure out how much of the classic Bayesian picture still applies to the sorts of agents people actually build now. The thing I keep going in circles on is the common prior assumption, and in particular Aumann's agreement theorem. As I understand it, Aumann showed that two rational Bayesians with a common prior who have common knowledge of each other's posteriors can't agree to disagree — their posteriors have to be identical. And the usual defense of the common prior, going back to Harsanyi, is that any difference between rational agents has to be traceable to different information, because rationality itself fixes the prior you'd have before any information arrives. So on that view there's a fact of the matter about what the fully rational prior is, and divergences from it are just non-rational baggage.

Here's what's bugging me. The agents we actually build now — LLMs and trained RL systems — dont have priors that anyone chose or justified. Their priors are induced by the architecture, the initialization, and a finite messy dataset. Two systems can both be pretty rational in the sense of being near-optimal predictors or planners within their own inductive biases, and still end up with different priors because they were trained on different corpora, different objectives, or just different random seeds. There's no common prior to point to, and arguably no fact about what a fully rational agent would have in advance. So the premise that rationality pins down a unique prior looks descriptively false for the agents that actually exist.

I'm not interested in the boring point that Aumann's theorem is conditional and just doesn't apply once common prior fails; I already get that. I want to know whether the argument for the common prior assumption survives as a normative claim, or even as a useful modeling claim, now that the paradigm agents are trained systems rather than ideal Bayesians with hand-specified beliefs. Which premise is actually under pressure here — the idea that rationality implies a common prior, or the common knowledge setup, or something else? And is there a repaired version worth keeping, like: two trained systems can still be forced to converge on a question if they can communicate and update over enough rounds, or their disagreement is bounded by the overlap in their training distributions? Or does the whole common prior idea just collapse into an empirical claim about architectures and datasets, with nothing distinctively a priori left over?
