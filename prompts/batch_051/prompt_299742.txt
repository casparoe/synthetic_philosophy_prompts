Whenever the problem of divine foreknowledge and free will comes up in threads or in the literature, the picture I keep encountering for Boethius's solution is an analogy from space: an observer on a ridge sees a whole road at once — every bend, every fork, every bridge, and the walker's position at each point along it — while the walker below meets the road one stretch at a time and must choose at each fork as it arrives. The work this analogy is made to do is the following: the observer's seeing the walker take the left fork at the bridge no more compels that choice than watching someone choose tea from across a room compels the choice. Seeing is not forcing, so foreknowledge is not preordination. On this reading, God does not know the future in advance; God enjoys an eternal present in which all times are before the divine mind at once, and that is what makes freedom compatible with divine knowledge.

Audit the analogy. Lay out the correspondences — road, bends and forks, walker, observer, ridge, the act of seeing — and then identify where the mapping breaks. Separate the disanalogies that are decisive for the argumentative use of the analogy from the ones that are merely cosmetic. In particular, I want to know which features of the source the analogy quietly imports into the target: a finished road is already there, and an observer on a ridge is still a temporal being looking from a single moment at something that pre-exists the looking, so is this really a picture of Boethian eternity or is it a disguised picture of a block universe with a pre-existing future? Finally, say whether the compatibility conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if the analogy cannot carry the conclusion, what a better analogy would have to look like.
