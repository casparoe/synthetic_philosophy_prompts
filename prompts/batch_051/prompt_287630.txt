I've been trying to pin down why some definitions in mathematics feel as though they capture the right notion while others, extensionally equivalent to the first, feel like mere repackagings. My working criteria for a definition's being the right generalization are these: (1) Reach: it should include the paradigm examples and preserve the theorems that motivated the generalization, without silently leaving out cases the generalization was meant to cover. (2) Coordinate-freedom: it should be stated using only the structure of the generalized setting, with no detour through a preferred basis, metric, or atlas. (3) Closure: the class it defines should hold together under the constructions that matter, so that products, subspaces, and quotients of good objects remain good. (4) Honesty: each axiom should do genuine work, in the sense that weakening or deleting it yields a recognizable, well-populated class with its own theory, rather than an arbitrary restriction.

Now consider a borderline case I cannot settle: the modern definition of a topological space as a set together with a family of open sets closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections. It plainly generalizes metric spaces, supports continuous maps, compactness, and convergence, and it is coordinate-free and closed under the usual constructions. Yet the very same class of objects can be defined through neighborhood systems, closure operators, or convergence structures, and one might suspect the open-set axioms are merely a convenient packaging of the real notion. The finite-subcover form of compactness appears to do its work without drawing on the full open-set apparatus, and the axioms are permissive enough to admit the indiscrete topology on an arbitrary set, or spaces that fail even the weakest separation property, which look nothing like the metric setting the definition was meant to generalize. My residual worry is that my four criteria mostly test whether a definition picks out the right class of objects, whereas the open-set case exposes a different demand: that a definition should pick the class out in the right way, making visible the structure that actually does the work. Go through the four criteria one by one and argue whether the open-set definition of a topological space qualifies as the right generalization of distance, or only as a convenient equivalent of it. Then tell me whether this case shows that the criteria themselves need repair — for example, by adding a fifth criterion about how a definition presents its objects — or whether that demand should be rejected.
