Bradley argued that no relation can unify a fact: if we explain a's standing in R to b by saying that a and b are joined by R, we must then explain how R itself is joined to a and to b, and the demand repeats. Much later, logicians proposed treating relations extensionally, as sets of ordered pairs, so that a's standing in R to b is simply the ordered pair (a,b) belonging to R. Treat this extensional account as a serious answer to the unity problem for a relational fact such as a's being taller than b, not merely as formal notation. Does Bradley's regress survive this development, or was the regress an artifact of treating relations as further entities that need tying to their relata? If it survives, say which premise the extensional account does not touch and restate the regress in extensional form; if it does not survive, say what a defender of the regress could still mean by the unity of a fact that set-membership has not supplied. In either case, give the strongest objection to the verdict you defend. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
