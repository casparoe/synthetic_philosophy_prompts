I have been trying to fix a working account of what it takes for something to serve as an arrow of time, and I have settled on three conditions. A process counts as an arrow of time only if (1) it is systematically lopsided in time: its time-reversed counterpart, though compatible with the underlying laws, is practically never realized; (2) it points in the same direction over long stretches rather than fluctuating or reversing; and (3) it is coupled to the buildup of records, so that later states contain traces from which earlier states can be partly reconstructed. Now consider a universe whose total entropy is lowest at the middle of its history and increases toward both temporal ends. An observer on either side of the middle would see entropy rising, records accumulating, and a local past-future distinction, but the two sides pick out opposite future directions, and the history as a whole is unchanged when the order of events is reversed. Does this universe contain an arrow of time, does it contain two opposite arrows, or none? Go through my three conditions one by one, note exactly where they fail to force a verdict, and then say whether the case shows that my conditions need revision or that the question itself was badly posed.
