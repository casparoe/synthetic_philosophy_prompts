I am a teaching assistant for an upper-level course on classical Chinese philosophy, and I am currently grading a midterm essay on early Daoist accounts of action. The question I set was as follows. In this unit we have distinguished two broad interpretations of wu-wei: on the first, it is non-action in the sense of withholding coercive, calculating, goal-directed intervention in the course of things; on the second, it is not the absence of action but a quality of action, an effortlessness, responsiveness, and absence of strain that can characterize even highly skilled activity such as carving, swimming, or governing. Write an essay explaining why, on either interpretation, the ideal of wu-wei appears self-undermining: if a person sets out to acquire a state whose defining feature is the absence of deliberate effort, has the effort not simply reappeared at the level of the project itself? Assess whether this paradox can be dissolved by treating wu-wei as a criterion of right action, distinct from a description of a manner of acting, and discuss whether the early Daoist texts give us reason to think the problem is a genuinely practical one or a merely verbal one. Your answer should explicitly address the objection that someone who decides to stop trying has only relocated the trying one level up.

One student wrote the following. Wu-wei is standardly rendered as non-action, and in my view the texts mean this more literally than most modern readers admit. The sage does not initiate projects, deliberate over options, or impose a plan on the world; what looks like his activity is really only the situation acting through him, a response that arises without prior intention. This is exactly what generates the paradox: the very description of wu-wei as something to be attained turns it into an end one sets for oneself, and setting ends is the kind of calculative effort wu-wei is supposed to exclude. One familiar answer is that effortlessness can be produced indirectly, through training and repetition: once a form of action has been internalized, it issues from habit rather than deliberation, so the strain of trying drops out on its own. But this answer fails, because if wu-wei cannot be pursued directly, then any conscious project of cultivating it is self-defeating for the same reason, and the only consistent advice is to stop trying altogether. The distinction proposed in the question does not help either. An action is right, on this view, when it is effortless, and a manner of acting counts as wu-wei when it is unforced; since both sides refer to the absence of effort, the distinction between a criterion and a description collapses. What remains is a genuine practical paradox rather than a merely verbal one: the texts recommend a state that cannot be intentionally reached.

Please grade this answer. I need a letter or percentage grade and, more importantly, a point-by-point assessment: say precisely what the answer gets right, identify any subtle errors, conflations, or inferences that do not follow, and explain how a more careful use of the distinctions from the unit would change the argument. Do not merely summarize the answer; give the comments a grader would write to justify the mark.
