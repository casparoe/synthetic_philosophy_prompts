I keep noticing that the fundamental problem of causal inference is, on paper, nearly identical to the basic problem of counterfactual history. In causal inference you want the effect of a treatment on a unit: Y_i(1) minus Y_i(0). But for any given unit you only ever observe one of those two potential outcomes, and the other is unobservable by definition. Everything downstream of that—randomization, exchangeability, matching—is an attempt to build an observable stand-in for the unobservable comparison. Now look at counterfactual history. When a historian asks what would have happened if Napoleon hadn't invaded Russia, they're asking for the untreated potential outcome of an actual historical episode, and nobody gets to observe the untreated timeline. And what disciplined counterfactual historians actually do looks a lot like informal matching: find cases that were similar before the event and let them act as controls.

So my instinct is that counterfactual history just is the potential-outcomes framework with messier units, messier treatments, and more interference. But maybe that's exactly where the analogy collapses. SUTVA (roughly: no interference between units, and no hidden variations of the treatment) is false for history; the intervention isn't sharply scoped; and there's no stable superpopulation from which countries and centuries are drawn. I can read that two ways. Either the parallel is superficial because respecting those assumptions is the whole point of causal inference while history violates all of them, or the parallel is deep and it just tells you which historical what-ifs are well-posed and which aren't.
