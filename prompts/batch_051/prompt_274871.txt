I have come to accept a strong view of corporate moral blame: a corporation can itself deserve backward-looking blame—not merely be a useful target of forward-looking criticism or regulation—for what it does, in a way not reducible to blaming its members, and this can be warranted even when no individual employee is blameworthy for the same wrong. What would count against this? I want a map of the view's exposure, not the stock reply that corporations lack minds. Which facts about how organizations actually decide, which constraints on a defensible account of blame, and which legal or social consequences would most seriously undermine entity-level blame? Rank candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward—for example, that corporate intention is always derivative from individual intention, that blame as a reactive attitude requires a unified subject capable of feeling itself blamed, and that entity-level blame does no explanatory work beyond blaming individuals and structures. For each, indicate whether a committed defender should concede it as a genuine vulnerability or may dismiss it. Then give an honest assessment: has the position been qualified enough to survive everything, and is that degree of insulation itself a defect in a view of this kind?
