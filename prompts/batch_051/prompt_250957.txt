Two people in my philosophy of physics reading group have been fighting all semester about whether spacetime is a thing in its own right or just the web of spatiotemporal relations among matter and fields. One is a substantivalist of a fairly modern stripe: she thinks spacetime points exist independently of whatever occupies them, and that this independent existence is what makes absolute rotation and acceleration physically meaningful even if every material clock and rod in the universe happened to fall silent. The other is a relationist: he thinks defining motion relative to space itself explains nothing, and that all the physical content lives in relations among bodies and fields, so any leftover empty spacetime structure is a kind of reification. They've agreed to write an adversarial-collaboration statement instead of continuing to talk past each other. Draft the document they'd produce: a list of claims both can sign about Newton's bucket, the rotating globes, and what the hole argument actually rules out; then the exact disagreements in wording each side would accept for their own view; then the cruxes (I suspect one is whether a one-particle universe could count as rotating, another is whether, once everybody agrees the hole argument kills primitive point-identity, there is still anything left for spacetime itself to be, and a third is whether a field counts as matter for the relationist's purposes or is just a way of sneaking absolute structure back in). For each crux, give a concrete case or result both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If part of the fight turns out to be verbal, say which part and why. No citations needed; the point is the positions, not the scholarly record.
