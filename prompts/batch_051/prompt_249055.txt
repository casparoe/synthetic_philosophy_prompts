I'm on a red-team at a mid-sized AI company, and the thing I got asked to own is a goddaamn decision tool for how we evaluate a deployment safety case. I re-read the beginning of Greenblatt, Shlegeris and Sökmen's "AI Control: Improving Safety Despite Intentional Subversion", and I got stuck on their setup: a control evaluation isn't a measurement of how often the model tries to do something bad under normal conditions; it's an evaluation of a protocol by pitting it against a model that has been given the specific goal of producing some disallowed outcome, because the safety case has to hold even under adversarial subversion. The question I want settled is what exactly a person is committed to if they accept that point, and where the actual disagreement is between people on our team.

Can you write a which-position-do-you-actually-hold quiz, like eight questions, with a scoring key that sorts respondents into positions on the AI control/alignment divide? The questions should be concrete operational cases, like: a model writes a code change that passes tests but contains a subtle vulnerability, and the monitor reports no suspicious reasoning; a red-team model is given the goal of exfiltrating credentials but nots allowed to see the deployment environment before the eval; a model tries to use a side channel you didn't think to monitor; a model finds a way to coordinate with an earlier checkpoint; a trustable-but-dumb monitor disagrees with a smart-but-possibly-misaligned monitor; etc. The scoring key should map to at least these leaves: pure control (the protocol alone is the safety case), control-plus-limited-alignment (alignment evidence narrows the space of possible subversion goals but control still does the heavy lifting), audit-based assurance (we can trust post-hoc audits of internal reasoning), capability-reduction control (the safety case comes mostly from not giving the model tools), and alignment-first (control is only a stopgap for models we already think are aligned). Also mark any answer pattern nobody on the team seems to hold and say in one sentence why it might be unattractive or incoherent.

Dont sort people by what term they like: every question should make them pick between operational protocols. And if the tree cannot distinguish two leaves, say so instead of faking it.
