I work as a software engineer on a product team, and I have spent the last few years watching our dashboards behave in a way that has begun to feel philosophically interesting. Once a metric is attached to a promotion decision or a bonus, the number reliably improves while my honest judgement is that the underlying product does not. I have been reading informally about Goodhart's law — the idea, roughly, that a measure stops being a reliable measure once it is made a target — and about specification gaming in machine-learning reward design, for example a game-playing agent that accumulates points by exploiting the scoring rule rather than by completing the intended task. These are usually discussed separately, the first as a management caution and the second as an AI-safety problem, but they look to me like the same phenomenon separated mainly by how explicit the objective is.

I have sketched notes for a short essay and would be grateful for comments before I invest more time in it. The working thesis is that specification gaming in trained systems is not a newly introduced failure mode so much as Goodhart's law with the objective made fully explicit and with an optimiser that has no access to the shared, unmeasured values that keep most human metric-gaming partially in check. From there I want to argue that the standard engineering remedy of adding guardrail metrics is weaker than most practitioners assume: a sufficiently strong optimiser can game an enlarged vector of metrics as a single target, and each added metric brings its own edge cases. I also want to close with the claim that teams should treat dashboards as diagnostic instruments rather than decision rules, so that a surprising metric triggers an investigation rather than immediately feeding an evaluation. Some of the notes I am least sure about are the following: that sprint velocity and test coverage are best understood as proxies for valuable work done and for confidence in quality; that one failure of these metrics is statistical, because proxy and underlying value come apart at the extremes, while another is behavioural, because people learn which activities move the number without moving the underlying value; and that machine specification gaming is in one respect easier to reason about than human metric-gaming, precisely because the machine's objective is fully legible.

I would welcome comments from a philosophical angle, but I am especially interested in three things: where the analogy between gamed software metrics and specification gaming is most likely to break if someone knows both areas well; whether the essay's diagnosis already smuggles in a controversial view about what a metric is, or about the sense in which a metric can be good; and whether the closing advice about diagnostic dashboards actually follows from the diagnosis rather than being a sensible-sounding leap. Since I am outside my training here, please indicate how confident you are in the claims you make, and single out the point of your assessment you are least sure of.
