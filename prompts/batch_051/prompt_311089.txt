I lead the model-assessment group at an AI lab, and I need a decision procedure for reviewing what my team and I have been calling "interpretability sign-offs." These are internal documents in which a team claims to have used an AI system — a purpose-built interpretability tool, a probe network, or a general-purpose model prompted to explain — to show that a target model has some specified property: it is not using a forbidden feature, its safety behavior generalizes for the right reason, it actually followed the stated chain of thought. My group's job is to decide whether the claim is strong enough to be entered into a deployment or compliance file. We review about a dozen a month. I can give each sign-off at most half a day of analyst time before it must be marked yes, no, or "go back with conditions." A false yes is expensive in exactly the way a false safety certificate is expensive: we would be asserting, to ourselves and eventually to a regulator, that we understand something we do not.

I have spent years absorbing the standard rationalist-adjacent points on this topic: a plausible explanation is not a faithful mechanism; saliency is not causal evidence; a more capable model can confabulate a fluent account of a weaker model's behavior; an interpreter cannot report a distinction it cannot itself represent; and using one opaque system to certify another looks like the recursive-interpretability problem, an infinite regress. But scattered principles are not a procedure, and at present each review is genuinely ad hoc — the de facto test is whether the explanation reads well and whether the team ran a familiar benchmark.

Please write me a reusable checklist / decision procedure my analysts can run on any interpretability sign-off, grounded in what is actually known about the limits of AI-as-interpreter. It should translate the theory rather than preserve its vocabulary: my analysts are competent engineers, not interpretability specialists, so the procedure must be operational, not a reading list. Specifically, it should specify: (a) the questions to ask, in order; (b) what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; (c) where the procedure stops with a no; (d) where it hands the decision to a human because the remaining question is one of risk tolerance or values rather than fact; and (e) where the procedure is likely to misfire. Also be honest about what the procedure cannot do — in particular, what it cannot extract from a fluent story, and what it cannot decide on its own.

Then run it on three kinds of sign-off we actually see, and for each say what the procedure would conclude and where you expect the requesting team to be unhappy with the answer:

1. A saliency/attention-based explanation, produced by a general-purpose multimodal model, claiming that a toxicity classifier keys on derogatory terms rather than protected-category identifiers. No ablations or interventions were run.
2. A chain-of-thought-style explanation produced by the target model itself, offered as evidence that a reasoning model did not use a forbidden shortcut. The requesting team wants to accept it partly because the model is the same one that produced the behavior.
3. A circuit-level explanation in a small transformer, validated by activation patching and ablations, which the team wants to treat as evidence about a production-scale model with different training data.

Be explicit about the one step I suspect the whole thing turns on: where the regress of interpreters gets cut. What, in practice, counts as ground truth in this loop? And flag which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.
