I chair the clinician credentials and discipline committee of a three-hospital system. Each month we review clinician-level safety indicators—risk-adjusted mortality and complication rates, controlled-substance discrepancy reports, near-miss counts by provider—and decide whether to take no action, require monitoring or education, impose conditions on practice such as proctoring or restrictions, or recommend suspension or revocation. Our bylaws require only reasonable concern for monitoring, preponderance of the evidence for conditions, and clear and convincing evidence for suspension or revocation. We have one 45-minute meeting per case, we are a mix of clinicians and lay trustees without legal training, and our decisions get appealed, so we need a procedure that can be written down and justified.

Our recurring disagreement is whether aggregate statistics about a clinician can carry these burdens of proof when there is no direct evidence that any particular patient was harmed by substandard care. Legal theorists call this problem naked statistical evidence. Consider two classic scenarios (the blue bus and the gatecrasher). A bus company runs 80% of the buses on a route; a pedestrian is hit by an unidentified bus on the route and sues the company on the statistic alone. Or: a spectator is selected at random from a rodeo crowd in which only 70% of attendees paid admission, and the organizer sues that spectator for gatecrashing on the base rate alone. Courts have been reluctant to treat such bare base rates as proof of what this company or this spectator did, even when the statistic clears the relevant probability threshold, and the reluctance grows as the standard of proof rises.

Please turn that idea into a decision procedure our committee can actually run. Give us the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with no action, where it sends a case for further investigation instead of decision, where it must hand the decision to a human because the question is one of values, and where it flags a case as one the procedure cannot settle. Make the procedure distinguish clearly among the three burdens of proof; do not erase the in-the-weeds distinctions—aggregate rate versus case-specific evidence, the choice of reference class used to compute the rate, likelihood versus posterior probability, and whether a statistic can justify precautionary conditions even when it cannot justify a punitive finding.

Then run the procedure on these three cases, stating for each which action the procedure mandates and at which question it is decided:

1. A general surgeon has a risk-adjusted 30-day mortality of 3.6% over 140 elective colectomies, against a peer benchmark of 1.9%. There are no incident reports or complaints, and an independent review of ten deaths found no deviation from the standard of care. A local newspaper has run a story on the numbers, and several trustees want to recommend suspension.
2. An obstetrician has a risk-adjusted rate of severe perineal lacerations of 7.1% over 250 deliveries, against a benchmark of 2.8%. Three patients have filed credible complaints describing failures to perform an indicated maneuver in their own deliveries; the rest of the excess rate arises from cases with no individual allegation.
3. A nurse anesthetist is associated with 12 of 14 wrong-count near-miss events in an operating suite over one year, though they worked only 22% of the shifts. Each event is documented as a team event; none alleges a specific act by this clinician.

At least one of the three should force the procedure to return an answer the committee will dislike; identify which one it is and state the disliked answer without softening it. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot do. End with a one-sentence summary of the procedure as the last line, beginning exactly "Summary:".
