I am a retired physical scientist, and after a career spent using probability as an instrument, I have lately been trying to understand what probability itself rests on. Bertrand's chord problem has become my test case. A circle contains an inscribed equilateral triangle. A chord is drawn at random; what is the probability that the chord is longer than a side of the triangle? If the two endpoints of the chord are chosen uniformly on the circumference, the answer is 1/3. If one chooses a radius and a point uniformly along it, then draws the chord perpendicular to the radius there, the answer is 1/2. If one chooses the chord's midpoint uniformly in the interior of the circle and draws the chord through it, the answer is 1/4. Each procedure looks like an application of the principle of indifference, but to a different parameterization, and the principle does not say which parameterization is privileged. A familiar proposal is that the ambiguity disappears if one insists that the distribution assigned to the configuration be unchanged when the whole setup—circle, triangle, and chord—is rotated, translated, or rescaled, and that this constraint singles out the second answer, 1/2. Others take the original question to be ill-posed until a generating process or measurement procedure has been specified, so that 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 are answers to three different problems rather than three competing answers to one problem.

I would like you to give me your credence in the following proposition: Bertrand's chord problem has a uniquely correct answer determined by symmetry and invariance considerations alone, prior to any physical chance setup, and that unique answer is 1/2. Please decompose this credence into at least three components. (a) The probability that some invariance, symmetry, or maximum-indifference principle can be formulated precisely enough to pick out a unique probability distribution over chords in the first place. (b) The probability that, conditional on such a principle being successfully formulated, the distribution it picks out gives the answer 1/2 rather than 1/3 or 1/4. (c) The probability that, conditional on such a principle picking out that distribution, the selection corresponds to a fact about the unqualified notion of a random chord rather than to a convention about parameterization. For each component, give a number and one sentence of justification; then state how you combine them and what your overall credence is. Finally, tell me which component the overall number is most sensitive to and what evidence or argument would move that component the most. At the very end, give your overall credence as a percentage in the form \answer{...}.
