I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language, and in our weekly seminar on Grice we have been circling a distinction that everyone invokes but nobody can draw cleanly: the contrast between what a speaker has said and what she has merely implicated. The shared examples go fine—a driver says she is nearly out of petrol, someone points out a service station around the corner, and we all agree the speaker implicated that it is open and sells petrol but did not say it. The trouble starts as soon as the cases stop being that cooperative. I'd like a working definition of "what is said" that draws the line in a principled way, even if it ends up being narrower or wider than the standard Gricean picture, and even if it ends up splitting the notion in two.

Here are the cases I want the definition run against, deliberately mixing clear inclusions, clear exclusions, and borderline cases:
(a) In a parking lot, A says to B, "Your headlights are on." (A clear case of saying, presumably.)
(b) B says, "I'm nearly out of petrol," and A replies, "There's a service station around the next bend." (A implicated that the station is open and has fuel, but did not say it.)
(c) Caught in a downpour, Alice says, "Lovely weather we're having." Did she say that the weather is lovely, or only make as if to say it and implicate the contrary?
(d) A says, "Some of the students passed," while knowing that all of them passed. Did A say that not all of the students passed?
(e) A says, "Some, in fact all, of the students passed." Does the second clause show that the not-all reading in (d) was never part of what was said, or does it merely cancel something that would otherwise have been said?
(f) Asked for her honest opinion of a colleague, A writes only, "Her handwriting is legible and she has not been convicted of any felonies." Did A say the colleague is a poor scholar?
(g) At dinner, A asks, "Can you reach the salt?" and B passes it. Did A say that B can reach the salt, or did she merely ask for it?
(h) A says, "Even Marcus passed the exam." Did A say that Marcus was among the least likely to pass, or is that something else—conventional implicature, presupposition, or whatever your definition settles on?

My own suspicion is that "what is said" is doing two jobs at once: one job picks out the truth-conditional content for which the speaker is most directly answerable, and another job tracks how an ordinary hearer would report the speech and where they would attach the phrase "she said." If you think that is right, please give me the two notions and say which cases change their classification as we move between them; if you can make one definition do all the work, all the better. Where the definition gives what looks like the wrong verdict, tell me whether to revise the definition or accept the surprising verdict.
