I run a small late-night comedy monologue, the kind of ten-minute topical desk thing, and I'm the one who has to green-light jokes before they go on air. We punch up roughly 40 bits a week, so I can't do philosophy on every joke; I need something the writers can run in about ten minutes before taping. I keep hearing people gesture at the big four theories of humour — superiority (we laugh because we feel above the target), incongruity (expectation gets violated in a harmless way), relief (laughter as a release of nervous energy), and benign violation (something wrong that also feels safe).

I want you to turn these into a reusable vetting checklist for a joke or bit. The writers are comedy people, not theorists, so it has to be in plain language: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to move on, which question kills the joke outright, and where the procedure should stop and hand the decision to me or the showrunner because its a judgment call about taste rather than structure. Be honest about what the checklist can't catch — rhythm, delivery, topical timing, whatever. Then actually run it on three bits: (1) a self-deprecating bit where the host says his sleep schedule is so bad he's basically a 34-year-old infant with a credit card; (2) a dark joke about the victims of last month's ferry sinking that one writer insists is 'just gallows humour'; (3) our long-running bit where the host mocks the quiet intern's outfit every Friday. The third one is our highest-testing recurring segment and I will be annoyed if the procedure kills it, so show your work and don't soften the result to spare me. What does the procedure say, where exactly does each bit pass or fail, and what's the point where a human has to override the checklist?
