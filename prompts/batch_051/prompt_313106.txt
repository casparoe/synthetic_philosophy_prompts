You are an expert on intergenerational justice and the economic theory of climate policy. Start by laying out the structure of the question, then commit to numbers; do not retreat to "it depends."

Let P be the following proposition:

There exists a feasible policy package — deployable by mid-century in a world with institutions roughly like today's (ordinary states, markets, debt markets; no benevolent global planner) — that is a safe Pareto improvement relative to a baseline in which no new climate policy beyond what is already in place is adopted. "Safe" means the package keeps peak global warming below 2°C above pre-industrial with at least two-thirds probability. "Pareto improvement" means that, with each generation idealized as a representative agent, no generation from now through 2200 is worse off than it would have been under that baseline, and at least one is strictly better off.

Three things I want you to treat as live problems rather than assume away. First, compensation can run backward in time only through real channels — bequests, public debt, innovation rents, and the capitalization of future climate benefits into present asset prices — so whether future generations can "pay" the present for mitigation depends on how much value those channels actually transmit backward. Second, the welfare comparison presumes that losses of non-substitutable goods (species, glaciers, habitable places, cultural landscapes) can be offset by extra consumption in richer future generations; that may be false. Third, the representative-agent idealization papers over both within-generation inequality and the non-identity problem: if the policy changes which people exist in 2100, it may be conceptually unclear whether "the same generation" is being compared.

What is your actual credence in P? Then decompose it. Identify the central sub-claims your credence rests on — including at minimum whether the representative-agent comparison is well-defined, the magnitude and tail risk of climate damages, whether future generations will be richer absent mitigation, the capacity of future-to-present transfer mechanisms, and the substitutability assumption — assign a rough probability to each, and show how they combine. Tell me which sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move it most.
