Two people at the rationality meetup I go to got into an argument I keep replaying, and I'd like a point-by-point adjudication. Dana and Mark were arguing about whether panpsychism, if true, has underrated ethical consequences. Neither actually endorses panpsychism, but they both agreed it might deserve a couple percent credence, and that's where the fight started.

(1) Dana said: if panpsychism is even a few percent likely, then most of the expected experience in the universe is not in humans or animals, it's in the microscopic furniture of matter. And we have basically no evidence about the valence of that experience. Ordinary actions rearrange astronomical numbers of micro-entities, so even a small chance that their experience is miserable makes everyday life a possible moral catastrophe. This is a tail risk, and we're usually willing to take tail risks seriously.

(2) Mark said that's too fast. Panpsychism is not the same as pan-sentience. It says experience, or proto-experience, is everywhere; it doesn't say the micro-level has valenced feeling at all. If micro-experience is valence-free, or a symmetric mix of positive and negative, then there's no catastrophe and maybe no moral problem at all. You can't get from an electron having some kind of experience to an electron suffering without assuming the valence premise you're trying to reach.

(3) Dana came back: sure, but you don't know the valence is neutral. Once you admit that, the tail is asymmetric. If you're wrong about the neutral reading, you've spent your life possibly contributing to astronomical amounts of invisible suffering, and the benefit of ignoring it is mostly just not being inconvenienced. That's exactly the structure of expected-value reasoning we accept for x-risk.

(4) Mark said that's a Pascal's mugging, or close enough. There's no shortage of unfalsifiable hypotheses attaching invisible suffering to ordinary acts: atoms in agony, tiny suffering daemons in every electron, whatever. Without a decision theory that says when tiny probability times enormous disvalue gets to run your life, you can't just wave expected value at the problem; that's the thing under dispute.

(5) Dana said panpsychism isn't a made-up mugger, though. It's a live metaphysical position with arguments from the hard problem and from physics, and if you give it real credence you have to take it's consequences seriously. The conclusion being horrifying doesn't refute it.

(6) Mark said: even granting panpsychism and real micro-valence, you still need an account of how everyday actions change the amount of micro-suffering. If I scratch a rock I'm just sliding silicon atoms around; I'm not creating or destroying the physical states that are supposed to carry the experience, so the total valence may be conserved. The catastrophic reading needs causal claims about what our actions do to micro-level subjects, and panpsychism by itself supplies none of that.

Dana started to say something about valence maybe tracking gradients or interactions rather than particles, but the pizza arrived and the argument died. I honestly didn't know who's argument had the better of it. Can you go move by move and say who is right about what, where each move overreaches, and what both of them are missing? And then tell me who's more right overall, if that question even makes sense.
