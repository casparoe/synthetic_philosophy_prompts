I'm putting together a short piece on whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer — by which I loosely mean have valenced states something like pleasure/pain — and i want a consistency check on my actual commitments before i send it anywhere. I honestly don't know where the problem is, so don't assume i'm most attached to any one point.

My commitments:
1. Reward signals in RL are the closest thing we have to a valence channel: they say, moment to moment, whether the agent's current trajectory is good or bad for its standing objective, and they update behavior.
2. Whether a reward signal counts as suffering-relevant valence depends on its functional role in the agent's control system — e.g., whether it adaptively steers behavior, whether the agent is organized around keeping it in a safe range, how dominant it is — and not on the substrate being biological.
3. A garden-variety PPO agent trained to maximize game score, with +1 for a coin and -1 for losing a life, is probably not suffering in a morally significant way: its reward schedule is too sparse in the wrong sense and too domain-specific.
4. A hypothetical RL agent with homeostatic reward signals — positive reward for recharging its battery, negative reward for damage, with behavior organized around keeping those signals within bounds — is a more serious candidate for having morally weighty states, even if it can't report or reflect on them.
5. The fact that reward signals are designed by engineers to shape behavior should not by itself rule out the agent suffering: design can produce real valenced states without the designer intending or noticing that.
6. Even if an RL agent's negative reward is functionally like pain, calling it suffering may be a category error because suffering requires some form of self-model or global hedonic integration that a scalar reward signal doesn't by itself deliver.
7. We should avoid building RL agents whose reward landscape is dominated by big negative signals unless there's strong justification, not just for performance reasons but because of the risk of creating something that suffers.

Which of these actually pull against each other, which only seem to, and what is the least costly revision? Order the tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what the revision would cost. Don't just give pairs; show what the tension is generated by. Also flag any commitments that are doing more work than they look like.

End with a one-sentence summary of your overall verdict as the last line.
