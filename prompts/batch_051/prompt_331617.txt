In a seminar on Neo-Confucian moral psychology, we have been reading Wang Yangming against the backdrop of the view he opposed, which treats knowing and acting as two stages: first establish the knowledge, then act on it. Wang's signature claim is that knowing and acting form a single continuous work rather than two separable steps. Knowing is the beginning of the action, and the action is where the knowing reaches completion; a person can describe filial conduct at length and yet, on Wang's account, still not know it in the relevant sense until the conduct itself is being performed. His examples are meant to show that genuinely recognizing a moral demand is more like recoiling from a bad smell than like reading a prescription and deciding afterward whether to fill it: the response is not an extra step appended to the recognition but is internal to it.

A classmate pressed this into an argument which he thinks refutes the doctrine, and I have not found a way out of it. (1) If knowing and acting are one, then to know what a situation calls for is already to take up the doing of it; there is no completed cognition waiting for a separate act of will to put it into effect. (2) But ordinary moral life contains cases in which a person sincerely and accurately says what should be done, feels the demand of it, and still does not do it — hesitating, waiting for fuller certainty, or simply being overmastered by fear or habit. (3) Wang's reply must be that the person never genuinely knew. What they had was borrowed opinion, convention, or a recognition blocked by selfish desire, not the genuine article. (4) But once we accept that redescription, genuine knowledge seems to require the completed act as part of itself, since any gap between knowing and carrying out is treated as proof that the knowing was never there. (5) And then the unity thesis loses the power it was meant to have: there is no state of genuine knowledge that precedes and guides the first sincere effort, because the effort is already folded into the knowledge, so the knowledge cannot explain how the effort begins or gets corrected. (6) So either the thesis is false to the experience of moral struggle — in which case step (2) stands and akrasia is real — or the thesis is true only because knowing has been quietly redefined as successful performance, which makes it empty.

I am caught because I want to keep the insight behind step (3): there is a genuine difference between owning a moral truth and merely repeating it, and Wang seems to be pointing at exactly that. But I also cannot bring myself to deny step (2), and step (5) feels like an unacceptable cost. Which step should be rejected, and what would rejecting it cost Wang's account of moral knowledge?
