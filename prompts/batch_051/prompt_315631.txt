So here's what's bugging me. We routinely compare everyday risks in micromorts—one micromort being a one-in-a-million chance of dying—and end up with neat tables saying that driving, cycling, flying, and scuba diving all have their price in these tiny units. Yet almost nobody treats these numbers as uniformly decision-guiding: we drive constantly but get very exercised about much smaller micromort-scale hazards, and regulators do the same. I want to know what philosophy is built into the micromort way of framing risk. Does it presuppose that a death is a homogeneous, fungible bad, so that all micromorts are interchangeable? That risks aggregate by simple addition? That a person should act as an expected-mortality minimizer? And what should we make of our willingness to drive while shunning lower-micromort activities—is that incoherent, or does it reveal a different and maybe more defensible theory of practical reason? In short, what philosophical commitments does the practice of comparing everyday risks in micromorts assume, and where is it just underdetermined between rival theories?
