I was arguing with my cousin over the holidays about whether there's any way to get people on opposite sides of something like abortion or immigration to actually trade concessions instead of just digging in, and she came up with this idea for a concession exchange. The way it would work: you register with the platform and state one moral-political commitment you hold strongly, the kind of thing you wouldn't just set aside. Then you list two things — a concession you'd be willing to make (for example, a pro-choice person might commit to donating five hundred dollars to a group that funds prenatal care and adoption services, or signing a public letter endorsing a specific restriction) and the concession you'd need from the other side to make yours worth doing. The site matches you with someone across the aisle whose offers and demands line up with yours, and both concessions go into escrow: each side has to actually complete their pledge by a deadline, with receipts or signed statements uploaded for verification, before the other side's act is credited as complete. If one side reneges, the other side is released from their pledge and the reneger get's a public mark on the platform. The idea is that no one has to give anything up unilaterally — the losses are simultaneous and conditional.

Treat is as a mechanism, not as a moral position on whether people should compromise. How would it actually play out? Who would use it and who would refuse? What would it incentivize — would people register fake or exaggerated positions to extract easy concessions, or pledge things they were going to do anyway and collect a cheap win? How would it reshape the causes themselves and the norms around them in a polarized environment? Where does it fail badly, and can any of those failures be fixed without gutting the scheme, or is the whole thing self-defeating?
