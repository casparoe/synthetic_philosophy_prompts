im a novelist, not a philosopher, but I keep tripping over a version of a problem in my own work and I'm told the philosophers have it worked out under the name "causal exclusion." Here's my working vocabulary. When I revise a scene I ask why a character did something, and I have two registers for answering. The motivational register: she leaves him because she's humiliated, or because she wants, finally, to be free. And the bodily/situational register: she's running on two hours of sleep, her adrenaline is up, she grew up in a household where leaving was the only way to win an argument. In my craft both registers can be true at once, and I usually try to keep them braided together. But the more complete the bodily register gets, the more the motivational register starts to feel like commentary — as if the real causation is in the chemistry and the conditioning, and the motive is just the story we tell afterward.

Which parts of the argument map cleanly onto the novelist's problem, which ones you had to bend or fudge, and which ones have no expression at all in my vocabulary? And be straight with me: does the residue show that my framework is missing something real, or that the philosophers' version is carrying some dead weight — or a bit of both?
