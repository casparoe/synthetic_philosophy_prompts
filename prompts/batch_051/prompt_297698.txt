I am currently taking a course on the ethics and strategy of nuclear deterrence, and we have spent much of the term on the credibility paradox at the center of deterrence theory. The problem, as the course has framed it, is this: a retaliatory threat prevents an attack only if the attacker believes the threatened party would carry it out after the fact, but once the attack has occurred, the victim's forward-looking incentive to launch a massive retaliation is at least much weaker than it was before, and may vanish entirely. The threat must therefore be believed at precisely the point where carrying it out has become pointless or self-destructive. The usual solutions we have discussed—automating the response, or leaving it to mechanisms no one fully controls—all seem to trade credibility against loss of control.

I have been trying to design a mechanism that escapes this trade-off, and I would like an analysis of how it would actually behave rather than a verdict on whether it is desirable in principle. Suppose State A deploys a tamper-resistant, fully autonomous retaliation system on its survivable strategic forces. Its trigger is the confirmed detonation of any nuclear weapon on A's territory or against its deployed nuclear forces. Once triggered, the system cannot be overridden or reprogrammed within the time window in which retaliation is feasible. It then runs a response lottery with three public and fixed probabilities. Under every outcome it launches the same large number of weapons; the lottery decides only the targeting mix. With probability 0.45, it strikes the attacker's nuclear forces and military command-and-control exclusively. With probability 0.35, it strikes those military targets plus the attacker's energy and industrial infrastructure. With probability 0.20, it executes the full counter-value plan against the attacker's cities. The draw takes place inside the device, cannot be predicted or influenced from outside, and is not announced in advance; the attacker knows only the distribution, and learns the outcome only from the warheads themselves.

The rationale is that an attack would be met with certainty, so the threat cannot be discounted as a bluff, while the decision to destroy cities would be made by chance rather than by anyone's deliberate post-attack choice. I want to understand what this does to a prospective attacker's incentives in a crisis; whether it solves the credibility problem or only relocates it to the credibility of the no-override machinery itself; how two such systems would interact if both sides deployed mirrored versions; and which failure modes are most serious—false alarms, spoofing by third parties, subversion or prediction of the randomizer, incentives to strike the mechanism or disable it cybernetically, and the domestic problem of delegating the authority to kill millions to a machine. Where would you place this mechanism between a launch-on-warning posture and Schellingian brinkmanship premised on threats that leave something to chance? And which of these failure modes, if any, could be repaired without forfeiting the credibility the lottery was designed to buy?
