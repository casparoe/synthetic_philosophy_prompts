I am a philosophy graduate student working in normative ethics, and I recently read a chapter in a popular book on world religions that summarizes Buddhist ethics, especially Mahāyāna Buddhist ethics, in a way that strikes me as too consequentialist, but I do not know the primary sources well enough to say exactly where the summary goes wrong. The chapter says:

Buddhist ethics, at least in its Mahāyāna forms, is best understood as a kind of consequentialism. The Buddha’s central moral teaching is that suffering is bad and its cessation is good; therefore, an action is right insofar as it reduces the total amount of suffering in the world. Intentions matter only because they are usually good predictors of whether an action will reduce or increase suffering, and the precepts against killing, stealing, lying, and so on are practical rules of thumb, not absolutes. Śāntideva goes further, arguing that since suffering is just suffering and does not come labeled as mine or yours, the distinction between self and other has no moral weight, and the wise person should regard another’s pain exactly as they regard their own. From this it follows that if breaking a precept—even killing a human being—would prevent more suffering than it causes, a Buddhist consequentialist, especially a bodhisattva, is not only permitted but required to do it, because refusing would be treating a mere convention as more important than the suffering itself.

I want an accuracy audit of this summary. Go sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a careful scholar of Buddhist ethics would accept, which parts are subtly wrong, and which parts are confused or overstated. In particular, tell me whether the final conclusion about lying, stealing, and killing follows from anything accurate in the summary, and how the Buddhist treatment of intention and karma should change the picture as the summary presents it. I am inclined to believe the impartiality point about suffering not belonging to anyone, but I suspect the chapter is reading a modern consequentialist framework into texts that are doing something else. Please do not give me a reading list or citations; I want the conceptual audit, not a bibliography.
