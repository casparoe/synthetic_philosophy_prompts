Suppose a major funding body for fundamental physics adopts a Closure Rule. A subfield — particle physics, cosmology, or any area with a core set of equations — is declared Mature when, for fifteen consecutive years: (i) no free parameters or fields have been added to those equations; (ii) every quantitative parameter in them has been measured to at least one part in 10^6; and (iii) no anomaly above four sigma has remained unexplained by the equations for more than five years. Once a subfield is Mature, proposals whose primary stated motivation is discovering new fundamental physics in it become ineligible for the body's funding; money is redirected to precision tests of the existing equations, computational methods, and engineering or medical applications. A panel may revoke Mature status if a later replicated anomaly survives two years. Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position. Walk through what it incentivizes for experimentalists, theorists, journal editors, and rival funders; the first three ways it gets gamed; the points where the criteria and the underlying property — the subfield actually being finished — come apart; the second-order effects on what gets published and built; and whether these failure modes are fixable within the rule. End by saying what the institution's behavior suggests about whether a final theory, if one existed, would be recognizable from inside the scientific community.
