I am a retired physicist who has lately been reading moral philosophy, and I keep finding simplicity invoked as if it carried the same weight in ethics that it carries in the physical sciences. I am planning an essay titled something like "Parsimony in Ethics: A Borrowed Virtue?" and would be grateful for comments on the following sketch.

The central claims I want to defend are:

1. In physics, parsimony is not an independent mark of truth. It is a practical bias toward theories that are easier to use, test, and falsify; we abandon it as soon as the data demand added structure, as current cosmology has done with its elaborate but well-supported machinery. The data get the final say.

2. In ethics, the closest analogue to "data" is the set of considered judgments or intuitions that a theory is meant to fit. But these data are unlike physical measurements: they are more theory-soaked, more variable across persons and cultures, harder to weight, and not generated by a process known to be sensitive to a common underlying structure. So we cannot assume that the simpler theory is more likely to track the true moral order, because the fit between theory and intuition is not an independent test of anything.

3. What ethical parsimony really buys is cognitive manageability: a one-principle theory is easier to teach, apply, and criticize. That is a pragmatic virtue, not an epistemic one. The essay's main thesis would be that simplicity in ethics should function only as a tiebreaker between theories that are otherwise equal in fit with reflective judgments, and that even then simplicity should be credited to the theory as an instrument of understanding rather than as evidence of truth.

4. A corollary: the fact that a moral theory needs epicycles—special exemptions, auxiliary duties, agent-relative permissions—is not by itself a strong objection to it. The strong objection must be that the added complications are unprincipled or fail to fit the considered judgments at issue, not merely that they are complications.

I would particularly welcome comments on (a) whether my contrast between physics and ethics is too neat, (b) whether my use of "intuitions as data" misrepresents how contemporary moral philosophers think about theory choice, and (c) whether distinguishing epistemic from pragmatic parsimony in the way I do simply relocates the problem rather than solving it.
