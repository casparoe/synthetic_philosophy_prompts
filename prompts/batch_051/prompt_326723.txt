Late one evening, a long-running argument between two people I know came to a head. Rina and Theo had been circling the so-called main-character intuition: the nagging sense that one's own inner life is vivid, immediate, and somehow guaranteed, while other people's inner lives are comparatively shadowy, perhaps no more real than the non-player characters in a game. What started as banter became serious, and I ended up listening rather than participating.

Rina opened with what she called a perspectival illusion. Each of us has exactly one kind of access to our own mind, she said — introspection — and that access cannot be applied to anyone else. Other people are available to us only through their bodies, words, and behavior. So of course others feel less real; the feeling tracks an asymmetry in our access, not an asymmetry in the underlying facts. Treating other people as NPCs, she argued, is like concluding that the sun stops existing when you shut your eyes. Theo pushed back. He said he was not defending the claim that other people actually are NPCs but two narrower points. First, the asymmetry Rina described is not merely a quirk of perspective; it is structural. No observation of another person could distinguish a genuinely minded human being from a being that behaves identically but has no experience at all. Second, this means my warrant for my own consciousness is different in kind from my warrant for anyone else's — not just differently acquired, but differently grounded. The phrase "main character" is misleading, he admitted, but the specialness it gestures at is real. Rina replied that Theo was setting a standard no ordinary empirical belief could pass. You also cannot directly verify that your friend sees green rather than blue when she agrees the grass is green, yet nobody treats that as a live skeptical scenario. We infer other minds as the best explanation of the behavior we encounter, she said, and that is precisely what makes the belief rational. Theo answered that this inference is built on exactly one known case. The analogy from one's own case to others carries only if one has already assumed that other bodies are relevantly similar to one's own, which is the very thing at issue. His conclusion was not that solipsism is likely, but that the usual inference is far less neutral than it is advertised to be. Rina then shifted ground. The NPC alternative is not a serious competitor, she said, because it buys empirical equivalence at the price of a baroque story: billions of social agents navigating the world with no experience, coordinated by mechanisms that must do all the causal work experience was supposed to do. Simplicity, she thought, decisively favors the view that other people have minds. Theo resisted that appeal to simplicity. Once experiential states are treated as something over and above physical processing, he noted, simplicity may point the other way: a world with exactly one center of experience can in principle explain all the public appearances just as well as a world with billions, while positing fewer experiential states. He granted that no sane person would live as though the NPC hypothesis were true, and that moral life requires treating others as minded. But practical indispensability, he insisted, is not the same as evidential grounding. What Rina calls an inference to the best explanation is, on his account, closer to a practical postulate than to a neutral conclusion from the evidence.

They asked me to adjudicate, and I honestly could not. Then say who is more right overall, taking care to separate the metaphysical question of whether other people have minds from the epistemological question of what kind of warrant we have for that belief, and both from the practical question of how we ought to treat people.
