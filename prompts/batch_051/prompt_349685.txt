Analyze Frederick Douglass’s 1860 Glasgow address, “The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?”, through the lens of the philosophical distinction between what a text says and what its authors privately intended, treating that distinction as a general theory of legal interpretation.

The episode is this: Douglass was answering the Garrisonian charge that the U.S. Constitution was a pro-slavery compact, and that abolitionists were morally required to refuse to vote or hold office under it. Douglass denied that the Constitution guarantees a right to hold property in man, and he supported that denial with an argument about how the Constitution ought to be read. The passage I want you to build the analysis around is this:

“It should also be borne in mind that the intentions of those who framed the Constitution, be they good or bad, for slavery or against slavery, are so respected so far, and so far only, as we find those intentions plainly stated in the Constitution. It would be the wildest of absurdities, and lead to endless confusion and mischiefs, if, instead of looking to the written paper itself, for its meaning, it were attempted to make us search it out, in the secret motives, and dishonest intentions, of some of the men who took part in writing it. It was what they said that was adopted by the people, not what they were ashamed or afraid to say, and really omitted to say.”

I am not asking you to settle the historical dispute between Douglass and the Garrisonians. I want a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions.

First, reconstruct the interpretive theory that Douglass is invoking here. What exactly is the rule he proposes for reading the Constitution, and what does he treat as the object of interpretation—text, public meaning, the act of the people who ratified it, or something else? Then map his argument onto the standard distinction between textualism and original-intent methods. Which claims in the passage would a textualist accept, and which would she reject or modify? Does Douglass’s argument require the strong thesis that private intentions are never admissible evidence for meaning, or does it require only the weaker thesis that unexpressed intentions cannot override or supplement what the text itself says?

Then turn the lens around. Where does the theory misdescribe what Douglass is actually doing? In particular, is he offering a neutral methodological claim about how to read a written constitution, or is he making a political claim about whose ratification constitutes the document—such that the framers’ closed-door debates have no more authority than the secret motives of any other private individual? Does the passage’s phrase “what they said that was adopted by the people” smuggle in a theory of collective authorship that textualism does not normally defend?

Finally, say what the episode shows about the theory itself. Does Douglass’s move successfully refute original-intent methods, or does it expose a hidden normative premise in textualism—namely, a commitment to deferring to the text over the framers even when doing so has radical moral consequences? Be precise about which step of the theory is doing the work, and say explicitly where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
