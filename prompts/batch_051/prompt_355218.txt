I want a working definition of an inspired text — the property a community ascribes when it calls a text inspired — and I want that definition to settle, rather than stipulate away, the question whether a translation can be inspired. Test your definition against the following cases. Treat (1) and (2) as clear positives and (7) as a clear negative; the rest are the hard cases.

(1) An autograph in the original language, held by its community to be revealed.
(2) A scrupulously faithful translation into another language, prepared by believers for liturgical use.
(3) A deliberately free paraphrase that preserves the sense but not the wording, used for teaching but not for worship.
(4) An ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures, made before the canon was fixed, which diverges in places from the now-standard Hebrew text but is later quoted as scripture.
(5) A later translation that a community treats as uniquely authoritative, superior to the available original-language manuscripts and to all subsequent revisions.
(6) A corrected edition that revises a long-revered translation where most scholars agree the older rendering misread a word.
(7) A manuscript copy containing a probable scribal error, never accepted as authoritative by any community.

I suspect 'inspired' runs together a claim about the text's causal origin and a claim about its standing or authority within a community. If you agree, give me the two notions and trace how the cases shift between them; if you disagree, give a single definition, run it through the cases, and defend any surprising verdicts it forces.
