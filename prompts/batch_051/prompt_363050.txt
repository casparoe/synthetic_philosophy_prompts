I'm giving a seminar paper defending a neo-Humean claim about miracles: a well-attested report of a miracle should not move a rational agent to believe the event occurred on the strength of testimony alone, no matter how many witnesses or how apparently credible they are, unless the agent already assigns a non-negligible prior probability to some supernatural hypothesis that would make the event expected. My argument has three moving parts. First, testimony is evidence about human reliability as much as about the reported event, and judgments of reliability cannot be separated from background beliefs about what normally happens. Second, the aggregation of independent witnesses is much weaker than people assume once any shared source of error is allowed. Third, a miracle contradicts a regularity so well established that its prior should be treated as vanishingly small, so the posterior cannot cross any reasonable threshold.

The audience is a philosophy department colloquium: mostly philosophers of religion, two of whom work on early modern debates about miracles; two Bayesian epistemologists who work on the aggregation of testimony and think in likelihood ratios; and a historian of science who studies how early modern naturalists treated prodigies and anomalies and is suspicious of appeals to uniform experience. They will not ask the standard undergraduate counterexamples, or at least not in the standard form.
