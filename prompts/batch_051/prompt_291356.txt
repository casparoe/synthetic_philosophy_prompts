I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have agreed to run a forty-five-minute session for the newsroom's early-career reporters on the distinction between epistemic bubbles and echo chambers. I am not asking for the explanation itself; I need a plan for how I should explain it. The reporters already use "echo chamber" loosely, mostly as a synonym for seeing only like-minded content online. By the end, they should be able to say of a concrete case whether the better diagnosis is that contrary evidence is not reaching the person, or that contrary evidence is reaching the person but has been pre-emptively discredited, and to see why the obvious remedy for the first condition can actively reinforce the second. Please give me the order in which the components must be introduced and what depends on what; one carrying example that should do the main work, with the features it must have and must lack, and a note on whether an algorithmic social-media feed is a good choice or a trap; the misconceptions the reporters will most likely bring or form and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; a closing exercise or question that would show they can apply the distinction to a case they have not seen rather than restate definitions; and a short statement of what the session should explicitly leave aside. Include one brief segment on cases in which a person is in both a bubble and a chamber at once, so the distinction does not harden into a false either/or.
