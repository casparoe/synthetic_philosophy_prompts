I keep coming back to this argument and half-suspect it proves too much, so I want a precise verdict on which step, if any, fails. (1) On the multiplicative account, the value of an experience is the product of a consciousness factor and a happiness factor: v = c·h. (2) On the lexical account, consciousness is lexically prior to happiness: for any two experiences, if one involves even slightly less consciousness, no amount of added happiness in the other can compensate; a small loss of consciousness is worse than any gain in happiness. (3) Let experience A have c = 1 and h = 1, so its value is 1; let experience B have c = 0.5 and h = 100, so its value is 50. (4) Since 50 > 1, the multiplicative account ranks B above A. (5) But B is exactly the kind of compensation the lexical account forbids: half the consciousness, made up for by a hundred times the happiness. (6) Therefore the multiplicative account and the lexical account cannot both be right. I believe the arithmetic is correct, so the fault, if any, must lie either in the way the lexical view is stated in (2) or in the step (4) assumption that the product yields a single cardinal scale on which experiences at different consciousness levels can be compared. Is the argument valid? If so, and the lexicalist is to survive, which premise must they restate, and how? If it is invalid, say exactly which step fails.
