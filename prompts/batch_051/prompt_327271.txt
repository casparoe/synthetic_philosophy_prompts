I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm trying to get straight about a claim that comes up whenever people compare the value of groups of different sizes. The claim is that ordinary evaluative thought is average-sensitive rather than total-sensitive: when people judge whether one family, city, or species is better off than another, they respond mainly to the quality of a representative life and regard a larger population of equally good lives as more numerous but not better. The rival view is total-sensitivity: once the numbers are made clear, ordinary judgments track summed well-being, so a group with the same average but twice the members is judged better, everything else equal. It is sometimes said that the average-sensitive pattern is not just a numerical error but a genuine feature of how the ordinary notion of better off works. I want to know how one could test that stronger claim properly.

For each condition, state exactly what a total-sensitive judge and an average-sensitive judge would choose, and identify any result that would embarrass both hypotheses.

After laying out the design, say what a clean result would and would not show. In particular, address whether the study can distinguish between: a genuinely average-based concept of group welfare; an ambiguity in the phrase better off that different probes bring out differently; and a total-based concept that is masked by confused arithmetic. Say also whether the study would bear on the normative question of whether a bigger population of good lives is better, or only on a descriptive question about what ordinary people think. Finally, tell me whether the same test transfers from a city and a family to a species, or whether the scale changes the question in a way that would require new controls. I am not looking for a literature survey or citations; the value is in the design itself. Please write the answer in American English.
