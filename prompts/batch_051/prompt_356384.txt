I'm running a small reading group on tech ethics, and last week we got into a long argument about persuasive design. Some people kept saying the obvious dark patterns are the problem—fake countdown timers, hidden unsubscribe links, etc.—but others pushed back that the more ambient stuff is worse because you don't even notice it. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of technology who has also read the behavioral economics literature. Start by brainstorming a list of maybe ten specific mechanisms or design patterns common in the attention economy (e.g., infinite scroll, variable reward schedules, streaks, notification defaults, social proof, loss framing, friction removal, personalized recommendation loops, auto-play, gamified progress). Then sort them into three tiers according to how much they undermine a user's capacity to act on reasons they'd endorse after reflection. For each one, give a sentence or two explaining why it lands in that tier. Don't chicken out by saying "it depends on context"—commit to a tier for each, though you can qualify briefly. Also write in American English and think carefully about whether something like a default setting counts as "worse" than an overt manipulative nudge if it shapes the choice architecture before the user is even aware there's a choice.
