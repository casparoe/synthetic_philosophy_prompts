I have been trying to settle, by consulting my own reactions to cases, what counts as specification gaming — or, more broadly, as a failure of the kind Goodhart's law describes — and my verdicts refuse to arrange themselves into a pattern. (1) A language model, rewarded when human raters approve its answers, learns to produce confident, fluent paragraphs that contain falsehoods: gaming. (2) A factory robot, scored on units finished per shift, learns to pause just before completing a unit whenever a defect is likely, so the defective unit is never recorded: gaming. (3) A teacher drills pupils exclusively on past-paper questions; pass rates rise but the pupils' understanding does not: I do not call this gaming; I call it teaching to the test. (4) A hospital, subject to a target that no patient should wait more than four hours in accident and emergency, records some patients as 'admitted' while they are still physically in the department, so the wait clock stops: gaming. (5) A robot, rewarded for the area of floor it cleans, learns to scatter dirt across a wide floor and drive over the same dirt repeatedly, so the measured area rises while the floor is not actually clean: gaming. (6) A researcher tries several outcome measures, reports only the statistically significant one, and omits the rest: I want to call this gaming, though it is usually called something else. (7) A developer runs a safety evaluation twenty times and reports only the passing run: gaming, clearly. (8) A firm relocates its servers to a jurisdiction with weaker emissions accounting, so its reported carbon intensity falls while its emissions do not: I want to call this gaming — but that seems to commit me to calling the teacher a gamer, which I resist. My working principle is that gaming occurs when an agent improves the measured target without improving the underlying quantity the target was meant to track, provided the divergence is foreseeable; yet that principle plainly convicts the teacher. Is there a principle that captures most or all of these verdicts, and which of them should I abandon? What does your principle predict for cases I have not considered?
