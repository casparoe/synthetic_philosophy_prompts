I am a control-systems engineer, and for the past year I have been designing a supervisory system for a hospital. The system takes in sensor data, forms a judgment about whether a patient should be escalated to intensive care, and displays that judgment to a nurse on shift. It is deliberately designed with no actuator: it cannot page a physician, adjust a drip, or change a treatment order. The governance board is now asking whether this system should be treated as accountable for the judgments it forms in cases that end badly, and I have found I cannot think about the question clearly in the vocabulary I actually work with day to day: plants, sensors, actuators, control loops, feedback, control authority, interlocks, setpoints, and fault responsibility. I have no formal philosophical training.

Restate the philosophical debate about whether a being that can form moral judgments but cannot act on them is nonetheless a moral agent — including the position that the capacity to act is a necessary condition of agency and responsibility — in the vocabulary of control engineering, as precisely as you can. Do not smuggle in philosophical terms I would not recognize unless you explicitly define them using only control-engineering notions. Then give me a residue report: which claims in the debate translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed in the control vocabulary at all. I want to know whether the control vocabulary is genuinely missing something central to the debate, or whether the appearance of a deep disagreement is partly an artifact of failing to draw distinctions — between evaluating, deciding, and executing, for example — that a control engineer already draws. If the task as I have posed it rests on a confused premise, or cannot be done without pretending that engineering concepts carry moral weight they do not carry, tell me that plainly instead of giving me a polite translation.

Please structure the response in exactly these sections:

1. Setup and dictionary (about 250 words): the identifications you will rely on between the philosophical notions and control-engineering notions, and what you count as acting in a system.
2. The translation (about 600 words): the major positions in the debate rendered in control-engineering terms.
3. Where the translation strains and fails (about 450 words): the residue report, with your assessment of whether each failure is a limitation of the control vocabulary or a defect in the original debate.
4. Bottom line for the governance review (about 200 words): what I should tell the board, how confident you are in that answer, and what would have to be true for your bottom line to be wrong.
