Two people in my game-theory study group got into it over a centipede game, and I honestly couldn't referee it. The game has two players, A and B, alternating at four nodes, with payoffs written (A, B). At node 1, A can take (4, 1) or pass. At node 2, B can take (2, 8) or pass. At node 3, A can take (16, 4) or pass. At node 4, B can take (8, 32) or pass, and if B passes there the game ends at (64, 16).

(1) Rina said backward induction settles it: B takes at node 4, so A takes at node 3, so B takes at node 2, so A should take at node 1 and pocket 4. Passing first can only be a mistake or a friendly gesture.

(2) Marcus said wait—if I'm B and I actually see A pass at node 1, I've just learned she isn't the kind of player who backward-inducts. So at node 2 I should pass too, because if she then passes at node 3, I get 32 at node 4 instead of taking 8 now.

(3) Rina answered that Marcus is using an impossible move to dodge the theory: at node 3 a rational A takes 16, since passing there leaves her with only 8 when B takes at node 4. Marcus's plan at node 2 is good only if he stops modeling A as a payoff-maximizer, which means he's letting his own move justify abandoning the part of the theory he doesn't like.

(4) Marcus came back: that's exactly the flaw in Rina's argument. Backward induction needs common knowledge of rationality at every future node, and A's first pass already destroys that. After a zero-probability move, no theorem forces B to keep modeling A as a payoff-maximizer at node 3. If B instead models A as a pass-then-pass type, passing at node 2 is plainly better for B, and if A anticipated that, her first pass could be rational too—it makes B pass, and she ends up with at least 8 instead of 4. Rina says that makes rationality self-undermining; Marcus says it just shows the backward-induction prediction depends on ruling out the very move A is contemplating on the grounds that it wouldn't happen.

They asked me to sort it out and I couldn't. Please go through this move by move: say which specific claims are right, which are wrong or overstated, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Also separate what is mathematically settled from what is a genuine live dispute in game theory, and don't present the contested part as if it were the consensus.
