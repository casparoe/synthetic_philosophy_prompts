After I retired (physics, then a bit of dabbling in philosophy of mathematics) I got interested in strategy-stealing as a piece of argumentation, not just a game theory trick. The pattern, for two players, perfect information, finite, no draws: suppose the second player had a winning strategy; the first player makes a harmless throwaway move, then pretends to be the second player and copies that strategy. Whenever the copied strategy tells him to make a move that the throwaway already used up (or to play on a spot he already owns), he makes another harmless move. Since that extra move can never hurt—that is the whole load-bearing assumption—first wins, contradiction, so second cannot have a winning strategy, and with no draws first wins. I keep turning over what that "can never hurt" condition amounts to; it looks like a monotonicity assumption, but its doing almost all the philosophical work.

The examples I've actually seen worked out: Hex (extra stones don't hurt), Chomp (eating extra chocolate doesn't hurt), Bridg-It (extra bridges don't hurt), and the game of Y on the triangular board (same flavor as Hex, but a different connection condition). I'd bet there are more, but I don't want a reference list; I want a game I haven't met.

So: give me one more game where the strategy-stealing argument goes through, rules concrete enough to actually play. Genuinely new: not Hex on a different shape, not "Chomp but with an L-shaped bar," not a relabeling or a special case of the four above. Then show me the argument working in it, and show me one specific rule you could change that would break the argument, and why that matters for the assumption itself.

Structure the answer like this:

The game (~150 words): rules, board, win condition, number of players, and why it's finite and draw-free.

Why the argument goes through (~150 words): run the strategy-stealing proof for it and name exactly where the "extra move can't hurt" assumption is doing the work.

What makes it new (~100 words): contrast it with the four above so I can see its not just a retread.

Where it breaks (~100 words): the rule change that kills the argument and what that shows about the assumption.

No pressure to be fancy; I care more that the game is playable and the argument actually works. If, fiddling with rules, you end up with something that collapses into one of the four under pressure, say so plainly; I'd rather have an honest near-miss than a disguised retread.
