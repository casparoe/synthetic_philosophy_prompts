I write novels, and I've been carrying around a private distinction I'd like checked. I call it narrative locality: a scene's effect on a reader should be determined by the words of that scene plus the state she's accumulated from earlier scenes, updated locally. A detail in chapter two can't legitimately depend on chapter twenty unless there's a chain in the text connecting them.

Then there's narrative nonlocality: the ending acts backward and changes what earlier scenes mean, without those scenes already containing the new meaning. The twist ending — the narrator was dead the whole time — is the pure case. I've started suspecting this is just Bell's theorem dressed up. Locality says distant parts of the story can't correlate beyond what the text's local machinery allows, with the author's outline playing the role of a hidden variable. But good plots violate that bound constantly: the ending reaches back and rearranges the meaning of everything you already read.

So my homemade theory is that narrative nonlocality is real, and that plotting is mostly the art of smuggling in nonlocal influence so the reader doesn't protest. My questions: is there a genuine structural parallel here, or is a metaphor doing all the work? Could it be made precise — reader as some kind of finite automaton, scenes as measurement settings — and would anything survive the translation? And where does it break: what about fiction is most unlike a quantum system? Also be honest about wether this is just reader-response criticism in physics vocabulary.
