I teach sixth-form citizenship at a British secondary school. A pupil recently dismissed a genuine news video of a local politician as "probably AI anyway," and she said it with relief rather than concern. That unsettled me more than a routine source-evaluation mistake would have: she had not been taken in by a fake, but had stopped treating recorded evidence as an independent referee. Since then I have been trying to think through what some writers call the epistemic backstop — the role photographs, audio, and video play as a largely trusted anchor that lets people push back against official or convenient falsehoods. On this view, the deepest damage done by synthetic media is not that a fabricated recording occasionally deceives people, but that the mere possibility of fabrication lets anyone dismiss an inconvenient genuine recording as fabricated, removing the backstop at precisely the moment it is needed.

I am to lead a one-hour enrichment session on this idea for a mixed-ability sixth-form group. Please do not explain the idea to me; plan how I should teach it. I would like the explanatory arc: which components must be in place before the backstop claim even makes sense and what depends on what; the single carrying example that should run through the session, and what it must have and must lack so that it does not collapse back into the ordinary worry that people are fooled by convincing fakes; the misconceptions these pupils will predictably bring or form and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off; one question I can pose at the end to check whether they have grasped the backstop rather than merely learned the word; and what I should openly leave out at this level. Please write the plan in British English.
