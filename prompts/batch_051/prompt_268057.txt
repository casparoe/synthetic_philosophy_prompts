I spent my career in biochemistry and retired a few years ago; since then I have been reading philosophy of action, largely because of a puzzle I never resolved while teaching techniques in the laboratory: the difference between following a protocol attentively and performing it with a fluency that no amount of attention seems to improve. I have joined a reading group that asked each of us to write out our current position on effortless action, and I would like a consistency check of the position before I present it. Here is the set of commitments I have settled on, stated as plainly as I can.

(a) There is a real phenomenon, which the Daoist tradition discusses under the name wu-wei, in which skilled activity unfolds without felt deliberation or conscious monitoring, and often with an accuracy that deliberate effort could not reproduce.

(b) Wu-wei is not the mere absence of effort or the indulgence of a first impulse. A novice flailing freely at a piano is not in the state, while a jazz improviser drawing on years of internalized harmonic and motor patterns may be. The ease is earned; it rests on previously acquired structure.

(c) I am convinced that the structure behind such ease is ordinarily built through long periods of the opposite condition: effortful, self-monitored, error-correcting practice, of the sort studied as deliberate practice.

(d) It seems to me that directly aiming at wu-wei is self-defeating. Trying to be spontaneous makes one less spontaneous; trying not to try reintroduces the trying. Any technique, being something one does on purpose, appears to lapse back into the very controlling posture one intended to leave behind.

(e) And yet the traditions do not treat the state as an unteachable mystery. They transmit drills, breathing exercises, ritualized forms, and imitation of a master, as though one can be schooled into the condition described in (a).

(f) As a naturalist, I do not believe in a special mental act of the will inserted into an otherwise causal chain. Trying, deliberating, and attending are themselves natural processes, so the difference between the master and the novice is ultimately a causal difference in nervous systems and training histories, not a difference in how much non-natural volition each applies.

(g) Still, I think we are right to admire the effortless master more than the straining novice, and this admiration is not merely aesthetic. The master's ease appears to me to be a genuine achievement and a virtue, not just a fortunate temperament.

I cannot locate where this package is under strain. Please say which pairs or triples of commitments are in genuine tension and which only look as though they are, rank the real tensions by severity and explain what each would require me to give up, and then identify the single revision that would restore the most consistency at the least philosophical cost.
