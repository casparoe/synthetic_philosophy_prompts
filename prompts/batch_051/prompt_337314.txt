I've been going back and forth on the objective theory of assent and I think I'm stuck. The theory, as I understand it: a contract is formed by the outward manifestations of assent, not by what's in anyone's head. Whether you actually read the terms, understood them, or even knew they existed is legally beside the point; the law imposes something like a duty to read, and if you sign a form or click a button that says "I agree," you've assented, end of story. And I can see why this is attractive. It's simple, it doesn't require mind-reading, it avoids a million case-by-case inquiries into whether this particular person actually saw this particular clause on their particular phone screen, and it gives commerce a stable background rule.

But I also have an intuition I can't shake: clicking "I agree" on a 40-page set of terms you never opened just isn't agreement. If the arbitration clause was buried on page 23 and no reasonable person in my position would have read it, then saying I'm bound to it because I "agreed" feels like a legal fiction. The duty to read doesn't fix the problem — it doesn't tell you that I actually consented to anything, it just tells you that I should have. "You should have known" and "you agreed" seem like different things, and the whole reason the objective theory is tidy is that it dosen't want to notice the difference.

So I'm torn. Walk me through what each move costs. Is the right move to debunk the intuition — to say my sense of what real agreement requires is a folk-psychological hangup the law already and rightly ignores, because contract is about allocating risk and reliance, not about honoring inner acts of will? Or should the theory be amended or abandoned, maybe by requiring actual notice, a meaningful opportunity to read, or a separate, conspicuous manifestation of assent to the worst terms? Or is there a compatibility reading where my click isn't assent to each individual term but consent to a procedure that delegates the details to the vendor — and if so, is that a real distinction or just the objective theory with a nicer coat of paint?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the question.
