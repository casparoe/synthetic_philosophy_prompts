I'm putting together a handout for my social ontology seminar on whether kinds like money, race, and gender are socially constructed, and I want it in the form of a decision tree rather than a prose survey. Here's the angle I care about: build the tree out of the logical space of what "constructed" could mean, using a one-dollar bill as the anchor case, and only afterwards ask which positions survive the move to kinds that classify people. I don't want a tree that just lists authors; its got to be made of concrete questions a student can answer without knowing the literature, and a student should be able to answer each node before knowing which position it pushes them toward.

The positions that have to show up as leaves include at least: (i) a natural-kind view about money — for money this is basically unoccupied, and I want it flagged as such, though the corresponding slot for race is occupied and widely rejected; (ii) a dispositional/convention view where money's reality is exhausted by mutual expectations and dispositions to accept it; (iii) an error/eliminativist view where "this piece of paper is money" is strictly false but a useful fiction; (iv) a status-function view where the paper counts as money only in virtue of a collectively assigned status that carries rights and obligations (settling debts, being legal tender); and (v) a looping/interactive-kind view for race and gender, where classifying people as members of the kind feeds back into how the classified act and how institutions treat them, changing the kind over time. Add more leaves if the logic forces them, and for any leaf no one defends, say so.

Some diagnostic distinctions I want the tree to run students through, in whatever order works: whether the kind tracks an intrinsic property of the object itself; whether the kind is real or just a convenient way of talking; whether the construction runs through mere behavioral conventions or through rules and statuses; whether the "we" doing the constructing can be reduced to individual attitudes plus common knowledge; and whether the kind is indifferent (money doesn't respond to being classified) or interactive (people do). Flag any pairs of positions the tree can't actually pull apart.

Aim for something like six to nine nodes and eight to twelve leaves for a second-year undergrad audience. Then give me the tree as a JSON object with this schema:

- root_question: the first diagnostic question, as a string.
- nodes: an array of objects {id, label, question, if_yes, if_no}, where label is a short name for the distinction, and if_yes and if_no each point to another node's id or to a leaf's id.
- leaves: an array of objects {id, name, applies_to, reachable_by, awkward_commitment, occupied}, where applies_to says whether the leaf is about money, race, gender, or all of them, reachable_by is the path of answers like ["Q1=no","Q3=status"], awkward_commitment is one sentence on the most uncomfortable consequence, and occupied is true or false.
- empty_combinations: an array of objects {path, why_empty, note}, where why_empty is one of "incoherent", "unattractive", or "unexplored" and note is one sentence diagnosing why nobody is there.
- unseparated_pairs: an array of objects {leaf_a, leaf_b, reason}, for pairs of leaves the questions can't pull apart, with one sentence explaining the comittment they share.
