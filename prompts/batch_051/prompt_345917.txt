I'm a novelist, and I'm building a secondary world where the legal system has a category called a legal person. I know some real legal systems do this to, but my version is its own thing. I want to keep the metaphysics of my world consistent, so I've been checking my own definition against a weird case, and I'm going in circles.

My working definition: an entity is a legal person iff (a) it can hold property in its own name, (b) it can sue and be sued in court, (c) it can bear legal duties and be found liable for breaching them, (d) it has interests of its own that can be harmed, and (e) its interests get represented by some human decision-maker in court.

The edge case: the city of Veyne has declared the river Athas a legal person. The river holds the riverbed and some wetlands in its own name, and last year it sued a dye-maker for poisoning it and won—the damages went into a trust for restoring the river. Farmers downstream have also tried to sue the river after floods, and the courts let them name it as a defendant, but they've never held it liable; the judges keep saying a river is not the kind of thing that can be at fault. The trust, run by seven human trustees, decides what is 'in the river's interest.' Its charter defines that as the integrity and flow of the waters plus the health of the ecosystem. So the river can be a victim and own things, but it can't really be a culprit. And I can't tell whether it has interests in its own right or whether the trustees are just projecting human preferences onto a river.

So: is the river a legal person under my five criteria? And does this case show that one of my criteria isn't doing any real work, or that I should split 'legal person' into two different concepts?
