I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am applying for a six-month residential fellowship at a policy research institute for a project on official well-being statistics and what they are actually supposed to measure. For years I have watched the annual world happiness rankings generate headlines of the form that one country or another is the happiest on Earth, when in fact the underlying index ranks countries by average responses to a self-anchoring survey that asks people to place their present life on a ladder running from the worst possible life to the best possible life. My working suspicion is that the public debate has mostly been stuck on the wrong question. The familiar objections are measurement objections: the ladder item captures a reflective life evaluation rather than felt happiness; response styles and cultural norms around self-praise shift the numbers; people adapt to their circumstances and compare themselves with local peers, so the scale keeps being re-anchored; and the statistical apparatus that accompanies the rankings folds in objective indicators that blur the line between measuring well-being and explaining it. Those are fair complaints, but they leave a more consequential question untouched. Suppose a government set out, sincerely and competently, to move its country up the rankings. If it succeeded, would what it had brought about deserve to be called an increase in national well-being, or would it merely have improved the country's score on one particular metric of self-evaluated life satisfaction? And does the answer change once the ranking becomes an explicit policy target, given the familiar risk that a metric used as a target gradually stops being a reliable measure of the thing it was meant to track?

Please turn this into a two-page proposal. State the question in a form that has a determinate answer, and distinguish it clearly from the measurement critique so a committee can see that something more than better survey design is being proposed. Explain why the existing positions — the ranking's defenders, its methodological critics, and the standard arguments that governments should pursue well-being rather than mere economic growth — do not settle it. Then give a method: what I would actually do month by month, and why a conceptual rather than an empirical project is the right way to make progress on a question that is partly about what the numbers mean before and after they become targets. Say what a useful deliverable would look like for people who make or criticize policy, and be candid about what would count as the project failing. Keep the scope honest for six months, and do not include a bibliography or citations; I will supply those later.
