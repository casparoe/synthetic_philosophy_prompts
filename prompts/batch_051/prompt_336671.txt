I've been arguing about the paradox of fiction with my philosophy group for months and I want to stop doing it case-by-case. Give me the desiderata: what does a theory of our emotional responses to fictional characters and events actually have to get right? I want the phenomena listed with the reason each is a requirement—e.g., we feel genuine-seeming fear or pity for people we believe never existed; we don't usually try to intervene; we sometimes seek out fiction that reliably makes us sad or anxious; our bodily responses are similar to real-life emotion but not identical; people differ in emotional susceptibility to fiction; and spoilers change some responses but not others. Also mark the distinctions an adequate theory can't collapse: between having an emotion and imagining having one, between fiction-directed emotion and real-world emotion, between the content of the emotion and its behavioural output, and between what a theory predicts and what introspection reports. Then say which of these requirements you think are non-negotiable and which are parochial, and whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable. End with a short scorecard for the main families of solution—make-believe/quasi-emotion, thought, and direct realism—but only as a coda. British English please.
