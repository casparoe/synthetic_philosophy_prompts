I've been turning over the usual justifications for statutes of limitations and wondering how much of the argument survives in a world where digital evidence basically doesn't decay. The story I was taught has two prongs: an epistemic one, that old cases are unreliable because memories fade, witnesses die, and physical evidence degrades; and a repose one, that fairness to the defendant eventually wins out and people should get to live without a prosecution hanging over them forever. But those arguments were developed before your location history, messages, and purchase records sat in databases indefinitely, and before DNA could turn a cold case from 1987 into a near-certain conviction decades later. So here's what I want to know: if the evidence problem mostly disappears, is the repose argument strong enough by itself to justify a time limit, or was the whole thing secretly resting on the decaying-evidence premise? And if the argument can be repaired, what form does the repaired version take — does it still ground anything like a statute of limitations, or just a weaker, differently-keyed set of limits? I care less about actual reform proposals and more about which premise the digital world has actually removed.
