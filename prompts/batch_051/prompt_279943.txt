Please write a ten-question sorting quiz, suitable for an upper-level philosophy of artificial intelligence seminar, titled "What, if anything, is an LLM doing when it hallucinates?" The seminar has already studied Harry Frankfurt's distinction between lying and bullshit: on his account, the liar deliberately misrepresents what he takes to be true and must therefore be tracking the truth, whereas the bullshitter is defined by indifference to how things stand, which is why bullshit can be either true or false. The quiz should help students work out which diagnosis of large language model hallucination they actually hold.

Each question should present a concrete case in plain evaluative language, with two or three answer options, and no question should use the words "lying", "bullshit", or "error", or name any of the positions. The cases should include at least the following: a fabricated reference to a nonexistent legal case in a memo; a confident and fluent but false answer to a factual question; a true answer produced for reasons that have no connection to the relevant facts; a model fine-tuned on sources its developers know to be unreliable; and a model deployed behind an interface that describes its outputs as checked and authoritative. Further cases may be added only if they separate positions the required cases do not.

Build the instrument from the logical space first. Identify the distinctions on which the plausible positions genuinely come apart, such as whether an orientation towards truth can be attributed to the system at all, where in the human–machine arrangement any relevant intention is located, and whether the decisive wrong is falsity, indifference to truth, deception, or recklessness. Then locate at least the following five positions in that space as named leaves: (1) mechanical error or malfunction, on which hallucination is a failure of fit between a next-token predictor and the facts; (2) Frankfurtian bullshit, on which the system is constitutively indifferent to truth and aims at fluency and plausibility, so hallucination is normal operation rather than breakdown; (3) lying by design or by proxy, on which some responsible agent intends the model to deceive; (4) category error, on which none of the three descriptions applies because the model has no beliefs, intentions, or awareness; and (5) unwarranted assertion, on which the output functions as testimony offered without adequate warrant and the fault is epistemic recklessness. Add any further positions you think the cases genuinely force.

Provide a compact scoring key or short decision procedure mapping response patterns onto the positions. Note explicitly which combinations of answers no position occupies, and say of each empty combination whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pairs of positions the instrument cannot separate, and say what kind of additional case would be needed to separate them. Write the entire quiz and answer in British English.
