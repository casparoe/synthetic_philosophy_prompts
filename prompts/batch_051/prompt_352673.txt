So I was at a dinner thing with my brother-in-law, who is trying to get a small software company funded, and he kept saying the reason investors should believe him is that he's publicly promised all his early customers that he won't sell out or drop the product, so his reputation is on the line. A friend of mine who teaches behavioral ecology overheard and said, "talk is cheap." When I pushed, she insisted she didn't just mean "words aren't a guarantee," she meant something stronger about honest signaling — and then I got an earful about the handicap principle and peacock tails. But by the end she seemed to be using the same slogan to cover a whole pile of claims: that verbal promises carry no information at all, that any signal which is cheap can't be trusted, that only signals which impose a fitness cost can be honest, that reputation won't make talk costly because talk is too cheap relative to the benefits of bluffing, maybe even that language is kind of a proof-of-work scheme or something. I'm pretty sure some of those are trivial, some are defensible science, and at least one is overreach. Which distinct precise claims is "talk is cheap" actually gesturing at, which ones are which, and which interpretation was doing the work when she used it to dismiss my brother-in-law's point? I'd like it as a list of readings with verdicts, from the boring to the radical.
