In my psychology class we have been studying the debate between prototype, exemplar, and theory-theory accounts of concepts, and I have been trying to work out how one could tell which kind of representation a given concept actually uses. I have settled on the following rough criteria for a concept's being embedded in an intuitive theory rather than being a prototype or a set of stored exemplars. First, membership is driven by an underlying causal or explanatory principle, not by overall similarity to a summary representation or to remembered instances; a thing can count as a member even when its surface features are atypical, provided the relevant hidden property is believed to hold. Second, the features associated with the concept hang together through causal-explanatory links, so that the presence of one feature raises the probability of others through an assumed mechanism rather than through mere correlation. Third, possession of the concept supports explanations, predictions, and inferences that go beyond classification. Fourth, any fuzziness at the category boundary should come from uncertainty about the hidden property, not from graded similarity to a prototype or from conflict among remembered exemplars.

The case I keep going back and forth about is the ordinary concept of lying. On one hand, it looks theory-like: adults generally treat the speaker's mental state, rather than the surface form of the utterance, as what matters, so a false sentence uttered in sincere error is not called a lie, while a technically true sentence framed to leave a false impression may be called a lie because the speaker intended to mislead. The concept also does real explanatory work in social reasoning: deciding that something is a lie licenses inferences about the speaker's trustworthiness, what else might have been concealed, and how the speaker is likely to behave later. On the other hand, the concept shows strongly graded structure: a small falsehood told to spare someone's feelings can be classified as a white lie or as not really a lie, sarcastic and joking falsehoods get disputed, and judgments about borderline cases seem to shift depending on which particular examples come to mind, which sounds more like an exemplar effect than a theory. There is also a developmental side: young children seem to rely more on plain factual falsity, and they move toward intention-based judgments only as their theory of mind develops.

Is the mature, everyday concept of a lie a theory-structured concept by my four criteria? Please go through the criteria one by one, and where the verdict is mixed, say whether the difficulty shows that the criteria need amending, that the concept is a hybrid, or that it is a mistake to expect a single concept to have one underlying format. Do the fringe cases give me a reason to revise one of the criteria, and if so, which one and how?
