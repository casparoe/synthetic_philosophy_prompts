I've got a one-year postdoc in philosophy with a lot of latitude, after which the academic job market gets a vote, and I want to spend the year on something that actually matters rather than on a careful restatement of David Lewis's account of convention. The line I keep circling is whether the Lewisian machinery — a convention as a regularity everyone conforms to because they expect everyone else to conform and prefer to conform given those expectations, all of it common knowledge — can be stretched to cover how social norms get going and stick, or whether the interesting action is exactly where the machinery fails.

I'd like you to act like a brutally honest advisor: pick the two or three lines of work below (or something better I'm not listing) that are most worth a year of my life, and defend why those and not the others. The criteria I care about are how many other questions depend on the answer, whether a year of theory and conceptual work (plus maybe simple agent-based simulations, no lab experiments) could actually move it, and whether getting it right would change what people say about norms rather than just tidy up a definition.

The candidates I keep turning over:

1. The common-knowledge requirement looks too strong. Actual conventions seem to run on much less than "everyone knows that everyone knows that everyone knows..." all the way down. Is the infinite hierarchy doing real explanatory work, or is it an idealization we could replace with something like rough mutual belief — and what does that replacement cost the rest of the account?

2. There's a gap between "we'd all rather coordinate" and "you shouldnt break the convention." Conventions are supposed to be arbitrary and replaceable, yet once they exist people use ought-language about them and punish defectors. Is explaining how the ought gets attached to a coordination equilibrium the central unsolved problem in moving from convention to norm, or can a Lewisian say the ought is just useful talk?

3. The account might be too permissive. A stable pattern can look like a coordination equilibrium even when it's terrible for some people, because each person still does better conforming given that others conform. If a Lewisian story can explain deferential or oppressive regularities as conventions just as easily as helpful ones, does that mean the framework is empty at the explanatory point we care about, and the real work is about power, history, and equilibrium selection?

4. Lewis's definition is built for situations where everyone would rather do what everyone else does. A lot of real norms live in mixed-motive settings — keeping a costly promise, not overfishing a common resource — where there's a pull to defect once others comply. Should we try to extend the convention framework into mixed motives, or is that the boundary where we should switch to another tool?

5. Salience is a black box. Lewis says one equilibrium becomes the convention because it's salient or has precedent, but precedent pushes the origin question back to however the first instance got started, and salience looks like whatever we point to after the fact. Is there a rigorous way to fill this in, or is this a non-problem because history does the selecting and theory can take it as given?

6. The agent-based and evolutionary work shows that simple learners can converge on regularities without much common knowledge or common interest, and people present that as explaining norms. But the models mostly explain habits, not norms with expectations, feedback, and punishment. Is there a version of this work that keeps what's distinctively normative, and if so is that the most important direction or a sideshow?

So: rank your top two or three priorities for a year of theory-first work, defend the ranking against the ones you set aside, say what a good outcome would look like for your top pick, and say what finding during the year would make you reshuffle.
