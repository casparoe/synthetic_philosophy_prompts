In the philosophy of computational complexity, one sometimes hears the claim that membership in P is the right formal counterpart of the intuitive notion of a feasibly solvable problem. That is usually presented as a clarifying stipulation, but it appears to make a substantive empirical bet: that the polynomial/super-polynomial divide in worst-case behavior tracks the divide between what resource-limited agents can and cannot do in practice. Design the strongest test you can of that bet. The test should exploit the known mismatches rather than set them aside — polynomial-time algorithms with enormous constants or exponents that are useless in practice, and exponential-time algorithms that perform well on the instances people actually feed them. Specify what result would count in favor of the claim and what would count against it. Then be precise about what a positive result would and would not settle: would it show that P is the true boundary of feasibility, or only that P is a workable idealization given the particular problems, inputs, and hardware we happen to care about? Finally, say what residue of the notion of feasibility no empirical test could reach, no matter how careful the design.
