I am a sixth-form student preparing for an essay on the Turing test and behavioural criteria for mind, and a disagreement has come up in our discussion group. One person argued that if a machine could sustain a conversation well enough that a careful judge could not reliably distinguish it from a human, that would settle the question of whether it thinks. Another person replied that this only shows the machine can produce the right words, and that any purely behavioural test will confuse genuine understanding with clever mimicry. I do not want to settle that dispute directly. I want something more useful first: a specification of what any behavioural criterion of mind would have to achieve in order to be a good criterion, rather than merely a conversational benchmark.

Please write the list of desiderata that a behavioural test or criterion for mindedness must meet, with the case or thought-experiment behind each requirement. For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable for anyone who takes behavioural evidence seriously, or whether it is a preference of a particular camp—for example, that the candidate must learn the language rather than be preprogrammed, that the judge must be ordinary rather than expert, that the candidate must be tested over a long period, that it must have a body and causal contact with the world, or that the test must be double-blind. The part I most want to see is whether some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, or whether the list itself already smuggles in an internal notion of mind that the test was meant to avoid presupposing. Keep the weight on the criteria and the cases that motivate them rather than on scoring existing machines or programmes, and please write in British English.
