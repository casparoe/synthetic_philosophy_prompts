I've been going round in circles with a flatmate about how much we can ever know about what long-dead people believed, given that all we've got to go on is stone and bone and the odd bit of pollen. The case I keep coming back to is the Shanidar flower burial, because it seems to show both how tempting and how shaky this kind of inference is. The story as I understand it: in the 1950s and 60s Ralph Solecki's excavations at Shanidar Cave in northern Iraq turned up several Neanderthal skeletons, and around one of them (Shanidar IV) the soil contained unusually dense clusters of flower pollen, including species with medicinal uses. Solecki read this as a burial rite: the body had been deliberately placed with flowers, which he took as evidence that Neanderthals mourned their dead and perhaps had something like religious feeling. Some decades later other researchers pointed out that the pollen could have got their by much more mundane routes: a burrowing rodent called a Persian jird hoards flower heads in caves, and wind, trampling, and later disturbance can all leave similar traces. So the 'flowers' might be a fact about cave ecology rather than about Neanderthal minds.

The lens I want is the epistemology of archaeological inference about belief. A lot of work in this area seems to run on an inference-to-best-explanation picture: a pattern of material remains is evidence for a past belief or ritual only if the belief/ritual explanation beats all the non-symbolic rivals — natural processes, animal activity, ordinary practical behaviour. I'd like you to use the Shanidar case to stress-test that principle. Walk me through what exactly has to be assumed at each step to get from pollen clusters to 'deliberate ritual with symbolic meaning', and say which rivals the original flower interpretation needed to rule out and whether it actually did. Then run it the other way: does the jird challenge show that any attempt to reconstruct belief from objects is underdetermined in principle, or only that this particular inference was lazy? And what would a genuinely good argument for a past belief need to look like — what combination of features or absence of alternatives would move you from 'people did something' to 'people believed something'? Treat the facts I've given as fixed background; I'm after the reasoning, not the archaeology itself.
