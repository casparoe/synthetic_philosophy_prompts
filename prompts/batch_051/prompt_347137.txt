I write historical novels, mostly about political upheaval, and I've got a French Revolution project simmering in the background. I've read just enough of the historiography to know the camps are fighting, not enough to referee it, and the thing that keeps poking at me is how much the debate seems to be about stuff I already think about as a writer: agency, inevitability, what makes a character or a plot turn feel earned. So I want a translation job.

Take the main ways people explain the Revolution — roughly: the class-struggle reading where it's a bourgeois revolution against feudalism and the engine is social forces and material interest; the revisionist reading where the driver is ideology and a new political culture, discourse running ahead of material conditions; and the contingency reading where accidents, personalities, and particular moments matter and the Terror was not written in advance. Include the old line about revolutions eating their own as a phenomenon to be translated, not as a separate theory.

Restate each in the vocabulary I actually think in as a novelist: character agency versus structural pressure, motivation, want versus need, inciting incidents and turns, tragic inevitability, whether a plot beat feels earned or arbitrary, and so on. I want the translation to be faithful, not cute — tell me where the theory maps cleanly onto a craft decision and where you had to stretch it.

Then give me the part I actually want: the residue report. What doesent survive the translation? What had to be fudged, and which bits of the historians' disagreement have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? And rule on the residue: is what won't translate a sign that my craft vocabulary is blind to something real, or a sign that part of their dispute is less substantive than it looks once you strip off the academic prose? Also tell me how confident you are in the translation and especially the residue verdict, and single out the point you're least sure of.
