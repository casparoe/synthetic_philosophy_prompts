A friend of mine has settled views about when someone is morally complicit in another person's wrongdoing without having caused the harm. She explains the category through cases, and she treats her rulings on the following six examples as fixed.

1. Ana drives a friend to a store the friend plans to rob, waits outside with the engine running, and expects a share of the proceeds; she wants the robbery to succeed. The friend would not have attempted the robbery without a driver. Verdict: complicit.

2. Ben, a courier, carries a sealed envelope that he believes contains ordinary employment contracts; it actually contains a schedule of bribes to a public official. He never learns its contents. Verdict: not complicit.

3. Claude lends his nephew folding tables and a canopy for a weekend flea-market stall where the nephew sells counterfeit watches. Claude knows exactly what the nephew is doing and wants the scheme to succeed, though he is confident the nephew could have borrowed the equipment elsewhere without much trouble. Verdict: complicit.

4. Dana plays no role in a coworker's vandalism of their supervisor's car and learns of it only after it is done. She then writes the coworker a note saying she is glad someone finally did it. Verdict: not complicit.

5. Elif lets her brother store smuggled fuel in her barn for two days to keep it out of the inspectors' sight, knowing the details, because she wants the enterprise to succeed and wants to help him. Verdict: complicit.

6. Farid works for the power company and keeps electricity running to a district that includes an office running a fraudulent telemarketing operation; he does not know of that office or its activities. Verdict: not complicit.

Now I want to know how she would rule on these three further cases:

7. Gwen runs a small web-hosting company. A fake tech-support operation signs up for hosting. While setting up the site, Gwen recognizes that the business is fraudulent: it charges elderly customers for virus-removal software that does nothing. She does not want the fraud to succeed; she takes the account only because the monthly fee keeps her business alive, and she knows the group would simply move to another host if she refused. She hosts the site.

8. Hassan arrives at a warehouse ready to drive a load of stolen appliances for his friend, and wants the operation to succeed. The friend sends him home because a larger truck has become available. The theft proceeds exactly as it would have without Hassan, and Hassan knows this.

9. Imran runs a payment-processing firm. His dashboard shows that a cluster of merchants has chargeback rates far above normal, with customer notes reporting that their cards were charged for services they never ordered. He suspects what the full records would show and therefore avoids opening them, since he does not want to have to decide whether to terminate the accounts. He continues processing the payments.

Predict, with reasoning, how she is likely to rule on each of 7 through 9; where a prediction is genuinely underdetermined, say what consistency with her six settled rulings permits rather than requires. Then set out the rule or rules that best capture her six verdicts, and at least one competing rule that fits them equally well. Say which of the new cases would most cleanly separate the competing rules. Finally, give your best overall guess about her verdicts on the new cases, note the main assumptions on which those guesses depend, and describe any further case you would want to put to her before making up your mind, saying what it is designed to distinguish.
