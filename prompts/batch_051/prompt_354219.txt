I got into an argument with my friend after a seminar on Turing and philosophical behaviorism. He kept defending the Turing test with the old duck test—the idea that if an animal looks like a duck, swims like a duck, and quacks like a duck, you should just call it a duck. His line was that once a machine holds up its end of a conversation well enough for long enough, the only consistent move is to say it's inteligent, and anyone who keeps asking whether it's really thinking is just moving the goalposts. For him, observable behavior is all we ever have to go on, so passing the test just is the evidence that settles the question.

I half-agree, but the analogy feels like it's doing more work than he admits. The duck test was built for identifying ducks, where the category is already anchored to something like a biological species, and nobody worries about whether the animal is a duck on the inside. Mind or thought might be different—maybe it's a hidden state, maybe the appearance can be faked without the underlying thing. But I also don't want to be the person who throws out the only workable criterion we have.
