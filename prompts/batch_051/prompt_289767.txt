So a friend and I got into an argument last night that went on way too long, about whether groups really exist. The thing is, we agreed about every fact we could write down. There are individual people, they interact in patterned ways, and you can say "the committee decided" or spell out a long list of who said what and who ended up signing, and neither way of saying it loses any information. He said group-talk is just a convenient shorthand; I said no, if the pattern is genuinely there in the individuals, then the group is real. But whenever he pushed me to say what "really there" adds to "the individuals form a stable pattern," I couldn't say anything that didn't just re-use the word "really." He couldn't say what would change his mind either. Still, neither of us would budge.

Then I noticed I've seen the same stubborness before. People argue about whether numbers really exist ("there's no such thing as the number three, it's just a shared notation" vs "of course three exists, what else is prime?"), about whether holes exist, and about whether a corporation is something over and above its employees. In each case, both sides can restate the other side's view of the underlying facts perfectly, and they are not even disagreeing about how the word is normally used. The whole dispute seems to live in the word "really," yet calling it "just semantics" makes both sides feel misunderstood, because they both mean to disagree about the world, not about a label.

Is there a name for this — an existence disagreement that survives complete agreement on the facts and on the paraphrases, and that the people in it don't experience as merely verbal? And what, if anything, is the leftover disagreement actually about? I'm not just after a label. I want to know how you would tell whether a particular case is empty, and how to tell it apart from a plain verbal dispute where two people are just using a word differently.
