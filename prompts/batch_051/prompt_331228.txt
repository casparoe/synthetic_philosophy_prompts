In teaching the semantics of generics, I have been giving my students the following working account. A generic statement of the form "Ks are F" is true just in case either (a) F is true of a sufficiently large proportion of Ks, or (b) F is characteristic of Ks—where a property is characteristic if it follows from the kind's normal form of life, life cycle, or the way the kind is standardly understood, even when many individuals, such as all males, all juveniles, or all non-breeding members, are exceptions. This is meant to explain why "ducks lay eggs" is true although only mature females lay eggs, and why "mosquitoes carry malaria" is true although only a minuscule fraction of mosquitoes ever carries the parasite.

Now consider a case that seems to pull the two clauses apart. Adopt the simplified textbook picture of an ant colony: the overwhelming majority of individual ants are workers, and workers are sterile; reproduction is left to the queen or queens and, briefly, to the males, who together form a small minority of the colony. On this picture, "ants are sterile" satisfies clause (a) as decisively as any statistical generalization could. But one may well hesitate to call it true in the way that "ducks lay eggs" is true. Sterility is not a feature of the kind's life cycle; it is a feature of a caste. The kind reproduces and continues through its fertile minority, so any account of what ants normally are will have to make room for the queen.

Is "ants are sterile" true on the account I gave? Go through clause (a) and clause (b) honestly, and say exactly where the account underdetermines the verdict. If the account forces the answer "true" and that seems wrong, identify what is doing the forcing—the statistical clause, the characteristicness clause, or the assumption that the two are independent or exhaustive—and say whether the example shows that the account needs revising or that the intuition should be abandoned.
