I've been doing the Lewis scorekeeping stuff in my pragmatics class, and I've built an argument out of it that I'm pretty sure has to be wrong, but I can't find the weak step. Here's the machinery as I understand it. A conversation has a scoreboard, and one part of it is the common ground: the set of propositions everyone treats as taken for granted for the purposes of the conversation. A presupposition is a proposition that has to be in the common ground for an utterance to be a proper move. Then there's accomodation: if a speaker says something whose presupposition isn't on the board yet, the audience will normally just quietly add it, as long as it's uncontroversial, and the conversation rolls on. The audience's other option is the "hey, wait a minute" move — object to the presupposition and force the speaker to either assert it openly or drop it.

Now the argument that's bugging me:

(1) Under accommodation, a presupposition P that isn't already in the common ground gets added unless someone objects.
(2) Consider a loaded question like "Have you stopped leaking the briefings?" Answering either "yes" or "no" counts as not objecting; either way the presupposition that you leaked the briefings goes into the common ground.
(3) So whether P ends up in the common ground depends entirely on whether the audience interrupts. The speaker loads the question, and the audience carries the burden. Objecting mid-conversation is socially costly — you read as combative or evasive.
(4) The "as long as it's uncontroversial" restriction doesn't actually block this, because "uncontroversial" just means nobody objected: if no one says anything, it was uncontroversial by definition. The restriction is idle.
(5) So a speaker can get nearly any proposition they want into the common ground without asserting it, defending it, or taking on any burden of proof — just by presupposing it and counting on everyone's reluctance to interrupt.
(6) And once P is in the common ground, it's treated as true from then on. Later moves build on it, nobody re-opens it, and getting rid of it later takes awkward conversational surgery.
(7) Therefore, on the scorekeeping model, assertion is redundant as a way of moving the conversation: publicly putting a claim forward and being answerable for it does nothing that presupposition doesn't do more cheaply. Lying-by-presupposition should be an unstoppable move.
(8) But that's absurd. Real conversations don't work like that: people deflect loaded questions all the time, and saying "it's surprising that P" or "the fact that P" doesn't just make P common ground for everyone in the room.
(9) So either the scorekeeping model is broken, or one of (1)–(7) is doing something it shouldn't. Where does the argument go wrong? I've got a lot riding on the claim in (3) that loaded questions genuinely smear people, so I'd be happiest if the fix keeps that in place. But I'd rather know what each available fix costs.
