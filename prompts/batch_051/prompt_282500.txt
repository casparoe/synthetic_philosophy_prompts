I am a PhD student in the philosophy of mathematics and mind, working on mechanistic accounts of mathematical cognition. I would be grateful for your careful help with the following problem.

Please adopt the role of a professional philosopher of mathematics and of mind, with expertise in formal systems, Gödel's incompleteness theorems, and the Lucas–Penrose anti-mechanist argument. Think carefully before answering, and start by briefly setting out the structure of the problem and the possible positions one might take before committing to a view. Please write your final answer in British English.

One of the most durable anti-mechanist arguments runs as follows. Gödel's first incompleteness theorem shows that for any consistent formal system rich enough to express elementary arithmetic, there is a sentence — its Gödel sentence — which is true but unprovable within that system. Lucas and Penrose argued that a human mathematician reflecting on the system can see that this Gödel sentence is true; hence the mathematician's arithmetical insight cannot be identical with, or wholly produced by, any such consistent formal system. Since a candidate mechanism for the mind was assumed to be specifiable as such a system, the argument purported to show that the mind cannot be a mechanism of that kind.

Now consider a development that postdates the classic statements of the argument: large language models and related machine learning systems. These systems are mechanical in the relevant sense: they run as finite programs on ordinary digital hardware. But they are not naturally described as consistent formal systems with fixed axioms and a recursively enumerable set of theorems. They are trained on enormous corpora of human text; their behaviour is probabilistic; they can assert contradictory things on different occasions; their outputs depend on prompts, sampling parameters, and training data; and they can produce plausible mathematical reasoning, including sometimes correct proofs and correct identifications of mathematical truths.

Does the Lucas–Penrose anti-mechanist argument survive this development? In answering, please do the following:

1. Reconstruct the argument into explicit premises and identify precisely which premise the existence of large language models bears on — for example the premise that any mechanism is equivalent to a consistent formal system, the premise about what human mathematicians can see or know, or some other premise you identify.

2. Say whether the argument still goes through, needs repair, or is dead as a challenge to mechanism in light of large language models. If it still goes through, explain why the absence of a fixed axiomatisation in these systems does not allow mechanism to escape. If it needs repair, state the repaired argument precisely and say what work it can still do. If it is dead or largely defused, say what the remaining problem for mechanism should be called and how it differs from the original Lucas–Penrose challenge.

3. Consider the strongest objection to your assessment and answer it.
