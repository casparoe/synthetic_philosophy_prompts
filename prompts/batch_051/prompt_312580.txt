The student's answer reads: "Callosotomy patients have the corpus callosum cut, so the two hemispheres can't share information. If a word like KEY is flashed to the left visual field, it goes to the right hemisphere, and the patient says they saw nothing, or maybe just a flash, even though their left hand can pick out the key from a tray. The same stimulus in the right visual field gets named normally. So the left hemisphere does the talking and reports only what it receives. Some people read this as evidence that there are two separate consciousnesses in one skull. On the unity question, I'd distinguish phenomenal unity (all of a subject's simultaneous experiences are co-conscious in a single field) from access unity (information being available to a single system for reasoning, speech, and control of action). Split-brain patients clearly lose access unity across hemispheres, since one hemisphere can't verbally access what the other is seeing. But phenomenal unity is preserved, because the right hemisphere's percept is still conscious, just unreportable, and it remains part of one overall field. So the cases don't threaten the ordinary unity of consciousness; they only show that the reporting system is split.
