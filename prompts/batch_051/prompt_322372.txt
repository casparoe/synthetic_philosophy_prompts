Me and a mate went round in circles about this last night — he's doing law, I'm doing philosophy, and he kept saying I was being too abstract about fault while I kept saying he was too comfortable punishing people for bad luck. His argument, as best I can reconstruct it, ran like this. (1) It is wrong to punish someone who wasn't at fault; that is the whole point of the mens rea requirement. (2) Fault has to be a matter of how the person's will was engaged — they intended the harm, knew it would come about, or were at least reckless about it. (3) Negligence doesn't require any of that: the law convicts you for falling below the standard of the reasonable person, and it need not be shown that you saw the risk coming, or even that you could have met the standard. (4) So negligence liability punishes people without fault in just the sense that is supposed to make strict liability unjust. (5) So negligence and strict liability stand or fall together. (6) But we plainly cannot give up negligence liability; a criminal law that only punished people who intended the harm, knew of it, or were reckless about it would be unworkable and unfair to those harmed by carelessness. (7) So the principle in (1) has to go, and strict liability cannot be condemned for punishing without fault. I hate (7) and told him so. I'd much rather keep (1) than (6), but I can't see which step is the week one or what it costs to reject it. Which premise do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
