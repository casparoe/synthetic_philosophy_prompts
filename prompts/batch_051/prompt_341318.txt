Last night a friend and I argued for several hours about moral testimony — specifically, about whether you can responsibly form or revise a moral belief simply by taking another person's word for it, the way you accept a physician's diagnosis or a teacher's account of a historical date. I was defending the optimistic side: in principle, a trustworthy person's say-so can give you warrant for a moral belief even when you cannot reconstruct the underlying reasoning yourself. My friend was defending the pessimistic side: there is something distinctively deficient about moral deference even when the source is reliable — something absent when you defer about empirical facts. What surprised me was how much of the apparent disagreement evaporated once we stopped talking past each other, and how few stable disagreements remained; but neither of us could state, to the other's satisfaction, exactly what those remaining disagreements were. We eventually decided to treat ourselves as two camps in an adversarial collaboration and to write down the document such a collaboration would leave behind. I would like you to draft it.

The document should begin with a numbered section of claims both camps can sign without hesitation. These should include the uncontroversial paradigm cases each side uses as fixed points; any settled moral cases whose correct description both sides accept before either interprets them; and any asymmetry between moral testimony and ordinary empirical testimony that both sides acknowledge prior to the interpretive dispute. For the moral cases, you may use the example we kept circling back to: a colleague whose judgment you have long found sound in morally complex matters tells you that a particular investment practice is wrong, and you believe her although you cannot yet supply the grounds yourself. Is that an instance of ordinary testimonial warrant, a defective form of belief, or a third thing? I want the agreed-claims section to specify what about such cases is common ground. Then present the residual disagreements in a table. The table should have roughly these columns: matter in dispute; wording each camp would accept for its own position; the crux or cruxes beneath that dispute, such that resolving them would genuinely move at least one camp; a concrete case or consideration both camps agree bears on each crux; and the prediction each camp records in advance about how that case will turn out. In particular, separate the question whether moral testimony can transmit mere knowledge or justification from the question whether it can transmit moral understanding, and separate both from the question whether the unease people feel about moral deference is epistemic at all rather than a matter of personal autonomy or the shaping of character. Make the formulations neither vacuous nor slanted, and include in the table only disagreements you judge to be genuine rather than merely verbal; if one side is best expressed as a weaker disambiguation that both camps would then accept, record that in the agreed-claims section instead of manufacturing a disagreement around it.
