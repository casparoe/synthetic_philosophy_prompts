I went down a sweatshop rabbit hole recently (a Twitter thread that led to a blog post, then some long encyclopedia article I only half-read) and I've landed on a list of beliefs. I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so I'd like a sort of code review for my opinions — where are the merge conflicts, basically. Here's roughly where I've ended up:

1. Sweatshop jobs are often the best option the workers actually have, so taking the job is rational and genuinely better for many workers than their alternatives.
2. An exchange can be both exploitative and mutually beneficial — someone can come out ahead and still be exploited, because the owner comes out ahead by so much more in a way that seems unfair.
3. Whether an offer is exploitative doesn't just depend on the deal itself; it depends on background injustice. If the worker's alternatives are terrible partly because of a global trade system that favors rich countries and big capital, that can make the job offer exploitative even though it's the worker's best option.
4. But I also can't shake the intuition that if a firm offers someone a job that makes them better off than their actual alternatives, the firm hasn't necessarily wronged them just by not doing more. Not paying more isn't the same as harming, and improving someone's situation shouldn't count as exploiting them just because you could have improved it even more.
5. At the same time I think there's something wrong with the system itself when the baseline is that dire — its just that no individual firm can fix it unilaterally, so I'm not sure where the obligation actually lands.
6. I think consumer boycotts of particular factories are mostly bad, because if the jobs are the best option available, shutting the factory leaves the workers worse off.
7. I think the workers' consent isn't fully free, since the alternatives can be awful, but I also think treating their consent as invalid is super patronizing — they know their situation better than outside activists do.
8. I think the best thing outsiders can actually do is push for structural stuff: labor law enforcement, union rights, trade rules — not lecture individual firms about wages.

I honestly can't tell whether this is a consistent package or whether I'm quietly holding views that pull against each other. So give me the audit. Which pairs of these actually conflict, in order of how serious the conflict is, and what would I have to give up or weaken to fix each one? Which pairs only look like they conflict, and why does the apparent tension dissolve? And if I only get to revise one of the eight, which single change would buy the most coherence at the lowest cost?
