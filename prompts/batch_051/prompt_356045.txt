Here's a thing I keep going back and forth on: contracts of adhesion are everywhere now, and I've realised I don't actually know what it means to say someone "agreed" to one. I want a working definition of agreement — or maybe "genuine assent to contractual terms", if those come apart — that isn't just "whatever the courts happen to enforce". I care about the concept, not the case law, so please stay at the level of principles and don't go hunting for specific cases.

You are a professional philosopher who works on private law and speech acts. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming two or three candidate defintions before you commit; then run the best one through the cases below. I've put rough pretheoretical verdicts on some of them in parentheses, and I'm genuinely unsure on others. Treat those parentheticals as data to be explained or corrected, not as ground truth. Where your definition disagrees with me, either fix it or defend the surprising verdict.

The cases:

1. Two businesses negotiate a software contract over several weeks, read the final text, and both sign it. (Clearly agreement.)
2. A cat walks across a keyboard and clicks the I Agree button on a EULA. (Clearly not.)
3. An app shows the full terms in a scrollable box; the user reads the whole thing and clicks I Agree.
4. Same as 3, but the user scrolls straight to the bottom and clicks without reading a word. (I want to say this is agreement, but this is where it starts to feel uncomfortable.)
5. A paper contract that a person signs without reading, under no time pressure, with no one misleading them. (I'd have said yes in court, but conceptually I'm no longer sure.)
6. A website has a footer link to its terms, and the page says that by continuing to use the site you're bound by them. No click, no scroll, no acknowledgement. (No idea.)
7. Shrink-wrap software: the licence is inside the box, visible only after purchase, and the terms say that using or even keeping the product counts as accepting them. (Also no idea.)
8. An email tells you the terms have been updated and continued use after 30 days counts as acceptance. You keep using the service. (Leaning no, but I can't say why.)
9. A thirteen-year-old reads the terms and clicks the same I Agree button as in case 3.
10. You've installed a browser extension / LLM agent that auto-accepts cookie banners and terms popups on your behalf. (Feels not-agreement, but you did set it up knowing what it does.)

My actual request: propose the definition, show how it classifies each of the ten cases, and tell me where it gives a verdict that strikes you as wrong or leaves you stranded. If the cases push you toward two senses of "agreement" rather than one, go ahead — I suspect the ordinary word is doing both a communicative job (an act of accepting an offer) and a fairness-or-no-surprises job, and those may not always point the same way.

Be explicit about which parts of what you say are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who know this area; don't present a contested view as the consensus. And if your least secure verdict isn't one of my uncertain cases, flag it.
