I am putting together a diagnostic quiz for a reading group on the causal status of higher-level patterns, and I want it to press one concrete question: do symbols—plans, maps, representations, the things we cite when we explain a system's behavior as goal-directed—literally push the physical substrate around? The anchoring image is a careenium: a wheeled hull with no engine, whose only motive force comes from thousands of tiny creatures, simmballs, pushing on its interior walls according to simple local rules. The craft as a whole nevertheless follows a planned route, and we unhesitatingly say it turned left because its map said the path bends. The quiz should sort people by what they say about cases of this kind rather than by which label they already wear, so no question may ask a respondent to identify as a reductionist, emergentist, or anything else. I would like eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete case and two or three forced-choice answers about what does the causing. Possible cases include the careenium following its map, a thermostat switching on a furnace, a glider in Conway's Game of Life, a chess engine moving because its evaluation function returned a particular number, and a careenium whose map misrepresents the terrain yet still steers it into a ditch. Please also include at least one case in which two different higher-level descriptions make the same predictions, and at least one case involving a probabilistic higher-level regularity.

The main deliverable is the scoring key. Build it out of the logical space of answers rather than starting from a list of position names: first the branching claims or case judgments, then the occupied leaves. The leaves I want located are, in plain language: (1) the symbolic level is a causally inert summary and all real causation is bottom-up; (2) the symbolic level picks out a real pattern and can be causal without adding a further force over and above the substrate; (3) the symbolic and substrate levels are two descriptions of one and the same events, so the question dissolves; (4) the two levels are genuine and distinct causes, so the effect is overdetermined; and (5) the higher level exerts a downward influence not exhausted by the simmballs' pushings. For any combination of answer patterns with no leaf, say briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of positions the quiz cannot separate, and specify what further case would split them. Throughout, define every leaf by its pattern of case judgments; where a standard label exists you may mention it, but do not make the quiz depend on attributing a view to any named author.
