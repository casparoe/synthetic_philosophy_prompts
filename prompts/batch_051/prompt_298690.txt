In a reading group on early Greek metaphysics I reconstructed an argument from the Eleatic tradition associated with Parmenides, and it now looks to me like a chain of reasoning I cannot escape. Each step seems right on its own, yet the conclusion proves too much: it does not merely overturn common sense, but appears to destroy the very resources needed to state it. Here is the argument.

(1) Whatever can be thought or said is something that is.
(2) What is not, in the strict sense — non-being itself — is nothing at all; so a thought or utterance directed at what is not would be directed at nothing, which is no thought or utterance at all.
(3) Therefore what is not cannot be thought or said; every genuine thought or assertion is of what is.
(4) Coming to be would be a passage from what is not to what is, and ceasing to be would be a passage from what is to what is not.
(5) From (3) and (4), coming to be and ceasing to be are impossible; and since every change requires something to come to be or cease to be, all change is impossible.
(6) Plurality is impossible as well: to say that the cup is not the table, or that one thing is not another, is to say or think what is not, which by (3) cannot be done.
(7) So nothing comes to be, passes away, changes, or is distinct from anything else. Reality is one, changeless, and undifferentiated; the plurality and change we seem to experience are entirely unreal.

I am loath to accept the conclusion, partly because it contradicts experience and partly because the act of stating (7) seems to draw distinctions — say, between appearance and reality — that (7) declares impossible. I should most like to keep (1), which sounds like an innocent truism about thought and its objects, and I am not willing to surrender the reality of change.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I want more than a vote on which premise to discard. If more than one step could be rejected, say which rejection you would defend as least costly, and explain what the alternatives cost.
