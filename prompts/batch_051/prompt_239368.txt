I write about the science and philosophy of moral judgment for a general audience, and I am working on a piece about whether disgust and the sense of the sacred mark out a distinct category of moral wrongness or whether they are ultimately roundabout ways of talking about harm. One of the researchers I have been covering for years keeps a running file of vignettes and gives what she calls provisional verdicts rather than settled theory. Her verdicts on the cases I have collected so far are as follows.

1. Publicly mocking a ritual that a religious minority treats as sacred. She judged it morally wrong, and in our interview she emphasized the anguish and humiliation it predictably inflicted on the minority rather than the fact that outsiders found the mockery profane.

2. A caterer secretly serves food that violates customers' religious dietary commitments and mislabels the menu so that they eat it unknowingly. She judged it clearly wrong, and said the wrongness lay in the deception and in the distress caused when some customers learned what they had eaten.

3. A man privately eats a small amount of tissue removed from his own body during a routine operation, harming no one and affecting no one else. She judged it not morally wrong, calling it viscerally repugnant but victimless.

4. A woman privately burns her own passport as a personal act of renouncing political membership, without deceiving or endangering anyone. She judged it not morally wrong; a passport, she said, is a document, not a sacred object.

5. A man mails a defaced copy of a sacred text to a member of that faith, with no accompanying note but intending the recipient to be wounded by seeing it. She judged it morally wrong, and said the cruelty consisted in using the object as a weapon against a particular person.

Now consider a new case about which I cannot find a written or interview verdict. A conservator is commissioned by a small religious community to repair an icon the community venerates. Working alone at night in his workshop, he carves obscenities into the back of the icon in a place that will never be visible once the work is finished, then seals the surface so that no one will ever know. The icon is returned to the community in perfect working order, and the community's veneration continues as before; no member is ever aware of the carving and no one is deprived of anything they valued.

How would you predict she would judge this new case—wrong, not wrong, or somewhere in between—and with what degree of confidence? Then consider these follow-ups. What rule best accounts for all of her earlier verdicts, which alternative rules fit them nearly as well, and where do those rules diverge on the new case? Finally, propose one additional case or experiment that would most decisively distinguish the two or three most plausible rules, and state what each rule predicts for it. Please write your answer in American English.
