After I retired from geochemistry I started reading philosophy, and Harry Frankfurt's little essay about bullshit has been stuck in my head ever since. The part I keep chewing on is that a liar is still answerable to the truth — he has to know it to avoid it — but the bullshitter isn't: he's just trying to produce a certain impression and doesn't care whether what he says matches how things are. Lately I've been trying to fit this onto LLM "hallucinations," those fluent falsehoods generative models produce. A friend from the old lab — ice cores, so he's used to calling a bad fit to the data a bad fit — insists its a category error to call a model's hallucination "bullshit": a model has no intentions and no self to present, so it's just an error, a failure of accuracy. I keep pushing back that this is too quick, because the system is *built* to keep generating plausible text regardless of whether it's true, and that looks a lot like the indifference to truth Frankfurt described, except the indifference sits in the training objective and reward signal rather than inside the model's head.

I'd like you to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two camps — the Error Camp and the Bullshit Camp. They have been arguing past each other for months, so the deliverable is a document both could put their names to, not a verdict on who is right. Since they partly share ground (neither thinks the model is lying), the statement should: give claims both can sign, phrased so they aren't vacuous; state the residual disagreement precisely, with wording each side would accept for its own view; separate any part of the fight that is merely verbal; identify the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; and propose one concrete case or test both agree bears on the crux, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Format the whole thing as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion. For example, two agreed claims, one precise disagreement (or one verbal-dispute note if that's what it is), one crux, and one test-with-predictions — or whatever split you think covers the job.
