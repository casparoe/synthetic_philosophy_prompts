Me and a couple of friends run a small intentional community — not a religious thing, mostly just people trying to live below their means on purpose. We have a one-page set of bylaws, and one clause says: "Members shall keep their possessions few and their expenses small, and shall not permit their manner of living to become a source of income for them, nor a badge of distinction among members." When we adopted it back around 2014 we were mainly thinking about members flipping thrift-store finds on ebay, or getting smug at shared meals about who owned less.

One member, Dana, started blogging and then youtubing about living out of a 40-liter bag — mending clothes, cooking lentils, all that. She now has a couple hundred thousand subscribers and makes more from it than she ever did at her day job: ad revenue, sponsorships from companies that sell expensive "buy it for life" backpacks and travel gear, a paid newsletter, an online course on decluttering. She still owns less than any of us and lives on something like $14,000 a year, and she gives most of the income away to an environmental nonprofit. But she's also become a recognizable figure in simple-living circles and gets stopped at meetups. Priya thinks the clause doesn't reach her, since she's earning from writing and teaching about the lifestyle, not from the lifestyle itself. That sounds like a loophole to me, but I can't quite say why.

Does the clause, read literally, forbid what Dana's doing? Does it forbid it read in light of why we wrote it? What would we likely have said in 2014 if someone had asked us about a member running a monetized youtube channel? What would a reasonable person joining the community now, reading the clause cold, take it to mean? If those readings pull apart, which should govern? And does the fact that she gives the money away rather then spending it on herself settle the question, or is the real issue the income, the fame, or the way the two feed each other?
