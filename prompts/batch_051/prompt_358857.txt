In my seminar on the philosophy of photography, we have been working through the transparency debate: the question whether photographs, unlike handmade pictures, allow us literally to see the objects they depict, rather than merely to see representations of them. I want to test the criteria we have been floating against a case that seems to sit exactly on the boundary, and I am not sure what the honest answer is.

Let me state the working criteria for a picture's being transparent in the relevant sense. I state them as a package rather than as a definition I am attributing to any one author; if the criteria are wrong, part of what I would like to know is where they go wrong.

(C1) Counterfactual tracking. The picture-forming process is such that, had the relevant visible features of the scene been different, the corresponding features of the picture would have been different.

(C2) Belief-independence. At the formative stage, the content of the picture is not selected or adjusted by anyone's beliefs about how the scene looks; no human judgment intervenes between the scene and the registration of the image.

(C3) Particular-object contact. What the viewer sees through the picture is a particular thing or event that existed and was causally active at the time the picture was formed; the viewer is in contact with that thing itself, not merely with someone's thought about it.

Now the borderline case: a low-light photograph produced by a modern phone's night mode. As I understand the pipeline, the phone records a burst of ten to fifteen short exposures over about a second, each individually too noisy to use. Software aligns the frames, merges them to accumulate light, and applies a learned reconstruction model trained on large collections of nocturnal scenes. In the resulting image, some detail appears in no single exposure — a faint star, a distant sign — because it only emerges when several frames are pooled and the model rejects noise. Some edges and textures are re-synthesized by the model, and in flat regions the pixel values are better described as statistically plausible guesses about a night scene than as recorded light from that scene. The scene itself existed during the exposures; the photographer chose framing and moment, but nobody made an intervening judgment about how any region of the image should look.

The question is whether someone looking at this image sees the photographed street, star, or sign through the photograph, in the sense the transparency debate cares about — or whether the computational stage has broken the relevant chain, leaving something closer to a picture assembled from optical evidence the way a painter assembles a picture from perception and memory.

Please work criterion by criterion. For each criterion, say whether the case satisfies it, violates it, or falls into a gap the criterion does not settle; where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, say which residual considerations you would weight. Then give an overall verdict — transparent, not transparent, or an argument that the criteria themselves need revision — and, if the criteria need revision, say what sort of revision the case pushes toward. Keep the response under 800 words.
