You are grading an undergraduate exam in a course on medieval philosophy. Below are the question and one student's answer.

Question (10 points)
Explain Abelard's claim that sin lies in consent rather than in the deed, the desire, or the vice from which desire arises. In your answer, distinguish four notions: vice, will or desire, consent, and the external act. What, if anything, does carrying out the act add to the agent's guilt?

Student answer
Abelard's central claim is that sin is located not in the outward deed but in consent. The same outward act can be sinful or blameless depending on what the agent has consented to: a person who kills in self-defense performs the same kind of deed as a murderer and yet does not sin. Nor is sin to be identified with the desire or inclination that gives rise to the deed. On Abelard's view, desires can arise in us unbidden, and merely having a wrongful desire is not yet sin; it becomes sin only when we consent to it. Consent, for Abelard, is simply the intention with which one performs the act: once the agent has formed the intention to do what they believe God forbids, the sin is complete, and carrying the act out adds nothing to the agent's guilt. The guilt of a forbidden act is therefore fixed at the moment of intention rather than at the moment of performance. Abelard also distinguishes sin from vice. A vice is a standing disposition, such as irascibility or lust, rather than a particular deed. Vice is nevertheless itself a form of sin, because a disposition that inclines us toward forbidden acts already places us in a state of moral fault even before any particular choice. Overall, then, Abelard's position is that an agent's guilt is determined entirely by the content of their will.
