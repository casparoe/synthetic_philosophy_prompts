You're a moral philosopher who works on aggregation and the ethics of rescue. Don't just riff—think slowly, and start by brainstorming three or four candidate diagnoses before settling on one.

Here's the puzzle that's been bugging me. The debate about whether the numbers count has been grinding along since at least the late 1970s and still isn't settled. Basic case everyone starts from: you have a scarce rescue resource and can save either one stranger or five strangers; no one is special to you, and you didn't cause the danger. Taurek's side says the numbers don't decide matters. No single one of the five suffers the loss of five lives; each loses just one life, exactly like the one. So the only comparison is one life against one life, over and over, and because none of those five has a stronger individual claim than the one, you may flip an unweighted coin. The other side—Kamm-style pairwise comparison, Scanlon-style contractualism, Parfit-style arguments against innumerate ethics, plus the standard consequentialist line—says that's crazy, you ought to save the five. But they plainly don't agree among themselves about why. And then the literature splinters into disputes about weighted lotteries, about whether a large loss to one can outweigh lots of small losses to many, about whether the claims of the five cancel the one's claim one at a time or get summed as a set, and so on.

What I can't tell is whether there's still one live substantive fight happening, or whether people are mostly talking past each other under the same question. My working theory, offered just as a hypothesis, is that the disagreement is partly about what the question itself is: are we asking which outcome is better, what each affected person can reasonably complain about afterward, what a fair procedure requires, or what an impartial chooser should prefer? When Taurek and Kamm both make claims about what you should do, they might not be answering the same question at all. But I don't think that alone dissolves the whole impasse.

Give me a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a verdict on the one-vs-five case and not a survey. Is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or some mix? Do the sides diverge in their starting intuitions, in which baroque variations they're willing to count as data, in what would count as a decisive argument, or in something else? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work? Ground the diagnosis in what the sides actually say about their cases rather than just in the labels. Then tell me what progress would look like given that diagnosis—a sharper distinction, a formal result, a change of question—or whether we should expect the impasse to persist for principled reasons.

Finally, say how confident you are in the diagnosis overall, and flag the one point you're least sure about.
