I'm taking a philosophy of criminal law seminar this term, and we keep skipping the part that's actually keeping me up at night: statutes of limitations. People in class treat them like a scheduling technicality, but they encode a real moral bet -- that the passage of time can erase, or at least dull, a wrong -- and I dont have a worked-out view of when that bet is right. All I have is gut verdicts on cases, and the more I list them the less coherent they look.

A man is identified by new DNA evidence as the killer in a 34-year-old murder, he's 72 now and has been an unremarkable neighbor ever since: I still think he should be tried. An employee embezzled forty thousand dollars from a small family business 18 years ago and was never caught; he's 60 now and by all accounts has lived a blameless life, and the theft only surfaced through old records: I think he owes every dollar back, but I'd find prison pointless. A summer-camp counselor sexually abused a kid in 1987, and the person only told anyone in 2020; there's a possible charge and a civil claim: I think both should still be open. I loaned a friend six hundred bucks in 2011 and he never paid; at this point I think I should let it go, and bringing it up would feel petty. A family's painting was looted in 1939, bought in good faith by a museum in 1955, and located by the heirs in 2023: I think the heirs should get it back, 85 years notwithstanding. A factory dumped solvents into a river in 1974, legal at the time, and the stuff only reached the drinking water and showed up as cancer clusters in the 2010s: I think the victims can still sue. In 1919 soldiers massacred a village; no one who did it is alive; the state is being asked to acknowledge it and compensate descendants. I'm sure there should be no prosecutions, but I'm genuinely 60/40 on whether compensation is owed now.

So: is there one principle about the moral relevance of time that gets all of these, or almost all, and which of my verdicts do I have to drop for the cleanest version? The pair that bothers me most is the embezzlement and the abuse: both were covered up for a long time, but in one I'm inclined to let time absorb the punishment while in the other I'm not; I can't tell whether that's about severity, about the kind of wrong, or about who had the power to speak up. Also tell me what the principle predicts for the case I keep going back and forth on: my spouse cheated once in 2011 and only told me this year -- does the time passed count in their favor, and by how much, in deciding whether to forgive and stay? And I want to know whether time does the same sort of work for punishment, restitution, apology, and forgiveness, or whether they answer to different prinicples.

- verdicts: an array of {case_id, case, my_verdict, my_confidence}
