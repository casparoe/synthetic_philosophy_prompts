I keep seeing this move in arguments about historical linguistics where someone says that asking when Latin became Italian is a confused question, its exactly like asking when one species became another. A friend of mine put it stronger: there's no fact about when a hominin lineage first counts as Homo sapiens, so there's no fact about when Latin stopped being Latin—the boundary is just retrospective bookkeeping. I don't buy that the analogy does that much work. Lay out what maps onto what between a language changing and splitting and a species lineage evolving and speciating (populations, reproduction versus learning, isolation, traits, branching), where the mapping breaks down, and which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion that there's no fact of the matter. Does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are in? Or does the language case rest on something else entirely—like the fact that "Italian" is a standardized literary language with a political history—and if so, does that give a different kind of boundarylessness? If there's a better analogy than species, say what it is.
