His line was: the payoff matrix is litterally the same 2x2 table after any agreement, defecting strictly dominates cooperating no matter what the other player does, so a shared intention is just cheap talk layered on top. My line was: a real shared intention changes the menu of actions, not just the beliefs — breaking our joint plan isn't the same act as defecting in a one-shot game, and it carries a cost the original matrix doesn't list.

So here's the version I want run. Take the classic one-shot prisoner's dilemma with T=5, R=3, P=1, S=0. Before choosing, both players form a shared intention to cooperate: each intends that they both cooperate; each knows how the other will do their part and the subplans mesh; and this is all out in the open between them. No external enforcement. Now add this wrinkle: each player bears an internal cost b on any outcome in which they themselves defect — think guilt, loss of self-respect, or the fact that the other can rightly resent them for violating the joint plan. Treat the two moves as honor (cooperate with the shared intention) and break (defect from it, incurring b).

Work through this in order:

(a) With b=0, confirm that defection strictly dominates and we still have a prisoner's dilemma.

(b) With b>0 included in the matrix, write out the new 2x2 payoffs and say for exactly which ranges of b defection still strictly dominates, for which ranges honoring strictly dominates, and what structure the game has in between if there is an interval with no dominant strategy. Give the exact thresholds, including what happens at the boundary cases b=1 and b=2.

(c) Does this vindicate my friend or me? Is putting b into the payoff matrix fair, or does it just redefine the game so cooperation wins by stipulation? Where do an individualist about joint intention (the shared intention is just two interlocking individual attitudes) and a joint-commitment theorist (the shared intention is its own thing) come apart here — over whether b exists, over whether b belongs in the payoffs, or over whether break is a different act at all?

(d) Come back to my friend's exact words, that the payoff matrix stays the same. Is the right reply that the matrix stays the same and the game changes, or that the matrix changes once b is let in? Say which reply is philosophically better and why.
