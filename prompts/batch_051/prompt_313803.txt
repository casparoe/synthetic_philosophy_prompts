For the past couple of weeks I've been reading about logical uncertainty and logical induction on and off, and I've reached the point where I can repeat the jargon but I can't say what problem is actually being solved, or whether the solution is fixing anything real. The way I currently understand it, Bayesian reasoners are usually modeled as logically omniscient: their credences already respect every logical consequence of what they know, so there is no room for the ordinary experience of discovering that something followed all along from things you already believed. Real reasoners obviously aren't like that. The logical induction framework is supposed to fix this by having an algorithm assign probabilities to every sentence in a formal language, like Peano arithmetic, and refine them over time, with the sequence of probabilities judged by something like a market of traders betting on the truth values of sentences. But here's the thing I don't get: why isn't the obvious fix enough? Just let a Bayesian assign ordinary subjective probabilities to mathematical sentences, and condition on the event that a proof or a disproof is discovered. To me that naive patch seems fine, and I can't see what the trader/market machinery adds except formal clothing.

So what I'd like is an intuitive explanation of what the naive patch actually breaks -- whether it's Dutch books, a problem with defining the conditioning event, incoherence across time, or something else -- and what the logical induction criterion is actually buying you over and above that naive patch. Then give me your honest verdict: is the apparatus solving a genuine problem in the foundations of probabilistic reasoning, or is it mostly an artifact of starting from an over-idealized Bayesian picture? I know basic probability, but I want the gut-level version, with only as much formalism as you need to make the point.
