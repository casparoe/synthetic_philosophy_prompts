Last week in my philosophy of logic seminar we read the Carroll tortoise thing, and I walked out convinced that the whole project of justifying deduction is doomed. My friend says I've made some elementary mistake but refuses to say which one, so I want a second opinion. Quick setup in case you haven't read it recently: the tortoise accepts the premises of a valid argument but won't accept the conclusion, and when Achilles adds the new premise that if the premises are true then the conclusion must be true, the tortoise accepts that too but still won't move, and then they just keep going forever.

Here's the argument I gave my friend, cleaned up a bit:

(1) When you infer Q from P and if P then Q, your move is only justified if you're justified in accepting modus ponens.
(2) The only two options for justifying modus ponens are to give an argument for it, or to take it as an unargued primitive.
(3) Any argument for modus ponens will itself be a piece of reasoning, so it uses some inference rule or other.
(4) If that rule is modus ponens again, the justification is circular; if it's a different rule, we can run the exact same challenge on that rule instead.
(5) Taking a rule as a primitive isn't a justification, it's just drawing a line somewhere and refusing to answer.
(6) So modus ponens cannot be justified without circularity or an arbitrary stopping point.
(7) Therefore no deductive inference is ever justified, and deduction is in exactly as bad a shape as induction.

I'm pretty confident about (1) through (4). My friend says the mistake is somewhere around (1) or (5), but he won't spell it out, which makes me suspect he just doesn't like the conclusion. Is the argument valid, and if not, which numbered step fails and what would the corrected version of it say? Don't spare me: if the conclusion is right and my friend is just squeamish about it, say so.
