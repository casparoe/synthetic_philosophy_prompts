I got stuck in the pub after our language-sciences seminar with two coursemates, Mari and Dev, who spent the whole evening arguing about whether the English writing system is just broken. I mostly listened, because every time one of them landed a point, the other came back with something that made me wobble. Here's roughly how it went.

Mari: "An alphabet is a device for turning sounds into visible marks and back. English fails at exactly that job. though, tough, through and cough all end in the same four letters, but none of them rhymes with any of the others: tho, tuff, throo, coff. 'Colonel' is pronounced kernel. That isn't a theory of the language. It's a pile-up of old accidents."

Dev: "Only if a script's one job is phonetic transcription. English spelling does encode pronunciation, but it also encodes whether two words share a morpheme. 'Sign' has a silent g, but in 'signal' or 'signature' that g shows up: the spelling is telling you this is one word-family. The past-tense -ed in walked, danced, wanted is one spelling pronounced three ways, and it hands you the morpheme directly. Maybe the writing system is a theory of which words count as the same lexical item, not of the surface speech stream."

Mari: "That's backwards. You can't sound out 'sign' unless you already know the word; learners and second-language speakers have to memorise whole cases or guess. Spanish or Finnish children get transparent spelling and start reading faster. What you're calling lexical information is just an extra puzzle for anyone who doesn't already possess that information."

Dev: "You're mixing up learning to read with judging the design. Even fluent Spanish readers don't sound out letter-by-letter for long. And English isn't messy in the way you think: 'sign' is systematic, because the g is silent word-final but pronounced in 'signal'. A purely phonemic spelling would write 'sane' and 'sanity' so differently that the visible relation between them would vanish. English has opted to prioritise lexical identity over one-to-one sound."

Mari: "Then write Chinese. A morphosyllabary does lexical identity honestly. English keeps the alphabet but breaks the alphabetic bargain, so you get the worst of both: a huge memorisation load plus less information per character than hanzi. And some of it doesn't preserve morphemes at all: 'debt' has its b because scribes wanted it to look Latin, not because any modern word-family needs it."

Dev: "Fine, debt is clutter. But your Chinese point shows scripts can optimise for different levels of structure, and Arabic works while barely writing short vowels at all. Hangul even encodes articulatory features in the letter shapes. A script is a theory of what speakers should attend to. English's answer happens to be: attend to morphemes and word identity, not just pronunciation. I call that a feature, not a bug."

I've been turning it over since, and I still can't tell which claims survive scrutiny. Could you step in and adjudicate point by point? Act as a phonologist or philosopher of language who thinks about orthography. Start by laying out, neutrally, the actual claims each side is making (especially what each assumes an alphabet is for), put the strongest version of each point, then rule on the specific moves. In particular: is Mari right that the alphabetic principle fixes transparency as the standard? Is Dev right that English's morphophonemic spellings are a feature rather than a bug? Does either conflate ease of learning with quality of design? And what does their argument look like if you ran it on Spanish, Arabic, or Hangul? Please write your answer in British English, and don't just split the difference unless the truth really is a split.
