In debates in my department about reduction and emergence, a colleague keeps citing a passage from John Stuart Mill's A System of Logic, Book III, chapter VI, "Of the Composition of Causes," where Mill distinguishes mechanical cases in which causes compose additively from chemical cases in which the compound has properties that are not the sum of the properties of its components. My colleague's move is to say that living systems are like Mill's chemical compounds, not like Mill's mechanical cases: protein folding, metabolic networks, and neural dynamics produce effects that are not additive conjunctions of the parts' separate tendencies. From this he concludes that biology is autonomous from physics and chemistry in a way that makes strong reductionism false, or at least methodologically idle.

I want an audit of that analogy, not a general survey of emergence. What in the chemical case maps onto what in the biological case? Where does the analogy break? Which features of Mill's distinction quietly get imported into biology that may not carry over—for example, the idea that the "elements" are already well-characterized substances, or that the non-additive effect is a static compound property rather than a dynamically maintained pattern? Does the conclusion about autonomy survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Please give a committed verdict, with the mapping and disanalogies made explicit.
