Here's a thing that bugs me about how we talk about autonomous systems. If a human driver looks down at her phone for three seconds and runs over a pedestrian, everyone agrees she's blameworthy—maybe criminal. If a car with a Level 3-ish system misses the same pedestrian because its vision module was trained badly and the driver was told she could rely on it, the blame gets spread out: the company, the vendor, the regulators, the driver maybe a little. Both cases have a human who wasnt watching and a defect in an information-processing system. Why do we treat the human-as-system failure as a moral failing and the human-plus-automation failure as an engineering problem? Is that asymmetry defensible or just something we accept because it feels easier?

I've been trying to think about it through Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, book III, where he sorts out voluntary and involuntary action. His line is that an act is voluntary when the moving principle is inside the agent and the agent knows the particulars of what he is doing; praise and blame stick only to the voluntary. If we use that test on an autonomous car, the moving principle for the bad classification is the code, not the driver's decision, and the driver doesn't know the particulars of the misclassification. But the designers also didn't know the particular pedestrian; they wrote a general policy. So by Aristotle's test, is anyone voluntary in the crash? If not, does that mean no one should be blamed, or does it mean Aristotle's picture can't handle distributed agency? And if we decide the driver is on the hook because she chose to trust the system, should we blame nurses and doctors who rely on diagnostic AI the same way, or is there something special about driving that makes the asymmetry okay?
