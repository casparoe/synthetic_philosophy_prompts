i have a theory i keep using and want checked. i call it 'semantic quarantine'. The claim is that when a discipline coins a term from Latin or Greek rather than pressing an ordinary word into service, the real work the dead root is doing isnt precision or internationalism, though people always say it is. Its real job is to quarantine the new concept from the associations a living word would keep dragging in. A living word comes with neighbours: collocations, metaphors, old uses. So a term like 'affordance' keeps leaking — everyone hears 'what the thing affords me', and Gibson's relational property quietly gets replaced by something more commonsensical. 'Spillover' in economics never quite shakes the image of liquid sloshing over the edge of a container, which is partly why the discipline also reaches for 'externality': the Latin form is sterile, so the definition can be imposed once and left alone. Dead-language coinages are etymological clean rooms.

I know that sounds a bit grand. What i want is an honest assessment. Is this a real phenomenon or just a metaphor with a lab coat on? Has it already been worked out under another name (not asking for citations, just whether the idea is out there and what it's called)? And where does it break — dead-language terms that did leak, living-word terms that stayed perfectly technical, whole disciplines that manage fine with borrowed ordinary words? Give me the three strongest objections and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than just uncomfortable.
