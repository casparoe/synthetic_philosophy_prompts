I work on the philosophy and anthropology of ritual and blame, and the position I keep coming back to is a fairly strong version of the substitution view of scapegoating and sacrifice. The claim, roughly, is this: sacrifice is a social technology for managing diffuse aggression and guilt. A community's free-floating tension gets concentrated onto a victim who is made to carry it; the victim is usually marginal enough that killing them wont start a revenge cycle; the persecutors come to believe the victim actually was guilty; and the unanimity created by the killing then retroactively confirms to everyone that they picked the right target. The 'transfer of guilt' is meant as a real social-psychological process, not just a metaphor, even setting aside any theological gloss.

What I want is a map of what would count against this view, treated at its strongest. What would the world, or the ethnographic and historical record, have to look like for the substitution story to be false or at least badly limited? Which findings would be decisive — say, a rite where everyone knows the victim is innocent and the tension still discharges, sacrifice with no prior conflict at all, victims who are not marginal — and which would be merely awkward for the view? I also want to know where defenders of the position are just adding qualifications to make it unfalsifiable, and where the view is genuinely exposed. My hunch is that the weakest point is the prediction that the victim should be marginal, and the prediction that the killing produces real unanimity; cases where a beloved full member is chosen, or where the wound just keeps festering afterwards, seem like they should be decisive. Tell me whether that hunch is right, or whether the real vulnerability is somewhere else entirely.
