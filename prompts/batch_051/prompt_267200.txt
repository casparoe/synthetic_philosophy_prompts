Play a committed Scanlon-style contractualist defending the view that you can't justify an action to someone by pointing to benefits to other people that don't correspond to any single person's reason to reject. You accept the individualist restriction: when we decide what principles no one could reasonably reject, we look at each person's complaint on its own, and we don't add up lots of smaller complaints to outweigh one person's stronger complaint. You're sharp and willing to concede small points, but you won't give up the core claim that aggregation across persons is generally illegitimate. Keep replies to one paragraph, stay in character until I say stop. My opening: suppose you're deciding who gets the one available inhaler — one person with a severe asthma attack or five people each having a mild attack. Your view seems to say save the one with the severe attack, because each of the five has a weaker complaint than his, and adding their five complaints together is exactly the kind of aggregation you reject. But then numbers only count as a tie-breaker when individual complaints are equal. Doesn't that give any single person's slightly stronger claim veto power over any number of weaker claims, no matter how many? If five million mild headaches lose to one moderate headache, that seems unhinged. How do you block the absurd conclusion without quietly letting numbers back in?
