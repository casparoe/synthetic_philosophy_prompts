I'm a PhD student working on emotion and fiction, and the paradox of fiction feels like a debate nobody ever gets out of. The setup is three claims that cant all be true: (i) people have genuine emotional responses to fictional events — crying at a film, flinching at a horror scene; (ii) a genuine emotion normally requires belief that its object exists and has the relevant properties; (iii) audiences dont believe fictional events are real. The camps have been stable for decades: the make-believe/quasi-emotion side (Walton-style) denies (i) and says the tears are imaginative play; the thought-theory side denies (ii), since we can be genuinely scared or sad about mere thoughts; and a third side says the puzzle is ill-posed because it runs together a startle at a slasher, being moved by a tragedy, and a fictional romance. My hunch is that the real disagreement is upstream of fiction: the camps don't share a theory of what an emotion is — a feeling, an appraisal, a bodily change, an action tendency — and fictional cases just put that split on display. I want a diagnosis of the stalemate rather than a survey of the positions: is it verbal, is it a substantive dispute about the nature of emotion, or is it a dispute about which cases get to count as data — or some combination? And what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a constraint on a theory of emotion, an experiment, a new question? Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no conclusion.
