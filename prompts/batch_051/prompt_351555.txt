I spent most of my career as an ecologist, first on ant–acacia and ant–plant mutualisms and later on colonial marine invertebrates, and I've only started reading philosophy of biology in the last couple of years. When I read the debates about biological individuality — whether a holobiont is one organism, whether an aspen clone is a single individual, whether a termite colony is a superorganism — I keep reaching for tools from my old training: replicators and vehicles, levels of selection, inclusive fitness, germ–soma separation, bottlenecks, dispersal stages, life cycles. I'm fully fluent in that vocabulary but I never had the philosophical one.

What I'd like is for you to take the main philosophical positions in that individuality debate and restate them, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary of population genetics and evolutionary ecology. For each position, say what translates cleanly into something my field already says, what had to be bent to fit, and what has no counterpart at all in my vocabulary. Then give me the residue report: is the stuff I can't translate a sign that the philosopher's 'organism' does real work my framework is blind to, or is the debate partly three seperate questions (a functional-integration question, a unit-of-selection question, and a lineage/reproduction question) that share a word and keep slipping past each other? My half-formed suspicion is that 'organism' is doing all three jobs at once and the holobiont and superorganism cases are exactly where they come apart, but I'm not sure whether that's a substantive point or just a taxonomical tidy-up. Don't worry about citations; I'll add them.
