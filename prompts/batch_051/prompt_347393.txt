A grad-school friend laid out an argument last week and it has been eating at me. The conclusion is that conciliationism about peer disagreement — the view that when I learn an epistemic peer disagrees with me I should significantly lower my confidence — is false, because it leads to self-defeat and then to suspension of judgment about everything controversial. I can't see what's wrong. Here is the argument as I've reconstructed it.

(1) An epistemic peer is someone at least as smart, informed, and conscientious about the question as I am. (2) I believe p on the basis of evidence E, and I then discover that a peer, on the basis of the same E, believes not-p. (3) The fact that they believe not-p is exactly as likely under the hypothesis "I got it right and they made a mistake" as under "they got it right and I made a mistake"; as higher-order evidence, the disagreement is symmetric between us. (4) Since that higher-order evidence does not favor either error hypothesis, I should not remain more confident in my conclusion than in theirs. (5) So I should split the difference and suspend judgment, or at least drop to something near 50/50. (6) For almost every controversial belief I hold — free will, moral realism, macroeconomics, religion — there is an actual peer who disagrees, or if there isn't, I can easily construct an apparent one who would, given the same evidence. (7) Therefore, by (3)-(5), I should suspend judgment on nearly all controversial matters. (8) But the proposition "I should suspend judgment on nearly all controversial matters" is itself controversial, and there are peers who reject it; so by the same reasoning I should suspend judgment about it too. (9) A policy that tells me to suspend judgment on itself cannot be a policy I'm rationally required to follow; a self-undermining epistemic norm is no norm at all. (10) Therefore conciliationism is false.

I can't tell weather the problem is in the peer construction in (6), in the move from symmetric higher-order evidence to suspension in (4)-(5), in the self-defeat step in (8)-(9), or whether the argument is valid and the real question is just which plausible premise is cheapest to lose. One thing: I'd really like to keep (9), but I want to know what keeping it costs. So tell me which step I should reject and what rejecting it commits me to, or say the argument is valid and price out the cheapest premise to throw overboard.

At the very end, give me a one-sentence bottom line starting with "Verdict:" that names the step(s) to reject (or says the argument is valid) and the main cost.
