Here are some gut verdicts I have about naked statistical evidence, and I'm trying to figure out if there's a single prinicple behind them. A pedestrian is hit by a bus, and it's known that Blue Bus Co. ran 85% of the buses on that route and Red Bus Co. ran 15%, with no other evidence; I don't think the plaintiff should win against Blue Bus, even though the civil standard is just more likely than not. A rodeo promoter knows 600 of 1,000 attendees crashed the gate and sues a randomly selected attendee for the ticket price; I don't think he should win either. A guard is killed in a prison yard while 99 of 100 prisoners attack him; I wouldn't convict any one prisoner beyond reasonable doubt. But an eyewitness who is right 85% of the time about bus colors at dusk says the bus was blue, and that feels like enough to me. And I'd probably convict someone on a DNA match with a random match probability of one in a billion, even though that seems like the prison-yard statistic dressed up in a lab coat. Is there one principle that gets all of these verdicts, or at least most of them? If not, which ones should I give up, and what does the best candidate principle say about cases I havent listed? And is it a problem if the principle ends up tracking what the evidence feels like rather than what it actually shows?
