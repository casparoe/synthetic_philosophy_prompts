I'm a journalist who covers economics and the history of ideas, and I serve on the investment committee of the nonprofit foundation that funds our newsroom. The committee must decide whether to adopt a risk-modeling system proposed by an outside consultant. The system assigns a point probability and a confidence interval to every major market scenario and would require each proposed change in asset allocation to be justified by the model's expected-return ranking. Our finance director opposes it on Keynesian grounds: a numerical probability does not by itself reveal how much evidence stands behind it, and for many of the judgments this committee actually faces—rare political shocks, regulatory reversal, the behavior of other investors during a crisis—there is too little comparable experience for a precise number to be anything more than false precision dressed as measurement. The consultant replies that a disciplined model, even one built substantially on human judgment, beats the unstructured intuitions of a committee that meets four times a year. The committee is genuinely split, and the full board wants a decision next month. Draft the short decision memo I will circulate before that meeting: recommend whether we should adopt the system in full, adopt it only as one input among others with specified limits, or reject it, with your reasoning and a serious engagement with the strongest consideration on the losing side. Keep it under 600 words. The last line should begin "Verdict:" followed by the recommendation in a few words.
