I have been trying to decide whether my fellow citizens have a special claim on me — the kind that would explain why the harm my country's policies do to a neighbor should weigh on me differently than the same harm done to a stranger across a border. In trying to defend that difference, I built an argument that seems to show there is no such thing, and I cannot find the step at which it goes wrong.

If compatriots have special claims on me, it must be in virtue of some feature of the relationship I stand in with them; it cannot simply be that they are more valuable as people. So I went looking for that feature. Shared history? I have a shared history with my elementary-school classmates. Shared language and customs? I share those with hundreds of millions of people across several countries. Fellow-feeling and mutual aid? I feel more of either toward my online gaming group than toward most of my countrymen. Subjection to the same laws? I live under the same laws as long-term non-citizen residents, and I do not think I owe tourists what I owe compatriots. Every candidate I can think of is either present where the special claim seems absent, or absent where the special claim seems present. So:

(1) If compatriots have special claims on me, there is some feature F of my relationship to them in virtue of which they have those claims.

(2) Every candidate F I can think of — shared history, shared language, shared customs, fellow-feeling, mutual aid, living under the same laws — is also present in some relationship where I have no special claims, or absent in some relationship where I do.

(3) A feature that is present without the special claim cannot by itself explain the special claim, and a feature absent among many of my compatriots cannot be required for it.

(4) Therefore there is no F that explains why compatriots have special claims on me.

(5) Therefore compatriots have no special claims on me.

(6) But that conclusion is one I find impossible to accept: it would mean the harm my vote does to the person next door gives me no more reason to act than the harm it does to someone on the other side of the world, and no one actually lives that way.

(7) So one of the premises must be false.

If I am forced to give something up, I would rather give up one of the earlier steps than give up the thought in (6). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
