Me and a friend went in circles about this last night and I still can't find the hole. Picture a three-person hiring committee with a firm rule: offer the job if and only if the candidate is both technically qualified and a good team fit. The votes come out like this:
- Ana: qualified yes, team fit no → no offer
- Bo: qualified no, team fit yes → no offer
- Carmen: qualified yes, team fit yes → offer
So a majority thinks she's qualified, a majority thinks she's a good fit, but a majority votes against offering her the job. My friend argued:
(1) The committee's view on any question is just whatever the majority votes.
(2) So the committee holds that she's qualified, and holds that she's a good fit.
(3) The committee is committed to whatever follows from what it holds; since the rule says both conditions are required, it's committed to offering the job.
(4) But by (1), since a majority voted against the offer, the committee holds that no offer should be made.
(5) So the committee is committed both to offering and to not offering. It contradicts itself.
(6) A single body can't rationally hold contradictory commitments.
(7) So majority rule is an incoherent way to form group judgments, and either groups don't really have views or we should abandon majority voting.
I want to keep (6), and I'd give up majority rule before admitting the committee can be inconsistant, but (2) and (3) also feel like they have to be right. Which step actually breaks, and what do I give up by rejecting it?
