I've spent a lot of time in rationalist-adjacent forecasting circles, where calibration and epistemic status tend to get discussed more than the object-level weather, and I keep tripping over an apparent inconsistency in how the modeling community treats the atmosphere. Operational weather centers run ensemble forecasts: they perturb the initial state slightly and let the differences grow into a spread of possible futures. The motivation is usually stated in terms of chaos—the atmosphere is supposed to be sensitive enough to initial conditions that a proverbial butterfly matters, errors grow roughly exponentially, and deterministic weather prediction has a horizon of roughly a week or two. Yet many of the same institutions turn around and issue probabilistic seasonal outlooks for three to nine months ahead, and climate scientists are willing to make projections decades out. I know the stock reply is that weather prediction is an initial-value problem while seasonal and climate prediction are boundary-value problems—that we can predict statistics even when we can't predict states—but I want to know what theory of unpredictability that reply actually commits us to.

Treat the combination of practices as the object: one community runs short-range ensembles and long-range outlooks on the same chaotic atmosphere. Reconstruct the implicit account of what unpredictable means. Is unpredictability a property of the system itself, a fact about our information and computational limits, or something else? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out, and where is it underdetermined, compatible with several philosophical accounts? Then take a definite position rather than surveying options: if we adopted a purely epistemic view of unpredictability, on which a system is unpredictable only because we don't know its initial state or dynamics well enough, what would operational forecasting have to change, and would the seasonal outlooks still be licensed?
