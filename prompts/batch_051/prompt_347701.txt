I've been rereading Warren Quinn's self-torturer thing and I want to force myself to put a number on it, so do the same. The setup: a guy has 1001 settings on some pain device, from 0 to 1000. Each click up gives him a nice chunk of money and adds so little pain he genuinely can't tell adjacent settings apart. At every single step, taking the next click looks rationally fine, maybe even required. But by the end he's at 1000 and would pay basically all the money to go back to 0. Quinn's claim, as I read him, is that this shows rational preference doesn't have to be transitive: it can be rational to prefer 1 to 0, 2 to 1, and so on all the way up, while strongly preferring 0 to 1000.

I want your actual credence that Quinn is right: that this case shows rational preference, or rational permissibility, can be intransitive. Don't tell me it depends on the decision theory — give me a number and the decomposition. How much rides on whether an imperceptible difference can be a real difference at all? How much on whether rationality governs local pairwise choices or only global plans? How much on whether the whole puzzle dissolves once you add coarse-grained categories like noticeable pain or just-noticeable differences? What's the sub-question your number is most sensitive to, and what actual evidence or argument would move it the most?
