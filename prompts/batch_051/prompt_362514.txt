I'm a second-year undergraduate in a course on the ethics of emotion in ancient philosophy, and I'm starting to outline a term paper on whether the Stoic ideal of apatheia is a defensible picture of emotional health rather than just a recipe for numbness. A study guide a classmate passed around summarizes the Stoic position like this:

"The Stoics held that the good life consists in apatheia, freedom from the passions, which in modern terms is roughly equanimity. Since every emotion is a disturbance of reason caused by false judgments about what is good and bad, the wise person trains herself not to feel emotions at all: she does not get angry at injustice, does not grieve when a child dies, and does not rejoice at good fortune, because she regards everything outside her own character as indifferent. That is why the Stoic sage is often compared to a stone or a corpse. The obvious cost is that a person who has achieved apatheia can no longer love in any recognizable sense, because love is a passion and passion makes one vulnerable. Aristotle already saw this and defended metriopatheia, the moderate experience of emotion, which is why the Stoic alternative is usually treated as an overcorrection."

Something about this feels like a hostile caricature more than a fair statement of the view, but I've only read selections of Epictetus and Seneca and I can't tell which parts are genuinely Stoic doctrine, which are simplifications, and which a careful Stoic would reject. In particular, I have a hazy memory that the Stoics distinguished the passions from some kind of rational good feeling, and that they allowed the sage to be startled or moved without that counting as a passion, but I can't reconstruct the distinction. I'd like you to audit the summary so I can decide how much of it to use in my paper. Please go through it claim by claim, separating what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what a defender of Stoicism would deny outright, and then say whether the concluding verdict that apatheia is an overcorrection survives the corrections. I especially need to know whether the claim that the sage can no longer love in any recognizable sense follows from anything true in the summary, and whether treating apatheia as just equanimity in modern terms is defensible. I don't need citations or a reading list, just the conceptual distinctions. Please keep the whole answer under 800 words.
