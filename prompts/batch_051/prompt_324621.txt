I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind and I am writing the final examination. I want one timed essay question (50 minutes) on self-deception that cannot be answered by restating the familiar paradox or by listing standard positions. The question should be built around the following live tension.

Biased-belief accounts of self-deception remove the air of paradox by treating the self-deceiver not as someone who hides a known truth from herself, but as someone who forms a false belief through motivationally biased evidence-processing. That makes self-deception psychologically unmysterious, but a critic can charge that it collapses self-deception into ordinary wishful thinking and thereby changes the subject. Intentionalist accounts, by contrast, preserve the idea that the self-deceiver does something to herself, but only by positing a split between the deceiving part and the deceived part, which can look like a mere figure of speech or an incoherent multiplication of subjects.

Please write the question, a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain. The question should force students to do more than summarize the two approaches: they must either defend the biased-belief approach against the charge that it changes the subject, or defend the intentionalist approach against the charge that it cannot make sense of a single subject deceiving itself. The marking scheme should reward students who articulate what a process must be like in order to count as deception rather than mere motivated inquiry, and it should penalize answers that merely report both sides without taking and defending a position.
