I've been rereading Bernard Williams's essay The Makropulos Case: Reflections on the Tedium of Immortality and trying to turn his argument into a tighter version for a blog post. The conclusion is preposterous, and I can't see the flaw. Please diagnose it.

(1) Williams claims that an endless human life would become unbearable. His reason is that an individual's categorical desires—the projects and attachments that give her a reason to go on—are finite in number, and with endless time she would eventually exhaust them, after which no further life would be worth living.

(2) If endless life is necessarily unbearable, then no rational human can want to live forever, no matter how good the first few centuries are.

(3) But the argument cannot really depend on endlessness, because the set of categorical desires available to a human agent is finite in size. If the set is finite, then there must be some finite time by which an idealised human would have run through all of them.

(4) So Williams's reasoning seems to imply that a sufficiently long finite life—300 years, say—would also be necessarily unbearable by the end.

(5) That's absurd: plenty of ordinary people at 90 still have categorical desires, and it's at least not inevitable that a 300-year-old would have none.

(6) So either Williams's premise that categorical desires are finite in the relevant sense is false, or his inference from 'you will eventually run out' to 'endless life is unbearable' is too quick, or something else.

Where does this go wrong? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep the claim that immortality would be ghastly and reject the claim that a 300-year life must already be empty, but I'm not sure I can have both.
