I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on the philosophy of fiction, and next week I'm presenting my term paper. I'm defending the thesis that, in the passage from David Hume's "Of the Standard of Taste" (1757) that is usually treated as the earliest diagnosis of imaginative resistance, the weight of the explanation falls on the normative clause rather than the cognitive one. Hume writes that "where the ideas of morality and decency alter from one age to another, and where vicious manners are described, without being marked with the proper characters of blame and disapprobation; this must be allowed to disfigure the poem, and to be a real deformity. I cannot, nor is it proper I should, enter into such sentiments; and however I may excuse the poet, on account of the manners of his age, I never can relish the composition." My claim is that the "cannot" in "I cannot, nor is it proper I should" is not an independent report of a capacity limit; it is the downstream shadow of the "proper" — a reader who judges that entering into the vicious sentiments would be improper withholds imaginative cooperation, and the failure is best analyzed as a refusal governed by that normative judgment, not as a brute inability. I'll argue that this reading explains why the phenomenon is restricted to cases where the author's evaluative stance is itself the problem, and why the resistance dissolves when the fiction marks the vicious manners with the blame they deserve.

My audience makes this tricky. The seminar is mixed: the instructor is a Hume scholar who tends to warn against using "Of the Standard of Taste" as if it were a paper in contemporary philosophy of mind, and who thinks the operative notion in the essay is taste, not imagination; two students come from cognitive psychology and keep pressing for a processing story about why subjects stall; and one philosophy student is sympathetic to what she calls "cantian" accounts, on which imaginative resistance reveals a genuine limit on what we can imagine. I need a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from this room, in the order they are likely to come, phrased the way each person would put them, with a two- or three-minute reply for each, an honest note on where each reply is soft, what I should concede up front rather than be forced into conceding, and the one question I should be praying nobody asks.
