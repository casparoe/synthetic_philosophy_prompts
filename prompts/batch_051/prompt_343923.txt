One of my linguistics professors keeps a running list on the course site of which communication systems she'd count as "a language in the full sense." She never states the rule, she just give her verdicts, and it's become a running game among the undergrads to extrapolate it. So far we have: American Sign Language, yes; a deaf child's homesign invented with just his hearing mother, no; a signed pidgin used by deaf and hearing factory coworkers with no child learners, no; Nicaraguan Sign Language as used by the younger children who made it their first language, yes; a French-based creole with native speakers, yes; Yoruba talking drums, no, with her stated reason that they encode Yoruba rather than having their own grammar; and Esperanto, yes, even though its artificial. Nobody can agree on what single principle fits the whole list.

Next week the class is going to present her with a new case and I have to predict her call. The case is a signing system that hearing educators designed on purpose as an encoding of English — basically every English morpheme, including articles, plurals, and past-tense endings, gets its own sign — and the twist is that a group of deaf children were raised with this system from birth, use it among themselves as their main everyday language, and have started using it for novel jokes and gossip no adult ever taught them. Is she going to count it as a language in the full sense or not?

You're an expert in the philosophy of language and sign-language linguistics. Before you predict, do a short private brainstorm about the two or three most defensible rules that each fit all seven earlier verdicts, and don't include that brainstorm in your answer. Then give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or sign-off: (1) the candidate rules she could be running, (2) what each rule predicts for the new case, (3) your best guess at her verdict and the single closest earlier case, (4) the principle she'd be committing to either way, and (5) a further test case that would settle which rule she's really using.
