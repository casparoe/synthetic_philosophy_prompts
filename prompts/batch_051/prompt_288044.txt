I am a novelist, and I have been asked to present a paper to a university political theory workshop on Einstein’s 1949 Monthly Review essay, Why Socialism? My thesis is that the essay’s case for socialism is best read not as a moral appeal to equality or distributive justice, but as an argument about what capitalism does to the individual’s capacity for social consciousness: Einstein holds that the disorder built into capitalist production is the root problem because it deforms our social impulses, and that a socialist economy is justified chiefly insofar as it removes that deformation and allows individuals to develop social purposes of their own. I will anchor my reading in a passage from the first section of the essay where Einstein argues that social institutions form the individual’s social consciousness and that the core problem is the anarchic structure of capitalist production rather than individual selfishness; my claim is that this passage commits him to a broadly epistemic or developmental critique of capitalism, not a narrowly moral one. The audience is mostly analytic political philosophers, several of them sympathetic to Rawlsian liberal egalitarianism, plus one historian of German socialism and one economist who favors market socialism. Prepare me for the question period: what objections will I actually get from this audience, in their own terms, which ones have real force, what should I concede before they press it, and what is the single reply that must land for my reading to survive? Please order the objections by how likely and how damaging they are, phrase them the way this audience would phrase them, give a short reply to each, note where each reply is weakest, and include the objection I am hoping nobody asks. In your assessment, tell me what argument or consideration, if developed further, would lead to a different verdict about my reading, and where my position is most likely to fail.
