I am a novelist, and after two years of revising a manuscript I have ended up with something I do not know how to describe. Each round of edits was, by itself, an obvious improvement. We tightened a section's pacing, gave a secondary character a clearer motive, cut a subplot that had stopped earning its place, replaced a flat ending with a more surprising one. No single step was contested, and I cannot identify any moment at which we knowingly traded the book's larger aims for a local gain. Yet now that the final draft is complete, I find it worse than the original — thinner, colder, and somehow less the book I set out to write — and two readers I trust reached the same verdict when they compared the two drafts side by side. Had someone shown me both versions at the outset, I would have called the original the better novel without hesitation. But I still cannot locate any step in the sequence at which the manuscript went from better to worse; from inside each edit, the direction felt plainly upward. I have noticed the same shape elsewhere — a screenplay slowly improved into something nobody wanted to make, a collaboration that grew better at every meeting and ended in a place no one preferred.

I know philosophers debate whether a relation like better-than can fail to carry across a long chain of pairwise comparisons, but the examples I half-remember involve minuscule changes along a single underlying scale — warmth, pain, height, money. My cases are not like that. The successive drafts are not arranged along one obvious axis, and the comparisons were made by the same people over months, with our tastes and attachment shifting as the work changed. So my question is whether the thing I keep encountering has a name, and how, exactly, it should be carved up. Is the right diagnosis that better-than is not always transitive, even for something as unified as a novel? Or is this instead a change in my standards over the course of revision, a failure of memory about what the earlier draft was actually like, an effect of anchoring on the familiar original, or a distinct phenomenon with no standard label? I would rather you commit to a definite position on what this is and is not, and defend it, than lay out the available options.
