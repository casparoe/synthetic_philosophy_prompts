I'm in a course on vagueness, and I'm struck by an apparent inconsistency in how legal systems handle vague categories. In some places the law imposes sharp cutoffs: you become a legal adult on your eighteenth birthday, and in many jurisdictions a driver is over the alcohol limit at a blood-alcohol concentration of 0.08 but under it at 0.079. In other places the law uses deliberately vague standards: negligence asks whether the defendant acted as a reasonable person would, and criminal guilt requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt. What theory of vagueness does this mixed practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view, and distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits with — in particular, does a bright-line cutoff require the contentious claim that vague predicates secretly have sharp boundaries, or is it only a stipulation adopted for coordination? Then say whether any part of the practice would have to change if we instead adopted a theory on which vague predicates are genuinely borderline or fuzzy, and note where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.
