I am a novelist, currently writing a book in which the central institution is a large video-hosting platform and a recurring subplot involves its Trust and Safety team adjudicating synthetic-media cases. For the purposes of the novel I have drafted a short clause of the platform's Synthetic Media Policy, and I want its interpretive difficulties to be plausible rather than convenient for the plot. The clause reads: "Any uploaded video or audio that has been generated or materially altered by synthetic media, and that depicts an identifiable natural person speaking, acting, or appearing in a manner in which that person did not in fact speak, act, or appear, shall carry a prominent label identifying the content as synthetic. This obligation applies regardless of the uploader's intent and regardless of whether a reasonable viewer would recognize the content as fiction or satire."

In one scene, a historical institute uploads a film of a deceased statesman delivering a speech. Every sentence of the audio is taken verbatim from the published transcript of an address he genuinely gave, but in a different hall and on a different date; the voice itself has been synthesized from his surviving recordings, and the visuals have been generated by interpolating his photographs with a synthetic face model. The man really did say those words, but he was never filmed standing at that lectern delivering that speech that day. The institute presents the upload as a "restoration," not as a dramatization, and most viewers take it to be a contemporary recording of the event.

What does the clause require in this case, and how do the principal approaches to interpretation—the plain words, the evident purpose, what the drafters would likely say if asked, and what a reasonable reader of the policy would take it to mean—pull apart? I am especially interested in whether a rule keyed to "did not in fact speak, act, or appear" can function as an epistemic backstop against fabricated audiovisual evidence, or whether the case shows that the clause protects something narrower or broader than its language suggests. If the obligation must be amended, what would a revised clause need to say in order to reach this case without collapsing the distinction between a documentary restoration and an ordinary, clearly labeled historical dramatization?
