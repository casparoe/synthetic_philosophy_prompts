The debate over what "physical" means in physicalism has been running for decades without converging, and I want a diagnosis of why it has stalled rather than another definition. The impasse, as I understand it, is this. If "physical" means what current physics says exists, physicalism is almost certainly false, since current physics is incomplete and contains known idealizations and falsehoods; it also makes the thesis hostage to every textbook revision. If "physical" means what a completed, ideal future physics says exists, then either that physics is defined as the science that eventually captures everything (in which case physicalism becomes trivially true, since even a world with fundamental phenomenal properties could in principle be described by a broadened "physics"), or the thesis has no determinate content until inquiry ends. That is Hempel's dilemma. The standard escapes each seem to fail in a characteristic way. Via negativa accounts define "physical" as what is not fundamentally mental, but that swaps one contested term for another and turns physicalism into the bare denial of fundamental mentality. Contrast accounts define "physical" as what can be characterized without mental, normative, or teleological vocabulary, but the excluded vocabulary is itself a moving target. Method-based accounts define "physical" as what the methods of physics track, but that relocates the demarcation problem for science inside the definition. Each move seems to either falsify the thesis, trivialize it, or change the subject — and yet the debate continues with the same arguments recurring under new labels.

What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a survey of definitions. My working hypothesis is this: the debate is not a factual dispute about which definition is correct, and not merely verbal, but a dispute about what job the term "physical" is supposed to do in the physicalist programme — whether it marks an explanatory boundary, expresses a commitment to the methods and authority of physics, denies fundamental mentality, or confers a status of full reality on the things it applies to. The mainstream definitions each optimize for one of these jobs while quietly abandoning another, which is why no definition satisfies everyone. I want that hypothesis tested, and I also want the alternatives taken seriously: that this is a verbal dispute, that a hidden empirical bet about future physics is doing the real work, or that the question "what is the physical?" is simply ill-posed.

Please structure your answer as follows:

1. **The dialectical knot** (~200 words). Restate, as economically as you can, why the disagreement has survived this long — what genuinely forces the dilemma and why each major escape route fails in a way that leaves the debate alive.

2. **The diagnosis** (~450 words). Say what kind of disagreement this really is. Address my job-description hypothesis directly, use the behavior of the debate (the recurring dilemma, the charge of triviality, the charge of changing the subject) as evidence, and say which parts of the disagreement, if any, are verbal, which are substantive, which rest on hidden empirical bets, and which rest on starting intuitions no side can argue for. Where you can, locate the precise point at which the two sides stop disagreeing about facts and start disagreeing about standards.

3. **What would count as progress** (~300 words). Given that diagnosis, specify the concrete move most likely to end or reframe the debate: a distinction that splits the question, a reformulated question, a no-go result showing that no single definition can do all the jobs asked of it, or an argument that convergence should not be expected. Be specific enough that someone could actually do or apply the move, not just a call for more clarity.

4. **Bottom line** (~150 words). Your honest verdict on my hypothesis: which parts survive, which don't, and what you would say to someone about to write another paper defining "physical."
