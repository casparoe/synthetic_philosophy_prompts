I am a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I am preparing an essay on what happens to our talk about fictional characters when the line between fiction and biography becomes blurred. The classic puzzle, as I understand it, runs like this: the claim that Sherlock Holmes was a detective seems true in some sense, even though there is no flesh-and-blood Sherlock Holmes for the name to pick out. The main ways of handling this include treating the name as a disguised description, treating the sentences as true only within the fiction rather than true outright, and allowing the name to refer to an abstract object that the stories create.

Here is the variation I want you to work through. Suppose a newly discovered archive shows that the Holmes stories were in fact a lightly disguised memoir of a real nineteenth-century consulting detective named Sherlock Holmes. Nearly every detail in the stories matches the historical record: the address, the cases, the companion, the mannerisms. But one famous episode — the apparent death of the detective — never happened; the author invented it to give the narrative a dramatic conclusion. I am especially interested in this case because it pulls apart things that usually travel together in discussions of fictional names: the author's intention, the causal history of the name, and what readers were in a position to know. How should the standard analyses change under this variation? For each of the main approaches to fictional names, say what it now predicts about the name itself, about the accurate statements from the stories, and about the invented episode, and then judge whether the variation exposes a genuine weakness in that approach or leaves it essentially unchanged. In particular, does the claim that Sherlock Holmes does not exist become straightforwardly false, and does the invented episode remain true in the fiction even though it is false as biography? Work the analysis out directly; I am not looking for a bibliography or a set of readings, only for the reasoning.
