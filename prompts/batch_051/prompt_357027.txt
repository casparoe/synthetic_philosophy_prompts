I keep coming back to a disagreement I had a while ago, where someone invoked Haidt's moral foundations theory to argue that American liberals are working with a kind of reduced moral vocabularly: they lean heavily on care/harm and fairness/cheating (and maybe liberty/oppression), while conservatives draw on loyalty, authority, and sanctity too. His punchline was that conservative morality is in some sense more "complete," or at least that liberals are missing something real. My friend says this is obviously confused — it moves from a descriptive claim about how people report their moral intuitions to a normative claim about what actually matters, and you can't get an ought from a brain scan or a survey. But I'm not sure that's the whole story, because if the foundations are supposed to be evolved, intuitive modules for navigating social life, then ignoring some of them might be less like having different tastes and more like walking around with one of your senses switched off.

So: write an essay on whether Haidt-style moral psychology gives us any leverage on who is right, or whether it's just interesting anthropology with nothing to say about what we ought to believe. Weigh the main arguments on both sides, but don't end on a non-committal note — defend a verdict, even if it's a hedged one. And if you think the question itself is badly framed — because "who is right" is not something descriptive moral psychology can address, or because Haidt's taxonomy or the liberal/conservative asymmetry isn't solid enough to carry this weight — I'd want you to say that outright rather than politely playing along. Same goes for any empirical claim you end up leaning on: tell me how much confidence we should actually have in it, rather than just asserting it.
