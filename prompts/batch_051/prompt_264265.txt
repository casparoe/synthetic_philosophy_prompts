I'm a retired atmospheric chemist, and after forty years of measuring trace gases I've fallen into philosophy of probability, which may have been a mistake. Next month I'm giving our applied probability seminar, and I want to survive the Q&A.

The position I'm defending is a piece of old-school objective Bayesianism about priors. If all your evidence fixes a few expectations—say you know only the mean of a positive quantity, or the mean and variance of one on the whole real line—then the maximum entropy distribution subject to exactly those constraints is the rational prior. Maxent gives you the exponential for the first case and the Gaussian for the second, and my claim is that any other prior sneaks in information you don't have. My sketch: (1) entropy is the natural measure of how much a distribution asserts beyond the constraints; (2) there's a combinatorial argument that the maxent distribution is the one realized in overwhelming many ways when you spread probability uniformly over the microstates consistent with the constraint; (3) so any other choice amounts to smuggling in a hidden constraint.

The twist I want to defend is that this is evidence-relative, not "one true prior" stuff: the rational prior changes when your constraint set changes, and that's a feature, not a bug. I'm not claiming there's one cosmic prior.

The crowd: about half applied Bayesians who treat priors as modeling tools and don't much care whether they're "rational" in some philosopher's sense. Two physicists who use maxent in inverse problems and mostly agree with me. And one philosopher of probability who keeps muttering about reference priors and invariance under reparameterization. I'm most worried about the applied Bayesians: if I can't say what the rule buys them in terms of actual inference or prediction, I lose the room.

Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this group, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way these people would phrase them. For each one, give a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Make the reply aimed at the applied Bayesians about workability, calibration, or predictive value, not just philosophical hygiene. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
