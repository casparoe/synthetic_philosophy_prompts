I've become fairly convinced by the ergodicity economics line of thinking -- the idea that a lot of standard expected-value and expected-utility reasoning in economics and decision theory systematically asks the wrong question. The picture, as I understand and endorse it, is this. When an individual faces a risky opportunity repeatedly over time, what matters is the time-average growth rate of their wealth along their own single path, not the ensemble average across parallel copies or across a population. The standard coin-flip example: each flip, heads multiplies your wealth by 1.5 and tails by 0.6. The expected value grows by 5% per flip, so by ensemble reasoning this looks like a great bet; but the time-average growth rate is the average of the logs, log(1.5) + log(0.6) over two, which is half of log(0.9), which is negative, so any individual who plays this long enough almost surely gets ruined. I find it striking that this isn't a psychological bias story -- it's a mathematical fact about non-ergodic dynamics. It also seems to dissolve a bunch of old puzzles (why people don't maximize expected value, the St. Petersburg paradox, insurance, the equity premium) by saying that people are implicitly optimizing time-average growth rather than ensemble expectation, and that they're often right to do so.

Here's what I want. I want you to play it straight with me and map out what would count against this position. Not just the obvious objections, but the full structure of its exposure. What would have to be true -- formally, empirically, or conceptually -- for the time-average-vs-ensemble-average distinction to turn out to be less important than I take it to be? Which results in decision theory, economics, or mathematics would be fatal, and which would be merely awkward? For example, I can already feel some pressure points: is maximizing time-average growth rate just expected utility with log utility in disguise, and if it is, does that make the whole framework less revolutionary than its defenders claim? Does the view have trouble with one-shot choices, or with people who care about their descendants or about aggregate welfare rather than their own lifetime wealth path? Does the framework require the "wealth process" and the time horizon to be specified so flexibly that it can't be falsified by any observed risk preference? And are there actual empirical studies or experiments that could discriminate time-average maximization from rival accounts, or has this been insulated by re-describing the dynamics whenever someone's behavior doesn't fit? A committed defender would presumably concede that for genuinely one-shot, additive, or non-multiplicative situations ensemble reasoning is unobjectionable -- I'd like to know how large that concession really is. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me wich ones a committed ergodicity-economics person would accept as counting against them versus dismiss as category errors.

Also, I'd personally be most moved by formal results showing the time-average criterion gives the wrong recomendation in a case where the dynamics are cleanly specified, or by a clean derivation showing it reduces to something I already accept, rather than by armchair complaints. But don't let that bias you too much.
