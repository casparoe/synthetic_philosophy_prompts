Last weekend some friends and I got out a bunch of coins and played a few rounds of a centipede-style game: the pot starts at $2, and on your turn you can either grab the whole pot and end the game, or pass, which adds $2 to the pot and gives the other player the choice; if everyone passes through all six turns, the pot is split evenly. Two of my friends, Elena and Marcus, spent the rest of the evening arguing about the "right" way to play. Marcus said the rational move is to grab the $2 on the very first turn: at turn six the pot is $12, and taking $12 beats passing for a $7 split, so the last player always takes; then at turn five the other player would take $10 rather than pass into getting nothing, and so on back to turn one. He kept saying this isn't a psychological prediction, just what follows once you assume the players are rational and know the game. Elena said that's a reductio of the assumptions, not a usable guide to playing. Her point was that the argument only works if, at each step, you assume the other player will keep doing the grim backward-induction thing—but the argument's own conclusion is that the game never gets past turn one, so all those later steps are reasoning about a future the theory says won't occur. She said if someone actually passed on turn one, you'd have learned something that contradicts the model of the player that produced the prediction, and you should update and start thinking they might pass at turn six too.

Marcus came back that off-path play doesn't refute the logic: a strategy tells you what to do at every node, including nodes you never expect to reach, and the last node matters precisely because it pins down the one before it. He also said Elena's "we'd both get $7 if we kept passing" isn't actually available, since getting to the end requires someone to make a turn-six move that a rational player wouldn't make. Elena answered that this turns "rational player" into a label you can only safely apply before anyone has moved. In any real game, if your opponent passes, you're no longer entitled to the common-knowledge-of-rationality premise, so backward induction gives you no advice about the actual person sitting across from you. And if Marcus is the one with the theory and he doesn't grab the $2 immediately, she said, then by his own account he's behaving irrationally—so why should she play as if he'll suddenly turn rational at turn six?

I was listening and genuinely couldn't sort out who was right about what. Can you adjudicate their exchange point by point—who is right about which specific claims, who is overreaching, and what are they both missing? I'm less interested in "the right way to play" than in a careful sorting of what backward induction actually does and doesnt establish here.
