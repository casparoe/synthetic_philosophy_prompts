I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in political philosophy on constitutionalism and the problem of future generations. I am writing the take-home final, and I want one essay question on constitutional precommitment — specifically on whether the Ulysses-contract model of constitutional entrenchment can be defended against the objection that it lets the past bind the living. The question should be answerable in about 1,500 words within a 48-hour window, and I will mark it out of 20. It should be unanswerable by simply reciting Elster's example of Ulysses and the Sirens: I want it to force students to confront the dilemma that precommitment looks either toothless (a future majority can always undo any prior decision) or tyrannical (if the commitment genuinely binds, the living are governed by the dead), and to work out whether there is a defensible route between those horns.

Please provide the following three sections, in this order and with these approximate lengths.

1. Exam question (about 150 words). State the dilemma precisely, and require students to (a) distinguish at least two different things a constitution might be said to bind — for example, making a decision harder to take versus removing it from the agenda altogether, or constraining a pre-existing people versus constituting the people in the first place — and (b) say whether the Ulysses model survives the objection, needs repair, or should be abandoned, with reasons.

2. Model answer (about 700 words). Model the strongest available escape from the dilemma rather than summarizing the topic, and show how the distinctions required in the question do real argumentative work.

3. Marking scheme (about 400 words). Give five must-hit points, the marks each is worth out of 20, and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each point wrong.
