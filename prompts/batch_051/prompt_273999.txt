I've been trying to work out what I actually think about dispositions, and I keep circling around the old examples involving fragility, finks, and masks. I've arrived at six claims that each seem right to me, but I don't know whether they all fit together. Here they are:

1. A glass is fragile in virtue of its current internal structure — how its parts are arranged and bonded at that moment — not in virtue of how it happens to behave or how we talk about it.

2. A glass wrapped in padding so that a normal drop would not break it is still fragile; the padding masks the disposition without removing it. I would say the same about a glass permanently encased in a perfectly shock-absorbing shell from the moment it was made: as long as its own structure is ordinary, it is fragile.

3. If some mechanism would, at the instant of impact, instantly change the glass's internal structure to a tough, non-brittle one, so that it does not break, the glass is still fragile just before the impact. A fink removes the disposition at the last moment; it does not show that the disposition was absent earlier.

4. Any two objects that are exact physical duplicates at a time have exactly the same dispositions at that time, because there is nothing inside the objects themselves to ground a difference.

5. Even so, the point of ascribing a disposition is to predict and explain behavior, so if a claimed disposition would make no difference to any behavior we could in principle arrange, the claim is empty.

6. For any case and any disposition, there is a fact of the matter about whether the object has it. Two people who agree on all the physical facts of a case cannot both be correct if one says the glass is fragile and the other says it is not.

Could you audit this set and tell me whether it hangs together? Please give exactly five points, ordered by how serious a problem each reveals, and say for each what would have to give to restore fit. Keep each point to one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
