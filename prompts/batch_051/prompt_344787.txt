As a novelist, I have spent years with a conviction I have only recently tried to state plainly. I call it the Unclaimed Future. The idea is that nearly everything meaning-laden in my work — and perhaps in life generally — rests on an unstated premise: that the human story will go on after the book closes and after my own death. I do not mean that I write for posterity, or that I care about particular future readers, or that I hope to be remembered. The future in question is not one I claim; it has no faces. Yet it seems to me to be doing quiet structural work. The poignancy of a scene about a person slowly dying in a village depends on the unspoken assumption that the village, or some human life rather like it, is still going on beyond the scene's edge. If I tried to write the same novel for the last generation of readers the species would ever have, the book would not merely lose its audience; it would become a different kind of object, rather as a letter posted into a functioning postal network differs from the same letter left in a mailbox beside a road no one will ever travel again. The network was never the recipient, but it silently determined what the act of posting was. This, I think, extends far beyond fiction: a cancer researcher, a farmer rotating crops for soil she will never farm, a parent teaching a child how to live — all of them seem to be reaching into an unclaimed future they could not name.

From this I am tempted to draw a philosophically large conclusion: that human extinction would be bad not only because of the deaths it would involve, but because it would retroactively undo or drain the meaning of lives already lived by removing the background against which those lives became meaningful. I want an honest assessment, not a sympathetic one. Is this a real claim about value, or have I projected the architecture of fiction onto life? Has it already been worked out under another name, and if so, what is its strongest formulation? And where exactly does it break — does it overstate how many activities need such a future, or does it make the meaning of a life improperly hostage to events that occur after it ends? Please give me the three strongest objections and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. Please answer in British English.
