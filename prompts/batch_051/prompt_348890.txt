I'm currently taking a course on the philosophy of delusions, and we have been working through the two-factor theory of monothematic delusions. On that theory, a delusion such as Capgras requires two distinct deficits. The first factor is an anomalous experience: in Capgras, the usual autonomic response to a familiar face is missing, so the recognized face no longer feels familiar, and the person is experienced as somehow alien. The second factor is a failure of belief evaluation: the generated impostor explanation is accepted instead of being rejected as wildly improbable. The theory's appeal lies in its refusal of single-cause explanations — the anomalous experience can occur without the delusion, and a general evaluation bias cannot by itself explain the delusion's specific content. The two factors must co-occur.

I keep being reminded of something from an engineering course I took, and I cannot tell whether the resemblance is substantive or merely verbal. In the Swiss cheese model of accident causation, an accident is not traced to a single root cause. Instead, a hazard must pass through holes in several defensive layers at once. Each layer — training, procedures, alarms, physical barriers — has weaknesses, and an accident occurs only when the holes in the different layers momentarily line up. The point is that adverse events are conjunctions of multiple necessary failures, none sufficient alone.

The parallel I see is this: the two-factor theorist says an anomalous experience and a failed evaluation must occur together, just as the safety engineer says several barriers must fail together. Both are multiple-necessary-conditions models, and both reject the search for one sufficient cause. But I am unsure whether anything deeper follows. The defensive layers in the Swiss cheese model are supposed to be independent safeguards arranged in series, and the accident is a chance alignment of independent gaps. The two factors in the delusion case do not seem independent in the same way: the second factor is precisely whatever mechanism should evaluate the output of the first, so its failure matters only relative to that output. That looks more like a quality-control or feedback relation than a series of separate barriers.

What I want to know is whether this is a genuine structural analogy that can do work, or only a superficial shared shape. For it to be a real identity of structure, what would have to be true about the two factors — their independence, their ordering, their function — and about the defensive layers? And does the analogy overgenerate: is there something it would predict about delusions that the clinical picture actually contradicts? I am less interested in whether the Swiss cheese model is a good theory of accidents than in whether comparing it with two-factor theory tells me anything about delusions.
