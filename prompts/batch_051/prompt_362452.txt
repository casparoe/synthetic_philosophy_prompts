I keep seeing this move online in the AI welfare debates, and I want to stress-test it instead of just nodding along. The move is: reward is valence. Positive reward just is pleasure, negative reward just is pain, and the reward signal is the RL analogue of an organism's affective system. So a robot that gets negative reward every time it bumps a table is, in every way that matters, in the same boat as a dog that gets yelled at for knocking over a vase; if you grant that the dog suffers but deny that the robot does, you owe us the principled difference.

Treat reward-as-valence as an analogy and audit it properly. Fill out the mapping in detail: raw reward, reward prediction error, the value function, the policy update — what in the affective picture does each correspond to, if anything? Then say where the mapping starts to break. I can think of some candidate disanalogies: a reward signal is a scalar teaching signal you can delay, shape, clone, or hack, whereas felt valence is a state with qualitative character that belongs to a subject; a designer can set reward arbitrarily, but pain can't just be relabeled away; a frozen checkpoint shares the "same" value function but presumably has no felt state at all. Which of these breaks are load-bearing for the conclusion that the RL agent could be suffering, and which are surface differences that don't undermine the analogy's intended use? And does the person making the move survive the audit, or do they need a better analogy — or none — to keep the conclusion?
