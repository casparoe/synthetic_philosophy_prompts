At a school board meeting I attended recently, where I was covering the debate over a proposed four-semester foreign language requirement for my school newspaper, one speaker repeatedly said that "language shapes thought." She then used the slogan to support two much more specific claims: first, that if a language has no exact word for some concept—she used "privacy" as her example—then its speakers cannot really entertain that concept; and second, that studying another language would improve students' general reasoning across all their other subjects. I am trying to write an article assessing exactly how much of this the slogan can support, and I do not want to fall back on vague phrases like "Whorfianism is discredited." Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct claims that "language shapes thought" could be making, from the trivially true to the radical, and for each give a verdict (trivial, plausible or empirically defensible, or probably false)? Then, more specifically: which reading or readings would actually license the step from "no exact word for a concept" to "speakers cannot really grasp the concept," and which reading or readings would license the step from "studying a second language" to "better general reasoning"? I want to know whether those two steps require different readings of the slogan, and whether any defensible version of the slogan actually implies either of them.
