I've been reading Hume's Treatise — the bit in 3.2.7 on the origin of government — for a piece I'm writng on why people don't do their part, and this passage keeps pulling me back:

"This is the reason why men so often act in contradiction to their known interest; and in particular why they prefer any trivial advantage, that is present, to the maintenance of order in society, which so much depends on the observance of justice. The consequences of every breach of equity seem to lie very remote, and are not able to counter-ballance any immediate advantage, that may be reap'd from it. They are, however, never the less real for being remote; and as all men are, in some degree, subject to the same weakness, it necessarily happens, that the violations of equity must become very frequent in society, and the commerce of men, by that means, be render'd very dangerous and uncertain."

Here's the thing I can't shake. Hume doesn't seem to diagnose the person who takes without contributing as malicious or even as particularly selfish in the ordinary sense. He says we all have the same weakness: we prefer the present and contiguous to the remote, however real the remote consequences are. So the free rider isn't doing a different kind of psychological thing from the rest of us; he's just the one whose failure is visible because the others didn't fail. I've started calling this "quiet parasitism." The claim is: most of what we call free riding isn't a plan to exploit anyone, it's just the usual contiguity bias doing its usual work, and the expploitative language — greed, taking advantage, selfishness — is describing a much rarer creature than we pretend.

But then what, exactly, is the wrong? If the wrong is located in the outcome — the "dangerous and uncertain" commerce of men — it seems like a quiet parasite among millions of other quiet parasites is no worse than a raindrop in a flood. If it's located in the psychology, then the person who struggles and fails to contribute anyway might be doing the same wrong as the person who never tried. And if it's located in the message — Hume says your example "affords me a new reason for any breach of equity, by shewing me, that I should be the cully of my integrity" — then the wrong seems to happen almost by accident, just by being seen. Can a wrong be real when there's no exploitative intention at all? And is my "quiet parasitism" an actual concept, or have I just renamed weakness of will with better PR?
