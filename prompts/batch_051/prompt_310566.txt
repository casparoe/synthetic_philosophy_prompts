I have been trying to reconcile two commitments that I cannot quite hold together. The first is a version of falsificationism I find hard to refuse: when a long-run empirical prediction fails on schedule, the right response is to abandon the theory behind it, unless one can point to an auxiliary assumption that was independently identifiable to begin with and whose replacement yields a new, testable prediction. A prediction that survives only by pushing its target date further into the future never seems to die, and that should make me suspicious of it.

The second commitment is that Thomas Malthus was not merely wrong. As I understand him, Malthus argued that population tends to increase geometrically while the food supply can grow at best arithmetically, so that some check — famine, disease, war, or deliberate restraint on births — must eventually bring the two into line. On any plain reading, his timetable has not held: agricultural productivity has expanded in ways his model did not anticipate, and the great check keeps failing to arrive. Yet I cannot dismiss him the way I would dismiss a failed stock-market prophet. The mechanism he identified strikes me as real even if his timing was not.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for a serious falsificationist to grant that a theory can be "right but early" as a legitimate verdict rather than a face-saving label; what it would take for a defender of Malthus to admit that the prediction has been rescued so many times that it no longer predicts anything; and whether there is a stable middle position in which the theory is restated so that it makes a definite, falsifiable claim again. Which commitment should give way, and what exactly is lost if it does?
