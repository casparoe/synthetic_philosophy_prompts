I'm in a political philosophy reading group that's been going through Mill's *On Liberty* alongside bits of Thaler and Sunstein on nudging. The Mill passage that keeps tripping everyone up is the one right at the start, where he defines the subject as:

"The subject of this Essay is not the so-called Liberty of the Will, so unfortunately opposed to the misnamed doctrine of Philosophical Necessity; but Civil, or Social Liberty: the nature and limits of the power which can be legitimately exercised by society over the individual."

We all agree Mill's harm principle was designed for a world where interference meant law, punishment, or social coercion. But the nudge people claim you can change what people choose without coercing them, just by altering defaults, framing, or the order of options. Mill seems to assume there's a reasonably stable self-regarding/other-regarding line and that, within the self-regarding sphere, leaving people alone lets them get what they themselves prefer. But if preferences are partly an artefact of the choice environment, that premise looks shakier: someone has to set the default, and whichever way they set it, they're influencing the outcome. So my question isn't just "are nudges paternalistic?" It's whether Mill's whole framing survives this change in assumed facts about choice. Which premise in his argument is actually under pressure, and is there a repaired version that still does the work he wanted? Or does "liberty" end up meaning something thinner once you accept that choices are shaped by choice architecture?
