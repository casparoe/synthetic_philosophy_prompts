Next month I am presenting to a philosophy of science reading group a paper on whether theory choice during scientific revolutions can be rational. My thesis is that such choice is rational but not algorithmically rule-governed: Kuhn is right that a scientific community's shared values—accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness—genuinely constrain appraisal, yet they underdetermine a unique choice and must be weighed by trained judgment. I will argue that Popper's falsificationism cannot capture revolutionary rationality because it requires scientists to specify in advance what would refute their theory, whereas in revolutionary episodes the new program often begins with unresolved anomalies and promissory explanatory power; what makes the choice rational is that participants can give reasons rooted in shared values for believing the old program is degenerating and the new one is worth a costly bet.

The audience includes three types I need to prepare for. Two are Popperian philosophers of physics who defend a decision-theoretic reconstruction of corroboration and will say that appeal to scientific judgment is just subjective preference unless it can be represented as maximizing expected degree of corroboration. One is a historian of science who works on the acceptance of plate tectonics and will argue that my account rationalizes after the fact what was actually driven by institutional power, career incentives, and generational turnover. Another is a Kuhn scholar who will say I have domesticated Kuhn by dropping incommensurability and the claim that a revolution is ultimately something closer to a conversion than to cumulative argument.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they will arise, phrased as those three members would phrase them. For each objection, tell me what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest premise in my argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Then include the objection I am most hoping nobody raises, and explain why it is more damaging than I realize.
