I'm a software engineer (ML infra, mostly) and I don't have any formal philosophy background, so apologies if I'm mashing together terms from two fields. I've been going down a rabbit hole about whether natural selection is basically just an optimizer, and whether the intuitions actually transfer in either direction. On one hand, the analogy is really seductive: you've got a fitness landscape, allele frequencies as parameters, selection pushing the population uphill, and even a classic population-genetics result saying mean fitness shouldn't drop under selection alone (given the usual caveats). If I squint, evolution looks like a noisy, population-based, learning-rate-free version of the optimizers I babysit at work, which would mean a lot of what I know about loss functions, saddle points, and local minima can be reused to think about biology — and maybe the reverse too, that evolution has tricks like mutation, recombination, and huge populations that could teach me something about designing better optimizers.

But the more I look at actual biological cases, the more this collapses. Natural selection doesn't have a fixed objective function — the environment moves around, fitness is relative and frequency-dependent, and the sign of selection on a trait can flip. It isn't computing anything like a gradient; it accepts neutral or even slightly deleterious changes because of drift, and it has no notion of momentum, curvature, or step size. The products look like the output of a process that tinkers blindly: nerves that take absurd detours in a giraffe's neck, and so on. I've seen people call evolution a stupid optimizer, and honestly that phrase names my intuition pretty well: the useful parts of gradient descent — a stable loss, a direction that reliably reduces it, monotone-ish progress — are exactly the parts natural selection seems to lack.

So I'm left holding both. The optimization lens feel's like it should be true, because it would unify two things I care about and give me real conceptual traction, but my firm intuition is that evolution is precisely not doing gradient descent and the analogy is more of a vibes-based metaphor than a claim I can stand behind. Which should yield? Is there a defensible version where natural selection counts as an optimizer in a rigorous sense while the stupid-optimizer intuition survives, or do I have to either debunk the intuition or abandon the analogy? I want a negotiated verdict — what would justify each move, not just a pick-a-side answer. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
