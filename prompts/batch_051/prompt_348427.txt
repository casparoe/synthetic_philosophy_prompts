Question: Explain Berkeley's Master Argument against mind-independent objects, and assess whether the argument succeeds.

Student's answer: Berkeley's Master Argument aims to show that the very idea of a mind-independent object is incoherent. He asks us to try to conceive of an object—say, a tree—existing without being conceived by any mind at all. Berkeley claims the attempt fails, because whatever tree we bring before the mind is thereby a tree we are conceiving. So the feat would require us to think of a tree while the tree is not being thought of, and that is a contradiction. Since a contradiction cannot describe a real possibility, no object could exist unconceived; Berkeley concludes that the being of sensible things consists in their being perceived and that matter, understood as something existing behind our ideas, is unnecessary.

A standard objection is that Berkeley conflates the act of thinking with the object of thought. From the fact that my thought of the tree is mental, it does not follow that the tree I am thinking about is mental: I can think of a tree as unconceived, just as I can think of a meeting as unattended without thereby attending the meeting.

But this objection is less damaging than it first appears. Even when I represent the tree as wholly unconceived, the representation is still someone's representation, and its content is present only as the content of a mind. Strip away every conceiving mind and no such content remains; the supposed mind-independent tree is never available to us except as something thought. So the objector has not shown how a mind-independent object could be brought before the mind. If no mind-independent object can even be conceived, then none can exist, and Berkeley's conclusion stands.
