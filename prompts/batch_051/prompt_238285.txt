I have no academic background in this; I have just been reading on my own and trying to work an idea out. There seem to be two different ways people rank how advanced a civilization is, and they do not obviously point in the same direction.

The first looks outward, at energy. I have seen it called the Kardashev scale. A civilization is ranked higher the more usable energy it can command: first roughly all the usable energy available on its home planet, then all the energy its star produces, then all the energy of its galaxy, and so on. The second looks inward, at control. I have seen it associated with John Barrow. A civilization is ranked higher the smaller the structures it can deliberately build, rearrange, or take apart: first whole objects, then molecules, then individual atoms, then atomic nuclei, then the particles inside nuclei, and so on down toward whatever fundamental limits nature has.

These two scales can pull apart. A civilization could command enormous energy and still do only coarse things, such as strip-mining planets or building very large structures, while another could do extremely fine manipulation with very little energy, such as editing DNA or moving individual atoms in a small laboratory without ever being able to shift a planet.

Please compare these two scales as measures of what it means for a civilization to be advanced. What does each scale implicitly assume about the point of civilizational or technological progress? Do they measure two sides of one underlying thing, or two genuinely different things? Is one a better measure than the other, and under what conditions would their rankings come apart?
