I spent three decades in isotope geochemistry before retiring and beginning to read formal decision theory. I now advise a small family foundation whose grants-and-legal committee occasionally receives demands from parties who claim to have predicted our payout decisions. The committee meets monthly, but these anomalous demands arrive only a few times a year; we can give each case about ninety minutes, and no one on the committee has formal training in decision theory. I want a reusable procedure the committee can actually run, not another essay about whether to one-box on Newcomb's problem.

The two structures that keep appearing are counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail. I will describe them as I use them here, with deliberately altered dollar amounts and details so that the procedure has to handle the structure rather than discharge the case by pattern-matching to a familiar example.

By counterfactual mugging I mean this. A party says it ran a fair coin toss before approaching us. The announced rule was: if the coin had landed heads, the party would have given us $100,000 if and only if we would have paid its $1,000 demand in the tails branch; if the coin landed tails, the party would ask us for $1,000. We learn the coin landed tails, and the demand arrives. Paying in the actual tail branch returns nothing; any payoff existed only in the counterfactual head branch.

By XOR blackmail I mean this. A party holds damaging material about the foundation and says it made a prediction about whether we will pay $5,000. The material will be released just in case its prediction and our actual action disagree: the harm is triggered by the XOR of predicted action and performed action. The party claims high predictive reliability, and for the purposes of the procedure we may take that claim as stipulated when applying it to our worked case, though the procedure itself should tell us how to handle weaker or unverifiable reliability claims.

Please write a decision procedure in American English that the committee can follow in under ninety minutes for demands of these two structural kinds. Present it as a numbered sequence of gates, where each gate poses a question, states what counts as a sufficient answer to continue or stop, and notes whether the step is factual, decision-theoretic, or a values call. At each point where judgment cannot be eliminated by the theory, flag that explicitly rather than dressing the judgment up as a mechanical step.

The procedure should make explicit which assumptions about prediction reliability, branch structure, and the foundation's long-run policy value it is leaning on at each step. I especially want to see the distinction between what a purely causal expected-value calculation of the actual branch would recommend and what a policy-level or functional decision rule would recommend, translated into language a nonspecialist committee member can apply. Where the two would diverge, the procedure should say so and say which divergence the procedure is willing to enforce, including at least one worked case in which the procedure recommends paying even though a direct cost-benefit calculation of the actual branch says the money is simply lost.

Then apply the procedure to two worked cases, one of each structural type, using the parameters above. Use a short table only for the worked applications if that is clearer, but keep the procedure itself in prose. End by stating the two most likely ways the procedure would misfire if someone applied it mechanically.

The response must be no more than 1,200 words.
