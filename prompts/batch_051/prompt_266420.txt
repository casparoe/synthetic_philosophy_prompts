The Becker-style deterrence argument for capital punishment runs roughly as follows. Prospective killers weigh expected costs against expected benefits, and death is the one punishment whose severity cannot be traded off or discounted; therefore, retaining the death penalty should reduce murders at the margin. Now imagine a well-identified natural experiment across several U.S. states over four decades. After controlling for policing, demographics, and economic conditions, abolition produces no detectable change in the overall homicide rate, but it does produce a small, robust reduction in the rate at which police officers are killed during traffic stops and domestic-violence calls. At the same time, interviews with men serving whole-life sentences for aggravated murder find that they describe the sentence as a living death, not as a lesser penalty they rationally prefer. Assess whether the deterrence argument survives this combination of findings. I want the response to identify which premise of the argument each finding bears on, say whether a repaired version can still support the death penalty, and specify the one further observation that would most strengthen or falsify that repair. Lead with your bottom line and keep the whole response under 900 words.
