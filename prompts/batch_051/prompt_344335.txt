Suppose that the Netherlands—or, if you prefer, a polity exactly like it in wealth, legal culture, and administrative capacity—introduces a comprehensive parental licensing regime on the first of January of next year. Carrying a pregnancy to term, adopting, and taking on long-term foster care all require a license. The license is obtained through a standardized course in child development and the legal duties of parents, a psychosocial and financial interview with a caseworker, a single home visit, and a two-year probationary period beginning when the child arrives, with quarterly visits from a family support officer; every adverse decision can be appealed to a specialist tribunal. There are no quotas, no means-testing, and only a small administrative fee. Anyone who has a child without a license triggers child protection proceedings, and the child is in most cases placed with licensed foster carers while the parent applies or appeals. Existing parents are grandfathered, but any subsequent child requires a license. I want a reasoned prediction of how this polity looks thirty years later—not a story, and not primarily a moral verdict.

I have been arguing about this with friends who read the same rationalist forums; one side treats the proposal as obviously dystopian, the other as obviously overdue, and both keep sliding from "this would be good" into "this is what would happen." Please take the role of an applied political philosopher who has also done policy evaluation, begin by brainstorming the main candidate mechanisms before committing, and then give a visible, mechanistic chain of reasoning. Cover at least: who delays or forgoes childbearing; whether marginal prospective parents alter their behaviour before conception; how a bureaucracy that must both help and judge manages those two roles; whether child maltreatment, developmental outcomes, and inequality actually change; what kinds of evasion or black markets emerge—informal adoptions, home births, cross-border births; how the social norm around becoming a parent shifts; whether the policy serves or undermines a child's right to an open future; and what coalitions would keep the regime alive or repeal it.

Throughout, please mark which predictions you would defend as robust and which are genuine guesses, with rough confidence levels where you can give them. End by saying which of your predictions is most likely to be wrong, what argument or empirical finding would most change your mind, and which unstated cultural or institutional assumption your whole projection depends on.
