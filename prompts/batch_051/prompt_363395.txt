You are a philosopher who specializes in the ethics of narrative and the epistemology of history. Think carefully, and begin by explicitly distinguishing the different questions that might be tangled together before you settle on a diagnosis.

I moderate a small online discussion group on historical fiction and biopics, and for the past several months our central dispute about what we owe the real dead when we dramatize them has been at a standstill. The group keeps returning to three cases: a biopic that compresses a decade into a single year and invents private dialogue; a prestige novel that assigns a real historical figure a fictional motive for a well-documented act; and a film that depicts a real person committing a crime of which they were acquitted, with no on-screen disclaimer. Everyone accepts the same factual record in each case, and everyone agrees that comparable distortions of the living would wrong the living when they are defamatory or misleading.

From there, the group has divided into three camps, and no one has shifted. One camp holds that we owe the dead truthfulness: a serious distortion treats a real life as raw material, corrupts the public record of what that life meant, and wrongs the subject even after death. A second camp holds that the dead cannot be wronged, so historical fiction is answerable only to norms internal to fiction—coherence, insight, aesthetic value—plus our duties to the living, such as survivors and communities who may be harmed. A third camp holds that the real issue is the audience rather than the dead: because audiences absorb historical fiction as history, inaccuracy is wrong chiefly because it predictably produces false beliefs, and the question whether the dead can be wronged is beside the point.

My working hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but three running under a single label: a metaphysical question about whether the dead can be wronged or have interests; an aesthetic question about what standards govern fiction as fiction; and an empirical question about how audiences form beliefs from fiction. I suspect the first is driven by bedrock intuitions, the second by conflicting values, and only the third could be moved by evidence, but I am not confident.

Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, using a charitable reconstruction of what each camp is committed to. Say which parts are merely verbal, which parts are substantive but shielded from evidence by prior intuitions or values, and whether a hidden empirical or conceptual question is doing the work. Then explain what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis—a distinction, a study, a reframing, a change of question—or whether no progress should be expected, and why.
