My working test for whether two apparent values are really two rather than one thing under different descriptions has four parts: (1) conflict without a common measure — there are choices where each points a different way and no neutral third value settles how they compare; (2) independence — someone can achieve a great deal of one and little of the other, and the other way around; (3) a leftover — when you try to state everything that matters about one value entirely in terms of the other, something important still escapes the reduction; and (4) a different shape of reason — one presents itself as a constraint, say, while the other presents itself as an aim.

Now take the case of two apparent epistemic values: getting it right and seeing it whole. Suppose a scientist must choose between two accounts of a domain. Account A is a large set of true, well-confirmed claims about particular cases, but the claims do not fit together and no one can see how they form a single picture. Account B is a unified, illuminating framework that makes the domain intelligible, though it is known to misdescribe several cases. If only truth mattered, A wins; if understanding also matters for its own sake, B has a claim.

I want you to run this case through the four criteria and tell me whether "seeing it whole" (understanding) comes out as a genuinely distinct, irreducible value, or as truth plus a taste for elegance, compressibility, or aesthetic satisfaction — in which case it is either instrumental to truth or not an epistemic value at all. But also check the criteria themselves: does this borderline case show that one of my four parts is doing too much work, or too little, or smuggling in a prior verdict? Please go criterion by criterion, say where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, and throughout separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on value theory, without presenting a contested view as the consensus.
