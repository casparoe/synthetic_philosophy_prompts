I'm putting together a handout for a reading group on the justification of deduction, and we're doing Carroll's dialogue 'What the Tortoise Said to Achilles'. You know the shape of it: the Tortoise accepts the premises A and B, and accepts the conditional that if A and B are true then Z is true, but he still refuses to accept Z; every time Achilles turns the logical step into a new premise, the Tortoise accepts that premise too and still won't move, so they set off on an infinite regress. I'd like a poem that does the regress structurally instead of just decribing it.

You are a poet who also works on philosophy of logic. Think carefully about the form before drafting: a villanelle seems right because its two refrains keep returning and only meet side by side in the final couplet, which is exactly the way the Tortoise's standing refusal and Achilles's endless new premise keep passing each other without ever forcing the conclusion. Write a villanelle (nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain) from the Tortoise's point of view, addressed to Achilles. The two refrains should be something like, though you may reword them so they rhyme and scan: one for the move of accepting each new conditional while still refusing the conclusion, and one for Achilles's promise that just one more rule will finally compel the step. The refrains should be repeated verbatim in their slots, as the form requires. The closing couplet should put the two refrains together in a way that makes clear the conclusion is still not reached. It should be accurate enough that a logician would nod at it, and good enough as a poem that someone who has never read Carroll can still follow the frustration.

1. The poem itself — the full villanelle, with the two refrains used consistently and the final couplet doing the structural work.
