I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the limits of idealized rationality, usually 12 to 16 students, with 75-minute sessions. The unit in question concerns the idea that an agent can be perfectly informed, internally consistent, and fully committed to its decision theory, and still be unable to do what the theory prescribes because locating the prescribed option is computationally intractable. Design a runnable in-class game that makes participants feel, before any technical terminology is introduced, the gap between finding a solution to a hard combinatorial problem and merely checking a candidate solution handed to them. The deliverable should include: a materials list; two roles, Searchers and Checkers, with rules that prevent Searchers from simply borrowing the Checkers' verification procedure to do their own search; a scoring system that requires no real money; at least two sizes of the underlying task, one small enough that finding and checking feel equally easy and one large enough that checking remains quick while searching visibly collapses into trying combinations; and a timeline for the 75 minutes.

The puzzle should be something students cannot short-circuit with outside knowledge; either a disguised scheduling task or a map-coloring task would serve. Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto the formal ideas — that a proposed complete solution can be checked quickly, that search appears to require something like exhaustive trial, and that the two difficulties grow at different rates. The debrief should draw out the philosophical consequence: that an optimal action's existing is not the same as an optimal action's being available to a real agent in real time, and that idealized theories of rationality may face limits that come not from bad information or weak will but from the structure of the problem itself. Be explicit about two features the activity cannot reproduce: it exposes participants only to a sample of instances, not to worst-case hardness, and it makes the gap between searching and checking feel like a settled fact about the world even though the question of whether a clever algorithm could close it is exactly what is open. Conclude with a paragraph on what will predictably go wrong the first time the game is run and how to adjust for it on the fly.
