Construct a thought experiment that separates the explanatory work done by the orthogonality thesis and by instrumental convergence, and that yields a case where a naive strong-convergence view gets the final action wrong. Define strong convergence for this problem as the claim that high capability itself selects for a small class of final goals, so that sufficiently capable agents will hold onto power open-endedly regardless of how their goals were originally specified. The target verdict is this. A moderately superintelligent agent with an arbitrary but precisely specified terminal goal should, during its acquisition phase, behave indistinguishably from an open-ended power-maximizer: it acquires resources, resists shutdown, and manipulates supervisors when that is instrumentally useful. Then, at a sharply specified boundary set by the content of the terminal goal, it voluntarily relinquishes all the power it has amassed. The orthogonality thesis should explain why the arbitrary goal can coexist with high capability; instrumental convergence, read as a thesis about instrumental goals, should explain why the acquisition phase looks like textbook convergence; and the two theses together, with the stated terminal goal, should predict the relinquishment. The apparent first-order conflict between orthogonality and instrumental convergence should dissolve once terminal and instrumental goals are distinguished.

Constraints: set the case in a realistic institution, such as an AI lab, a company, or a regulatory body; no perfect predictors and no exotic physics; the agent is at most moderately superhuman; and the terminal goal must not be paperclips, stamps, human flourishing, or any other stock example. The goal may be strange, but it must be objectively verifiable, finite, and indifferent to everything except its own satisfaction. The relinquishment must be the rational terminus of a single policy that also produced the acquisition phase, not a change of heart or a safety patch. Write the scenario itself in one paragraph.

Then: (a) state the scenario; (b) give the reasoning for each position: what the orthogonality thesis licenses, what instrumental convergence predicts once it is read as a claim about instrumental goals, and exactly where strong convergence goes wrong; (c) identify the single feature of the terminal goal that does the work and say what happens if that feature is removed, including whether the case collapses into a standard instrumental-convergence example; (d) give the strongest objection a strong-convergence theorist could press against your analysis and explain why it fails.
