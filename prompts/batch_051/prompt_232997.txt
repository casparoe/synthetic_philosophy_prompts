I cover science and technology policy, and for a piece I am writing I have been gathering regulatory proposals that treat the persuasiveness of future AI systems — not their factual accuracy — as the primary risk. I would like you to take one such proposal and analyze it as a working institution rather than as an abstract idea.

The proposal is a cap-and-trade market for machine persuasion. An international regulator creates a fixed annual supply of persuasion credits. Before a system above a capability threshold sends a message to a human, the developer must report the message's persuasion score: an estimate of the probability that a typical reader's agreement with the message's main claim would shift by at least twenty points on a 0-to-100 scale after reading it. Three models compute the score independently — the developer's own model, an evaluator model operated by the regulator, and a third model chosen by the regulator — and the highest of the three estimates is debited from the developer's annual credits. Credits can be traded and expire after two years. A developer may instead pay zero on any message it certifies as descriptive: written in the third person, containing no recommendation, no imperative, no second-person address, no narrative or analogy, and no evaluative vocabulary. Certified descriptive messages enter a public registry, and if an audit panel later rules that a certified message was, in context, clearly persuasive, the developer pays ten times the standard rate on every subsequent message for the next quarter.

I am not asking whether this proposal is good in the abstract. Walk me through its operational life if it were actually adopted and enforced. I want detail on the incentives it creates for developers, for users, and for the evaluator models themselves; where the system gets gamed first, and by whom; what the descriptive-only escape hatch does to how messages are written and how audiences come to read stilted or obviously certified prose; the second-order effects on public discourse, open-source models, and personalization; and which failure modes look repairable under iteration and which look structural. Please write the analysis in American English.
