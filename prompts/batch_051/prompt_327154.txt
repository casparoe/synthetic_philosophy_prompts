Last night a friend and I spent a long argument on a single question: whether a single-story 'a' in a geometric sans and a double-story 'a' in an old-style serif are genuinely the same letter. He kept insisting that beneath all the stylistic variation there must be one structural feature — the essence of 'a' — shared by every genuine 'a' and absent from every non-'a', and that once specified, it would settle all borderline cases. I took the opposite view: the harder one tries to state that essence as a set of explicit criteria, the more the exceptions pile up, until the definition is either gerrymandered beyond use or denies that plainly legible glyphs count as 'a' at all.

I would like a piece of sustained satire to make the point for him. Please write a deadpan mock regulatory document, around 800 words, from a fictional Ministry of Letter Purity announcing a binding standard titled "The One True Essence of the Letter A." The document should begin with a plausible-sounding definition of 'a' stated as numbered criteria a glyph must satisfy to be certified as an 'a,' and then add enforcement provisions: specimen inspection, licensing of type foundries, and penalties for nonconforming glyphs. The satire should come from watching the definition collapse on contact with the actual variety of 'a'-forms — single-story and double-story, italic, blackletter, handwriting, small-size optical adjustments, legible sans-serifs with open forms — so that the ministry is forced to issue circular amendments and ad hoc exemptions until the regulation either excludes almost every real 'a' or licenses things that are not 'a's at all. Keep the irony deadpan throughout; the target is the essentialist position about letterforms and the bureaucratic fantasy of legislating a category that is actually held together by family resemblance rather than a common feature. It should be funny to someone who knows type design and readable to someone who has never thought about the difference between a letter and a glyph.
