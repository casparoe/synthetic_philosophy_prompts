I'm a retired chemist—spent four decades in a physical chemistry lab—and I've only started reading philosophy seriously since I stopped working. I'm stuck on a passage in chapter 3 of John Stuart Mill's On Liberty, the chapter on individuality. Mill writes:

"Where, not the person’s own character, but the traditions or customs of other people are the rule of conduct, there is wanting one of the principal ingredients of human happiness, and quite the chief ingredient of individual and social progress."

Everything in that sentence sounds like my old job. I inherited my research program from my advisor, my methods from the field, and my standards of evidence from journals and grant reviewers; we all did things because that's how our corner of the discipline did them, and the field progressed anyway. If I take Mill at his word, isn't he saying that letting inherited custom run your conduct removes the chief ingredient of progress? Does that make a working scientist, by his lights, someone who is supressing their individuality—and would science be better if researchers cared more about originality than competence? Or have I misread him, maybe by treating technical conventions as if they were the traditions and customs he has in view? Set me straight. And when you do, tell me what would change your mind about your reading: which feature of Mill's text or of actual scientific practice would push you the other way, and where you think your reasoning is on shakiest ground.
