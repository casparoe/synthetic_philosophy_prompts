I manage water-supply planning for a mid-size regional utility in a drought-prone region. Roughly twice a year we decide whether to put tens of millions of dollars into reservoirs, groundwater banking, stormwater upgrades, or treatment capacity that must still be adequate fifty years from now, and every decision has to survive review by a board of elected officials. We have two staff hydrologists, no one on staff who can build or modify a climate model, and about a week to assess each proposal. The projections we receive from consultants usually arrive as climate-model ensembles, and the convention is to treat the spread of the ensemble as a bracket around the truth. I have come to mistrust that convention. Many of the models share code, assumptions, and the observational records they were tuned to, so the runs are not independent witnesses: agreement can mean one shared mistake repeated many times, and disagreement can be an artifact of model quirks rather than genuine physical possibilities. But "use your judgment" is not a workable answer under public scrutiny.

Please write a decision procedure my staff can actually run on each proposal: a numbered list of questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and which questions must be handed to a person because they call for a value judgment rather than a factual finding. Then apply the procedure to these three proposals: (a) doubling a stormwater culvert because the wettest five percent of ensemble runs exceed the current design; (b) approving a recycling project I have personally championed whose costs are recovered only if regional runoff falls by at least 20 percent by 2060, an outcome appearing in about a third of the runs; and (c) adopting a conservation target based on the ensemble mean even though the individual models disagree widely. Make clear where the procedure would return an answer I would not like, and what it cannot do. Keep the whole response under 900 words.
