I teach philosophy of mathematics, and my colleague in the logic group has this wierdly specific set of verdicts about when a finite string of 0s and 1s is "really random." I've been trying to reverse-engineer her principle, and I'd like you to commit to a prediction about a new case rather than just laying out possibilities.

Her earlier verdicts:

1. A 100-bit string of all zeros, which came out of a certified quantum random number generator. She says: not random.
2. The alternating 100-bit string 0101...010, also produced by coin flips. She says: not random.
3. The first 200 bits of the binary expansion of π. She says: not random, because there's a pretty short program that computes π's digits, even though the string passes ordinary statistical tests for randomness.
4. A 60-bit string with no obvious pattern, generated by a pseudorandom number generator from a 10-bit seed. She says: not random, because the generator plus seed is much shorter than the 60 bits.
5. Another 60-bit string with no obvious pattern, generated by a certified quantum random number generator, and the shortest way to specify it is just to list the 60 bits themselves. She says: random.

New case: a 60-bit string with no obvious pattern, no description shorter than listing the string itself, and it passes all the statistical tests, but she learns it was produced by a deterministic lookup table inside an old machine—the machine simply prints exactly that string out, and the table is the same length as the string.

Predict her verdict. Along the way, identify the two main principles that fit her earlier five verdicts, say what each one predicts about this new case, and then give me your best guess as to which principle she's actually running, with reasons. Also tell me what further case, if any, would settle it beyond doubt. I want a definite prediction from you, not a menu of options.
