I have no academic training in philosophy or the sciences; I have been thinking about this on my own, and I have put together an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept. I would be very grateful for a diagnosis of where it goes wrong.

The question that set me off was a practical one. When a system we think we understand does something unexpected — a patient suffers liver failure after taking a drug that is normally harmless, or a monitoring programme flags a pattern that looks perfectly ordinary — investigators ask whether the unexpected case was produced by the same mechanism as the familiar cases, or by a different one. I wanted a principled criterion for answering that question. The criterion I arrived at, however, seems to show that whether two cases are produced by the same mechanism or by different mechanisms is not a fact about the world at all. That seems to prove too much.

Here is the argument.

(1) Two particular cases are produced by the same mechanism just when there is one kind of mechanism — a definite arrangement of components and activities — that is present in both cases and that produces the outcome in each.

(2) To say that both cases exhibit the same kind of mechanism is to say that they resemble one another in the respects set down by that kind.

(3) Any two cases resemble one another in indefinitely many respects and differ from one another in indefinitely many others; similarity and difference are both easy to find.

(4) A kind of mechanism picks out some of those respects — certain components and activities at a certain level of grain — and takes no account of the rest.

(5) Nothing in the cases themselves dictates the level that gets picked out. The cases are equally real as collections of molecules, as cells, as organs, and as larger functions; no one level is intrinsically privileged.

(6) So the selection of respects that fixes what counts as the same mechanism is not read off the world; it is supplied by us, in the service of whatever we are trying to predict, explain, or control.

(7) Therefore, whether an anomalous case runs through the same mechanism as the normal cases, or through a different one, is not settled by the cases alone; it is settled partly by the purposes of the investigator.

(8) But the reasoning is perfectly general. It carries over to any classification of concrete things by selected similarities — species, diseases, minerals, mental disorders.

(9) Yet at least some classifications of that sort record facts that obtain independently of anyone's purposes: two samples containing the tuberculosis bacterium do not merely count as the same disease relative to a purpose, and a wolf and a fungus are not merely different relative to a purpose.

Since (9) is undeniable, the argument must have gone wrong before it; the question is where and how. I would most like to keep (1), since it seems to say no more than what a mechanism is, but I cannot see how to keep (1) while avoiding (7). Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
