I serve on an internal board at my company that reviews new workplace-monitoring tools, and I have to rule on a proposal next week. Our precedents are these. We approved a keyboard-activity logger that counts active typing minutes and discards all key content, on the grounds that it measures work output without capturing what anyone wrote. We rejected a proposal for routine webcam capture during remote-work hours, even with employee consent, because the camera would collect images of non-employees and off-hours domestic life; we said that consent cannot repair overcollection of that kind. We approved with conditions a badge that records movement within the office and reports only anonymized, aggregate occupancy data, with an individual opt-out, reasoning that coarse location data produced during work was not especially sensitive. And we rejected a tool that would have inferred each employee's stress level from their calendar and message metadata and then flagged burnout risk to HR, because it generated a mental-state classification the person had not chosen to reveal—not because any raw message content was exposed.

The new proposal is a headset with scalp sensors that detect electrical brain activity during focus blocks, infer lapses in attention and accumulating fatigue, keep the raw neural signal on the device, and show the employee only a daily one-to-five focus score visible to no one else, to help them schedule demanding work. I want to decide this in a way that is consistent with our own record rather than my instinct. Which of our four earlier cases is the closest analog? What are the plausible rules that fit all four decisions, and what does each rule say about the headset? What should we decide—and if the precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle would we be committing ourselves to by deciding either way?
