I'm a novelist, working on a book about a fictional country that loses a war and then spends the next century turning the defeat into its founding myth — the thing schoolchildren learn and the national holiday commemorates. Researching it I kept running into real versions of this, and something about the pattern bugs me. The American South lost the Civil War and then put up a huge number of monuments to Confederate generals decades later, long after the war. Masada, where the defenders apparently killed themselves rather than surrender, is a national pilgrimage site in Israel. Kosovo is remembered in Serbia as a lost battle but also as the spiritual heart of the nation. Dunkirk was a military disaster but gets remembered in Britain as a kind of victory of collective will. We dont put up statues to failed business ventures or lost court cases, so clearly defeat is not normally treated as a source of honor.

So what does this practice presuppose, philosophically? It looks like these communities are treating loss as a better source of collective identity than winning. Is that just a local emotional quirk, or does it presuppose a real theory of history or value — something like, what matters in a collective act is fidelity or sacrifice rather than outcomes, or that the meaning of an event only becomes fully visible after the cause is lost, or that a defeat proves the cause was noble because people held onto it even at great cost? I want to know which of these commitments the practice genuinely rests on, in the sense that if you took the commitment away the monument and the ritual would no longer make sense, as opposed to which ones it merely fits well with or flatters people to believe. And I'd like to hear where the practice is underdetermined — i.e., where several different theories would license the same monuments and rituals, so the practice alone doesn't settle which view is right. Then tell me what a society's memorial practice would look like if it rejected all of that and only commemorated successful outcomes.

One more thing, since I don't want to embarrass myself by putting a crank theory in a character's mouth: when you answer, please seperate what's actually settled among people who study this from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
