I've been roped into moderating a session at an aesthetics workshop where two people who've been arguing about photography for years finally have to produce something together instead of just doing the usual back-and-forth. The first is a transparency theorist: she holds that when you look at a photograph you literally see the photographed object, in roughly the way you see through a telescope or a live CCTV feed, and that the object's being temporally or spatially distant doesn't disqualify the relation. The second is an opacity theorist: he holds that you see the photograph — a flat marked surface — and then infer or interpret what it depicts; for him "seeing through" a photograph is at best a metaphor, because genuine perception requires a present causal connection to a contemporary object, not a chain that bottoms out in a past event. They have been argueing past each other, and I've promised them the output will be an adversarial-collaboration document neither of them is allowed to disown.

Draft the actual document they'd sign, not an essay about why such a document is hard. It should contain: (1) a numbered "agreed statements" section with claims both can accept, going beyond the trivial ("photographs are caused by light from a scene") and including at least a few claims about what would undermine each side; (2) a residual-disagreements table where each disagreement is stated twice — once in the transparency side's wording, where the opacity side must sign off that the wording is fair, and once in the opacity side's wording, where the transparency side must sign off that it's fair; (3) two or three cruxes, stated as subquestions such that a settled answer would actually move at least one side; and (4) a short test-case battery the two camps agree to score in advance: a live CCTV image, a mirror seen through a window, a long-exposure star-trail photograph, a photograph of a building since demolished, and a photorealistic hand-drawing made from the same spot as a photograph. For each case, record what each side predicts, and what pattern of answers each side has agreed in advance would count as evidence against their own view. At the end, say which of their disputes you think were substantive and which mostly dissolved once the wording was tightened.
