So I just got out of a long argument with a friend about the meta-problem of consciousness—not really about whether zombies are conceivable, but about why the hard-problem intuition is so stable. He kept saying that once you explain all the functions, there is still something left over, and that the fact we find this so compelling is itself a deep fact. I started wondering what exactly that fact does for us.

Here is the counterfactual I want you to reason through. Starting tomorrow, every adult human loses exactly one thing: the conviction that there is a hard problem of consciousness. They no longer have the sense that explaining discrimination, report, and control leaves out what it is like to be the thing. Nothing else changes initially. People still have vivid experience, still introspect, still describe pain as awful and sunsets as striking. But when they read the explanatory-gap argument, they now respond the way we respond to someone who is puzzled about how a bachelor can be unmarried; it just looks like a confusion about concepts. Their reports about experience are otherwise the same, and their day-to-day behavior is the same for the first few weeks.

Predict, with your reasoning visible, what happens over the next twenty years to academic philosophy of mind, to consciousness science and its funding, and to ordinary talk about experience and the self. I want you to separate the effects that follow almost mechanically from the ones that depend on institutions, status, or culture. For each major prediction say how confident you are, and what hidden assumption would most undercut it. Then tell me the single overall point you are least sure of. Also, at the end, say whether you think this counterfactual tells us anything about what the hard-problem intuition actually does in our world, or whether it is just a way of imagining a world with different epistemic tastes.
