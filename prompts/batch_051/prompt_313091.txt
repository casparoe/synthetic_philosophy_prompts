A couple of friends of mine got into it at a dinner party last week and I can't stop replaying it. Dana said she's been reading about whether parenting should be licensed, and she's come around to thinking it should be—not a full parenting exam, but something like how we license foster parents or drivers, because the decisions you make for a kid determine which adult selves are even available to her later, so it's the highest-stakes relationship there is. Tom immediately pushed back: a license presumes a test of competence, but there's no such thing for raising kids. Every upbringing forecloses something. His parents made him practice violin every day for years, which pretty much closed off being a serious athlete; were they incompetent? If a child's right to an open future means keeping every option open, it's impossible, and if it doesn't mean that, someone has to decide which options count—which is just imposing someone's values.

Dana said that was a caricature. The right to an open future isn't about keeping every door open; it's about the child retaining the capacity to revise or exit the life she was raised into once she's an adult. Making a kid practice violin builds something in—she can put the violin down. Raising a kid in a walled-off community that teaches her that leaving is a sin, with no secular education and no contact with outsiders, forecloses by design: it shrinks the option space rather than filling it. Tom said the walled-off case is just as much "building something in" as violin. A comprehensive way of life makes exit costly because your identity and relationships are tied up in it, and the same exit cost applies to someone raised secular-humanist who'd have to abandon her whole community to become a nun. Then he said once you start licensing, the "reasonable range of options" is just whatever the licensing board thinks is reasonable, and it becomes a machine for punishing minority religions and cultures. Dana came back that nobody needs to pick a religion to enforce a floor: literacy, numeracy, knowledge of your legal rights, exposure to people outside the group. That's already how things work with compulsory schooling—parents can't pull a seven-year-old out entirely—and that's just the open-future principle wearing a different mechanism. Tom said there's a big difference between intervening ex post for demonstrated harm and screening people for a license before they've done anything at all. And besides, a kid can get great schooling and still be psychologically unable to leave because her whole self is bound up in her community; you wouldn't call that a violation unless you're imposing a substantive ideal of autonomy on everyone. Dana said fine, she doesn't actually care whether it's licensing or aggressive intervention. The test is whether a reasonable adult looking back could say she left childhood able to see live alternatives, and when parents deliberately keep kids from knowing other ways of life exist, that's closer to captivity than to education.
