I work as a back-end engineer on a team that maintains a large, mostly unstructured internal knowledge base: several years of design documents, postmortems, code-review threads, vendor documentation, and archived mailing-list discussions. When a technical disagreement breaks out—say, whether a particular caching strategy fails under write-heavy loads, or which of two indexing schemes degrades more gracefully—I have settled into a fixed routine. I encode the disputed claim exactly as one of the parties phrased it, using our standard embedding model, pull the fifteen nearest passages by cosine similarity, discard anything below a 0.7 threshold, and read only what remains. If the retrieved passages mostly read as supporting the claim, I treat it as corroborated; if they mostly read as undermining it, I treat it as weakened. I never read the long tail below threshold.

Two things now bother me. First, because I am usually the one supplying the phrasing, the retrieved evidence tends to come back looking favorable to my own position, and I cannot tell whether that is because the evidence really is favorable or because the query phrasing already encodes my conclusion. Second, the embedding model was trained for topical similarity and semantic search, not for distinguishing support from contradiction, and I am unsure whether ranking by cosine similarity is tracking "relevance to the decision" at all, or just rhetorical adjacency to the query. I want to know whether this routine is defensible as an epistemic policy—a reasonable triage heuristic given that nobody can read the whole corpus—or whether it is a bias wearing the costume of a retrieval pipeline. Under what conditions would a policy like this be reliable, when would it predictably fail, and is there a version of it that keeps the efficiency benefits without quietly deciding the outcome in advance? I am asking as an engineer with no formal philosophy background, so please do not assume I know the literature; I need the reasoning itself, not pointers to it.
