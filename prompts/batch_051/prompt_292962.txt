A friend and I have been going back and forth about a policy thought experiment from a seminar on community and belonging, and I want someone to actually trace the causal chain instead of reaching for vague talk about the social fabric. Suppose a small, wealthy, high-trust democracy of about five million people—strong local associations, generous welfare state—passes a Rotation Statute in 2030. Every seven years each household is randomly reassigned to a different municipality of comparable size and average income; moving costs are paid and there's a year of relocation support. The stated aim is to dissolve place-based loyalties, inherited local patronage, and NIMBY obstruction, making citizenship more mobile and impartial. People may keep their jobs (a lot work remotely or commute), but no household may remain in the same municipality beyond the seven years, with narrow exceptions for the very old and those in long-term care. Refusal rates run around two percent per cycle, and enforcement is mostly financial rather than criminal. Schools, congregations, clubs, and firms stay where they are while their membership churns.

Now run it forward fifty years; I'm not after a portrait of 2080, I'm after the mechanisms. What forms of belonging quietly lapse, and what substitutes appear? For instance, does generalized trust survive while particularized trust collapses? Do religious, occupational, or online communities soak up the need for belonging—and do they become more insular because of it? How do local government, long-term business relationships, casual mutual aid, and care for the elderly shift when everyone knows their neighbors will be gone in seven years? Then tell me which of the links in your chain are near-inevitable, which are hunches, and what would have to be true about human sociality for the policy to actually achieve what its drafters wanted rather than just moving where people park their loyalties.
