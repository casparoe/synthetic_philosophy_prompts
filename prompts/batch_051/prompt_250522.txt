In the philosophy club at my school, we have been reading about Douglas Hofstadter's view that a self is not a thing sealed inside a single brain but a kind of pattern, and that such a pattern can be partly copied into other brains through shared experiences, habits of thought, and memory. On this view, when someone close to you dies, a part of their self can literally persist in you — not just as an influence or a memory, but as a continuation of the same pattern.

That idea collides with an asymmetry I notice in the way people around me actually talk and judge. After my grandmother died, my relatives said that she lives on in us, and they clearly meant it as more than a pretty phrase. Yet if I had asked whether a fragment of my grandmother's self is now literally present in my mother's brain, they would have said no: a self belongs to one brain and one body, and it cannot be spread across several people. So the same people seem to talk as though selves can persist in other minds and judge as though they cannot. Is there a principle that justifies drawing the line where ordinary judgment draws it, or is this simply an inconsistency we live with? Could Hofstadter's pattern view make better sense of the lives-on talk than the ordinary view does, and if it does, what would we have to give up in order to accept it?
