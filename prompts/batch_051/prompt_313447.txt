As a philosopher of mind who has spent several years working alongside causal modellers, I am now reasonably fluent in the vocabulary of structural causal models: I can specify directed acyclic graphs, structural equations and exogenous noise variables; I understand interventions via the do-operator, conditional independence, identifiability and the back-door and front-door criteria; and I have some familiarity with the literature on variable choice and causal abstraction, though I am not a contributor to it. I would like you to restate a philosophical position in that vocabulary and, more importantly, to tell me what does not survive the restatement.

The position is the causal exclusion argument, understood as an argument for the conclusion that mental properties are epiphenomenal with respect to physical effects. In its familiar form it runs roughly as follows. Under physicalism, mental properties are distinct from physical properties but supervene on them. The physical domain is causally closed: every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause. If a physical effect has both a mental cause and a distinct physical cause, the effect is causally overdetermined. But systematic overdetermination of this kind is too implausible to accept. Therefore the mental cause is excluded, and mental causation with respect to physical effects is either redundant or illusory.

Please restate this argument, together with the main replies in the mental causation debate, entirely within the vocabulary of structural causal models. Your translation should cover at least the following: how to represent mental and physical variables, including whether a mental variable should appear as a separate node or as a deterministic function, coarse-graining or abstraction of physical variables; how to formulate supervenience and physical causal closure as constraints on a graph, on structural equations, or on the allowed interventions; how to express the exclusion principle itself, whether as a constraint on admissible graphs, on admissible parametrisations, or on how interventions may be defined; and how the standard replies fare in the formalism, in particular the interventionist reply according to which mental and physical causes may both be genuine if each supports true intervention counterfactuals, and the proportionality or grain reply according to which the appropriate variable depends on the effect and the contrast class one selects.

The centrepiece of your answer should be a residue report. For each component of the original argument, classify it as translating cleanly, translating with distortion, or having no expression at all. Where translation fails or distorts, say which diagnosis is more plausible: that the formalism is missing something the philosophical claim is genuinely tracking, or that the philosophical claim was less precise than it appeared and the translation exposes that. I would also like an overall verdict: once translated, does the causal exclusion argument survive as a thesis about the causal topology of the world, or does it collapse into a thesis about variable or model choice, concerning when it is appropriate to include a mental variable in a model at all? If some part of the argument cannot be expressed in the formalism, tell me whether that is a reason to doubt the formalism or to doubt the argument.
