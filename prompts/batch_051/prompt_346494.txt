I have spent much of the past year trying to bring my views about natural selection and machine-learning optimisation into a single defensible position, and I would like a consistency audit before I have to defend them to my reading group. The passage I keep returning to is Charles Darwin, *On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection, or the Preservation of Favoured Races in the Struggle for Life* (1859), Chapter IV, "Natural Selection":

> Not that, as I believe, any extreme amount of variability is necessary; as man can certainly produce great results by adding up in any given direction mere individual differences, so could Nature, but far more easily, from having incomparably longer time at her disposal. Nor do I believe that any great physical change, as of climate, or any unusual degree of isolation to check immigration, is actually necessary to produce new and unoccupied places for natural selection to fill up by modifying and improving some of the varying inhabitants. For as all the inhabitants of each country are struggling together with nicely balanced forces, extremely slight modifications in the structure or habits of one inhabitant would often give it an advantage over others; and still further modifications of the same kind would often still further increase the advantage.

I read the passage as committed to two claims at once: selection is cumulative in something like the way a slow optimiser is cumulative, and the work it does is opportunistic — improvement is always relative to local competitors and whatever places happen to be open, never towards a fixed target. Here is where I have currently landed:

(1) Natural selection is a genuine optimiser, though a stochastic, gradient-free one. It lacks foresight, but it does more than filter whatever happens to survive; it accumulates slight favourable variations into design.

(2) Gradient descent can in principle extract structure from data that natural selection cannot. It gets dense parameter-level feedback, can reuse the same experience without limit, and can follow smooth local geometry, whereas selection is confined to sparse, organism-level accept/reject signals at generation boundaries.

(3) Even so, natural selection has produced cognition of a kind no gradient-trained system currently approaches — compressed, generalising, energetically efficient — so its search process should not be dismissed as mere satisficing.

(4) There is no coherent global objective that evolution can be said to optimise. Once a fixed fitness function is specified and a population is compared with its optimum, one is no longer describing selection; one is doing engineering. Darwin's "unoccupied places" and "modifying and improving" language is the more honest account.

(5) The distinction between optimising and satisficing, applied to evolution, marks a substantive empirical difference and is not merely verbal.

Do these five commitments hang together? I am not sure where the real fault is, so please do not treat the set as an undifferentiated muddle. Steelman each commitment before pressing on it, separate the pairs that are only apparently in tension from those that are genuinely so, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and say which single revision would do most to make the package coherent and what it would cost. Where the Darwin passage cuts one way rather than the other, say so explicitly; I want it to carry real weight in the audit, not serve as decoration.
