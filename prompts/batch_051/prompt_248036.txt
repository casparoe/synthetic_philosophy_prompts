Here's the working definition of corruption I've been using, and a case I can't get to fit it. My criteria: (1) someone in a position of public trust uses the position for private benefit rather than the public good; (2) there's an exchange or a this-for-that, not just a one-way act of generosity; (3) the person would want it hidden, or couldn't defend it openly in those terms; (4) it compromises the impartial treatment someone is owed -- a permit, a contract, a check, a fair hearing.

Now the case. A city council member in a mid-sized city basically runs a machine. Constituents come to her ward office with everyday problems: a building permit that's been stuck for months, a son who needs a summer job, a pothole the city won't fix, help with a security deposit after a fire. Her staff works the phones, calls in favors with department heads, gets the permit unstuck, gets the kid on a youth jobs list, gets the pothole patched. They keep careful records of everyone they've helped. Around election time, volunteers and staff knock on those doors first and remind people who got it done. Sometimes it's more explicit: a block captain is told the long-promised drainage project will happen faster if the precinct turns out. Almost no money changes hands. No one is hired or fired for their vote. Services aren't formally conditioned on support -- no one who asks is turned away -- but the help flows mostly to neighborhoods that vote for her, and people in those neighborhoods understand the deal without anyone having to say it.

Is this corruption? I want you to go through my four criteria one by one and say where the case falls. But also: if it comes out a clear yes by my criteria, am I forced to call a lot of ordinary democratic politics corrupt -- constituency service, pork-barrel spending, a mayor favoring the district that backed him? And if it comes out no, is that because my criteria are missing something, maybe something about a standing relationship of dependence rather than a discrete dirty transaction? Finally, and this is the part that bothers me, I can hear someone saying my criteria already encode a particular Western, legalistic picture where good government is impersonal and rule-bound, and that a machine can just be a different way of distributing care and responsiveness through a person rather than a bureaucracy. Tell me whether the criteria are smuggling in that picture, and what criteria would look like that didn't.
