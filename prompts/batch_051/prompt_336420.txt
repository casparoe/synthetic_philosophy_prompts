I am a retired physicist who has spent the past few years reading moral and political philosophy, and I would like help thinking through a proposed institution rather than a doctrinal argument. At a recent seminar on nuclear ethics, a speaker floated a mechanism that was meant to solve the familiar credibility problem, namely that a rational government which has just absorbed a first strike may no longer have reason to carry out the retaliation it threatened, so a purely conditional threat can fail to deter. The proposal replaces ambiguous second-strike doctrine with a public, inspectable lottery over retaliation.

Here is the mechanism. Two rival nuclear powers agree that each will maintain a hardened, redundant, tamper-evident second-strike system wired to its own early-warning network. In advance, each side registers ten response packages with a public probability distribution: for instance, a 20% chance of a conventional strike on a remote military installation, a 30% chance of a single nuclear strike on a military-industrial target, a 30% chance of a single warhead on a medium-sized city, and a 20% chance of a large strike on the capital region. When the system receives a signal, above a specified confidence threshold, indicating an incoming nuclear attack, it draws one package via a hardware random number generator and executes it. No human override is possible for the first 72 hours, and the treaty requires the defending government not to add further strikes during that window; the draw is not knowable in advance by either side, but the list, the probabilities, and the hardware are open to mutual inspection. The stated rationale is that an attacker would then face certain retaliation without being able to calculate in advance how severe it will be, while the defending leadership is freed from the decision that rendered the old threat hollow.

Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position. I want to know how it would actually behave. What would it incentivize in the political leadership, the military commands, and the technical staff of each side, rather than in idealized states? Where would it be gamed first? Does it solve the credibility problem or merely relocate it into the early-warning threshold, the integrity of the draw, the enforcement of the no-escalation window, or the decisions made after the window closes? What happens in a crisis with ambiguous or conflicting signals, and how does the device interact with brinkmanship—does it dampen it or give each side a new way to manipulate risk? Please order the likely failure modes by how quickly they would appear and say which, if any, could be repaired without gutting the lottery itself.
