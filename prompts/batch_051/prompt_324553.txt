You are an expert in epistemic game theory and an experienced examiner in an upper-level undergraduate seminar called “Rationality, Information, and Conventions.” You are going to write a 75-minute closed-book final-exam question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The exam must be completely self-contained: it should not ask for or use citations, bibliographies, or outside readings, and every technical concept it needs should be defined or implied by the question itself.

Before writing, think carefully about the iterated-deletion structure of the game below and the exact beliefs each player needs at each step. Do not show this private rough work in your final answer, but let it guide the model answer and marking scheme.

The question must be built around the following original two-player game, the Procurement Game. Anya chooses the row; Bo chooses the column. Payoffs are (Anya, Bo):

|         | Left   | Centre | Right  |
|---------|--------|--------|--------|
| Top     | (5, 4) | (4, 2) | (0, 1) |
| Middle  | (3, 3) | (3, 2) | (10, 0) |
| Bottom  | (1, 1) | (1, 5) | (10, 0) |

The question should be an extended problem, not multiple-choice, and should carry 100 marks. It should require the student to do all of the following:

(a) State the outcome obtained by iterated deletion of strictly dominated strategies, showing each round of deletion and whether the relevant dominance is in the original game or in a reduced game after earlier deletions.

(b) For each round, identify whose strategy is removed and specify the exact epistemic condition that justifies the deletion. Count the levels of reasoning from that player’s point of view, treating “Anya is rational” as requiring no knowledge of Bo’s rationality; “Anya believes Bo is rational” as first-order; “Anya believes Bo believes Anya is rational” as second-order; and so on. The question should explicitly require students to treat belief as probability-one belief within each player’s information set, and to say when the justifying condition concerns one player’s beliefs rather than a symmetric mutual-belief condition.

(c) Explain why common knowledge of rationality licenses the whole sequence, even though no fixed finite level of reasoning licenses the whole sequence in arbitrary games. The explanation should also distinguish common knowledge of rationality from common knowledge of the game itself, and from the assumption of common priors.

Then write:

1. The exam question itself, with subparts (a)–(c) and a sensible point allocation summing to 100.

2. A model answer of about 800 words. It should give the deletion sequence — Right; Bottom; Centre; Middle, leaving (Top, Left) — and should be precise about the epistemic conditions at each round. In particular, it should explain why Bo’s individual rationality alone eliminates Right, why Anya’s individual rationality alone does not eliminate Bottom, why Bo’s elimination of Centre requires second-order reasoning about Anya, and why Anya’s elimination of Middle requires third-order reasoning about Bo. It should not round every step off to the same epistemic level, and it should not treat common knowledge of rationality as something like a sixth sense over and above the hierarchy of finite levels.

3. A marking scheme that separates an A from a B answer. The point allocation should be built into the scheme, and for each subpart it should name the specific moves an A answer must contain and the specific mistakes that reduce an otherwise-sound answer to a B. At minimum, the scheme must address: (i) correctly identifying the deletion order and whether each dominance is in the reduced game; (ii) correctly assigning 0, 1, 2, and 3 levels to the four deletions rather than treating them all as the same; (iii) recognizing that the epistemic burden falls on a specific player’s beliefs at each stage, not on a vague symmetric “mutual” knowledge; (iv) distinguishing common knowledge of rationality from common knowledge of the game and from common priors; and (v) explaining why common knowledge, as the conjunction of all finite levels, is enough for this dominance-solvable game while no fixed finite level suffices generally. For each of (i)–(v), state the particular error a B answer would make — for example, counting the first deletion as requiring first-order knowledge, or saying that common knowledge of rationality produces a Nash equilibrium independently of the game’s payoff structure.
