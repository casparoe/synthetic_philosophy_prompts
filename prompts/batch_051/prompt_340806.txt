In my course on measurement and incentive design, we have been discussing Goodhart's law: roughly, that once a metric is made into a target, the behavior the metric was supposed to track and the metric itself tend to come apart. The same debate keeps appearing in a health-policy case we read. Hospitals are graded on thirty-day readmission rates, and after readmission became an explicit performance target, the measured rate fell. One side says the decline is mostly gaming, through reclassification, coding changes, and other maneuvers that lower the reported number without much improving patient outcomes. The other side says the decline reflects genuinely better discharge planning and follow-up care. I would like to know whether this disagreement is empirically resolvable, even in part. Please design the strongest test you can for distinguishing genuine improvement from Goodhart-style gaming of the readmission metric. Say what data, comparison, or result would count as evidence for each interpretation, and then be explicit about what even a clean result would not settle, including whether the line between gaming and improving can be drawn without already taking a stance on what health-care quality consists in.
