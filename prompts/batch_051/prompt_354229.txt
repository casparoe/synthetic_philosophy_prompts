I am a PhD student in philosophy working on early modern theories of the passions, and I want to use Spinoza’s treatment of hope to read Voltaire’s Candide. In the definitions of the affects appended to Part III of the Ethics, Spinoza characterizes hope as a pleasure arising from the idea of a future or past thing whose outcome we partly doubt; he notes that once the doubt is removed the affect changes into confidence, and that fear is the matching painful affect of doubt. Then in Part IV, Proposition 47, he argues that the affects of hope and fear cannot be good in themselves, because each contains something of sadness; his concession is that they can nevertheless be useful insofar as they check excessive pleasure. The larger suggestion seems to be that hope is not a virtue but an epistemic defect with a pleasant gloss: a fully rational person would live by what is certain and present, and would have less need of hope.

I want to read Candide through this lens without forcing it into a morality tale. Voltaire’s explicit target is Pangloss’s theoretical optimism, the doctrine that all events serve the best of all possible worlds, and the book punishes it relentlessly. But I am interested in whether the novel also tracks the passional economy Spinoza describes: again and again, the characters’ suffering seems to consist not just in the disasters themselves but in their attachment to uncertain future outcomes, reunion with Cunégonde, escape from Lisbon, the wealth of Eldorado, the eventual garden, and the narrative often treats that attachment as part of the disease rather than the cure. The ending, in which the survivors form a small society devoted to cultivating their plot, seems to replace hope with something Spinozist: present, local, controllable activity.

Please give me a close philosophical reading of Candide through Spinoza’s account of hope. Does the novel dramatize the claim that hope and fear cannot be good in themselves, or does it leave room for a modest form of hope that Spinoza’s definition would miss? Which episodes would you point to, and where does the work resist the Spinozist reading? I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: do not reduce the novel to an illustration of the Ethics, and do not soften Spinoza to make the pairing neat. If you think the pairing is basically mistaken, for instance because Voltaire’s target is a metaphysical doctrine about the world’s goodness while Spinoza’s target is a passional response to uncertainty, say so directly and tell me which philosophical issue remains live after the failed reading.
