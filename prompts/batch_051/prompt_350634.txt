A public-health agency establishes the following rule for its disease-forecasting unit. Every quantitative forecast submitted for publication must be stated as an imprecise probability: for each proposition of interest, the forecaster gives a lower and an upper probability (for instance, 35 to 60 percent for the proposition that a pandemic begins this decade), and no point estimate may appear in the written summary. Forecasters are scored annually by a rule that rewards intervals when the outcome falls within the stated range and penalizes average width, so that both the vacuous zero-to-one interval and an over-narrow interval fare poorly. Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a position in the epistemology of imprecise probabilities. Analyze how it would actually behave: what it would incentivize among forecasters, how supervisors and outside readers would use or work around the intervals, how it could be gamed, what second-order effects it would have on the published record, and whether an additional rule could fix the most serious failure mode. Answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
