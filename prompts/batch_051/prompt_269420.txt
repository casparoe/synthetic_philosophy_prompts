In our ethics seminar we have been discussing rescue cases, and after last week's session two of my classmates got into an argument I could not settle. The setup, which both accepted, was this: a runaway rail car is heading toward five stranded strangers, and you can redirect it onto a spur where it will kill one different stranger. You can save exactly one group or the other, and none of the six differs in any other morally relevant respect.

The first, Daniel, said the right act is obviously to redirect and save the five: five deaths are worse than one, and anyone who flips a coin is treating one life as if it were as important as five. The second, Mara, said that this is precisely the move she rejects, and she defended the position from a reading by Taurek, namely that the numbers do not by themselves settle what to do. For her, each of the six has an equal claim to your help, and the only procedure that respects the equality of those claims is to give each an equal chance—hence a coin toss. She said aggregating the five deaths into "the worse outcome" is the mistake: losses are suffered one life at a time, and there is no single subject who bears the loss of all five.

Daniel answered that equal claims do not require equal chances; they require equal consideration, and considering five people equally just is counting each of them once. That means the side with five claims has a claim five times as strong, he said. A coin toss instead lets one person's claim nullify five other people's claims, which is not equal consideration at all. Mara replied that this already presupposes that claims can be added across persons. A claim is only ever held by an individual, she said, and there is no separate claimant called "the five" whose claim outweighs the one's.

Daniel then pressed her with numbers. If it were one against a thousand, he asked, would she really flip a coin? She said the implication is stark but she accepts it: each of the thousand-and-one gets an equal chance, and no individual has been treated as less than any other. Daniel called this a reductio. He said it shows her view ceases to be about rescue at all—a rescuer who refuses to count the people he is saving has abandoned the point of saving. Mara said the point of saving is to give each person what they are owed, and the one person on the spur is owed no less than any one of the thousand merely because he is outnumbered.

They asked me to referee, but by then I could not see which of their points were right and which were rhetoric. Could you go through their exchange and say who is right about what, and who has the stronger overall position?
