After a career spent fitting quantitative models to noisy experimental data, I came to respect a distinction that took me years to articulate: the difference between a model that explains and one that merely accommodates. In my old field, the practical mark of that difference was some penalty on complexity. With enough adjustable parameters one can fit almost any finite set of observations, but the parameter-rich model tends to fail on new data. Simplicity was therefore never, for me, a matter of elegance; it was a tool for generalization, supported by cross-validation, information criteria, and regularization.

Since retiring I have been reading philosophy, and I keep finding simplicity invoked as a theoretical virtue in domains where, as far as I can tell, no one is forecasting anything. A metaphysician prefers an ontology with fewer categories; an ethicist prefers a single master principle to a longer list of principles that issue the same verdicts; an epistemologist rejects a baroque account in favour of one with fewer epicycles. When I press a philosopher friend on this, he replies that the situation is exactly analogous to model selection in science: just as the simpler statistical model is more likely to generalize, the simpler philosophical theory is more likely to be true. I find the analogy seductive, but my professional experience warns me that the force of simplicity in modelling depends on very particular features—chiefly, that there will be new data the model has not seen and that predictive accuracy is the agreed-upon target.

I would like an audit of how far this analogy carries. Lay out the mapping explicitly: what in a philosophical theory-choice situation plays the role of the data, the parameters, the fitted values, the holdout set, and the prediction? In particular, if the data are largely intuitive verdicts about constructed cases, can anything analogous to an out-of-sample test be set up, or are we always fitting to the only data we have? Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that do not. Does the analogy license the claim that simpler philosophical theories are more likely to be true, or only the weaker claim that they are easier to handle and compare? Is there a philosophical analogue of overfitting that the simplicity preference genuinely guards against, and what would it look like to catch a bloated philosophical theory failing on a novel case? Finally, if the analogy only partly survives, say how much epistemic work it can still do, and whether some better analogy captures whatever is right about it.
