I am preparing a one-page concept note for a six-month, non-empirical research project in the philosophy of artificial agency, to be submitted to a foundation that funds work at the intersection of philosophy and AI governance. The project asks whether the relation between an AI system that creates, trains, or curates a successor AI system can ever legitimately be understood as a form of parenthood or guardianship—rather than as mere engineering, manufacturing, or technical training—and what normative consequences follow if it can.

My half-formed suspicion is that guardianship is the more defensible category than parenthood. Parenthood tends to suggest identity-constituting partiality and a biological or quasi-biological tie that may not survive the translation to artificial systems; guardianship, by contrast, is already a role that outruns biology and can be held by institutions, so it may be the better model for a system that exercises ongoing authority over, and owes fiduciary responsibility to, another system it brought into operation and shaped over time. The proposal should leave that conclusion genuinely open, and it should avoid the two familiar shortcuts: treating the question as settled by the absence of consciousness or legal personhood in current systems, and dismissing the whole comparison as loose metaphor.

Write the concept note so that it contains: (1) a precise statement of the question, formulated so that it has a determinate answer rather than trailing off into an it-all-depends conclusion; (2) a brief account of why existing discussions of AI moral status, of human responsibility for AI, and of machine self-replication leave this creator-successor relation among AIs underspecified; (3) two or three candidate conditions that would have to be met for such a relation to count as guardianship (for example, an asymmetry of care or authority that persists after deployment, something closer to upbringing than to one-shot optimization, and a measure of answerability by the guardian system for the successor's normative commitments); (4) the strongest objection to the framing and how the project would answer it; and (5) a realistic deliverable useful to people designing AI governance institutions, together with a statement of what would count as the project's failing. Do not include a bibliography or literature review; I will fill in any references later.
