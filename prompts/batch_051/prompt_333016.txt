I'm a physics PhD student and I've been going back and forth about Zeno's arrow paradox. The standard undergraduate story is that Zeno's mistake was treating an instant as a tiny interval: the arrow has a position at each instant but no velocity, so saying it's at rest at each instant is a category mistake—rest means same position across some interval, and motion is just different positions at different times. I always found that satisfying enough. But then I got into the quantum Zeno effect, where if you keep measuring an unstable system often enough you can freeze its evolution—the watched pot that really doesnt boil. The usual gloss is that repeatedly projecting the system onto its initial state suppresses the transitions into other states, so the thing never decays. And now I cant shake the feeling that this is basically Zeno with better PR: you check the arrow at every instant, find it 'not changing,' and the checked system then never changes. The difference, I guess, is that in the quantum case the measurement itself is doing physical work, whereas Zeno thought he could get the conclusion with no measurement at all—just from the fact that the arrow occupies a place equal to itself at each instant. So here's what I want to know: does the quantum Zeno effect actually shore up the arrow paradox, or is it a false friend that just shares the name? If it does shore it up, which premise of Zeno's argument does it rescue and what's the repaired argument? If it doesn't, what exactly does the quantum case show that the standard calculus-style reply to Zeno either misses or gets right?
