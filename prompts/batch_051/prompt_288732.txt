I was at a party the other night and a friend of mine, who's usually pretty careful about this stuff, was insisting that the Ship of Theseus has basically been settled by biology. His claim: since the human body replaces most of its cells over time, you are the ship. The material parts all get swapped out while the overall form — the organization, whatever that means exactly — stays the same, so worrying about whether you're the same person as the kid in the old photos is exactly like worrying about whether the museum's ship is the original one. He wasn't too precise about what maps onto what, and I think that's where the thing starts to leak.

Could you audit the analogy? Lay out the correspondences — planks and cells, the two ships (the continuously repaired one and the one rebuilt from the discarded planks) and the two counterpart "persons" if both a continuously lived life and an old-parts-reassembly were on the table; the shipwright who replaces planks and the whoever-or-whatever that replaces cells in my body. Then say where the analogy breaks, and please separate the disanalogies that actually matter for his conclusion from the ones that are just irrelevant differences. What does the analogy quietly import about identity just by putting it in those terms? And does the conclusion — that personal identity is the same kind of thing as artifact identity, so the question is just a practical label rather than a deep fact — survive once all that is out in the open? A table of correspondences is fine if that helps. Write it in American English.
