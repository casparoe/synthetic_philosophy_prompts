I'm a metaethics grad student and I've been going around in circles about whether the is-ought gap is a real constraint on derivations or just a way of saying you have to look at the premises harder. Here's an argument I've been workshopping. It starts from what look like purely descriptive facts about pain and ends with a claim about reasons. I can't tell if its valid, and if it isn't, which premise is doing the sneaky work.

(1) Pain is a state that, in any creature capable of having it, constitutively involves being disposed to avoid that state for its own sake and not merely as a means.

(2) To say a state is bad for a creature just is to say the creature is constitutively disposed to avoid that state for its own sake. (I'm treating this as a reductive unpacking of badness-for, the way some people take "adaptation" to be unpacked by selection history, not as an inference from one property to a distinct one.)

(3) So pain is bad for the creature that has it.

(4) If a state is bad for a creature, then, other things equal, anyone in a position to reduce how much the creature is in that state has a pro tanto reason to do so.

(5) Therefore, other things equal, anyone in a position to reduce a creature's pain has a pro tanto reason to do so.

My question is which numbered step actually carries the normative weight. Is (2) a disguised evaluative premise, in which case the inference to (3) is fine but the gap hasn't been crossed at all? Or is (2) a perfectly good descriptive definition and the action is all in (4), which I just asserted? Or does accepting (2) somehow make (4) trivial? I want a precise verdict: is the argument valid, and if not, exactly which step fails, under which reading of the disputed step?
