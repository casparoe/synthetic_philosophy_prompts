I am a clinical psychologist trained in motivational interviewing and trauma-informed care, with no formal background in philosophy. Restate the Schopenhauer–Nietzsche dispute about pity in the vocabulary of contemporary helping practice. Schopenhauer holds that compassion is the single genuine moral motive: to act well is to act from an immediate, felt participation in another's suffering rather than from self-interest. Nietzsche holds that pity is a pathology: it deepens the total quantity of suffering, demeans the one who is pitied, and feeds the pitier's hidden need to feel superior. Translate each position into the constructs I work with daily: empathic concern versus personal distress, compassion satisfaction and compassion fatigue, dependency-oriented versus autonomy-supportive helping, alliance and rupture in the therapeutic relationship, and the rescuer role in the drama triangle. Then give a residue report: which parts of the debate translate cleanly, which parts must be distorted to fit clinical language, and which parts cannot be expressed in this vocabulary at all, including what may be lost on the metaphysical side. I want to know whether modern psychology has effectively settled their fight by separating healthy compassion from pathological pity, or whether that separation merely rebrands a philosophical disagreement that clinical practice cannot adjudicate.
