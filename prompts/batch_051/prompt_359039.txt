I am a machine learning engineer with a solid working knowledge of supervised learning: training sets, labels, hypothesis spaces, empirical risk, overfitting, regularization, feature engineering, and model selection. I have no formal background in philosophy. I want you to restate a philosophical problem—Goodman's 'new riddle of induction,' built around the predicate 'grue'—entirely in the vocabulary I work with, and then give me a residue report.

Here is the canonical statement for this exercise. Fix a time t0 in the near future, say midnight at the end of this year. Define an observation of an emerald made at time t as grue if and only if: either t is before t0 and the observation is green, or t is at or after t0 and the observation is blue. Call H_green the hypothesis that every emerald observation is green. Call H_grue the hypothesis that every emerald observation is grue. Every emerald observation made so far was made before t0 and was green; by the definition, each of those observations is also grue. The two hypotheses therefore fit all the data equally well, yet they diverge on an emerald first observed after t0: H_green predicts green, H_grue predicts blue. The philosophical problem is to say what, beyond custom or habit, privileges H_green over H_grue—or, more generally, what distinguishes projectible predicates such as green from gerrymandered ones such as grue. To sharpen the point: define blue-before-t0-and-green-after-t0 as 'bleen.' Then green can be expressed as 'grue before t0, bleen after t0,' which looks just as disjunctive as grue. Any appeal to the greater simplicity of green must therefore explain why the green/blue basis is privileged over the grue/bleen basis.

Now translate. I want you to say precisely what plays the role of the training set, an individual observation, the label space, the candidate hypotheses, the loss or scoring rule, the claim that the two hypotheses are equally well supported, the post-t0 divergence, and the green/blue versus grue/bleen reparametrization as a change of basis or label encoding. Be exact about how you would represent H_green and H_grue, and what an inductive bias, prior, or regularizer would have to look like to select between them.

Then give me the residue report in three parts. First, which parts of the riddle map cleanly onto familiar facts about underdetermination by finite data, overfitting, and the representation-dependence of what counts as simple. Second, which parts you had to distort or fudge to force the fit. Third, which parts have no counterpart in machine learning at all.

Finally, tell me whether my working suspicion is right. My suspicion is that Goodman's riddle is nothing more than the everyday point that training data underdetermine the function and that 'simpler' is relative to a chosen representation—so the only real question is which prior or feature space to adopt. I want to know whether that suspicion captures the whole puzzle, or whether there is a residue in Goodman's problem—about which hypotheses are genuinely projectible, or about what justifies a feature basis—that ML vocabulary cannot state and that is not dispelled by pointing to a prior. Keep the translation honest: do not smuggle in a preferred feature representation or a prior and then declare the riddle dissolved.
