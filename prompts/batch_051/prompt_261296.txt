I am currently taking a philosophy of science course in which we have been working through Hempel's ravens paradox, and I also work part-time as a research assistant in a conservation biology lab. The lab runs a long-term monitoring program for a regionally threatened marsh bird — for present purposes, call it the bronze-winged coot — whose field identification relies on the universal claim that every breeding male shows a distinctive white wing bar. This field mark is used to decide whether a sighting is recorded as this species and sex, and it feeds into annual reports to the state wildlife agency. My supervisor has asked me to help overhaul the protocol we use when citizen-science photos and field logs arrive each week. The current protocol is simple and, I now suspect, philosophically naive: a record showing a breeding male with the wing bar is logged as supporting the universal claim; a record showing a breeding male without it is logged as refuting it; and every other record is ignored. This seems to run directly into Hempel's point, since the contrapositive of "All breeding males have the white wing bar" is "Everything lacking the white wing bar is not a breeding male of this species," and our current protocol treats almost all the records relevant to that contrapositive as irrelevant.

Please write a reusable decision procedure that our lab could actually run each week. It should be usable by undergraduate field technicians with no philosophy training, should take no more than fifteen minutes for a batch of records, and should walk through the questions in order: which records count as evidence for or against the universal field mark, how much each record should move our confidence in the field mark, what triggers a recommendation to update the field guide, where the procedure stops with "no decision," and where it hands the judgment to a senior ornithologist because the question involves modeling choices or values rather than data alone. Be explicit about what the ravens paradox does and does not force us to say at each step, about the role of background knowledge such as the fact that a record is already known to show another species, and about which distinctions from the philosophy must survive the simplification and which can be dropped. Then run the procedure on three records from the coming week: (1) a clear photo of a breeding male with the white wing bar; (2) a clear photo of an unmistakable female mallard with no white wing bar, taken at the same wetland; and (3) a blurry photo that probably shows a breeding male but in which the wing bar is simply not visible because of shadow. Make the worked applications concrete, including how the procedure handles a record that functions as Hempel's "white shoe," and ensure that at least one of the three cases yields a conclusion my supervisor will resist, with a note on why they will resist it. Please write the answer in American English.
