Paul Feyerabend's historical argument against a fixed scientific method has a pull on me that is hard to shake. He observes that rules once treated as constitutive of good science — staying consistent with established theory, rejecting ad hoc moves, demanding that new hypotheses yield testable predictions before they are taken seriously — were violated by episodes we now regard as exemplary, and he concludes that the idea of a universal scientific method is a myth: methodological rules have exceptions, and methodological standards are themselves historically mutable products of scientific practice. On this view, the only maxim with real historical survival value is something like: in methodology, anything goes.

I find that conclusion genuinely useful. It explains why science has succeeded through opportunistic, theory-laden, rhetorically situated work rather than through a clean recipe, and it inoculates me against textbook accounts of science that collapse on contact with the historical record. But I also hold a firm intuition that not all methods are on a par. Double-blind randomized trials are a real epistemic improvement over clinical anecdote for questions about treatment efficacy; preregistration and independent replication are not mere tribal customs; and there is a robust difference in reliability between, say, the practices of modern astronomy and those of astrology. I do not merely prefer the former; I think it is methodologically better, and I would act on that difference even in an unfamiliar culture. If anything goes, I am not sure I can say that.

The question is what should yield. If I keep the Feyerabendian conclusion, do I have to treat my intuition as a parochial preference that is itself just one more local habit, and thereby lose the ability to say that double-blinding was an advance rather than a change? If I keep the intuition, am I re-committing to a universal method whose failure the historians have already demonstrated? Or is there a third reading on which the two are compatible — for example, a position that denies only exceptionless, context-free rules while still allowing stable, domain-specific comparisons of methodological quality? I want a negotiated verdict. For each of the three moves — debunk the intuition, amend the theory, or reconcile them — spell out what would justify it and what hidden cost it incurs. In particular, if you defend reconciliation, say exactly what methodological anarchism means once one admits that double-blinding is better than anecdote, and whether anything goes can itself be stated without self-contradiction.
