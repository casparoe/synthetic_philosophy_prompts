I set out thinking I held the conventional view that in a system with a written constitution and a legislature there is no general doctrine of desuetude: an unrepealed statute is law until a court says otherwise. But my reactions to the cases below do not all fit that slogan.

(a) A city ordinance from the 1890s prohibits flying a kite inside the city limits without a police permit. It has never been repealed. No permit has been applied for or issued for over a century; the police have no forms for it and would not know how to issue one; and hundreds of people fly kites in the city parks every year without protest. I think a prosecution under this ordinance today would be invalid, not merely unwelcome. I would not call it law any longer.

(b) A provision in the rules of civil procedure says that notices of appeal must be served by first-class post. For the past fifteen years the courts have accepted service by email, the profession treats email as standard, and no one has relied on the postal rule in a contested matter. I think the postal rule has been overtaken; a party who insisted on strict compliance with it would be behaving vexatiously, and the court would be right to treat the email practice as the operative rule.

(c) Adultery remains a criminal offence in my jurisdiction. It has not been prosecuted for forty years, and prosecutors describe that as a settled policy. Yet when asked whether the statute is still law, I find myself saying: yes, it is law; it is merely a bad law that the authorities decline to enforce. I would not put it in the same category as the kite ordinance, even though the only difference I can immediately name is that adultery is still widely thought to be a serious wrong.

(d) A statute creating a minor regulatory fee gives the revenue authority a discretion about collection. The authority announces in writing that it will not collect the fee for five years while it upgrades its systems. At the end of the five years it announces that it will begin collecting the fee from the next tax year. I think it may do so, and that the fee was never repealed; it was merely held in abeyance by a lawful exercise of discretion.

(e) A by-law once required any resident who used their home for trade to maintain a hitching post for horses. It has been wholly forgotten since the motor car. I think it cannot possibly found a prosecution or a fine now; unlike the adultery statute, I would say it is not law any more.

(f) A criminal statute against sleeping rough in public has not been enforced for a generation, but only because the responsible officials have deprioritised it, and they have explicitly reserved the right to resume enforcement. A new administration begins using it in a targeted way. I think the statute is law; my objection to the new policy, and I have one, is about its cruelty, not about its legal validity.

(g) A constitutional rule requiring a government to resign after losing a vote of confidence has never been written down anywhere, but it has been followed without exception for as long as anyone can recall. I treat it as binding, and I am not moved by the fact that a government could break it without committing any literally enacted offence.

The case I cannot settle is this. A statute makes it an offence to sell certain traditional herbal remedies unless they pass a safety certification that nobody has ever, in practice, been able to obtain or been required to obtain for the past thirty years; an entire retail trade has grown up in reliance on its non-enforcement. A newly appointed regulator announces, without warning and without a grace period, that she will begin enforcing the statute immediately. I cannot decide whether the thirty years of non-enforcement have given the sellers a legal answer to the prosecution, or only a political grievance.

Is there a single principle that captures all seven of my verdicts, or close to all of them? If there is, I would like it stated precisely, with each of its clauses tied to the case that forces it, and with its verdicts on the herbal-remedies case and on one further case of your invention. If no single principle captures everything without becoming a list of exceptions, tell me which of my verdicts I should give up, and what the most defensible replacement principle then is.
