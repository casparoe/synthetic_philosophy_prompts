I keep returning to Dr. Strangelove as a way of thinking about what the autonomous-weapons debate means by meaningful human control, and I want a determined reading rather than a survey of positions. The film stages the problem from two directions at once. The Soviet doomsday machine is an autonomous retaliation system built to be beyond human interference: once triggered it fires automatically, and any attempt to stop it only sets it off. Meanwhile the humans nominally in control are a general who launches a first strike on his own authority, a bomber crew that becomes unreachable once the go-code is sent and proceeds on standing orders, and a room full of leaders who cannot recall the one plane that matters. My question is what the film actually shows about the demand for meaningful human control. Is it a reductio of that demand, because the humans in the film turn out to be no more meaningfully in control than the machine? Or does it show the demand is right but badly underspecified, because formal human control can be hollow when the human is mad, misinformed, or locked into procedures? I want you to commit to one reading and defend it against the strongest version of the other: say what the film complicates about the human-in-the-loop position, and where it nevertheless vindicates the thought that the real problem is the conditions under which human judgment can count as control at all.
