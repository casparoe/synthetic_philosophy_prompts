I've been rereading Spinoza's Ethics lately, specifically Part V where he talks about the intellectual love of God, and I've hit something that feels like it proves too much. I don't have a settled reading yet, and I'd rather not jump straight to "Spinoza contradicted himself" because that seems lazy. In the scholium to Proposition 36, Spinoza says something like the mind's intellectual love toward God is not really separate from God's own infinite self-love, just that divine self-love as it can be explained through the essence of a particular human mind under the aspect of eternity. So the human side of the blessedest state is, at bottom, God loving himself through a finite expression.

Here's the argument I've been toying with, put as formal steps:

(1) Spinoza's necessitarianism says every idea, every affect, and every state of a finite mind follows from the necessity of the divine nature in exactly the same way as everything else.  
(2) The intellectual love of God is one such state of a finite mind: a joy accompanied by the idea of God as its cause, arising from intuitive knowledge.  
(3) From (1), whether I reach the intellectual love of God, or remain stuck in passive affects, is itself necessary; there is no real open possibility of both outcomes from which I could choose.  
(4) But Spinoza's whole ethical trajectory in Parts IV and V seems to be about striving, increasing one's power of acting, and moving from bondage to the intellectual love of God; it's full of what reads as guidance about how to live.  
(5) If (3) is true, that guidance can't be doing what it looks like doing: it can't be giving me a genuine task whose outcome depends on my effort.  
(6) Yet Spinoza plainly treats the intellectual love of God as the highest blessedness, and not as something that merely happens to some people by luck.  
(7) So either Spinoza's necessitarianism undermines his account of the intellectual love of God as an ethical achievement, or the ethical language is not really normative, which then makes nonsense of "blessedness" as something worth orienting a life toward.

I'd most like to keep premises (1) and (6), because they seem the most clearly Spinozist. But something has to give. Where does this go wrong, if it does? Is the mistake an equivocation over what "necessary" means, or over what Spinoza means by striving, or is the conclusion actually forced and Spinoza's system is just in bad trouble? If you reject a step, say exactly which one and what the corrected version would be.
