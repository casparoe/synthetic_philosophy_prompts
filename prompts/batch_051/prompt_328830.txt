I have recently been appointed to a state law-revision commission, and in three weeks I will present a working paper to the commission's advisory workshop. The paper defends a middle position on desuetude: I do not claim that long-unenforced criminal statutes are void or automatically repealed, only that courts should treat open, consistent, long-standing non-enforcement as a rebuttable presumption that applying the statute would fail the rule-of-law requirement of fair notice, so that the burden shifts to the prosecution to show that enforcement would not be arbitrary or surprising. My compressed argument is that the damage to the rule of law is already done by the gap between the written criminal law and the law as actually enforced; a notice-based desuetude rule does not create that gap but makes it justiciable and reviewable, and it blocks the worst case, which is selective or sudden resurrection of a dormant offense. The audience will be mostly rule-of-law theorists with formalist leanings, plus two criminal law scholars who work on selective enforcement and suspect desuetude would merely ratify whatever enforcement patterns happened to survive, and one senior litigator who distrusts judicial discretion and keeps asking why legislative repeal is inadequate. They are broadly committed to law guiding conduct and will press on whether a doctrine with an inevitably vague threshold—how long is long, how open is open, how consistent is consistent—can itself satisfy legality.

I would like a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a defense of the thesis. Please give me the objections in roughly the order I should expect them, phrased as these particular people would put them, and for each objection give: its strongest version, what I should concede at once, what I should hold, a two- to three-minute reply, and a note on precisely where that reply is soft. Include the question I should be hoping nobody asks, and say why it would hurt. If the task as I have framed it rests on a confused premise about desuetude, the rule of law, or fair notice, tell me so plainly instead of writing the prep sheet politely around the confusion.
