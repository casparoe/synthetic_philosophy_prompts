I keep turning over an argument a friend made when we were arguing about frugality. The question was whether frugality is something you should *be* or something you should *spread*, and he talked himself into the conclusion that it is neither. I'll lay out his argument as best I can.

(1) There are two ways for frugality to be a good thing: either being frugal is a duty in itself (the deontological reading), or frugality is good only because of its consequences—less waste, lower consumption, a cushion for bad times (the consequentialist reading). No third option.

(2) The deontological reading fails, because a duty has to be universalizable. Try universalizing the maxim spend as little of your money as you can. If everyone followed it, demand would collapse, businesses would close, people would be thrown out of work, and the very security frugality was supposed to buy would disappear. So the universalized maxim undermines what made it worth willing, which means it is not a duty. Frugality is not a duty.

(3) So we are left with the consequentialist reading.

(4) If frugality is good because of its consequences, then what you ought to do is whatever act does the most toward the goods frugality is supposed to secure—waste avoided, resources conserved, resilience built.

(5) But a single person's frugality has no consequences worth mentioning. The shop orders the same stock, the factory runs the same line, the waste stream is the same, and no aggregate statistic moves. One frugal person is a rounding error.

(6) So on the consequentialist reading you should not really bother being frugal. What actually moves the relevant outcomes is getting many people to change their behavior. You should promote frugality—persuade, campaign, build social norms—and then spend your own money however you like, since that part does nothing.

(7) But the same reasoning applies to everyone. The ideal end state of the consequentialist reading is a society where everyone works to make everyone else frugal while nobody practices any frugality themselves.

(8) And that is absurd. A value that no one has reason to embody, only to promote, is not a value in the ordinary sense at all—its a marketing department.

(9) So both readings fail, and frugality is not a good thing in any sense.

My friend was unnerved by his own conclusion, and so am I, because the same train of thought would apparently destroy lots of everyday virtues: first demand they be universalizable, then show that universal performance would be self-defeating, then reduce them to things we merely tout. Frugality obviously is a real virtue. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost—what has to be true about duties, consequences, or collective action for the rest of the argument to stay standing?
