I have been working through Fear and Trembling and find myself convinced, or nearly so, of the position its pseudonymous author advances: that there is such a thing as a teleological suspension of the ethical — that an individual can stand in an absolute relation to God which justifies setting aside the universal ethical demand, so that Abraham's readiness to sacrifice Isaac is not murder wearing a religious costume but a genuinely different order of act, one that cannot be rendered intelligible in ethical terms or made legible to any third party. I want to stress-test this conviction rather than defend it. What would count against it? I am not primarily asking for the familiar charge that it licenses every zealot who claims a private revelation, unless that charge can be made precise enough to bite. I want a map of the position's exposure. Which observations, cases, arguments, or conceptual results would a defender of the teleological suspension have to concede as counting against the view — not merely as things its critics say, but as genuine costs the defender would themselves recognize? Which would be decisive and which merely awkward? In particular: is the fact that the knight of faith cannot be distinguished from the madman, even in principle, a defect in the position or the point of it? Which of the position's commitments — about the structure of duty, or the privacy of the God-relationship — is the most exposed? Does the position risk anything at all, or is it insulated by the claim that faith has no outward criterion? If it is insulated, is that insulation a sign of depth or of unfalsifiability in a bad sense? I would find it most persuasive if you could identify a specific condition under which the position would collapse — for instance, a successful argument that a suspension of the ethical would itself have to be licensed by an ethical consideration and would therefore be no suspension at all. Tell me, honestly, whether the position is well insulated, and whether that is a problem.
