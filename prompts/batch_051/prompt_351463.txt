A guy in my seminar is trying to get a group of us to actually run a small betting club along those lines, and I can't decide if his setup is a clean application of the lecture or a slow-motion train wreck. Here's what he's proposing. Five of us each put $50 a week into a shared bankroll, nobody can withdraw until end of semester, and then we split whatever's left evenly. Before any bet, each member privately submits their own probability for the outcome. The club uses the median of the five, and if those median odds give a positive edge over the bookmaker's line, the bet is on. The stake is then set to the full Kelly fraction of the current bankroll at the median odds — no fractional-Kelly haircut, no cap on how large the fraction can be. If several bets are available at once, he says we should size each one by full Kelly independently and then add the stakes, which I'm pretty sure isn't right when the bets overlap, but I couldn't say exactly how it breaks. His pitch is that this operationalizes geometric rationality: the median keeps any one person's crank probability from capturing the pool, full Kelly maximizes the expected growth of the log-bankroll, and because we only cash out at the end, terminal-bankroll reasoning is the correct frame. I go back and forth between thinking this is a neat little implementation of the lecture and thinking its a disaster with extra steps. Walk me through how it would actually play out over a semester: what each member is incentivized to do when submitting estimates, where the first gaming opportunities are (strategic medians, correlated bets, stalling losses into later weeks, whatever you think matters), what full Kelly does to the path of the bankroll when the consensus odds are imperfect, and what the most likely failure mode is. Then tell me whether it's salvageable with a small tweak — fractional Kelly, making people pay for their estimates, some kind of yes/no gate — or whether the whole structure is fighting itself.
