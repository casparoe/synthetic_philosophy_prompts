I'm a philosophy professor putting together a seminar on what makes something the same word, and I've run into a tangle I can't quite name. The canned story is that walked is just a form of the word walk, while walker is a different word: -ed is inflection and -er is derivation. But as I collect examples, the boundary gets less and less cooperative. The suffix -ish, as in greenish, doesn't change the part of speech, and speakers clearly treat greenish as a shade of green rather than a new lexical item, yet dictionaries gives it its own entry and no one calls it an inflected form. Diminutives like -ito in Spanish work similarly: casita feels like the little version of casa rather than a new word, can be stacked, and still gets filed under derivation in the textbooks. Then there's the classic case of the broken window: is broken a form of the verb break or a derived adjective? The usual tests — obligatoriness, productivity, category change, how close to the root the affix sits — point in different directions for the very same item.

I already know the textbook definitions of inflection and derivation; that's not the problem. What I want to know is whether this in-between territory has a recognized name of its own: is there an established concept for morphemes that are inflection-like in some respects and derivation-like in others, and if the middle zone has no standard label, what would you call it, and how exactly would you carve its boundries against the clear cases on either side? I'm not after a bare label; I need the carving — what the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where its edges are.
