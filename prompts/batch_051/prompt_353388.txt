In the synthetic-biology discussions at my company, a colleague keeps arguing that the genome is literally a program: the genome is source code, transcription is a copy routine, the ribosome is the interpreter, and the cell is the runtime. From this he draws a stronger conclusion. He points to typogenetics — a toy formal system in which strands of letters encode enzymes that cut, copy, and rearrange strands, and in which some strands happen to encode the enzyme that copies them — and claims that this self-replication is the same formal move as Gödel numbering, the scheme by which a formula is made to refer to its own number. Because a strand carries both its own data and the instructions for operating on that data, he says, a genome is a quine and a living system is a formal system in the relevant sense. On that basis he claims the limits of formal systems — incompleteness, undecidability, the halting problem — carry over to biology: some truths about an organism would be no more provable from its genome than a Gödel sentence is provable from its axioms.

I find the analogy appealing as engineering, but I want it audited before I accept the conclusion. Please work through it explicitly: what maps onto what when the genome is read as a program (codons, genes, ribosomes, proteins, mutations, regulation), and where the mapping breaks — in particular, which features of the program analogy are quietly imported that a cell does not actually have. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the Gödel-style conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Finally, say whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what a better analogy would be, if any.
