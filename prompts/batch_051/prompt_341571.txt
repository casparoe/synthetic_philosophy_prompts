I chair a small philosophical society whose constitution I compiled some years ago, and we are now in the middle of a procedural dispute that has split the membership three ways. The governing clause reads:

"This constitution may be amended at any general meeting by a resolution supported by two-thirds of the members present and voting, provided that written notice of the proposed amendment, including its full text, has been circulated to all members at least fourteen days before the meeting."

At our last meeting, a faction proposed — with the required notice and with every other procedural requirement satisfied — the following amendment: "Any future amendment of this constitution shall require a three-quarters majority of the members present and voting, and this requirement shall apply to the amendment by which it is adopted." The resolution received seventy per cent of the votes cast. One group says the resolution passed under the old two-thirds clause and simply failed to raise the threshold for itself, since no threshold can retroactively govern the very vote that created it. A second group says the resolution must be treated as having failed, because it expressly made the higher threshold applicable to its own adoption, and seventy per cent is less than three-quarters. A third group says the amendment clause cannot be used to alter its own requirements at all, since a rule cannot coherently authorize a change to the conditions of its own validity.

I care less about the immediate parliamentary outcome than about the underlying interpretive question. Starting from the quoted clause, spell out what each of these would say about the case: (i) a plain textual reading of the amendment clause; (ii) the purpose of an amendment procedure of this kind; (iii) what the drafters would most plausibly have said if, when the clause was adopted, they had been asked whether it could be used to raise its own threshold and apply that threshold to the vote doing the raising; and (iv) what a reasonable member reading the constitution today would understand it to permit. Then say which of these approaches should govern here and why, and assess the third group's claim that self-amendment is incoherent rather than merely unintended.
