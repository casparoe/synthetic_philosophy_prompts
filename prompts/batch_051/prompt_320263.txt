I have been trying to settle a dispute about statutes that remain on the books but are never enforced, and my usual test for when a law is in force comes apart on the key case. The test has four conditions. A law is in force only if (1) it was validly enacted by a proper authority through a proper procedure; (2) it has not been repealed, expired, or been struck down by a court; (3) relevant officials still treat it as a reason for action in practice — prosecutors charge under it, judges apply it, regulators issue citations under it, or at least officials with authority to enforce it openly stand ready to do so; and (4) the conduct it regulates is still the kind of conduct that can be brought under the terms of the statute without distortion.

Now consider a case that strains these conditions. In 1925 a state enacted a statute making it a criminal offense for a retail store to open for business on Sunday. The statute was actively enforced for roughly forty years. Then, after a state supreme court decision in 1965 upheld the statute against a constitutional challenge, enforcement quietly stopped. For the next half-century, retail stores opened on Sundays openly and on a large scale; no prosecutor brought a charge, no regulator issued a citation, and no official of the state so much as warned that the statute might be enforced. The legislature never repealed the statute, even while repeatedly amending the surrounding commercial code. Last year, a newly elected prosecutor revived the statute and brought charges against a chain of grocery stores for Sunday opening. The defendant argues that the statute has lapsed through desuetude — the gradual loss of legal force through prolonged, universal non-enforcement. The prosecutor replies that a validly enacted statute remains law until the legislature repeals it, and that anyone doing business on Sunday in the state was on notice of the risk.

Apply my four conditions and say whether, on the best reading of them, the 1925 statute is still a law in force. Work through the conditions one by one, and say plainly where they conflict with one another or fail to determine an answer. Should I revise the test — drop a condition, add one, or reweight them — and if so, how? And then face the wider question: is the prosecutor's revival of this statute better characterized as applying law that was already in force, or as making new law with an old text? Does the rule-of-law value of fair notice favor the defendant or the prosecutor, and does your answer about desuetude commit you on that?
