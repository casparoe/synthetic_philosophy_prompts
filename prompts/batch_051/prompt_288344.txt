I cover the science and ideas desk, so I've sat through more consciousness-research talks than I can count where the speaker waves at 'heterophenomenology' as if everyone in the room knows what it is. As far as I can peice together, it's Dennett's method for studying consciousness: you collect what subjects say about their experience, treat those reports as the data, and refuse to settle the question of whether the reports are true about some inner realm. I keep thinking this sounds exactly like what we do in journalism, and nobody has ever managed to convince me it isn't.

So here's the task. Restate heterophenomenology in the vocabulary of my trade. Use the full toolkit: sources and source work, quoting and paraphrasing, attribution, fact-checking, on the record and off the record, 'according to a person familiar with the matter', the rule against putting words in someone's mouth, and the way a careful newsroom writes 'X said Y' precisely because it can't stand behind Y itself. I'm a practising journalist, so you don't need to explain the journalism — pitch it at someone who's been doing this for years.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of Dennett's position map cleanly onto how journalists handle sources and claims? Which parts only map if you fudge them? Which parts can't be expressed in newsroom vocabulary at all? And say, at the end, whether the parts that don't survive are a defect in my toolkit — journalism simply isn't built for this — or a sign that the original position was sloppier than its defenders let on. My working theory is that heterophenomenology is just 'report accurately what the subject said and don't endorse it', but I've a feeling that's another way of missing whatever makes it interesting.
