Last night I spent the evening arguing with a friend who defends a form of political realism: he thinks our hopes should be disciplined by an honest picture of how history actually goes, and that the idealist habit of hoping for deep transformations is a way of refusing that picture. By the end he had built an argument, sharper than any I had heard before, which seems to prove that it is never rational to hope for a political transformation whose success is antecedently improbable. I cannot accept the conclusion, but I cannot yet locate the step I reject.

Here is the argument as he stated it.

(1) To hope for an outcome is, at minimum, to represent it as possible — not merely logically possible, but possible in the sense that it could come about through some continuation of actual history.

(2) To represent an outcome as possible in that sense is to assign it a non-negligible probability on one's total evidence; a "possibility" assigned effectively zero probability is not a live possibility but a notional one.

(3) The total evidence available to anyone in a given historical situation includes the base rate of comparable attempts at fundamental transformation, and that base rate is very low: most such attempts fail, and the ones we remember are a small, selected sample of successes.

(4) So, for someone in 1800 thinking about the abolition of slavery, someone in 1880 thinking about universal suffrage, or someone today thinking about the elimination of war or severe poverty, the probability that the hoped-for transformation will be achieved is effectively zero on the evidence available at the time.

(5) A rational agent does not devote years, sacrifice stability, and ask others to sacrifice for a goal she assigns effectively zero probability.

(6) Therefore, the abolitionists and the early suffragists were irrational to adopt the goals they adopted; their later success cannot correct the ex ante judgment.

(7) But that conclusion cannot be right. Those cases strike me as paradigms of clear-sighted political judgment, not failures of rationality; an argument that retroactively convicts them has proved too much.

So the question is: where does the argument go wrong, if it does? If a step is to be rejected, I want to know what the rejection costs. If instead the argument is valid, I want to know whether (7) is really as secure as I think. I most want to keep (1) and the judgment in (7); that means I would rather surrender something in the middle of the argument than either of those. But I do not know which middle step is actually false, or whether rejecting it leaves intact some form of the realist claim that hope must answer to evidence.
