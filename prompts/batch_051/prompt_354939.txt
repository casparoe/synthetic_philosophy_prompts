I teach an upper-level seminar on Renaissance political thought with around fourteen students, and I have one 75-minute session to spend. A friend and I argued late into the evening about what Machiavelli wants us to take from the pairing of fortuna and virtù: he read virtù as mostly prudent adaptation, timing, and preparation, while I kept finding a text that says fortune governs roughly half of what we do, that audacity often beats caution, and that virtù includes knowing when to throw caution aside and match the temper of the times. I don't want to settle this by lecture; I want the students to collide with the problem before they open the book. Design a runnable classroom game that makes them feel the force of that question: concrete rules, ordinary materials, a non-monetary payoff scheme, and a clear timeline for the 75 minutes. The game should place them under an unpredictable fortune mechanism while they form and revise plans, and it should be arranged so that cautious hedging, bold commitment, and flexible adaptation each succeed under some conditions and fail under others. Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto Machiavelli's contrast between fortuna and virtù, including what the text's virtù contains that the game cannot easily reproduce — the taste for glory and risk, the gendered imagery, the idea that fortune is something to be attacked rather than merely weathered — and say what will predictably go wrong on a first run and how to handle it.
