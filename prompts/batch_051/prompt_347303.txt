I am a PhD student in philosophy of technology working on digital afterlife systems, and I would like a specific policy proposal for a griefbot platform stress-tested—treated as a mechanism rather than as a moral position. The platform trains a chatbot on a deceased person's text messages, emails, and voice notes, and lets a bereaved subscriber interact with it. The proposed policy has four parts. First, every generated message is prefixed by an automatically inserted, technically unremovable line stating that the bot is an AI simulation of the deceased and not a communication from the deceased. Second, the original subscriber must complete an affirmative re-consent process every twelve months. Third, each renewal halves the bot's permitted daily message volume. Fourth, after five years the interactive bot is permanently disabled and the account is converted into a read-only archive of the generated correspondence and the original training data; a simple majority of the deceased's surviving first-degree relatives may petition to archive the account earlier, but no party, including the subscriber, may extend it. Assume the platform enforces these terms through its software and cannot quietly waive or reinterpret them. Walk through what this scheme actually does to the firm's incentives, to subscriber behaviour over the five years, to bargaining among relatives about the archive, and to how the product itself gets designed in response. Then say where the system gets gamed first—backup copies, third-party retraining outside the platform, reclassification of what the service is called, pressure around the five-year cliff, or any other failure mode you notice—and assess whether any of these failure modes can be fixed by amending the rule without turning it into a different mechanism altogether. Please keep the response to at most 800 words, and organise it around concrete incentives rather than general ethical verdicts.
