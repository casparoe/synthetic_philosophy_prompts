A colleague of mine works on collective intentionality and has given verdicts on a series of joint-action cases. She counts these as genuine joint actions: two hikers who meet on a trail and walk to the summit together, adjusting their pace and pausing for each other; a string quartet rehearsing a piece, each member following the score while watching the others; two cooks who decide by text to make dinner together and then divide the sauce and the pasta; and people at a vigil who, with no prior arrangement, begin lighting candles and standing in silence, each joining because the others are joining. She denies that these are joint actions: commuters in a station who adjust their paths only to avoid bumping into each other; and two assassins who plan to shoot the same victim at the same moment, each aware of the other's plan, but each prepared to shoot regardless of what the other does.

Next week she must rule on this case: two runners who run a route together every Saturday, plan the route the night before, and adjust their pace to stay together, but who have explicitly agreed that their arrangement is noncommittal—either may skip without notice, and neither has standing to complain about the other's absence. Predict her most likely verdict if she is to remain consistent, identify the closest earlier case, say which account of joint action she is implicitly running (interlocking intentions, joint commitment, a primitive we-intention, or something else), what each candidate account predicts about the runners, and what further case would settle which account she holds.
