I got into a long argument last night with a friend about Jeffrey conditionalization, and we spent an hour going in circles over an argument neither of us could diagnose. His line was that any "uncertain evidence" is secretly certain evidence about your own perceptual state — that when you glance at something and your credence moves without anything becoming fully certain, what really happened is that you learned, outright, a proposition about how things looked to you (or about how your credence got moved). I thought this was smuggling in a redescription, but when I tried to formalize it, the argument came out looking too clean. I can't tell which step is the bad one.

(1) Suppose your credence that the bus left the depot is 0.4. You glance across the lot and, without becoming certain of anything, your credence in Left moves to 0.75. Jeffrey conditionalization says: for any H, P_new(H) = P_old(H | Left)·0.75 + P_old(H | ¬Left)·0.25, holding your conditional probabilities on the partition fixed.
(2) Evidence is a fact you learn. So whatever happened in the glance must be expressable as a learned proposition — something like "I had an experience as of a departing bus", or "my credence in Left was raised to 0.75 by that glance".
(3) If you learned that proposition, you learned it outright, so call it X: a perfectly certain proposition.
(4) Then the rational update should just be ordinary conditionalization on X: P_new(H) = P_old(H | X).
(5) But ordinary conditionalization is order-independent — condition on A then B and you get P(H | A∧B), the same as B then A — whereas two Jeffrey updates are order-sensitive: glance at the bus, then squint at the schedule, versus the reverse, and you'll generally end up with different final probabilities.
(6) So Jeffrey conditionalization gives a different answer than ordinary conditioning on the evidence you actually received. Either Jeffrey's rule is wrong, or the whole category of ineliminably uncertain evidence is incoherent.

Where does this fail? My friend thinks it's sound and I'm just refusing the conclusion because I like the rule; I keep oscillating between step (2) and step (4), but neither of us can make the charge stick.
