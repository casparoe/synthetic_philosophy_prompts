Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper is aimed at what they call the adaptationist programme, which is the habit of assuming that every trait is an optimal adaptation shaped directly by natural selection for its current function. They use San Marco in Venice as their example. The triangular spandrels formed where the rounded arches meet beneath the central dome were not designed as surfaces for mosaics; they are an unavoidable byproduct of mounting a dome on rounded arches, and the mosaics were added afterwards. Their point is that an adaptationist looking at the mosaic-covered spandrel would invent a just-so story about how the surface was selected for displaying mosaics, when the structure is actually a byproduct of another architectural decision.

What the critique shows is that adaptationist explanations are, as usually practised, effectively unfalsifiable. Because a plausible story can be invented for any trait, no observation can ever settle whether a trait is a byproduct rather than an adaptation, and that is what makes the programme Panglossian. Gould and Lewontin's remedy is pluralism: developmental constraints, allometry, pleiotropy, drift and historical accident should all be treated as genuine evolutionary causes instead of being ignored.

But the critique does not show that adaptation is rare, and a spandrel is not simply any trait that is currently useless. A trait that starts as a byproduct can later be co-opted for a function, which makes it an exaptation. An exaptation is a feature that originally evolved for one purpose and was later co-opted for another, and the San Marco spandrels are a clear case: they began as a structural byproduct and were then co-opted as mosaic surfaces. So Gould and Lewontin's point is not that adaptationist thinking is always wrong, but that adaptation cannot be treated as the default explanation.
