I'm a PhD student in physics education, and next semester I'm in charge of training the eight undergrad TAs who grade and run recitations for the intro mechanics sequence — about 400 students, weekly quizzes, low stakes but we have years of data showing the same wrong answers comming up every single semester. The professor wants TAs to start doing a quick diagnostic pass on quiz mistakes instead of just marking them wrong, which I'm happy about, but the TAs are sophomores and juniors who have just taken the course, not ed researchers, and each one grades maybe 25 quizzes on a tight deadline. I need something they'll actually use, not a lecture on theory. Here's the principle I think is doing the work, and I'm not sure how much of it survives contact with actual grading: a lot of persistent wrong answers aren't random noise, they're systematic, like the student is running an alternative framework that's consistent on its own terms, makes its own predictions, and keeps showing up — for example, a student who treats force as a kind of stuff that gets used up as a ball flies through the air. But other wrong answers are just slips, sign errors, or students being sloppy with words, and I dont want a TA to start diagnosing a coherent alternative theory every time somebody drops a minus sign.

So what I'd like is a one-page decision procedure, basically a flow chart in words, that a TA can run in about three minutes per wrong answer while grading. For a given wrong answer it should say: what to look at to distinguish a theory-like misconception from a one-off slip from an under-specified answer; what follow-up question to ask when they can't tell; and then what to actually do with each verdict — what kind of written comment to leave, whether to ask the student something in recitation, and when to flag the answer for me or the professor. It should be honest about which steps are real judgment calls and where the procedure will misfire. Then run it on these three cases and don't soften the results: (1) a student says a ball thrown straight up keeps rising because the throw put force into the ball, and it falls once the force runs out; (2) a student gets a velocity negative when it should be positive, but their written reasoning is correct and they just used the sign convention backwards; (3) a student says a heavier rock falls faster because there's more gravity pulling on it. I particularly want to know whether the procedure stops case (3) from being overdiagnosed — I suspect a lazy TA will call it a misconception, but it might just be a sloppy way of saying the net force is larger. Finish by saying what a TA can't conclude from a single wrong answer and what the procedure is blind to.
