I'm a novelist, so fair warning: I think in scenes and characters more than in numbered premises, but I've been asked to write down my views on originality, conformity, and how a society should treat its oddballs so a reading group can poke at them. Here's where I stand right now.

One. I think Mill was basically right that a society needs people running experiments in living. Someone who lives in an unusual way is doing something like R&D for the species: we learn from their successes and, just as much, from their failures. The conformists are the control group, not the point. Without a steady supply of people trying out strange ways to live, we'd be stuck with whatever arrangements happened to survive, and survival is a poor test of whether a way of life is good.

Two. But the informational value isn't the main reason I care about self-expression. I think there's a separate, more personal reason: a life lived on someone else's template is less good for the person living it, regardless of how it turns out. Saying something of your own—in how you live, not just in what you make—is a constituent of a good life, not merely a means to discoveries for other people.

Three. My trade has taught me something that seems to cut against that. There is no voice before imitation. The writers I know who found an original style fastest are the ones who shamelessly copied the widest range of models early on. Borrowing at the level of sentence and structure is what builds the machine that later produces novelty. So I don't actually think imitation and self-expression are opposites, at least in art.

Four. When it comes to law and policy, I'm for strict neutrality. The state should not subsidize experimental lives, pay excentrics a stipend, ban them, or nudge anybody toward either the conventional or the strange. Keep the peace, enforce the harm principle, and let the experiments run on their own.

Five. I also believe the usual public-goods logic about risk: people underinvest in risky experiments whose lessons mostly benefit other people. The experimenter bears the costs while everyone else freerides on the results, so a society that merely tolerates oddballs will get fewer live experiments than it actually values. In science we fix the analogous problem by funding basic research. I'm not sure we do anything analogous for ways of life, and I'm not sure I want us to.

I can tell parts of this pull against each other, but I can't tell which are real problems and which are just my trade's habits talking. Could you go through and say which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look that way, and rank the real ones by severity? For each real tension, give me the cheapest fix, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
