I serve on my university's doctoral policy committee, and next month we vote on a proposal to abolish the long-standing requirement that every PhD student pass a reading-knowledge examination in a language other than English before advancing to candidacy. The reformers argue that the requirement was built for an era when staying current in most fields meant reading French and German scholarship that was otherwise inaccessible; today, they say, it has become a costly delaying filter that rewards students whose secondary schools taught languages well and duplicates what translation and machine-assisted reading now offer. The defenders reply that departments have long granted quiet waivers, that the exam still enforces a kind of close attention to sources that no coursework replaces, and — most of all — that the rule is older than anyone now on the faculty and that we should hesitate before tearing out a fence whose purpose we cannot fully state.

Last night I had a long argument about exactly this with a friend, a political theorist who works on Burke, and he pressed the Chesterton's-fence point much harder than my colleagues have. He granted that the original rationale has largely lapsed, but he insisted that an institution can encode more practical knowledge than its current members can reconstruct in a committee meeting, and that our inability to say precisely what the requirement is now for is a reason for caution rather than demolition. We then fought over where this deference is hard-won wisdom and where it is status-quo bias wearing a costume of humility.

I have been asked to draft the short written opinion that will frame the committee's discussion. Please write the memo I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action for the vote — keep the requirement, abolish it, or replace it with a specified alternative — and it should give our committee workable criteria for deciding when an inherited practice of this kind deserves preservation and when it does not. Put the strongest version of the Burkean defense on the page and answer it rather than dismissing it, and say plainly where that argument would and would not justify deferring to inherited practice. If your recommendation depends on information we could gather before the vote, specify exactly what would change the recommendation and what we should do if it cannot be gathered in time.
