I'm working through Section VII of Hume's *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, and the following passage has me stuck:

> When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other.

I keep reading this as saying that there is no real necessity in the world, and that causation is just regular succession. But I know the "New Hume" interpretation denies that Hume is a pure regularity theorist, and holds instead that Hume allows there may be objective necessary connections in nature, while denying we can know or represent them. I want to get clear on what this specific passage actually rules out.

Could you walk me through what the passage does and does not commit Hume to? In particular: does Hume here deny that necessary connexion exists in objects, or only deny that we *discover* or *have an impression of* it? What work is the phrase "in a single instance" doing in the argument? And how would a New Hume reading accommodate this passage without making Hume contradict himself? I'm less interested in scholarly labels than in the precise contrast between a world containing objective necessary connections and a world in which regular successions, together with the mind's habit or custom, exhaust what we can mean by "cause."
