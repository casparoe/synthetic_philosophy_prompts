I recently retired after a career in the physical sciences, and for the last few years I have been reading my way through moral philosophy. One comparison I keep meeting—and which, I admit, I find seductive because it flatters my old training—is that choosing among ethical theories is like choosing among scientific theories. On this picture, our considered moral judgments about particular cases (promises, trolley cases, rescue cases, distributive choices) play the role of the data; the candidate moral theories (some version of consequentialism, Kantianism, a Ross-style plurality of prima facie duties, or, at the far end, particularism) play the role of the hypotheses; and simplicity or parsimony is a virtue in both enterprises. The method of reflective equilibrium, in which principles and considered judgments are adjusted to each other, makes the comparison with theory choice almost explicit. The analogy is put to work as follows: a theory that needs only one fundamental principle is favored in something like the way a physical model with fewer free parameters is favored, and a theory that posits many mutually irreducible duties—fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, non-maleficence, and the rest—is criticized as unparsimonious, ad hoc, or baroque. The conclusion this is used to license is that we may discount some recalcitrant intuitions from the outset: they are noise, the moral equivalent of data points that the simple theory cannot be expected to capture, rather than refutations of it.

I spent a career seeing exactly why a fitted curve with too many free parameters should be distrusted, so this line of thought has real pull for me. But I also notice that moral data are not read off an instrument: they are the deliverances of intuition, and the theory is not making predictions that can fail in any straightforward sense. I would like a careful audit of how far the analogy actually holds. Please give the correspondence explicitly—what maps onto what—and then identify the points where the mapping breaks, distinguishing the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that are merely superficial. In particular, I want to know which features of scientific theory choice the analogy quietly imports into ethics (for example, that the data are largely independent of the theory being tested, that nature is likely to be simple, or that simplicity is a guide to truth) and whether those features carry over once the context changes. Does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing all the work? If the analogy fails at the crucial points, is there a better one from some other domain—linguistics, law, or elsewhere—or should the comparison simply be dropped? Finally, please be explicit about which parts of your answer reflect broad agreement among philosophers working on this topic and which parts are genuinely contested, rather than presenting a contested view as the settled one.
