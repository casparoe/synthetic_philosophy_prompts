I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether it could have turned out that neither of us existed. I wanted to say that of course there is a possible world in which I am never born, one in which you are never born, and perhaps even one in which no concrete thing exists at all. My friend, who has been reading modal logic much longer than I have, said this is exactly the kind of thing people believe before they see the consequences of the Barcan formulas. He then laid out the following argument, which he claims shows that everything whatever exists necessarily. I have gone over it several times and cannot find the sleight of hand, but the conclusion seems to prove far too much. Here is the argument as he gave it:

(1) Necessarily, everything is self-identical: no matter how things had gone, it would still have been the case that ∀x(x = x).

(2) If necessarily everything is self-identical, then everything is necessarily self-identical; my friend called this an instance of the converse Barcan formula (□∀xφ → ∀x□φ, with φ being “x = x”), which he treats as part of the uncontroversial core of quantified modal logic.

(3) So each thing is such that it is necessarily self-identical; in particular, I am necessarily self-identical, and so are you.

(4) But a thing can be self-identical only if it exists. If I had not existed, there would have been no me for the claim “I am self-identical” to be about, and so it could not have been true of me. Hence, if I am necessarily self-identical, I necessarily exist.

(5) Therefore I, and everything else, exist necessarily; there is no possible world in which I fail to exist, and my original thought that I might never have been born is simply incoherent.

I want to know whether this argument is valid as stated and, if it is not, exactly which step fails and on which reading of it. If it goes through only under specific assumptions about modal semantics, I would like to know what those assumptions are, and whether step (2) or step (4) is the better place for someone who wants to resist the conclusion to dig in. At the very end of your response, on its own line, write \answer{...} containing either “valid” or the number of the step you take to be the primary failure point, followed by a short phrase.
