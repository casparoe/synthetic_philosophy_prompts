Next month I am giving a seminar paper at a university centre that brings together technology researchers, social scientists, and philosophers. My thesis is that at least some technical systems carry politics in their material form, not merely in how they are used or in the intentions of their designers. The case discussion is built around automated eligibility systems in public administration: the software that turns criteria for benefits, permits, or exemptions into fixed numerical thresholds. My argument in outline is this. First, criteria that were formerly applied with case-by-case discretion become effective constraints once they are encoded as thresholds, so the written policy remains formally more permissive than the system actually is. Second, because the resulting modules tend to be reused across institutions with quite different governing norms, the normative choices made in one site travel silently into others. Third, this means the artefact itself excludes part of the space of possible social arrangements before any user, operator, or policymaker makes a choice. From this I conclude that we should say the system itself has a politics, not merely that politics gets attached to it later.

The audience is not a neutral one. Roughly a third are science and technology studies scholars of a strongly constructionist bent, who will be inclined to say that the politics is in the discourse and interpretive flexibility around the artefacts, not in the things themselves. Another third are software and systems engineers who regard their work as implementing requirements that were decided elsewhere and the technology itself as neutral. The remainder are political philosophers and public-policy scholars, together with one programme officer from an engineering ethics funder, and they tend to ask about responsibility, legitimacy, and unintended consequences rather than about ontology.

Please prepare my question-time sheet for the seminar. I want the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased in the vocabulary each group would actually use, with an estimate of how much damage each could do. For each objection, note what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. I also want to know up front which commitment of mine is most likely to be exploited, and, at the end, the one question I should be hoping nobody asks.
