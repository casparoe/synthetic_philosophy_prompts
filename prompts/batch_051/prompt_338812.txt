I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I have contrived an argument that I now cannot dispose of. I am drafting a novel whose time machine runs on a fixed timeline: the past can be visited but not altered. The grandfather paradox was meant to sit quietly in the background while I worked on the plot, but in making the book's rules explicit I produced an argument that seems to prove far too much. It appears to show that a fixed timeline requires something like a cosmic censor policing the traveler's every move, and that the same censor would have to police my own future actions if the block universe picture is right. I am unwilling to accept that conclusion, and I want to know exactly where the reasoning fails.

Here is the argument as I set it out in my notes.

(1) In a fixed timeline, it is fixed that the time traveler's grandfather was not killed before he had children. Call this H.

(2) Holding H fixed, the traveler cannot make it true that H and that she killed her grandfather before he had children. So, relative to the fixed past, she cannot succeed in killing him.

(3) Yet the traveler can form the intention, and no physical or local obstacle blocks her: the weapon is loaded, the aim is clear, the opportunity is real, and the resolve is hers. No law of nature forbids the killing.

(4) If her failure is guaranteed despite all that, then some event must occur to stop her — a misfire, a slip, a loss of nerve. And such an event must arrive in every run of the scenario, exactly when consistency requires it, in whatever form is needed.

(5) Those stopping events would be correlated with the logical structure of her biography rather than with any physical mechanism. But causality is local and mechanical; it does not answer to logical bookkeeping. So a fixed timeline appears to force a choice: no time travel, branching timelines, or a hidden principle that enforces consistency.

(6) Now run the same reasoning forward. In a block universe, tomorrow's events are just as fixed as yesterday's. Suppose it is fixed that I do not raise my hand at noon tomorrow. Then, holding the fixed future fixed, I cannot make it true that I raise my hand. But if I were guaranteed not to raise my hand despite intending to and lacking any impediment, then — by the reasoning of (4) — some event would have to intervene at noon to stop me, an intervention correlated, by the reasoning of (5), with the logical structure of the future rather than with any mechanism.

(7) That is absurd. Determinism does not need a hidden censor to keep my hand down. If I do not raise it, I simply do not raise it; no conspiracy of accidents arranges my failure.

(8) So the original argument must go wrong somewhere, since the backward case and the forward case are structurally alike except for the direction of time.

Where does the argument go wrong? I am most attached to the fixed timeline, because the novel is built on it, and beyond that I have no loyalty to any particular step. I want a diagnosis with a number attached: which step I should reject — or, if the chain is valid, which premise is the least costly to abandon — why that step looked like a principle of physics rather than a trick of modal thinking, and what the surviving account says about the time traveler's agency. In particular, does the sentence "she could have killed him" come out true, false, or true in one sense only, and what would that mean for the rest of the book?
