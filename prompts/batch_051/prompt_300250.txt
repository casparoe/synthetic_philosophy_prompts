Okay so im a junior, and I got asked (volunteered, same thing) to give the student presentation to our school's new Digital Citizenship Committee, because I wouldnt shut up about this in AP Gov. The last meeting was mostly adults agreeing that students just need better fact-checking skills, and I sat there thinking they're treating us like we've just never been shown the good information. My slot next week is the dissenting pitch. The committee is: Mr. Okafor, the librarian, who is all about reading laterally and tracing claims back to primary sources; Ms. Alvarez, the media studies teacher who wrote the district's fake-news unit and uses that credibility-checklist handout like it's scripture; a parent rep who works in cybersecurity and mostly wants the school wifi filtered harder; and the student council president, who wants us to formally disinvite a guest speaker because she thinks his podcast turns kids into a cult.

My thesis is that the school keeps diagnosing students as being in an epistemic bubble—just missing outside information—when the students they're actually worried about are in echo chambers, where they actively distrust the school's 'neutral' sources. So media-literacy worksheets, balanced-source exercises, and especially banning stuff will all fail, because they try to shove facts at people whose trust is what's broken. My whole talk turns on this passage from C. Thi Nguyen's 2018 Aeon essay 'Escape the Echo Chamber':

"In epistemic bubbles, other voices are not heard; in echo chambers, other voices are actively undermined. The way to break an echo chamber is not to wave “the facts” in the faces of its members. It is to attack the echo chamber at its root and repair that broken trust."

I close by proposing we swap a chunk of the fact-checking unit for what I'm calling "trust literacy": mapping who students actually trust, where that distrust came from, and what would count as repairing it. Write me the Q&A prep sheet, not a pep talk: the objections these four people will actually raise, phrased the way they'd say it, ordered by how likely and how damaging each one is, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, the one reply that has to land or the whole proposal's dead, and the one question I'm praying nobody in the back has thought of.
