I'm a retired physical chemist who got into philosophy embarrassingly late, and one thing keeps nagging me about how people — including old lab colleagues — talk about coincidences. Take the plane story: you sit next to a stranger who shares your birthday and grew up on the same street you did, and the whole cabin treats it as meaningful. Yet those same people will nod along at the birthday paradox, that in a room of 23 there's already a better than even chance two people share a birthday. So a shared birthday is boring in a lecture hall but spooky on an airplane.

I don't think this is just innumeracy, and I'm not asking for the math. I want the philosophical commitments underneath the practice of telling and debunking coincidence stories. The practice seems to presuppose that the right reference class is the exact event — this person, this flight, this day — while the paradox pushes toward a different class: some event roughly this surprising happening somewhere or sometime. And the practice seems to treat an improbability as demanding explanation only if it was fixed in advance, but almost no coincidence anecdote is fixed in advance; the event is always identified after the fact. So what does the practice presuppose about what an event even is, about when an improbable event calls for an explanation, and about whether improbability by itself is ever evidence for something? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology (or several), seperate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely sits alongside, and tell me what would have to change in ordinary coincidence-talk if we actually adopted the reference-class-sensitive view. If several coherent positions could underlie the same habits, say that too.
