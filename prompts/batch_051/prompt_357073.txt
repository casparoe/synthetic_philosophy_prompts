Since retiring from a career in condensed matter physics, I have been reading philosophy of science and now convene a small seminar of working scientists and philosophers. For our next session, on reduction and emergence, I would like you to write a classification instrument: a "which position do you actually hold?" quiz that sorts readers by their commitments about concrete scientific cases rather than by which label they would apply to themselves.

Design eight to ten questions. Each question should present a concrete case or a tightly specified claim and offer two or three answer options; no option should announce a philosophical label or use terms like "reductionism" or "emergence." Build at least the following cases into the questions: a glider in Conway's Game of Life; the multiple realizability of pain; a hypothetical complete molecular-dynamics simulation of a bacterium; a Laplace's demon in possession of the exact quantum state of a nervous system; the fact that certain liquid-gas and ferromagnetic transitions are described by the same renormalization-group universality class; the appearance of idealizations such as the thermodynamic limit in equilibrium thermodynamics; and natural selection changing allele frequencies. Add one or two further cases if they would sharpen the sorting.

Construct the quiz from the logical space first, not from a list of names. Identify the two or three diagnostic yes/no questions that do the underlying work, for example: whether a complete lower-level description contains all the facts about a higher-level phenomenon; whether any apparent shortfall is merely practical or epistemic rather than metaphysical; and whether higher-level states ever have causal powers their constituents lack. Let those questions define the axes, then locate in the resulting space the standard positions: eliminative reductionism, derivational reductionism in the Nagelian style, non-reductive physicalism, weak emergence, strong emergence, and any pragmatist or deflationary stance that treats the contrast as poorly posed. Provide a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto those positions.

Finish by flagging unoccupied combinations of answers and say briefly, for each, whether it is empty because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored. Also flag any two positions the key cannot reliably separate, rather than implying the results are finer-grained than they really are.
