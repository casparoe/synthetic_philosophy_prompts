Immanuel Kant's essay Toward Perpetual Peace, in the fourth preliminary article of its first section, argues that states must not incur national debts in connection with their external affairs. Kant's worry is not that public borrowing is intrinsically bad: he concedes that borrowing for domestic infrastructure can be unobjectionable. The problem is cross-border debt as a credit-funded instrument of military competition and as an obligation whose repudiation can injure other states. As a journalist covering European fiscal politics, I want you to reconstruct and then extend that argument. Would Kant have endorsed the post-eurozone-crisis framework of supranational limits on state borrowing, even when the borrowing is for pensions and domestic welfare? Would he have counted modern sovereign bond markets, integrated across borders, as falling under his prohibition? Or does his distinction between domestic improvement and external military rivalry break down when capital markets are globally integrated?
