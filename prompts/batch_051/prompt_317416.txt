I serve on the release-review team of a research institute whose default rule is that any individual researcher may publish to the institute's public repository without prior sign-off; review happens after the fact. The decisions are frequent (several releases a quarter), the stakes range from negligible to serious and hard to reverse, and the people deciding are researchers with no training in decision theory and at most an hour to spend. Our recurring failure is this: several researchers act separately on the same reasoning — if this release is harmful, someone else in the larger community will release equivalent material anyway, so my contribution cannot be the decisive one — and the aggregate of these individually reasonable acts is a harm none of them wanted. I am told this is an instance of the unilateralist's curse: a gap between what each individual's local calculation supports and what the group should want, driven partly by each actor ignoring the evidential connection between their own decision and what others are likely to do.

Please turn this into a written decision procedure that an individual researcher can run before publishing. Ground it in what the curse involves: the difference between individual and collective expected value, the chance of being the pivotal or precedent-setting actor, and what one should infer from the fact that others have not yet published or will predictably publish. The procedure must be usable in under an hour by someone without formal decision theory. It should be a numbered sequence of questions or tests; for each, say what counts as a sufficient answer, and say explicitly when a no stops the process with a recommendation not to publish or a recommendation to escalate. It should state where the procedure hands the decision to a group or an outside body rather than allowing the individual to decide alone, and it should mark every step that is a judgment call dressed up as a mechanical test.

Then run the completed procedure on these three cases, and include at least one case in which the procedure returns a result the researcher would not accept on their own and at least one in which it routes the decision to group review:

(1) A researcher wants to post a short note showing how two already-public techniques can be combined into a markedly more effective bypass of widely used AI safeguards. The pieces are public, the combination is easy once stated, and the researcher argues the note will help defenders more than attackers.

(2) A researcher wants to release a lightly redacted corpus of several hundred thousand real prompt-injection and misuse attempts logged by our services. They argue attackers already trade larger datasets, and the field needs raw examples to study real misuse.

(3) A researcher wants to publish an automated evaluation harness that, among its defensive uses, can also be aimed at any production model to generate policy-violating outputs at scale. They argue it is defensive tooling and that comparable tools are being built outside the institute regardless.

Structure the response in these named sections, in this order, with these approximate lengths:

Summary (150–200 words): the procedure in miniature and its main recommendation.
The decision procedure (600–700 words): the numbered steps, sufficient answers, stopping conditions, escalation points, and judgment calls.
Rationale (250–300 words): how the steps connect to the curse and prevent the failure mode.
Worked applications (600–700 words): apply the procedure to all three cases, showing where it stops, where it escalates, and where it returns a decision the individual does not want.
Limits and judgment calls (200–250 words): what the procedure cannot settle, where it might misfire, and what residual risks remain even when it is followed.
