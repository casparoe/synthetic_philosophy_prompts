A friend doing an MA in political philosophy leant me a short trade book on equality, and the chapter on luck egalitarianism left me with a tidy summary I'm not sure I should trust. To paraphrase it: luck egalitarians say an inequality is unjust just in case the worse-off person ends up worse-off through no choice or fault of their own. They distinguish brute luck (genes, disability, being born into a poor family) from option luck (the risks you knowingly run, like gambling or starting a precarious business), and they say society should compensate people for the first kind but not the second. Dworkin's version, the chapter says, is equality of resources: everyone gets an equal starting bundle, and after that the market and people's choices are allowed to determine who ends up where. Then it says Cohen sharpened Dworkin's account by proposing equality of resources, pure and simple, as the way to eliminate all involuntary disadvantage. It presents Anderson's objection as mainly that luck egalitarians would have to abandon the imprudent, and then it concludes that since nobody can draw the line between brute luck and option luck, the whole framework is unworkable in practice.

I half-remember from a course that Cohen actually rejected Dworkin's resources-only currency, so maybe the book is running two positions together, and the ending also seemed like a leap to me. But its a professional book, so I want to be careful before I go correcting anyone. Is this a fair summary? Please go through it and seperate which parts are accurate, which are common simplifications, what a defender of any of these views would say is misattributed, and whether the unworkability conclusion follows from any of the accurate parts. I'd especially like to know if the Dworkin-as-equal-starting-bundle picture and the Cohen-as-resources picture are right, or are the kind of thing a textbook gloss gets subtly wrong.
