I'm in a philosophy of math class and we've been doing paradoxes of the infinite. I've been trying to write out the Thomson's lamp reasoning as a clean argument, but I keep going back and forth on where it breaks. The setup: at t=0 the lamp is on; at t=1/2 it's switched off; at t=3/4 on; at t=7/8 off; and so on, with the switches at times 1 − 1/2^n, and all the switching finished by t=1. The question is what the lamp is doing at exactly t=1.

Here's the argument I want to check:

(1) At every instant, the lamp is either on or off — and that includes t=1.

(2) The lamp cannot be on at t=1. If it were on at t=1, it would have to have been switched on at some time before t=1 and then left on after that. But every switching-on before t=1 is followed by a switching-off before t=1.

(3) The lamp cannot be off at t=1, for the parallel reason: if it were off, it would have to have been switched off at some time before t=1 and left off after that, but every switching-off before t=1 is followed by a switching-on.

(4) So at t=1 the lamp is neither on nor off, contradicting (1).

So the supertask looks impossible, or at least incoherant. I suspect (2) and (3) are smuggling in the idea that the state at t=1 has to be "inherited" from some last switch, and there is no last switch. But I'm not sure whether that's a genuine flaw or just something I want to be true because the conclusion bothers me. Is this argument valid? If it fails, which step exactly fails and what would the correct version of that step say? And if it is valid, does that really show the lamp scenario is impossible, or just that the description is incomplete?
