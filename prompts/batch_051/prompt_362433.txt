I chair the living donor transplantation committee at a large NHS teaching hospital. A case has been referred to us. A twenty-six-year-old woman, L, has volunteered to donate a kidney to her thirty-four-year-old sister, who has end-stage renal disease and faces several years on dialysis without a transplant. L has a mild learning disability; she lives with her parents, depends on them financially, and manages day-to-day decisions well but struggles to hold several pieces of sequentially presented clinical information in mind. When the transplant surgeon discussed the operation with her, he described laparoscopic donor nephrectomy as a routine, low-risk operation and, when L asked how donation would affect her own future health, told her that donors live as long as anyone else, if not longer. The patient information leaflet, by contrast, says that living kidney donors face an elevated long-term risk of kidney failure compared with similar non-donors, and that a minority of donors report chronic pain, fatigue, or low mood. Our independent assessor, who is not part of the surgical team, interviewed L alone. L told her that she did not feel able to withdraw because, in her words, if she said no her mother and sister would never let her hear the end of it, and she would lose her family. She also said she had not fully grasped the risks until the assessor read the leaflet aloud to her slowly. The social worker's report notes that the family has taken charge of arranging L's tests and that relatives refer to L as the designated spare part, which L finds upsetting. The committee is divided. Several members argue that L's consent is not valid: the surgeon's framing was misleading, the family's pressure amounts to coercion, and her capacity to weigh long-term risks is uncertain. Others argue that L has repeatedly said she wants to donate, that she understands her sister may die without a transplant, and that refusing the donation would treat her altruism as a symptom of her disability rather than as her considered choice. Draft the committee's decision memo, in which we must approve the donation, reject it, or approve it subject to conditions. The memo should lead with a clear recommendation, give the reasoning for it, and address the strongest consideration on the losing side without dismissing it. Please write it in continuous prose, in British English, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as a formal letter from the committee would be written.
