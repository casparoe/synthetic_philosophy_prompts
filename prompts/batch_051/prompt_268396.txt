Chaos theory is usually introduced with the butterfly effect: sensitive dependence on initial conditions means tiny measurement errors grow exponentially, so after a while you can't predict the state at all. That's supposed to establish that chaotic systems are unpredictable, full stop. What I want to pick at is whether that conclusion actually follows once you change the thing you're trying to predict.

Most of the predictions we actually want from chaotic systems aren't point predictions of an exact future state. Weather forecasting gives up on predicting whether it will rain in my neighborhood at 4:07pm three weeks from now, but climate models still claim to tell us something about long-run averages, how often extreme events occur, the shape of the attractor, or which regime the system is heading toward. So the changed assumption is: the target isn't a trajectory, it's a statistical or coarse-grained property of the system.

Does the standard unpredictability argument survive this? Is sensitive dependence really an argument that all prediction is impossible, or is it only an argument that a particular kind of prediction is impossible? I want you to take a definite position. Either say the unpredictability conclusion really does carry over to statistical/coarse-grained prediction (and explain what it is about the attractor or the invariant measure that dooms the statistical case too), or say the two come apart, and then say exactly what a statistical prediction needs to be true—e.g. how stable the long-run frequencies have to be under parameter drift or model error. Don't just lay out both interpretations and leave it to me; pick one and defend it.
