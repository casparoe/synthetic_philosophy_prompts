I've been trying to get clear on whether a nation can be guilty in a way that outlives the actual perpetrators, and I've built an argument that now scares me because it seems to prove to much.

(1) If a nation can be guilty of a historical wrong, the guilt has to be a kind that can persist after everyone who actually did the thing is dead. Otherwise it's just the guilt of individuals, and talk of national guilt is loose talk.
(2) The obvious candidate is the thing Jaspers called political guilt: citizens are answerable for what their state does simply in virtue of belonging to it, independently of what they individually chose or did.
(3) What is supposed to carry this answerability across generations is continued membership in the same intergenerational group, plus the fact that later members benefit from what the group accumulated (wealth, institutions, standing).
(4) But that relation isn't special to states. Families, universities, churches, firms, and professional bodies are also intergenerational groups with continuous identities that pass down benefits built partly on past wrongs.
(5) So if political guilt passes down the generations through membership plus benefit, then by the same standard present-day members of all those other groups are answerable for their group's past wrongs: a Quaker for 18th-century Quaker slaveholding, a graduate for their university's part in eugenics, a shareholder for a company's colonial-era labour practices.
(6) That's absurd — we don't, and shouldn't, treat all those people as guilty. It would make nearly everyone answerable for nearly every historical wrong, since every long-lived group has some blood on its hands.
(7) Therefore (2) is false: membership plus benefit cannot make later generations answerable for what their state did before they were born.
(8) But if (2) is false, then present-day citizens owe nothing in particular for their state's past wrongs — no reparations, no land back, no formal apology — beyond the general duty to fix present unjust arrangements. And that also seems false, or at least too convenient for the heirs of the wrongdoing.

So where does it go wrong, if it does? I'd most like to keep (2): I want it to be true that, say, British or German citizens today owe something specific for what thier states did. I thought Jaspers' distinction between criminal guilt (which can't be inherited) and political guilt (which maybe can) was meant to do exactly that. But I can't see how to block step (5) without drawing a line between states and other groups that looks arbitrary. And if I give up (2) instead, I'm left with only a generic duty to fix present injustice, which feels too weak to underwrite reparations.
