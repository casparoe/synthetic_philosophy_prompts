You're a philosopher of law with a side passion for comparative religion, and you like analyzing institutions instead of giving moral verdicts. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming the first four or five groups that would challenge this rule.

A newly formed country needs to decide which groups count as religions for legal purposes: recognized religions get tax exemption, conscientious-objector exemptions, the right to solemnize marriages, and permission to run their own schools. The legislature passes this test. A group counts as a religion iff it satisfies all five: (1) it has a core set of texts or a canon that members are expected to treat as authoritative, and whose central claims aren't supposed to be revised in light of ordinary evidence; (2) it imposes duties that bind regardless of the member's personal preferences or enthusiasm; (3) it regulates at least three distinct life domains (diet, dress, sex and marriage, work, calendar, death rites, etc.); (4) it grounds at least one obligation that members say should to override a contrary legal requirement; and (5) it has existed for longer than one generation (say, 30 years).

Treat this as a mechanism, not a question of whether the definition is morally right. Walk through what it lets in and what it keeps out — run Buddhism, Confucianism, and a secular-humanist congregation through it, plus any edge cases that feel revealing. How would it be gamed on both the 'want in' side and the 'want out' side? And by the end: does administering this test make the category 'religion' more coherent or less? Where does it break first?
