I've been prepping a session for a graduate seminar on the problem of induction, and I keep going back and forth on Goodman's new riddle because everyone in the room knows Hume but almost no one has worked through grue. For background: by 'grue' I mean the usual predicate, true of a thing just in case it is either examined before some future time t and green, or not examined before t and blue, so the two hypotheses 'all emeralds are green' and 'all emeralds are grue' are supported equally well by the same observations of green emeralds despite making opposite predictions about emeralds first examined after t. The question is what, if anything, makes 'green' projectible and 'grue' not.

Responses I already have and don't need repeated: (1) the entrenchment answer, where 'green' wins because it has a record of successful past projections and 'grue' doesn't; (2) the naturalness answer, where green is a joint-carving property and grue is gerrymandered or disjunctive; (3) the Bayesian answer, where the green hypothesis gets a higher prior so the same evidence doesn't leave it tied with the grue hypothesis; (4) the simplicity answer, where some formal measure of simplicity rates green above grue; (5) the qualitative-predicate answer, where grue is legimitately disqualified because its definition essentially names the particular time t; and (6) the skeptic answer, where there is no non-question-begging difference between the two predicates and the riddle just shows induction cannot be justified.

I want one more response to the new riddle that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or combination of anything on it. Then tell me honestly whether it is any good as a solution, and how it relates to at least one of the listed responses.
