I am the budget director for a midsized city, and the mayor wants us to pilot a futarchy-style process for selected decisions: the council votes on which outcome metric a policy should maximize, and then a decision market—not council deliberation—selects the option by having participants bet on which policy would produce the best measured score on that metric, with the council committing in advance to adopt the market's preferred option. Before any proposal reaches the council, I have to screen it: is this a decision the market treatment can handle, or should it stay in the ordinary process?

My office sees ten to fifteen consequential proposals a year, including capital projects, fare or fee changes, zoning changes, and service reorganizations. My staff are capable policy analysts, not economists or market designers; they have two days per proposal and access only to existing city data. The council must approve both the metric and the use of a market, and the mayor is eager for the pilot to succeed, so I expect pressure to stretch eligibility. I need a procedure that lets me tell the mayor, on principled grounds rather than taste, when a proposal is fit for a decision market and when it is not.

Please write the procedure as an ordered checklist in words that my staff can actually run. For each question, say what the analyst should look at, what counts as sufficient to proceed, and which answers route the proposal to a no or up to a human decision. Ground the checklist in what is actually known about prediction and decision markets and the futarchy split between value choices and factual bets: the need for a metric that can be specified in advance and observed later by an agreed source; whether the market would be liquid enough and whether anyone with relevant information would have both the knowledge and the incentive to trade; manipulation and self-reference, including what happens when the market's own verdict changes the outcome it is predicting; whether the market can resolve before its answer loses its usefulness for the decision; and the danger of letting a market settle a question that is really a disagreement about values. Mark the steps that are judgment calls rather than mechanical tests, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot do.

Then apply the procedure to three proposals:

1. Choosing between two alignments for a new bus rapid transit line, with the proposed metric "new weekday boardings per $100,000 of annualized cost," measured three years after opening by the independent regional transit authority.

2. An upzoning that would allow fourplexes on most residential lots, with the metric "additional housing units permitted within five years"; council members disagree about effects on displacement, tree canopy, and neighborhood character.

3. A convention-center expansion strongly backed by the mayor, where the proposed metric is "a rising score on the city's annual downtown-vibrancy survey"—a survey designed and administered by the city's own economic-development office, with the expected benefits arriving over ten to twenty years.

The convention center is the mayor's pet project. If the procedure says it should not be market-decided, say so plainly, with the reasons, rather than hedging.
