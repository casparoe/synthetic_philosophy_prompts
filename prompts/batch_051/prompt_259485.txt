Write a short research proposal, around 1,200 words, for a philosophy-of-science workshop or a dissertation prospectus chapter, on the empirical content of the Principle of Computational Equivalence (PCE) in Stephen Wolfram's A New Kind of Science. I have a half-formed idea and I want you to turn it into a tractable project rather than simply polishing it.

The idea is that PCE is usually either attacked as unfalsifiable or defended as a sweeping empirical claim, but it is better treated as a family of claims of very different strength. At the lowest end is the claim that simple computational rules can generate complex behavior. In the middle are claims about particular systems: Rule 30 behaves as if random, and Rule 110 has been shown to support universal computation. At the top is Wolfram's distinctive claim that almost all processes that are not obviously simple are computationally equivalent, so that no natural or artificial system occupies an intermediate tier between trivial and universal. The proposal should ask which of these versions, if any, is both recognizably Wolfram's and strong enough to be tested, and then specify what a test would actually look like.

Structure the proposal accordingly. State a central question determinate enough that the project could fail rather than merely peter out; explain why existing discussion leaves it open, taking seriously both the unfalsifiability charge and Wolfram's reply that PCE makes concrete predictions; describe the approach you favor — for instance, searching cellular-automata space for robust exceptions after making "intermediate tier" precise, or recasting PCE as a thesis about modeling irreducibility, or concluding that only the weak and middle versions are genuinely empirical — and say why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives. Work through the possible conclusions, what evidence or argument would decide among them, and what a null result would show. Then give an honest assessment of scope: what can realistically be done in one semester and what must be set aside.

Refer to positions and arguments by description, not citation; do not invent a bibliography, and I will supply references later. If the half-formed idea misstates what PCE actually says, or if the task as posed rests on a false dichotomy between "testable" and "empty," say so plainly and reframe it — do not write a polished proposal around a confused premise.
