I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. I build multi-agent systems, and my current project is a forecasting pipeline in which several automated agents each observe a different slice of a shared data stream, publish probability estimates, and may revise their estimates after reading the others'. I keep running into a claim I'm told goes back to something called Aumann's agreement theorem: very roughly, two agents who are both perfect Bayesians and share a prior cannot rationally "agree to disagree"; if they update on each other's announced probabilities, they will converge to a common posterior.

That claim sits awkwardly for me between mathematics and an empirical description of how information systems actually behave. I want to know whether I can test it in my own code, and what such a test could possibly establish. If my agents converge, is that evidence that their updates are doing something beyond averaging or copying? If they don't converge, is that a bug in my implementation, a mismatch with the theorem's preconditions, or a strike against the claim? And because the claim is supposedly a theorem, what would I even be testing: the math, or only whether my system instantiates the math?

Please structure your answer in the following sections, in this order and with these approximate lengths:

1. The claim, made operational (~200 words). State for my setup what would and would not count as sharing a prior, as updating rationally, and as agreeing to disagree. I want to know exactly which antecedent I commit to when I run the test.

2. The test setup (~450 words). Design a concrete two-agent experiment I could implement: what signal each agent observes, the exchange protocol, and a benchmark that would distinguish convergence from mere averaging. Be specific enough to implement in a week. Include one control condition in which convergence is expected for trivial or information-free reasons, and explain how the main test evades it.

3. What to count as a result for or against (~300 words). Specify the data patterns that would favor the reading that the agents converge through rational updating rather than mimicry, and one negative pattern that would favor mimicry or mere averaging. Address timing, the final estimates, and any asymmetry in who moves toward whom.

4. What the result would and would not settle (~400 words). Assume first that the test comes out as the theorem predicts: say what I have then learned, and what I haven't, about whether my agents ever really shared a prior, whether the result generalizes to human forecasters, and whether the theorem is doing empirical work or just serving as a specification my code either meets or violates. Then say what a failed test would establish.

5. The residue (~200 words). Identify what part of the original claim no test of this kind can reach, no matter how clean the result, and why. If your honest answer is that, once operationalized, the claim becomes close to a tautology under the chosen definitions, say that plainly.
