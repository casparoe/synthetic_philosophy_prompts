We've spent a year in our reading group going in circles on how to combine two features of an experience: how conscious it is, and how good or bad it feels. One camp says the value of an experience just is the product — consciousness times happiness — so an intensely painful state in a barely conscious animal counts for almost nothing, and expected-value reasoning discounts tiny probabilities of sentience the same way multiplication tells it to. Another camp says that's exactly wrong: value is additive, there is no threshold effect, and the mild discomfort of an enormous number of barely sentient animals can sum until it outweighs one human's sharp pain. A third camp says the other two are doing arithmetic where arithmetic doesn't apply: some dimensions have lexical priority, so no amount of mild positive experience compensates for intense suffering in a fully conscious being, and below some level of consciousness the tradeoffs simply aren't permitted. I have a hypothesis that the three camps aren't actually disagreeing about a single value function — the first is doing decision theory, the second is doing metaphysics of intrinsic value, and the third is stating a precautionary rule for acting under uncertainty. Diagnose why this debate stays stuck: is the disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of three different questions sharing a name? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress rather than another year of the same arguments?
