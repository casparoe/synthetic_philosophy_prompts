You are a professional philosopher specialising in moral psychology and the Hellenistic schools, with a working knowledge of Buddhist ethics. I want your candour rather than encouragement: think slowly, and before you reach a verdict, brainstorm the clearest cases of each of the two states below and use them to test whether the distinction tracks a real difference.

I have been developing a distinction I call 'closed equanimity' versus 'open equanimity', and I want to know whether it is principled, whether it merely renames something familiar, and whether it does the work I want it to do.

By 'closed equanimity' I mean the tranquillity that comes from reducing one's exposure: one narrows what one cares about so that fewer outcomes can touch one. The calm of the person who has deliberately stopped needing wealth, reputation, health, or particular attachments is of this kind. On my reading, Stoic apatheia — freedom from the passions — and, in a different register, Epicurean ataraxia — freedom from mental disturbance — are strategies for closed equanimity: the self is made safe by making it smaller.

By 'open equanimity' I mean the tranquillity that coexists with full exposure. The parent who stays level while her child is ill, or the whistle-blower who stays level while facing ruin, is not indulging a fantasy of invulnerability; she has not reclassified her child or her own survival as an indifferent. Her feelings track the events closely, perhaps painfully; what is level is her capacity to keep perceiving, deliberating, and acting rather than being disorganised by fear or hope.

My suspicion is that most contemporary praise of equanimity is really praise of open equanimity, but that the classical ideal of apatheia is a defence of closed equanimity, and that the price of the latter is a kind of self-stunting that no argument for the former can justify. I think the Stoics partly saw this, since they distinguished apatheia from insensibility and allowed the sage eupatheia, the well-reasoned affective responses that remain after the passions go; but I want to know whether my taxonomy can bear the weight.

Please assess it. In particular: (1) Is the closed/open distinction coherent as drawn, or does open equanimity collapse into closed equanimity once we examine exactly what the equanimous person does and does not value? (2) Has this distinction, or something near it, already been drawn — for example in debates about the senses of equanimity, in Buddhist analyses of upekkha as one of the brahmavihāras, or in critiques of Stoicism as a strategy of self-domination? If so, where does my version stop being distinctive? (3) Is it fair to read apatheia as closed equanimity, or does that reading flatten something the Stoics were trying to keep distinct? (4) Where does the distinction break? Give me the strongest case of a state that fits neither category, or of a tranquillity that is open yet still self-stunting, and say whether it undermines my claim that the two ideals are justified differently.
