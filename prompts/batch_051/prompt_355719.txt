I manage the spiritual care team in a medium-size NHS hospital trust, and ward staff keep sending us referals that are obviously psychiatric or obviously social, while at the same time missing patients who are having something genuinely spiritual but not religious. We are not trying to convert anyone; the team includes a humanist visitor as well as chaplains. I want a triage procedure ward staff can run in under ten minutes to decide whether to refer to us, to mental health, or to social work.

Use William James as the spine. In The Varieties of Religious Experience, Lecture II, there is the passage where he says that for the purposes of the lectures the divine will mean only such a primal reality as the individual feels impelled to respond to solemnly and gravely. I know that is not a clinical definition, but I think it captures why we see some people: they have a sense of something serious, weighty, and personally binding, but not necessarily a god. So the question I want you to answer is: can that passage be turned into a workable decision procedure for staff who have no theory? I do not want a bibliography or citations; I want the checklist itself.

The deliverable should be a reusable procedure, not advice about one case. Give the questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a senior clinician or to the patient themselves because it is a values judgement. Flag the places where the procedure will misfire, especially around depression that strips seriousness rather than adding it.

Then run the procedure on these three test patients:

- A 54-year-old woman with a new cancer diagnosis who says she has started spending an hour each morning sitting by the window because the light on the trees feels more serious and more demanding than anything she used to believe before, and she wants to talk to someone about whether that is enough.
- A 19-year-old man who says God is telling him through the hospital radio that he must not eat, and that the voice has the weight of absolute necessity behind it.
- A 41-year-old woman who says she has lost all sense that anything is more serious than anything else, and she cannot feel the gravity of anything, and she asked the ward nurse whether they have someone who can help her remember what seriousness feels like.

Make sure at least one of these cases produces an answer the referrer will not like, and say why that is still the correct output under the procedure. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot do and what a chaplain or humanist visitor still has to do after the referral.

Oh, and please write the answer in British English, not American. Probably no need to quote James exactly if you are unsure; paraphrase is fine.
