My dad died last year, and while I was clearing out his flat I found the paperwork for the extension my dad had his brother build in the late 1980s. Turns out my uncle — the builder — charged him nearly double the original quote, and from the letters it's pretty clear it wasn't a mistake: my dad hinted at it more than once and got fobbed off. The extra payments set the family back for years, and I don't think my dad ever really got over the stress of it.

My uncle is still alive: 82, not in great health, and honestly quite changed — he does a lot for his grandchildren, volunteers, the whole thing. There's no legal route anymore (a solicitor mate says any claim would be time-barred by now), and I'm not sure I'd sue even if there were. But my sister thinks I should drop the whole thing moraly too. Her line is that the man who did that doesn't really exist anymore, that fourty years of decent living has to count for something, and that at some point a moral claim just expires, the way the legal one does.

I keep going back and forth. I should say, I wasn't the one wronged — though I'm now the one carrying the anger. My dad never actually forgave him either; they stayed civil but the closeness never came back. So I can't work out whether my sister is right that time itself changes the moral situation, or whether time only changes the practicalities (evidence, memory, what punishment would even achieve) and I'm muddling those with the moral question. Which of us is closer to being right, and why? Treat the law as settled and irrelevant — I'm not after legal advice. Also, could you write your answer in British English.
