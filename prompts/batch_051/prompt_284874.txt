Please argue that a court can coherently adopt only the weak form of the absurdity doctrine in statutory interpretation. By the weak form, I mean the principle that the apparent absurdity of a proposed reading may weigh heavily among otherwise available readings of ambiguous text. By the strong form, I mean the principle that a court may set aside what it concedes to be the plain meaning of an enacted provision when that plain meaning would produce a result the court regards as absurd. In making the argument, address at least the following: (1) why the strong form is not a mere application of legislative supremacy but a covert delegation to courts of the power to revise enacted rules; (2) how the strong form differs from the correction of a genuine scrivener's error; and (3) how to answer the objection that general rules necessarily yield some unforeseen perverse applications and that it is better to have judges correct these than to require legislators to anticipate them.
