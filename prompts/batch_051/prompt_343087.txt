I serve on the emergency-preparedness committee of a regional health authority, and we are revising our scarce-resource protocol for mass toxic-exposure events. One issue has split the committee for two meetings, and we need a decision memo before the protocol goes out for comment. The trigger was a case from last year: during a chemical spill, a field hospital had enough of the only effective antidote to save either one severely poisoned patient, who would have required the entire remaining supply, or five moderately poisoned patients, each of whom could have been stabilized with one-fifth of the supply. The treating team gave the drug to the five, and the single patient died. Our committee must now decide what the standing rule should say. Stated abstractly, the question before us is whether the numbers should count.

One faction wants a maximization rule: where needs and prospects are otherwise comparable, direct the resource to the larger group of patients whose lives can be saved. They hold that a public agency cannot knowingly let four extra people die for the sake of an abstract scruple, and that any alternative would leave field staff with no defensible basis for choosing. Another faction wants an equal-claims rule: each patient has the same urgent claim not to be left to die, no one's claim shrinks because other claimants happen to exist, and the fact that more people were saved on the other side is not an answer to the one who was left. They favor an unweighted lottery among all eligible patients, or first-come treatment, but not group size. A third faction has floated a weighted lottery that gives each affected patient an equal chance, so that the group of five would receive five chances in six and the single patient one chance in six.

Draft the committee's decision memo. Recommend one of these three policies, or a clearly constrained variant of one, with reasoning the members could actually adopt in a public protocol; state honestly the strongest consideration on the losing side and how your recommendation answers it; and end with a bottom-line rule that field staff could apply without calling the committee.
