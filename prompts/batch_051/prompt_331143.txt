I am drafting a position statement on decision-making under ambiguity for a graduate workshop, and before I circulate it I want a careful consistency audit of the package as a whole. To fix ideas, use the standard three-color Ellsberg setup: an urn contains 90 balls, of which 30 are red and the remaining 60 are black or yellow in unknown proportions; a bet pays $100 if the named color is drawn and $0 otherwise. Here is the set of commitments I am inclined to defend:

1. I strictly prefer a bet on red to a bet on black, and I strictly prefer a bet on black-or-yellow to a bet on red-or-yellow. I regard both preferences as rationally permissible rather than as errors to be corrected.

2. I think the correct formal representation of these preferences is two-level Bayesian. The agent assigns a probability distribution over the possible compositions of the urn — in my case, since I have no reason to favor one black/yellow split over another, a symmetric distribution over the 61 possible numbers of black balls — and then evaluates an act by first computing its expected utility conditional on each composition, applying a strictly concave function to that conditional expected utility, and taking the expectation over compositions.

3. I accept a reduction principle for compound uncertainty. If an act involves two stages of uncertainty — first which urn composition obtains, and then which ball is drawn — the rational evaluation of the act should depend only on the unconditional probabilities of the final outcomes obtained by compounding the two stages. The staging itself should carry no weight: a two-stage act is to be evaluated by the reduced single-stage distribution it induces.

4. I deny that I am doing worst-case reasoning. The concavity in (2) is meant to capture a general aversion to second-order uncertainty, not the idea that the worst composition, or a maximally unfavorable one, is the one that matters. I would reject maxmin-style accounts that evaluate an act by its minimum conditional expected utility.

5. For decisions in which all relevant probabilities are known exactly — such as bets on a fair coin or an urn with a known composition — expected utility with a single probability is the correct theory, and I would not apply any concavity or ambiguity premium to them.

6. I treat stochastic dominance as non-negotiable: if one act yields at least as good an outcome in every state and a strictly better outcome in some state, choosing the dominated act is irrational.

Go through these six commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension, which pairs are merely apparently in tension, and which are fully compatible. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say precisely what has to give and what the cheapest repair would be. I am not sure where the problems are, so please audit the whole set rather than treating it as already coherent.
