Take California's Proposition 8 (2008) as a case study for theories of direct democracy. Quick facts to anchor you: it was a citizen-initiated constitutional amendment banning same-sex marriage, passed with about 52% of the vote a few months after the California Supreme Court had held that same-sex couples had a state constitutional right to marry; the federal courts later struck it down. I don't need the legal blow-by-blow, I want the theory. Pick the main pro-direct-democracy positions—popular sovereignty, checking an unresponsive legislature, better civic outcomes, whatever you think is the strongest version—and run Prop 8 through them. Does the episode mostly refute those defenses, or does it just show that direct democracy needs rights-based side constraints, the kind the initiative process had temporarily overriden? And in the other direction: what does Prop 8 expose that both pro- and anti-direct-democracy theory are bad at explaining, e.g. that it was an initiative rather than a legislature referring a question, that it amended the constitution on a bare majority, that the campaign was lopsided and heavily funded? Be disciplined about the mapping; if a theory is silent about some feature of the case, say so instead of making it speak. And if I've set the case up wrong or picked a case that can't actually test these theories, tell me that plainly rather than playing along.
