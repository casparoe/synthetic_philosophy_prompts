Arrow's impossibility theorem is standardly presented as a theorem about aggregating ordinal preference rankings. If each voter supplies nothing more than an ordering, and the social ranking must satisfy unrestricted domain, transitivity, unanimity, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship, then the only possible aggregation rule is dictatorship. The theorem was proved when the administratively realistic input was a paper ballot, so it treated the inability to elicit and compare intensities of preference as a fixed feature of the problem rather than as an assumption worth flagging.

But that feature has shifted. Quadratic voting makes the cost of votes rise quadratically, so voters reveal how much an issue matters to them by how many votes they buy. Score voting and approval voting ask for more than a single ranking. Online deliberation platforms can now collect cardinal or continuous judgments from large populations at low cost. Suppose we treat this as a changed assumption rather than a trivial extension of the old framework. Does the impossibility result survive the move from ordinal rankings to cardinal preference reports? Which of Arrow's conditions is actually under pressure once voters can express intensity, and is there a repaired version of the theorem that still rules out a class of seemingly attractive rules? Or does cardinal information dissolve the original problem, and if so, does the impossibility merely relocate to a neighboring theorem about strategy-proofness or interpersonal comparability? The task is not to recite Arrow's proof, but to say whether his argument depends essentially on the ordinal-input assumption, and what remains of it if that assumption is removed.
