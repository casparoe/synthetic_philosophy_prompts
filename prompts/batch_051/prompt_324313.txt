I'm giving a talk next month at a philosophy department colloquium defending a version of expected value fanaticism. The thesis is roughly: once you grant expected utility theory, there's no principled, non-arbitrary way to stop the tiny-probability/huge-payoff moves that get called fanatical while keeping everything else. My argument in one paragraph: bounded utility just relocates the problem to wherever you set the bound; treating probabilities below some epsilon as zero violates continuity and gives you intransitive preferences across chains of bets; and risk aversion can't help because it's already baked into the utility function, so invoking it again double counts. So the anti-fanatic is left with a deontic side-constraint they never paid for.

The audience is a mainstream analytic department: mostly decision theorists and epistemologists, two or three ethicists, one historian of philosophy, and a handful of grad students. No hardcore effective altruists. The decision theorists come out of the Savage/Jeffrey tradition, the epistemologists are precise-credence people, and the ethicists lean consequentialist but with contractualist sympathies. I expect the objections to be about representation theorems, the interpretation of tiny probabilities, and whether expected value makes sense for genuinely unprecedented stakes — not the popular complaints about the conlusion being absurd.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get in the question period, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way this audience would phrase them. For each: a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is softest. Then give me the one question I should be hopeing nobody asks.

And be committed about it: tell me which objections are the real threats, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and where my own argument is exposed, rather than laying out the options.
