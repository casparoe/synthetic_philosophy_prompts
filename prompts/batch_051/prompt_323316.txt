I keep coming back to a thought experiment that has started to undermine a principle I otherwise find almost undeniable, and I want to know which one should give way.

The principle is that rational preference is transitive and that the rationality of a sequence of choices is fully determined by the rationality of each choice considered on its own. If I prefer A to B and B to C, I am committed to preferring A to C. And if each step in a process was the best available option when I took it, the endpoint cannot have been a rational mistake. This is how I already think about engineering decisions: a release is judged sound if each reviewed change was sound, and I don't see why practical reasoning should work differently.

Against that, here is the case. A person is fitted with a device that delivers an electric shock at an enormous number of finely graded settings, from none to excruciating. The difference between any setting and the next is too small for the person to detect. At each step, someone offers the person a meaningful amount of money to click up one notch, and since no single click makes the experience noticeably worse, each individual trade looks clearly worth taking. But after enough clicks the person is at a setting that is simply agonizing — a state they would have paid almost anything to avoid if it had been offered to them as one jump at the beginning. My intuition says something has gone seriously wrong with this person's choices, even though I cannot point to the particular click where the mistake occurred. The stepwise trades seem not to add up to a real preference for the endpoint.

I am not looking for an answer about willpower or hyperbolic discounting. I want to know whether the theory or the intuition should be revised, and on what grounds. Should we bite the bullet and say the endpoint is perfectly rational, because any objection to the sequence is really just a bias toward judging whole histories rather than individual choices? Or should we amend the theory — for example, by denying that preferences must be transitive, or by insisting that two settings can be indistinguishable without being equally valuable, or by requiring that options be judged only over complete courses of life rather than locally? What exactly would each side have to demonstrate before the other is rationally required to yield, and is there a reading on which the stepwise choices and the rejection of the endpoint are both correct?
