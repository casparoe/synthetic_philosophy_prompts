I've been in a philosophy of language reading group for a few months and we keep getting stuck on the same thing: the fight between deflationists and substantivists about truth. As far as I can tell this fight has been running in place since at least Ramsey and Tarski, and it doesn't seem to have moved. The deflationist says there's no interesting property of truth: the entire job of the word 'true' is exhausted by biconditionals like the sentence 'snow is white' is true just in case snow is white, plus a few rules for generalizing over sentences. Everything else—assertion, belief, validity, meaning—gets explained with other resources, not by pointing at some truth-property. The substantivist (correspondence theorist, pragmatist, whatever) says no: truth has to be a substantive relation or property, because otherwise you can't explain why we prefer true beliefs, what 'there are true propositions' commits us to, or why inquiry aims at truth rather than something else. But here's my frustration: when you press them, the two sides seem to agree on almost everything. The deflationist will happily say truth is a property, that truth exists, and that 'true' isn't just redundant in ordinary talk; the substantivist will grant that the disquotational biconditionals are correct as far as they go. They agree on classical logic, on all the T-sentences, on the everyday facts about usage, and even on most first-order claims about truth.

So my working hypothesis is that the debate has become entirely about explanatory bookkeeping—about whether a decent explanation of content and assertion has to route through a substantive notion of truth or can bypass it—and that's the kind of fight neither side can loose, because neither side can force the other to accept its standards for what explanations are allowed to invoke. Is that right, or is there still a genuine question in there (about whether we can quantify into the truth predicate when we say 'everything she said is true', or about whether truth is normative, or something else) that one side actually gets wrong? I don't want a survey of deflationism and its rivals; I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Take a definite stand: say whether this is a substantive disagreement, a verbal-ish one, or two different questions sharing a name, and then tell me what would actually count as progress given your diagnosis—a formal result, a well-placed distinction, a reformulated question, or an honest concession that it's ultimately terminological.
