I am a novelist, and the question I am putting to you arises from a problem in the book I am drafting rather than from a paper I am writing. One of my characters, a cultural evolutionist with a forbidding manner, is trying to persuade a young archivist that the idea she has spent years trying to preserve cannot be what she thinks it is, because, as he would have it, a persistent antimeme is impossible. His argument, as I have drafted it, runs as follows. (1) In ordinary person-to-person cultural transmission, an idea persists only if it transmits at least at replacement: each holder must, on average, cause at least one new holder before the idea fades from them. (2) An antimeme is, by its nature, an idea whose content makes its holders remember it poorly and mention it less readily than they mention ordinary ideas. (3) Therefore antimemes transmit below the ordinary rate of transmission. (4) But any idea that transmits below the ordinary rate is a net loser in cultural competition, and a net loser will be driven below replacement by selection. (5) Therefore antimemes transmit below replacement. (6) Whatever transmits below replacement disappears from the culture within a few generations. (7) Therefore a persistent antimeme is impossible; if the archivist's idea has genuinely persisted, it cannot be an antimeme. My instinct is that the argument is too quick, but this character is meant to be rigorous rather than sophistical, and I do not want to put an unnoticed hole in his mouth. Tell me plainly whether the argument is valid as it stands. If it is not, tell me exactly which step fails, under what reading of its key terms, and what that failure does to the conclusion. If it is valid, say so and be precise about why the severe conclusion follows. Work only from the argument itself; give no citations, no bibliography, and no suggested readings. And reply in continuous prose, as you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
