Next month I'm giving a departmental colloquium at a philosophy-heavy cognitive science program, and I want to be prepped for the Q&A. My talk defends this: the multiple realizability argument for functionalism conflates two different things — synchronic multiple realization across organisms at a time, and diachronic multiple realization within one organism over its lifespan. Once you pull those apart, the intuitive cases (neural plasticity, recovery after lesions, prosthetic substitution) only support the diachronic kind, and the diachronic kind is compatible with a species-relative or even individual-relative type identity theory. So the premise that's supposed to force functionalism doesn't, and without it the main argument for functionalism collapses into either begging the question or a much weaker epistemic claim about our ignorance of neural kinds.

The audience is mostly empirically-minded philosophers of cognitive science plus a few neuroscientists who wander into philosophy talks. There's one hardcore computational functionalist who treats multiple realizability as basically settled, one person from the mechanistic explanation side who things mental kinds just are the mechanisms that do the causal work and who'll be suspicious of anything that smells like anti-realism about kinds, and several grad students working on predictive processing who'll want to know what this does to their representational taxonomy.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way these specific people would phrase them, with what to concede, what to hold, and the weak spot in my own argument I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
