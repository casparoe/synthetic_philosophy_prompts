A friend and I have been arguing about AI in medicine, and we keep landing on two judgments I can't get to cohere. Take two radiologists. Dr. A overrides her own read of a chest CT because the hospital's deep-learning system flags a nodule; when asked why, she says she doesn't know what the system saw, but it gets a lot of cases right. A review committee calls this automation bias and puts her through remedial training; the worry, they say, is that over time this kind of deference will erode her ability to read scans herself. Dr. B overrides her own read because a senior radiologist glances at the image and says "that looks wrong to me," without spelling out the finding. Nobody files an incident report; that's just a curbside consult, and if she had ignored the senior colleague she'd be seen as reckless. Structurally, both are deference to an opaque other: a recommendation arrives without accessible reasons, and the source's track record is doing the epistemic work. Yet ordinary practice treats the first as a loss of understanding to be prevented and the second as expertise working as intended.

I can't settle for the first-pass explanations. "Humans are accountable and machines aren't" seems weak, because the senior radiologist will face no consequences if her hunch is wrong, while the hospital will if the model errs. "The human can explain later" often isn't true when the consult is a gestalt impression. What I want is the strongest principle that actually generates both verdicts, stress-tested against nearby cases: the same call coming from a human-designed scoring rule printed in the report, a star consultant in another hospital she's never met, an AI that shows a tidy saliency map, a colleague she knows is sleep-deprived. Work through which candidate distinctions do the real work—shared context, stakes, liability, track record, an evolved tendency to defer to people, something else—and tell me honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. Please write the answer in American English.
