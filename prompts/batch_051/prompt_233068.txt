I've started to notice a habit of mine in arguments, and I can't tell whether it's a defensable policy or just a refusal to reason wearing a philosophy costume. When someone presents a deductive argument as settling a question, I do the move from Carroll's tortoise: I grant every premise, but I won't move to the conclusion until they justify the inferential step itself — e.g., modus ponens — without already using it. Since they basically never can do that, I treat the conclusion as unproven. Last week a friend argued that if rent control lowers new construction, and lower construction worsens shortages, then rent control worsens shortages. I accepted both premises and still refused the 'then'; I said he hadn't given me a reason to trust the transition from premises to conclusion, and he said that's exactly what reasoning is.

Is my policy defensible? What would even count as justifying an inference rule like modus ponens, and does the apparent impossibility of doing so show I'm right to withhold assent, or does it show my demand is confused? Where does the demand for justification properly stop, and how should I describe what I'm doing when I do move across the 'then'?
