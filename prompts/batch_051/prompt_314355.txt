A friend and I recently spent an evening arguing about whether any version of eternal conscious torment could count as a just punishment, and I noticed that I kept rephrasing every position in the only vocabulary in which punishment makes real sense to me: the criminal-sentencing framework of proportionality, mens rea, aggravating and mitigating factors, deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, parole, and life without parole. I want to find out how far that translation actually reaches. Restate the three main theological positions on hell — eternal conscious torment, annihilationism or conditional immortality, and universal reconciliation or universalism — one by one and as faithfully as you can in that sentencing vocabulary. For each, say which claims translate cleanly, which translate only under distortion, and which have no counterpart in the framework at all. Then give me the residue report: where the translation fails, is it because the sentencing framework genuinely cannot express something — say, a punishment that is not imposed by a temporal state or that continues after a character has become fixed by a final choice — or because the underlying theological claim was less determinate than it looked, such as what infinite punishment would even mean as a sentence? Please do not explain or defend the legal vocabulary; I am fluent in it, and I want the translation treated as a test of both frameworks rather than a defense of either.
