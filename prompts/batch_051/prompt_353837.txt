I have come to accept, at least provisionally, a version of the quantum immortality thesis. On my understanding, if the universe branches in roughly the way Everettian many-worlds says it does, then in any genuinely quantum life-or-death event there will be at least one branch in which the agent survives, and the agent's first-person perspective can only ever be located in a branch where they survive: in the dying branches there is, by stipulation, no surviving perspective to have the experience. From the first-person point of view, therefore, the agent should expect to survive such events, even a long series of quantum suicide experiments in which third-person observers would expect to watch them die with the usual Born-rule probabilities.

Tell me what would count against this position. I want more than the standard list; I want a systematic map of its exposure, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. In the course of that map, work through at least the following: (a) what the argument assumes about branch weights or a measure over branches, and whether first-person credences must track those weights; (b) what it assumes about personal identity across branches, and who exactly the 'you' is who is supposed to keep surviving; (c) how much of the view consists of the trivially true claim that you never observe yourself dead, as opposed to a substantive prediction; (d) which objections a committed defender would concede genuinely count against the thesis, rather than dismiss as misunderstandings of many-worlds; and (e) whether the thesis has to be qualified in ways that leave it insulated from any possible observation, and whether that insulation is a serious defect for a view of this kind. End by giving your own honest verdict on how exposed the position actually is, and identify the single development or argument that would most move you if you held this position.
