So I've been thinking about money pumps and time inconsistency, and I realized I don't actually know what a "preference" is. I want a working definition—not just the simple econ one where preferences are whatever choice behavior reveals, because that seems to beg the question on exactly the cases I care about.

Here are the cases I want the definition to get right:

1. The easy yes: you always pick chocolate over vanilla when both are available and you say you like chocolate more. That's obviously a preference.

2. The easy no: a thermostat set to hold 68 degrees "prefers" 68. I'm comfortable saying that's metaphorical, not a literal preference.

3. Tea and coffee: she says she prefers tea, but whenever she's actually offered both she takes coffee. Is the preference the avowed one, the behavior, or is it just underdetermined?

4. The cycler: in pairwise forced choices he picks A over B, B over C, and C over A. Under revealed preference there's simply no transitive ordering to attribute to him, but it feels wrong to say he has no preferences at all. Relatedly, he's money-pumpable: you can charge him to swap around among options he himself supposedly prefers.

5. Ulysses: before the voyage he prefers to be tied to the mast, and when he hears the sirens he prefers to be untied. Is that one person with changing preferences, one real preference and one local impulse, or something else?

6. The hyperbolic discounter: asked a year in advance, he prefers $100 in 13 months over $90 in 12. But when the $90 is available immediately one year later, he takes it. Does the preference itself shift, or does he never have one stable preference to begin with?

7. The smoker: she says she prefers to quit, plans to quite, and seems sincere, but she has a cigarette every evening anyway. I want the definition to let us say whether the quitting preference and the smoking preference are both "hers," and which one we should respect when, say, a friend hides her cigarettes as a precommitment device.

8. The corporation: a company's revealed behavior "prefers" this quarter's profits over long-term survival (it underfunds maintenance until the plant fails). Is that literally a preference, or is the word doing different work when we move from people to groups?

Propose a definition, run it through all eight, and flag the cases where it gives a weird answer or no answer. I suspect "preference" is doing two jobs—one descriptive (a stable disposition that shows up in choice) and one more normative (what a person "really" wants on reflection). If you end up going that route, give me both definitions and tell me which cases move between them. Bonus if your definition helps explain why being money-pumpable feels like a flaw in the first place.
