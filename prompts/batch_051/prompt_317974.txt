I'm teaching Groundhog Day (1993) in a unit on moral desert, and I want help putting pressure on the familiar reading of the film as a karmic fable. On that reading, Phil Connors is held in the loop until he has earned his release, and his eventual happiness is what he deserves. But karma as a philosophical theory of moral desert normally involves more than that: past actions generating future circumstances, across lives, without the agent's memory of the debits and credits. The film inverts one of those elements, since Phil remembers every iteration while everyone else forgets. Read the film through karma-as-desert anyway. Where does it genuinely dramatize the idea that one earns one's condition, and where does it show something closer to Aristotelian habituation or utilitarian trial-and-error, with desert dropping out? What should we do with the fact that Phil cannot save the homeless man no matter how virtuous he becomes? I'd like a reading faithful to both the film and the philosophical account, one that says plainly where the karma reading is the film's own logic and where it is a projection.
