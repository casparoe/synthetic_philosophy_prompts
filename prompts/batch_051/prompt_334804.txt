I've come to accept a broadly Schopenhauerian account of the will and of hedonic experience, and I want to know what it would take to move me off it. The view, as I hold it, separates into two claims. Metaphysically: what each of us most fundamentally is, beneath the individuated self, is a blind and insatiable will — a striving that ultimately wills only its own continuation, so that every satisfied desire at once throws up another, and rest is never more than the interval between two wants. Experientially: pleasure is in its ordinary cases negative — it is the felt suspension or removal of a striving, a pain, or a lack — while suffering is the positive and primary datum of experience. Satisfied desire therefore delivers no positive surplus of feeling, only relief, and relief is quickly displaced by fresh striving or, once striving subsides, by boredom. The evaluative conclusion I am drawn to is that no human life can be net-positive in felt experience for long, and that the only enduring respites are aesthetic contemplation and a kind of ascetic denial of the will.

I would like a map of this position's exposure, not a list of the usual criticisms. Treat the metaphysical claim and the experiential claim as separable where that helps: tell me which observations, arguments, intuitions, or empirical results would count against the experiential claim alone, which against the metaphysical claim alone, and which against the two in combination, with the defeaters ranked from decisive to merely awkward. Where the view makes predictions — about hedonic adaptation, the return of boredom after satisfaction, the contrastive character of remembered pleasure — say what it predicts that its rivals (desire-satisfaction theories, positive-affect theories of well-being, eudaimonism) do not, and what a fair test of those predictions would look like. I also want to know what a committed Schopenhauerian should herself concede counts against the view, as distinct from what its critics merely say counts, and whether the position as I have stated it has insulated itself against refutation: are the claims that ordinary happiness is self-deception and that genuine release requires will-less contemplation ad hoc shields, and is that insulation a problem for a doctrine of this kind or simply what one should expect from metaphysics? Finally, say which single finding or argument ought to move me most, and whether I could retain the experiential claim if the metaphysical claim collapsed.
