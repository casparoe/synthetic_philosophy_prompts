Two friends of mine were arguing about a made-up country—call it Vlandia—whose constitution has a gap. The constitution vests all executive power in a president with a fixed four-year term, and says the only way to replace the president is through an election run by a five-person Electoral Commission. The Commission's members are appointed by the president, and because he didn't trust anyone he left all five seats empty. Then he dies with two years left on his term. There is no provision for what happens if the Commission is vacant.

One said: this is an interregnum in the strong sense. The legal order literally runs out—nobody is authorized to act as president or to appoint anyone else, and whoever steps in is a usurper, even if everyone accepts them and things work fine afterwards. The other said that's treating a constitution like a machine that turns off when a step is missing. Legal systems carry implicit gap-filling norms; the senior judges (or whatever caretaker body exists) can run an emergency election, because some government is necessary and the drafters can't of intended the state to just dissolve. The first replied: "can't have intended" is fanfiction. If the rules authorize no one, then acting out of necessity may be morally justified, but it isn't lawful, and it's no different in kind from the coup it's trying to avoid. The second came back: every constitution eventually rests on officials acting and being accepted, not on an unbroken chain of prior authorizations—by your standard the original founding was also unlawful, which is absurd. So "lawful" can't mean "authorized by an earlier lawful act." The first said: now you've stopped doing constitutional law and started doing sociology. You're describing who will be treated as the government, not who is entitled to be, which was my point—the constitution itself gives no answer.

I couldn't tell who was winning. Can you adjudicate this move by move? For each step, say who is right about what, flag where the disagreement slides between "who may lawfully act," "who should act as a matter of necessity or reason," and "who will in fact be accepted as governing," and say what, if anything, both of them are missing.
