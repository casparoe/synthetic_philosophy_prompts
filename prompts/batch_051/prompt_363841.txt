I keep flip-floping on an argument I had with a friend who works in safety research. We both agree an LLM should be held to a worst-case guarantee: you throw thousands of jailbreaks and edge-case prompts at it, and one success in ten thousand is still a failure worth retraining over, because that one success can be copy-pasted and the model will keep producing it forever. But we don't demand anything like that from humans doing the equivalent job. A doctor can miss one diagnosis in a hundred and we call that good medicine. A teacher can give one genuinely terrible piece of advice a semester and keep their job. Nobody says a hospital is "misaligned" because somewhere a nurse is persuadable once if you ask the right way. So there's the asymmetry: worst-case standards for the AI, average-case standards for the human.

I want to know whether that asymmetry is actually defensible. List the candidate justifications people reach for—scale and copyability, auditability of internals, accountability, the fact that a human is a moral patient, the fact that a model can be redeployed cheaply so we can afford to raise the bar—and pressure-test each one against a matched pair of near cases: a human whose one bad answer gets screenshotted and retweeted to a million people, versus an AI deployed privately that can make at most one mistake before it's shut down. If copyability is really doing all the work, then is the principle actually "worst-case for AIs, average-case for humans," or is it "worst-case consequences get worst-case standards, for whoever or whatever produces them"? And if you could run the same adversarial red-teaming on the human—secret shoppers, angry patients, sleep deprivation, bribes—would you still hire the human who fails one time in a hundred? Give me the strongest principle you can build, and an honest verdict on whether we should keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up.
