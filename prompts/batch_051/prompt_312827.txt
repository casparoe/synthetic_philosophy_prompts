In my political economy course we have been working through the socialist calculation debate, and I am trying to pin down exactly what survives of the Austrian side of it once modern computation is granted. Let me lay out the dialectic as I currently understand it. Mises's original objection was that rational economic calculation is impossible without genuine factor prices, and such prices cannot arise in the absence of private ownership of the means of production, because there is no market in production goods to generate them. Lange replied that a planning board could dispense with markets in capital goods and still find equilibrium prices by trial and error: announce provisional prices, observe the resulting shortages and surpluses, and adjust until the economy clears, treating the allocation problem as one of iteration rather than simultaneous solution. Hayek then shifted the objection away from sheer computational infeasibility and toward the character of the knowledge itself: much of the relevant knowledge is local, tacit or unarticulated, dispersed across millions of agents, and in important cases generated only within a competitive process. On that view the planner's difficulty is not that the equations are too hard to solve, but that the data needed to formulate them do not exist apart from the market activity that produces them.

I want to think through whether this Hayekian knowledge argument survives the existence of modern algorithmic planning. Large firms now coordinate continental-scale logistics through algorithms and sensor data with little in the way of an internal price system, and they train demand forecasts on real-time transaction and behavioral data from huge populations. Suppose such techniques were extended to something closer to a whole economy: a continuously updated algorithmic planner fed by transaction records, sensors, and revealed-preference data at scale. I am not asking about the politics of any particular country, nor about whether building such a system is likely. I am asking what happens to the argument.

Specifically: which premise of the Hayekian objection is actually under pressure here? Is the knowledge problem he identified ultimately a computational problem in disguise, so that better computation and richer data could dissolve it, or does it persist even for an arbitrarily good algorithmic planner? And if full algorithmic planning is granted as technically feasible, can a weaker but still distinctively Hayekian version of the calculation argument be restated, or does the objection then collapse into a claim about incentives and motivation that is no longer about calculation at all? I am drafting a paper for the course and want to know whether the old argument should be treated as dead, as needing repair, or as largely untouched by the technological change.
