I'm a novelist, and I've been basing a minor character on a friend of mine, Dana, who is a biostatistician with very firm views about what the word probability actually means. I've been joting down her takes over the last few years because I want the character to be consistent, and now I need to predict how she'd react to a case I'm about to write into a scene. Here's what she's said so far.

About a fair die that's about to be rolled, she says 1/6 is a real, objective feature of the die and the throwing setup, not a degree of belief. She points out that it's the kind of fact that would show up as a stable relative frequency over a very long run of rolls, but she doesn't reduce the probability to a count of observed outcomes.

About a biased coin that a colleague flipped 100,000 times and got heads on 40,000, she says the observed 40% is a noisy measurement; the real probability is the stable value those frequencies are estimating, and it belongs to the coin-and-tossing arrangement, not to anyone's state of mind.

About the weather forecast's 30% chance of rain tomorrow, she says this is a different usage entirely. A day doesn't have a hidden physical quantity of that sort; the 30% is a summary of what the forecaster's model and information support, not a fact about the atmosphere.

About a coin that has already been tossed but hasn't been looked at yet, she says there's no 50% fact about the coin anymore. The toss has settled it; calling it 50/50 now is just a statement about the observer's ignorance.

About a lottery with one winning ticket among ten, she treats 1/10 as a real feature of the draw mechanism and the tickets, the sort of thing that would show up as a frequency across many draws.

About the standard epidemiology line that a smoker is, say, five times more likely to develop some condition, she says those numbers are frequencies within a reference class of similar people, not a hidden force inside any individual smoker.

Now the scene. An engineer has built a single custom machine, one of a kind, which will be activated exactly once and then destroyed. There's no class of similar machines and no sequence of prior activations to draw on. Its components have some known failure tendencies from other uses, but this assembly has never been tested. From the design and those component tendencies, the engineer says there's a 0.4 probability that it fails on its one and only activation.

What would Dana say about that 0.4, if I want her to stay consistent with all of the above? I care less about the dialogue than about the analysis. Specifically: which interpretations of probability fit her track record (frequentism, subjectivism, propensity, or some messier mix of them), what each live interpretation predicts she'd say about the one-off machine, which she'd most likely say and why, and one further case I could put to her that would decide between whichever interpretations are still open after the machine case. If her real view is messier than the textbook labels, say so rather than forcing it. Dana is the kind of person who has a considered view about exactly this, so don't settle for "she'd hedge." At the very end, put a final line that starts with "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase giving your best guess as to what interpretation of probability she's running.
