In the dominant design, a commercial AI assistant is a single model, trained once and deployed to every market at once, carrying one set of refusal categories, one understanding of what counts as a neutral or balanced answer to a contested political question, and one safety policy that applies in roughly the same form to a user in Lagos, a user in Riyadh, and a user in San Francisco. It is, in effect, one moral voice speaking to the whole world, while its developers describe it as neutral, pluralistic, or merely a tool. I have been trying to work out what this practice presupposes about whose values an aligned system should serve. Does shipping one model for everyone presuppose that there exists a shareable core of values that all reasonable users could accept, the way a liberal state is supposed to remain neutral among comprehensive doctrines? Or does it presuppose something closer to a covert majoritarianism, in which the values of one demographic, one legal culture, or one company get to be the system's values? Is the practice even internally consistent, given that its public justification is pluralist but its artifact is a single moral point of view? I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of value aggregation, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change in how these systems are built, trained, and deployed if the implicit theory were replaced by genuine value pluralism, by an overlapping consensus among the system's users, or by explicit majoritarianism.
