A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about what should and should not invalidate consent to sex, and I came away unsure which principle she was actually running. I would like help reconstructing it from her recorded verdicts and predicting how she would rule on two new cases.

Here is what she said, as best I can recall.

On deception, she held that consent is invalid when one person impersonates the other's long-term partner, and when a doctor represents a sexual act as a medical examination. She held that consent remains valid when one person falsely professes love in order to obtain sex — she kept this view even after I pressed that the other person later said they would not have agreed had they known the profession was false — and when a married person conceals the marriage during an affair.

On pressure, she held that consent is invalid when an employer threatens to fire an employee for refusing sex, when someone threatens physical force, and when someone threatens to publish nude photographs of the other person. She held that consent remains valid when someone threatens to end the relationship unless the other person has sex, and when someone offers a large sum of money to a person in desperate poverty.

On competence, she held that consent is invalid when the person is severely intoxicated to the point of drifting in and out of awareness, and when an adult in a psychotic episode agrees because they believe their partner is a deity. She held that consent remains valid when the person is mildly drunk but coherent and enthusiastic, and when an adult with a moderate intellectual disability understands the mechanics and the risks and enthusiastically agrees.

The two new cases are these. First, a person falsely says they have recently tested negative for a serious sexually transmitted infection, and the other person consents partly in reliance on that statement. Second, a person tells their partner that if the partner will not have sex, they will stop paying for the partner's mother's life-saving medical treatment, which they have been paying voluntarily and could stop at any time without legal consequence.

What should I predict my friend would say about each new case, if she is to remain consistent with the verdicts above? I am less interested in the bare answers than in the rule or rules that best fit her record, the other rules that fit it just as well, and which of the two new cases — or which further case I could put to her — would best tell those rules apart. Please separate what her existing verdicts actually settle from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on consent, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
