Take the eurozone crisis of roughly 2009–2012 and analyze it through Hume's price-specie-flow mechanism. I don't just want the usual story about capital flows and current account deficits; I want a careful mapping between the mechanism and what actually happened in the euro area, run in both directions.

For background, here's the mechanism as I understand it. Under metallic money, a country running a trade surplus receives specie, its money supply expands, domestic prices and wages rise, its exports get less competitive and imports get more attractive, and the surplus shrinks; the deficit country goes through the reverse. Hume used this to argue that obsessing over the balance of trade as a permanent state was misguided, because the adjustment is automatic.

Now the eurozone. The salient facts: Germany ran large, persistent current account surpluses, while Spain, Portugal, Greece and (somewhat differently) Ireland ran deficits. They share a currency, so there's no exchange rate to move and no gold or silver flowing between them. The relative-price adjustment largely didn't happen in time: German wages were restrained, periphery wages and prices had risen during the boom, and when the music stopped the adjustment came through crisis, recession and unemployment rather than smooth rebalancing.

I want the analysis to run both ways. What does Hume's mechanism say should have happened? What does it explain about the build-up? Where exactly does the mapping break — fixed exchange rates, no specie flow, sticky wages, capital flowing from surplus to deficit countries, the ECB, fiscal rules? And what does the eurozone episode show about Hume's view of money and trade: is it a confirmation, an anomaly, or evidence that the mechanism was only ever a special case?
