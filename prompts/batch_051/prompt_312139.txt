A friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about Kelly betting—what he insisted on calling geometric rationality—and I left the conversation more unsettled than I expected. I had been defending something like the following picture. When you face a favorable bet that will be offered repeatedly, the policy that maximizes the expected logarithm of your wealth is the one that maximizes your wealth's long-run growth rate. For a simple even-money bet with a 60 percent chance of winning, that policy stakes 20 percent of your bankroll each round. The policy never stakes everything, avoids gambler's ruin, and with probability approaching one ends up ahead of any other fixed-fraction policy in the long run. I find this compelling because it seems to deliver a unique, non-arbitrary answer to the question of how much to stake—an answer that does not depend on whatever mere risk preference an agent happens to have.

My friend pressed the part I cannot get comfortable with: the apparent conclusion that rational stake sizing should be insensitive to differences in risk preference. He imagined a single favorable bet offered to two people, one young with secure earning prospects and one retired and living off savings. Kelly, applied to each, seems to tell both to stake the same fraction of their respective bankrolls—20 percent in the even-money case. My firm intuition is that these two agents should rationally stake different fractions, or at least that a theory with no resources for saying what should lead them to differ is missing something important. My friend countered that I was leaning on a one-shot intuition against a repeated-bet theorem, that the two agents differ only in their non-bankroll wealth and time horizons, and that once those are properly written into the setup the intuition dissolves rather than refuting the theory.

I do not want an answer that settles the matter in one paragraph. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that gives way, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that gives way, and whether there is a reading on which both survive—for instance, one on which Kelly is a constraint on aggressive staking rather than a substantive claim about the correct utility function, or one on which the two agents differ for reasons the theory can already represent.

Be as committal as the topic warrants and no more. If I have misstated the Kelly criterion, or conflated one-shot and repeated gambles, or built the example in a way that makes the question misleading, tell me that plainly and correct the setup before answering.
