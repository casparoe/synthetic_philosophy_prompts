In a course on comparative political philosophy I keep coming back to Analects 13.23, where Confucius distinguishes the noble person from the small person by saying, in Legge's Victorian rendering, that the one is catholic and not partisan, the other partisan and not catholic; most modern versions have the contrast as harmony versus sameness. I know the exact translation is disputed, but the general idea I have been given is that noble persons can hold different views and still be in accord because they are ordered toward a shared standard of propriety, whereas small persons merely comply or echo because they lack that standard.

Our instructor built an argument from this passage and I cannot find the flaw, though the conclusion seems too strong. The argument runs: (1) to value harmony as a political ideal is to value a whole in which different elements are arranged according to a fitting order, not merely coexisting; (2) for Confucius, the fitting order that turns difference into harmony is the order of ritual propriety and the roles it assigns, so harmony requires a shared standard of right relation; (3) if a polity's harmony requires a shared standard, then disagreements about that standard are not healthy plurality but defects in the political order; (4) liberal pluralism, by contrast, treats permanent reasonable disagreement about the good and about proper social roles as something a well-ordered state need not and should not try to eliminate; (5) therefore a Confucian must see liberal pluralism as a mistake: either it is the partisan sameness of a modus vivendi or it is discord masquerading as peace; (6) and a liberal pluralist must see Confucian harmony as an illiberal demand that everyone converge on one thick ethical vision; (7) so any attempt to combine them is unstable, because either the Confucian harmony loses its objective standard or the liberal pluralism loses its permanent disagreement.

But this conclusion feels too quick. It seems to prove that no society can value both deep difference and real harmony, which would make every pluralist tradition that talks about civic friendship, reconciliation, or unity-in-diversity either naive or dishonest. I want to know which premise or step to give up, and what rejecting it costs for the Confucian and liberal pluralist positions respectively. In particular, is the slide from harmony as an ethical virtue of persons to harmony as a property of a political order doing the real work? If I am inclined to keep premise (1) and reject step (6), what am I paying?
