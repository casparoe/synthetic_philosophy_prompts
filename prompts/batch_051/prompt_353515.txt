In my welfare econ class we keep running into the line that interpersonal utility comparisons are just like comparing two thermometers with different, uncalibrated scales. Each person's utility is a reading on their own thermometer, and you can say A's is up or down relative to A's earlier reading, but you can't say A's 30 is more than B's 50 because the zero points and units don't line up. The analogy gets used to shut down the idea that we can weigh one person's gain against another person's loss. I'm becoming suspicious it's doing more work than it can justify. Could you audit the thermometer analogy for me? I want a table of what corresponds to what, the places where the mapping really breaks, and an account of which disanalogies matter for the normative conclusion. Does the conclusion (no interpersonal comparisons without an extra calibration assumption) survive, or was the analogy carrying the argument the whole time?
