I am helping to draft the next decision of the Priorities Commission, a standing public body that allocates fixed programme budgets among rival proposals for improving well-being. The Commission measures prospective gains in cardinal benefit points, treats those points as interpersonally comparable, and routinely sums them across people; its earlier decisions are treated as precedents. Four of those decisions matter here.

Decision 04-12. Between a proposal to serve 20,000 adults for an expected 0.2 benefit points each (4,000 points in total) and one to serve 400 adults with a rare condition for an expected 15 points each (6,000 points in total), the Commission chose the latter, withdrawing funds from the larger group. The larger total won, even though the benefit was concentrated in comparatively few hands.

Decision 11-14. Between a proposal to serve 50,000 adults for an expected 0.3 points each (15,000 points in total) and one to serve 250 adults for an expected 40 points each (10,000 points in total), the Commission chose the former. Again the larger total won, this time by spreading benefit broadly.

Decision 03-16. The Commission considered a proposal to divert the whole personal-support budget to a single adult, M, whose unusual condition, the proposers argued, meant that each additional unit of resource produced an unusually large and roughly constant gain in measured well-being, with no detectable point of satiation; the claimed total was 1,000,000 points, against 10,000 points for the existing service used by 5,000 people. The Commission declined. It wrote that the estimate of M's capacity to benefit rested on a single case series and an unvalidated model, and that it would not displace the existing service on the strength of that evidence. It did not address whether a well-evidenced single-person allocation of this kind could ever be fair.

Decision 07-18. Between two bids with equal expected totals of 8,000 points, one serving 8,000 people and the other serving 80, the Commission funded the former, citing its published guidance that ties in total benefit are to be resolved in favour of the proposal benefiting the larger number of people.

The proposal concerning M has now been resubmitted. The evidence has been strengthened to the Commission's usual high standard: controlled within-person trials, independent replication of the model's predictions, and a validated capacity-to-benefit instrument. The proposal is to assign the entire annual personal-support budget to M and discontinue the service currently used by 5,000 people. The current service yields 10,000 benefit points in total; the new proposal projects 1,000,000 points for M, and M's per-unit gain appears constant across the whole range the budget could buy and roughly a hundred times the per-unit gain of the average current user. The press has described M as a utility monster, and the Commission is being asked whether its own record requires it to approve the proposal.

I am less interested in the headline verdict than in the consistency reasoning. Please (a) set out two or three plausible principles that would fit the four earlier decisions; (b) say what each principle would recommend in M's case; (c) identify the closest earlier decision and say whether it should be read narrowly, as a ruling about evidence, or broadly, as expressing a fairness constraint of some kind; (d) give your best judgement about the decision that coheres best with the record as a whole, and state what principle the Commission would be adopting either way; and (e) propose a further test case that would most cleanly distinguish the remaining candidate principles. Please write your answer in British English.
