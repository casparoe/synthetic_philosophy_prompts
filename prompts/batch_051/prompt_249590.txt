I am drafting a policy paper on lethal autonomous weapons, and before I evaluate any particular system I want to fix the standard of evaluation itself: what would an adequate account of meaningful human control have to deliver? The phrase is doing a great deal of work in arguments about whether a system may lawfully be deployed under international humanitarian law, whether anyone can be held responsible when a system kills the wrong people, and how engineers ought to design the human–machine interface in the first place. But the term seems to me to be asked to cover too many things at once, and different participants quietly build in different requirements: a human authorizing each individual engagement; a human defining and bounding the target set in advance; a human able to predict and override the system in real time; a commander who can be held accountable afterward; a system legible enough that supervision is not a ritual. I also suspect three things get run together that a good account should separate — who sets the targeting rules ex ante, who can veto or redirect a particular engagement, and who is answerable ex post — and that it matters whether meaningful requires that a human actually understand and predict what the system will do, or merely occupy the right causal and institutional role.

Write the desiderata that any theory of meaningful human control over autonomous weapons must meet, with adequacy fixed by those three jobs at once: a criterion for lawful deployability, a basis for attributing responsibility after failures, and a design target rather than a post hoc rationalization. For each requirement, give the operational case, feature of human–machine interaction, or legal-ethical demand that forces it, and say what a theory loses if it drops or weakens it. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp — for instance, a prior commitment to human-in-the-loop rather than human-on-the-loop supervision, to ex ante authorization rather than a real-time veto, or to individual rather than distributed control. Finally, answer the question I most want answered: once realistic constraints are imposed — very short engagement timelines for defensive systems, automation bias and supervisory complacency, distributed teams in which no single person sees the whole targeting cycle, and the need for the system to be predictable enough that a human can meaningfully supervise it at all — is some subset of the desiderata jointly unsatisfiable? If it is, identify the conflict precisely, say which requirement should give way and why, and state what the account should say in place of the requirement it abandons.
