I am the registrar at a mid-sized encyclopedic museum that has been working through a queue of restitution claims for objects acquired during the colonial period, and I have hit an interpretive problem with our own collections policy. The clause in question was adopted in 2003 and has not been revised since. It reads:

"6.1(d) The Museum shall not acquire or retain title to any object whose acquisition involved theft, looting, or removal under duress. Where there is credible evidence that an object was removed from its country of origin under conditions of colonial occupation, armed conflict, or political coercion, the Museum bears the burden of establishing that the removal and subsequent transfers were consensual; absent such a showing, the Museum shall transfer the object to an appropriate custodian."

We are now in a situation the 2003 drafters did not anticipate. Under a negotiated agreement, a Benin plaque in our collection will physically return to Nigeria in six months. In 2016 we commissioned a high-resolution 3D scan of the plaque. The raw scan data, the cleaned mesh, and AR viewer assets sit on our servers; our licensing arm sells 3D-printed replicas, and the digital team now wants to keep licensing the files after the physical object departs and has floated issuing an NFT derived from the scan. The Nigerian representatives argue that retaining and profiting from the scan is a continuation of the same holding, and that the value the clause was meant to transfer would simply be taken back out through the digital files. The digital team replies that the clause governs the physical object, that the scan was made in-house in 2016, and that no theft or duress attaches to the scan data.

Please write an essay walking through what the clause requires under each of the main approaches to interpretation: the plain words, the evident purpose, what the 2003 drafters would likely have said if asked about digital twins and commercial scans, and what a reasonable reader of a collections policy would understand. Where the approaches diverge, say which one the board should actually follow and why, and give a definite ruling on the three live options: transferring the scan data as part of the return, keeping the data but stopping all commercial use, and continuing to license the files or issuing the NFT. I want the strongest version of each interpretation, including the ones I find unappealing, and I care more about sustained interpretive reasoning than about what external law would say.
