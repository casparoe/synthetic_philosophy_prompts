Exam question: "Do elementary quantum particles violate Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles? Discuss with reference to the contrast between bosons and fermions."

Student's answer: "Quantum particles of the same species share all their intrinsic properties — mass, charge, and spin — so if two of them are distinct, Leibniz's principle seems to fail. The usual resolution is that the two statistics pull in different directions. Fermions obey the Pauli exclusion principle: two fermions in the same system cannot occupy the same one-particle state. This guarantees they differ in at least one of their state-dependent properties, so fermions automatically satisfy PII. Bosons, by contrast, can all occupy the same state, as in a Bose–Einstein condensate, where the particles have no distinguishing properties at all. But that is not a genuine counterexample to PII, since PII is a principle of classical metaphysics and quantum mechanics replaces it with the symmetrization and anti-symmetrization rules. So PII turns out true for fermions and is violated for bosons only if one wrongly insists on applying the classical principle."
