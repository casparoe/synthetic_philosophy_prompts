I'm trying to get past the standard-reading version of Rousseau's Lettre à d'Alembert, and there's a bit in the preface I keep coming back to. After saying he'd rather not pick a fight with d'Alembert, Rousseau gives the rule he says justifies doing it anyway:

"Justice & vérité, voilà les premiers devoirs de l'homme. Humanité, patrie, voilà ses premieres affections. Toutes les fois que des ménagemens particuliers lui font changer cet ordre, il est coupable."

(My rough gloss: justice and truth are man's first duties; humanity and fatherland are his first affections; whenever private considerations make him reverse that order, he's guilty.)

I think I've got a list of the obvious readings of why he thinks a theater would be bad for Geneva: (1) the spectator's pity is fake and self-congratulatory, because you get the pleasures of sympathy without any of the costs of acting; (2) theater can't reform morals because playwrights have to flatter the audience to succeed; (3) the actor's trade involves a sort of self-alienation that weakens civic identity; (4) theater is a substitute for public festivals and the assembled citizenry, turning active participants into isolated viewers. I'm pretty sure there's another argument or reading that this preface passage points to, one not in my list and not just a restatement of any of these. I want the thing that explains why Rousseau orders justice and truth above 'humanity, patrie' and how that ordering is doing the philosophical work in the Letter. What is it, and is it any good?
