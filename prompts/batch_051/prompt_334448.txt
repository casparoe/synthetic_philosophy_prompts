A friend and I have just spent a long evening arguing about the color phi phenomenon, and we ended up with more candidate positions than either of us could defend in a single sentence. In the phenomenon as we discussed it, a red light is flashed briefly at one location, then a green light is flashed briefly at a nearby location a short time later. Observers report seeing a single moving spot that changes from red to green around the midpoint of its trajectory, before the green flash actually occurred. The dispute is over how consciousness produces that report. On the Orwellian picture, the observer first has something like real-time experiences and later, after the second flash is processed, suffers an unconscious retroactive revision of the memory of what was seen; the report is a tidied-up history. On the Stalinesque picture, conscious experience is delayed until the second flash has been integrated, and the observer then becomes conscious of a single, already edited, temporally coherent sequence, with the color change appearing before its cause. My friend insisted this is a straightforward factual question about where in the brain the editing occurs; I suspected the question may never get off the ground without a fixed moment at which an experience can be said to enter consciousness.

Please rate the following positions rather than merely discussing them. Sort them into tiers from most to least convincing as accounts of what is happening in the color phi phenomenon, assign each a score from 0 to 1 for how much of the truth you think it captures, and give a one-sentence reason for each placement. I would rather settle this on the merits of the positions themselves than on which author defended which label. Then state which tier you would defend if our argument resumed tomorrow, and what you think my friend most likely underweighted.

The positions:

1. Pure Orwellian: the observer really has the earlier experiences first, and the edited content exists only as a later memory.
2. Pure Stalinesque: the observer never has the unedited experiences; consciousness comes only after the later flash, presenting an already coherent sequence.
3. No fact of the matter: once one abandons a central theater with a definite arrival time for each experience, the two stories are empirically equivalent descriptions of the same process, and there is no hidden fact separating them.
4. Granular hybrid: some temporal contents are fused before they are consciously available, while the report-level narrative of those contents is revised afterward; which label applies depends on the grain of analysis.
5. Instrumentalist: the choice is a modeling decision, not an ontological one; we should adopt whichever description better predicts behavior across different task demands.
6. Eliminativist: both labels presuppose a dated event called the experience, and the phenomenon shows that the question is ill-posed rather than merely hard.
