I have recently started reading moral philosophy, and I keep wanting to translate the moral luck debate into that vocabulary. By moral luck I mean the familiar four-way taxonomy of resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, and causal luck, together with the charge that our practices of praise and blame are threatened because outcomes, character, and circumstance all lie partly outside an agent's control.

Please restate the problem of moral luck in the vocabulary of statistical inference and experimental design, as systematically and honestly as you can. My working hunch is that the four kinds correspond to different failure modes of inference: result luck as noise on the observed outcome conditional on the parameter; circumstantial luck as selection into the observed sample and the missing counterfactuals that selection creates; constitutive luck as the prior or the process that produced the parameter itself; and causal luck as the absence of any unconfounded source of variation in the parameter. Flag every modeling choice and assumption the translation commits to, and do not smuggle in statistical assumptions as if they were morally neutral.
