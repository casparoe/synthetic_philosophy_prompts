I direct the historical-reconciliation unit of a national ministry. Roughly thirty times a year, a group — an Indigenous nation, a diaspora organization, the descendants of an interned or displaced population — petitions the government for a formal state apology for a historical wrong. Some of the wrongs were committed by predecessor regimes or colonial administrations, some by the current state under laws since repealed, and some by private actors whose conduct the state is now being asked to own. Each decision is public, effectively irreversible, and treated by later petitioners as precedent. My staff are two lawyers and a historian; none of us is a trained philosopher. Our minister will sign an apology only if the file has been run through a written decision procedure that we can defend afterwards. We have about three weeks per file.

I would like a reusable decision procedure for screening and deciding these petitions. It should be a fixed sequence of questions with pass/fail gates, ordered so that two analysts working the same file would ask the same questions and usually land in the same gate. For each gate, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, what kind of evidence would satisfy it, and whether failure means refusal, or a route to some weaker response such as a statement of regret, a commemoration, or educational material rather than a full apology.

Ground the gates in the actual structure of the problem rather than bureaucratic instinct: when a present government is the same agent as an earlier government for purposes of apology; whether a collective can persist across regime change well enough to inherit either liability or standing; whether contrition is coherent when no individual decision-maker is alive or culpable; what an apology has to do beyond expressing sorrow if it is to count as an apology rather than sentiment; whether apology without repair can ever be more than cheap talk; and whether anyone can apologize to people who no longer exist while claiming to act on their behalf. Tell me which of these distinctions survive in the procedure, which you are deliberately dropping, and why.

Then run the procedure on three cases. The first: a nineteenth-century colonial administration removed an Indigenous people from its territory under land laws the present state retained until the 1950s; the petitioners demand an apology and the return of specific lands that are now in public ownership. The second: a religious minority suffered legal discrimination for a century; the laws were repealed forty years ago, the original victims are dead, and the petitioning organization represents their grandchildren, who request an apology but expressly decline financial compensation as insulting. The third: the state interned a group during a twentieth-century conflict under emergency powers; an apology would be inexpensive and arguably accurate, but it would materially strengthen the group's pending compensation claim in regional courts.

Add at least one further case of your own choosing on which a defensible run of the procedure yields an answer the minister will not like, and explain what feature of the procedure produces that answer. Then tell me what the procedure cannot do: which decisions remain judgment calls that no gate can eliminate, where the procedure is quietly enacting a normative theory, and how a sophisticated petitioner could learn to trigger the right gates — the Goodharting problem. I want a legible machine that is occasionally wrong in a predictable way, not a vague standard that lets the minister make the same decision by feel and then reach for a rationale.
