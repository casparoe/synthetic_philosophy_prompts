I am writing a chapter for a lay audience about whether quantum indistinguishability deserves its philosophical reputation, and the opening analogy I drafted now worries me more than it reassures me. The draft asks readers to picture two perfectly similar marbles, or two freshly minted dollar bills, and to notice that swapping them makes no observable difference. From there it moves quickly: quantum particles of the same kind are just like that, only more so — there is no hidden fact about which electron is which, any more than there is a hidden fact about which bill is which once both sit in the same wallet. The chapter uses this comparison to argue that the identity and individuality of quantum particles is a deflated, almost boring topic, and that philosophers who treat it as deep are chasing a mirage.

Before I commit to the section, I would like the analogy itself audited, because I can no longer tell whether it is explaining my conclusion or manufacturing it. Lay out exactly what corresponds to what: what plays the role of the marbles or bills, what plays the role of the swapping operation, and what the claim that the swap changes nothing amounts to in each case. Then mark the places where the comparison breaks down, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the deflationary conclusion from the merely superficial ones.

I especially want to know whether everyday look-alike objects presuppose distinct individuals whose identities are fixed by their separate positions or histories and are merely lost track of by the observer, while the quantum case denies that there is any such underlying distinctness to lose track of; whether the fermionic minus sign under exchange, the Pauli exclusion principle, and the difference between Bose-Einstein and Fermi-Dirac counting count as further disanalogies or as downstream consequences that leave the philosophical point untouched; and whether any everyday illustration can be repaired so as not to smuggle in classical individuality, or whether the whole project of illustrating this with classical look-alikes is bound to mislead. If the disanalogies do matter, say whether the deflationary conclusion can still be earned by other means, or whether it has been riding on the analogy the whole time.
