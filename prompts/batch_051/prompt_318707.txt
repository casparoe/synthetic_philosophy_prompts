High-trust Nordic societies such as Denmark and Norway sustain broad welfare states, low corruption, and comparatively high confidence in public institutions, while the United States combines lower expressed trust in government with a political culture organized around suspicion of concentrated authority. Treat this contrast as a live research problem rather than a settled vindication of either stance. Imagine a foundation is funding one five-year program, spanning empirical and normative work, on what cross-national differences in trust in government imply for institutional design. Within that program, which open question is most worth settling first? To anchor the ranking, evaluate at least these candidates: whether institutional trust is mostly a symptom of institutional performance rather than an independent cause of desirable outcomes; whether generalized interpersonal trust and vertical trust in institutions share a common underlying mechanism; whether changes in trust are best modeled as rational updating, as moralized judgments of legitimacy, or as culturally transmitted stances; and when citizens in low-trust polities should treat their suspicion as an epistemic asset rather than a bias to be corrected. Give a defended ordering, not a survey: rank by how much downstream design choices depend on each question, assess which are tractable with current comparative and experimental methods, identify which are neglected relative to their importance, and state what evidence or argument would most change your ranking.
