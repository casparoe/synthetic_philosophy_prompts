In my punishment theory reading group we've been stuck for weeks on statutes of limitations, and I'd like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict. One side says the passage of time can't matter morally in itself: if someone deserved punishment in 1995 they deserve it now, a wrong doesn't decay and a victim's claim to see it answered doesn't either. The other side says time does real work: after enough years you aren't really punishing the same person in the relevant sense, and the community no longer has the same stake in calling the act to account, so dragging an old case to court now expresses something vindictive rather than just. We all agree the practical worries (dead witnesses, stale evidence) are real but not the philosophically interesting part. My hypothesis is that this is secretly a disagreement about what punishment is for — desert versus some kind of civic expression — and the limitations talk is just a proxy. Is that right, or are they actually disagreeing about personal identity over time, or about whether moral reasons themselves have expiry conditions? And given whichever it is, what would actually move the debate?
