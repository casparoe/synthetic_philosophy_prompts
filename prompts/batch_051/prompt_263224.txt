You are a scholar of political philosophy who specializes in Hegel's Philosophy of Right, writing for advanced students who know the broad contours of his system but not the fine detail of his treatment of war. Think through the question carefully before you write, and reason from Hegel's actual commitments rather than from a generic view that war is simply evil or that sovereignty is simply absolute.

In the Philosophy of Right (1821), Hegel treats war as an integral moment in the ethical life of nations rather than as a mere collapse of order. Because sovereign states answer to no common earthly judge, their disputes are ultimately settled by war, and the claims of peoples and their governing principles are finally passed upon in the court of world history. War also performs, in his account, a positive ethical function inside the state: it breaks the pull of private property, commerce, and material comfort; it makes present to citizens the fact that the political whole is their highest end; and it protects a people against the stagnation and inner corruption that he thinks prolonged or perpetual peace would bring. He correspondingly regards modern warfare as more humane than ancient warfare in one specific respect—it is waged between states as such rather than against private persons, families, and the ordinary life of civil society. And for Hegel the state is not a mere instrument for securing individual rights; it is the concrete embodiment of ethical life, an end to which individuals are subordinated in their highest duty, and it is only through membership in it that individual freedom first becomes fully real.

Hegel never encountered, however, the contemporary practice known as humanitarian intervention: the use of military force by outside powers inside another sovereign state, ostensibly to halt genocide, mass atrocity, or ethnic cleansing, and justified in the name of universal human rights rather than self-defense or plain national interest. In its most influential recent form, the Responsibility to Protect doctrine, sovereignty is treated as conditional: a state that fails to protect its own population from mass atrocity is said to forfeit, at least in part, the protection that sovereignty normally confers.

Predict Hegel's view of this development. Do not merely restate his historical position; project it forward and argue for what a consistent Hegelian would say. Before drafting, mentally canvass at least five distinct commitments in his philosophy—concerning sovereignty, international right, the ethical function of war, the humanity of modern warfare, and the tribunal of world history—and let them jointly constrain your answer. Then write one sustained argument that:

- identifies which of his commitments carry the most weight in assessing a war fought to enforce universal human rights;
- derives from them a specific verdict about whether he would accept humanitarian intervention as a legitimate form of war, reject it as an incoherent or hypocritical moralization of power, or assimilate it to some further category of his own;
- marks the points where a projection from his system is genuinely underdetermined, and says so honestly;
- names at least one implication of his position that would trouble contemporary advocates of humanitarian intervention, and at least one feature of contemporary intervention that would sit uneasily with the kind of justification he himself gives for war;
- concludes with the clearest possible statement of what, on his behalf, humanitarian intervention gets right or wrong at the level of principle.

Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not spend time on biography or on the general reception history of his political thought. Answer only the assigned question.
