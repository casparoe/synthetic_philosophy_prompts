I am a novelist. My current draft concerns a self-made merchant who tries to improve his character by keeping a daily moral ledger, on the model Benjamin Franklin describes near the end of his Autobiography. Franklin's device was a technology of attention: a ruled page for each of thirteen virtues, a column for each day of the week, a concentration on one virtue at a time in weekly courses, and small black marks entered against each fault. He later reports that he never reached the perfection he had planned, that some virtues proved intractable to the method, and that he nevertheless believed the attempt had made him better than he would otherwise have been. The project assumes that the self can be revised in small, scheduled increments, and that a fault is above all something to be noticed, dated, and eventually eliminated.

For research, I have been rereading Stevenson's Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde as a dark control case. Jekyll wants the standing of a man of unblemished character without the slow labor of attaining it; his solution is to separate, chemically, the part of him that sins from the part of him that keeps up appearances. The novel is usually read as a fable of Victorian repression, of substance dependence, or of the disreputable underside of respectability. I want to read it instead through Franklin's project of moral perfection: as a novel about what happens when moral self-cultivation is replaced by a single technological shortcut, and about the relation between self-observation and moral decline.

Write the critical essay I should have in front of me. Assume I know both texts well; I am asking for a reading, not a synopsis and not page references. The reading should be faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the novel to an illustration of Franklin, and it should not distort Franklin's project to fit the novel. Among the questions I want addressed:

- Does the novel dramatize Franklin's conviction that virtue must be acquired as a discipline of attention, or does it treat that discipline itself as another form of self-splitting?
- Franklin's method treats the virtues as separable and individually trainable, one per week. Stevenson's Hyde is not an isolated vice but a coherent second self, drawn at once in terms of will, appetite, and anger. Does the novel count as evidence that some vicious traits are structural and resist piecemeal reformation?
- Jekyll's experiment drifts away from first-person account-keeping; the narrative reaches us through other people's documents until the closing statement, and that statement is a confession rather than a running ledger. What is the novel saying about the function of daily self-examination that Franklin takes for granted?
- Both texts put pride and performance under pressure: Franklin added Humility to his list only after a friend reported that he was thought proud, while Jekyll's undertaking is driven by the wish to be seen as exactly what he is not. Is Jekyll a failed Franklinian, or is he practicing a corrupt parody of the ledger in which reputation is the only virtue being tracked?
- Where does the novel resist this reading, and what does Stevenson's version add to the philosophical account of moral self-cultivation that Franklin's own memoir does not?

Write in a formal register, as a critic rather than a judge of the characters. If you conclude that part of the apparent disagreement between the two texts is only a difference in vocabulary or period manners, say so explicitly.
