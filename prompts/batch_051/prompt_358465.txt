I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am preparing a feature on a philosophical dispute that has dragged on so long that the two parties mostly argue past one another. The dispute concerns the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles — in its strong form, the principle that no two distinct things can share all their properties — and what to make of Max Black's thought experiment: a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres a fixed distance apart.

One party, whom I will call the Leibnizian, holds that Black's description is either subtly contradictory or incomplete. If the spheres are genuinely two, she says, some property, even if only a relational or positional one, must distinguish them; if the description leaves nothing that distinguishes them, then it has not described two things at all. The other party, the Blackian, holds that the universe is a genuine possibility and that the two spheres are numerically distinct while agreeing in every qualitative respect. On his view, distinctness can be brute, or can rest on a primitive thisness (haecceity) that is not itself a qualitative property, and the strong form of the principle is therefore false as a piece of modal metaphysics.

After a year of adversarial collaboration, the two have asked me to help produce the joint statement they could both sign, and I want you to draft it. It should be rigorous enough that neither side would see its position as a caricature, yet readable by the non-specialist audience of a science-and-ideas section. Do not include footnotes or a bibliography; the work is entirely in the precision of the formulations.

The document should contain three parts. First, a numbered section of claims both parties can sign. These should be non-vacuous and should cover: what Black's scenario does and does not stipulate; the distinction between qualitative identity and numerical identity; the difference between strong, weak, and trivial formulations of the principle, including which formulation is the one actually in dispute; and the agreed entailment that if the strong formulation is a necessary truth, then Black's universe is impossible.

Second, a disagreements table, with columns for the sub-question, the Leibnizian's position in wording she would herself endorse, the Blackian's position in wording he would himself endorse, and a verdict on whether that row marks a substantive disagreement or a merely verbal one.

Third, a cruxes section. For each sub-question that, if settled, would force at least one side to revise, give the concrete case, argument, or modification of the spheres that both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. In particular, address these pressure points if the collaboration reached them: whether spatial position and distance are qualitative properties or already presuppose distinct locations; whether the stipulated distance relation — which the spheres bear to each other but not to themselves — makes them weakly discernible, and whether weak discernibility preserves the point of the principle or trivializes it; and whether the strongest support for brute distinctness comes from Black's world itself or from elsewhere, such as the behavior of indistinguishable particles under permutation in quantum theory.
