For a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind, design a sorting quiz titled “Which position on multiple realizability do you actually hold?” The overarching text is Hilary Putnam’s 1967 paper *The Nature of Mental States*. Putnam’s argument there, roughly, is that pain should not be identified with a particular physical-chemical state, because a human, an octopus, and a Martian with a wholly different physiology could all be in the same pain state. The state should instead be identified with its causal-functional organization: what it typically does, what typically causes it, and how it interacts with other mental states. The quiz should take respondents through concrete cases and score them onto positions in the multiple realizability and functionalism literature.

Requirements:

- Eight to ten questions. Each question should present a concrete case or a precise sub-claim and offer two or three response options.
- The questions must not ask respondents which label they like or which philosopher they agree with. They should sort people by verdicts on cases, not by self-description.
- At least these five cases must be used: (1) a Martian with a hydraulic physiology but input-output and behavioral profile indistinguishable from human pain; (2) a human whose C-fibers are gradually replaced, one at a time, by silicon chips that preserve all relevant causal transitions, with no change in behavior; (3) an octopus with a different neural architecture that enters a state with the same coarse pain role; (4) a creature whose pain state fills the same coarse role but has a different fine-grained neural mechanism, with no common neural signature across species; (5) a human and a dog, where both satisfy the platitudes about pain but their internal states have only partially overlapping causal profiles.
- The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least these positions: analytic functionalism, psychofunctionalism, role functionalism, realizer functionalism, mechanistic role functionalism (or the view that multiple realizability holds only at coarse grains), type identity or carbon chauvinism, and some form of eliminativism about unified pain states. You may add one or two more if the answer patterns require them.
- For each leaf, give one sentence stating the position and one sentence naming the answer pattern that leads to it.
- Flag any pair of positions the quiz cannot separate, and explain in one sentence why the questions underdetermine that distinction.
- Note any logically possible answer combinations that no current position occupies. For each empty cell, briefly diagnose why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
- Assume respondents have some background in philosophy of mind, but do not assume they know the names of the positions before taking the quiz.
- The quiz should be useful pedagogically: after sorting, someone should be able to see which of Putnam’s commitments they accepted and which they resisted.

Deliver the quiz in three parts: the questions, the scoring key, and a short commentary on the empty cells and inseparable pairs. Do not include reading suggestions.
