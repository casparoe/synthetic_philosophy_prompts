I serve on a scientific advisory committee for a national wildlife agency. The statute we administer grants legal protection to threatened species but does not define the term species. We have been asked to decide whether a particular wolf population should be listed as a distinct species. The genetic data show that the population has been separated from neighboring wolves for several thousand years and forms a separate evolutionary lineage. However, in a narrow contact zone the two populations interbreed, and the resulting hybrids are fertile. One committee member argues that this ongoing fertile interbreeding settles the matter: they are one species. A second argues that the long independent evolutionary history settles it: they are two species. A third argues that neither the breeding data nor the lineage data really settles anything, because the only reason for our classification is legal protection; since the population is ecologically distinct and under threat, the statute's purpose should determine what counts as a species here. I have been asked to draft the committee's recommendation. I want a principled way to think this through rather than a diplomatic compromise. Is there a fact of the matter about whether this population is a species? If there is not, what should the definition track in this legal and conservation context, and how should the committee justify choosing one criterion over the others?
