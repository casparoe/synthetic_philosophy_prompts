The same legal system that makes it a felony to buy a kidney also criminalizes buying a vote. Many of the same states that prosecute a hardware store for tripling the price of generators after a hurricane allow companies to pay donors for blood plasma and allow employers to pay large premiums for dangerous work, and the same system gives makers of life-saving drugs broad latitude to set prices patients call extortionate. The law nowhere states the principle that organizes these boundaries, and it is not obvious that any single principle could. What theory of commodification does this body of practice presuppose—a view of which goods may be priced, which exchanges corrupt or degrade what they price, and why? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with and from what it leaves philosophically underdetermined. Then say what would have to change in the practice if the implicit theory were replaced by a straightforward welfarist rule: permit any voluntary exchange whose parties expect to benefit and that imposes no clear cost on third parties.
