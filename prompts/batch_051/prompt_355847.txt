I'm teaching an upper-year undergraduate seminar on conceptual change in the life sciences, and next week's session is meant to be about what happens when a classificatory concept that everyone uses begins to dissolve under pressure. I plan to build the whole session around one passage from the second chapter of Darwin's *On the Origin of Species*, where Darwin discusses how naturalists actually draw the line between species and varieties. Here is the passage I want to use:

In the section on doubtful species, Darwin writes that forms that are so closely linked by intermediate gradations that naturalists do not like to rank them as distinct species are "in several respects the most important for us." He then says that practically, when a naturalist can unite two forms together by others having intermediate characters, he treats the one as a variety of the other; and he notes that years later, though a few of these doubtful forms "were ranked by one author as distinct species, and by another as varieties," the disagreement itself depends on the facts about intermediate links. Then he writes:

Hence, in determining whether a form should be ranked as a species or a variety, the opinion of naturalists having sound judgment and wide experience seems the only guide to follow. We must, however, in many cases, decide by a majority of naturalists, for few well-marked and well-known varieties can be named which have not been ranked as species by at least some competent judges.

I have read this passage many times and I keep finding myself drawn to a very strong reading of it, one that probably goes beyond what Darwin himself would have accepted and which I am not sure I can defend. My strong reading is this: at least for the period Darwin is describing, there was no fact of the matter about whether a given graded series of forms was one species or two, and the appeal to "sound judgment and wide experience" is functioning as a disguised political criterion, a way of letting the social authority of certain naturalists determine the taxonomic facts. Now the puzzle for me is not whether Darwin was a nominalist in some metaphysical sense, but whether this passage supports a more specific claim: that when the evidential relation between a term and its domain is exhausted by the epistemic virtues of the people applying it, those virtues no longer function as evidence at all, but as something closer to credentials. And if that is right, is there any room left for the idea that such a term can be revised rather than merely replaced, or that one classification can be more reasonable than another? I want to press this because I read Darwin as saying, half reluctantly, that in the hard cases the naturalist's judgment is not a means of detecting the species boundary, because there is no boundary there to detect, and yet the practice proceeds as if competent judgment is still tracking something. So my question is: does the passage actually license this strong reading, or does it leave open, or even quietly invite, a weaker reading where the appeal to judgment is consistent with there being a real boundary that is simply difficult to determine with confidence? And does the difference between those two readings matter for how we should think about current debates over whether species-level taxonomy is a science of discovery or a science of pragmatic delimitation? I do not teach the seminar as an advocate, but I want to know before I walk in whether I am conflating two distinct crises, one in the history of a term and one in the authority of the people who use it, and whether the passage keeps those crises distinct or deliberately fuses them. Please do not answer as if the point is to adjudicate between me and Darwin; audit my reading, say where it gets purchase and what the cheapest correction would be, and tell me what a bright student should be prompted to notice that my formulation would tempt them to flatten.
