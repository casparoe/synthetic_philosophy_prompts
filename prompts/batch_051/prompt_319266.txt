I am a PhD student working between the philosophy of language and the epistemology of religious experience, and I keep running into a structural problem: the literature on ineffability is scattered across negative theology, Buddhist and Vedantic sources, and analytic treatments of the limits of language, and each subliterature treats its own cases as the center of the phenomenon. Before I go further with any particular account, I want the target specified. Please write out the desiderata that an adequate theory of ineffability must satisfy, where by a theory of ineffability I mean an account of what it is for something to resist or defeat articulation, and of how something can be in some sense beyond language while still being known, experienced, indicated, or taught. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether you take it to be non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp. I want the list itself, not a survey of who holds which view.

I would like the list to address at least the following pressure points, though the essay should not be a mechanical walk through them. First, the self-undermining problem: saying that X is ineffable appears to characterize X, and denying that the ineffable has any properties appears itself to ascribe a property to it, so a theory must say whether this is only apparent, and by what mechanism (levels, speech-act theory, a ladder of assertions discarded after use). Second, the distinction between the ineffable and the merely not-yet-expressed, the forgotten, the practically or socially unsayable, and the straightforwardly meaningless; a theory must not let the category collapse into any of these. Third, the question whether ineffability limits only propositional description while leaving room for something like knowledge by acquaintance, non-conceptual awareness, or demonstrative indication, and whether that knowledge is transmissible through teaching and practice. Fourth, cases in which an apparent barrier to saying may be an artifact of a particular grammar rather than a limit on thought as such. Fifth, the broad apophatic repertoire across traditions — the via negativa's language of divine darkness and negation by removal, the contemplative directive toward an unknowing beyond concepts, Zen koan practice, and Madhyamaka reasoning, which reduces every positive view to absurdity and then applies the same treatment to its own negation — where negation, paradox, and silence function less as description than as technique or therapy. A theory should also take some stand on whether the ineffable is one kind of thing across these cases or several related ones.

Finally, please flag any pairs or subsets of the desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say what each conflict would amount to: for instance, whether the demand that the ineffable admit no description and the demand that it nevertheless be knowable in some non-propositional way are genuinely compatible or only evade contradiction by operating at different levels. And identify which single requirement does the most work in excluding the popular deflationary positions — that talk of the ineffable is simply confusion, or disguised poetry, or a device for marking the boundary of a particular grammar — and briefly say why that requirement excludes them without begging the question against the apophatic traditions from the outset.
