I have been trying to get clear on the regulative principle of worship, because a friend from a confessional Presbyterian background defends some practices I find strange and I want to know whether his principle is stable. He states it as: in the assembled public worship of God, whatever is not commanded is forbidden. From this he derives the familiar conclusions — no musical instruments, no uninspired hymns, no Christmas or Easter services — and he regards those as settled rather than as features to be reevaluated. The trouble is that the principle seems to run past where he wants it to stop.

(1) In the assembled public worship of God, whatever is not commanded is forbidden.
(2) Instruments, uninspired hymns, and holy days are not commanded; therefore they are forbidden.
(3) A building, a fixed hour of meeting, pews or chairs, electric lights, heating, printed psalters, and microphones are also not commanded.
(4) Therefore, by the same principle, all of those are forbidden as well.
(5) But my friend's congregation uses all of them; on his own statement, its ordinary Sunday service is largely unlawful.
(6) The standard reply is that those are only circumstances of worship, not elements, and circumstances may be ordered by wisdom and good sense rather than by explicit command.
(7) But once the principle is quietly narrowed to uncommanded elements are forbidden, everything turns on the word element. Nothing in the principle tells us why a piano or an uninspired hymn is an element while a printed psalter, a metrical tune, or seating is a circumstance. The classification looks like it recapitulates the very line between the commanded and the uncommanded that the principle was supposed to draw.

So either the principle is strong enough to forbid heating and chairs, which no actual congregation accepts, or it is weak enough to be uninformative until someone supplies a non-question-begging account of what counts as an element of worship. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like to retain some version of the principle if possible, but I do not see how to defend the element/circumstance line without helping myself to a conclusion.
