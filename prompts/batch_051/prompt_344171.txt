In my political philosophy course on religion and public life, we have been studying the Rawlsian argument that religious reasons should be kept out of public political justification. As I understand it, the argument runs roughly as follows. In a free society, reasonable people will inevitably come to hold different comprehensive doctrines, including different religions. Because the exercise of coercive political power must be justifiable to all reasonable citizens, citizens debating constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice should not appeal to reasons drawn from their particular comprehensive doctrines, which others reasonably reject; they should instead appeal to shared political values, common sense, and noncontroversial scientific conclusions. A crucial empirical assumption seems to be that a substantial stock of such shared reasons exists and can carry real argumentative weight.

I have been trying to assess whether this argument survives the epistemic fragmentation produced by algorithmically curated media and personalized information environments. If citizens now inhabit different factual worlds, disagree not only about the good but about plain matters of fact, and treat even noncontroversial science as contested whenever it is politically inconvenient, then the shared public basis the argument presupposes may be much thinner than Rawls's account presupposes. Does the argument still go through in such a setting? Specifically, which premise does this development bear on, what does the argument need to be true there, and is the argument dead, merely in need of repair, or ultimately unaffected? If it can be repaired, say how; if the development instead does not undermine it, explain why the apparent loss of common ground does not matter.
