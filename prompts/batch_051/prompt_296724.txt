I have been thinking through a problem in early Daoist thought, and I would like help working out whether a certain modern finding changes it.

The problem concerns wu-wei, the ideal of acting without strain: the trained swimmer who does not decide to move and then move but simply moves with the water, the craftsperson whose hands know what to do without supervision. The puzzle is that aiming at this state seems to defeat itself. If I deliberately set out to become effortless, my performance is watched, corrected, and willed, which is precisely the opposite of wu-wei; the harder I try not to try, the more I am trying. The old texts seem to answer by describing practices of emptying and unlearning, but that only moves the difficulty back a step, because the practice is itself a project one must try to carry out.

Now bring in something from current psychology of action. Work on implementation intentions suggests that a person can spend effort in advance by deciding, for example, that if a certain cue occurs then they will do a certain thing; afterward the cue itself tends to trigger the action, without a fresh act of will at the moment of performance. Habit research and skill training point the same way: people deliberately drill a movement so that later it runs by itself, and performers are often told to stop monitoring what their hands are doing. In short, effortful intention can apparently be confined to the setup stage and then handed off to the situation.

I want to know whether the Daoist paradox survives this. Specifically: which premise in the argument that trying to be effortless is self-defeating is this finding meant to undercut, and does it genuinely undercut it? Does the paradox merely reappear at the level of choosing what to automate, or in the attempt to stop monitoring at the moment of performance? Tell me whether the argument is dead, needs repair, or goes through, and say what would have to be true for your answer to be wrong.
