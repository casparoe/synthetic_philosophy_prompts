I've been trying to sort out what I actually believe about how pain and pleasure relate, and for a little reading group I had to write down my views so the others can poke at them. I'd rather have a philosopher stress-test the set before I show up. You're a professional philosopher who works on value theory and formal ethics; please think through this carefully, maybe sketch what each pair of comitments implies before you commit to a verdict. I want an audit, not a summary.

Here's the set of things I'm inclined to believe:

1. Single scale. Pain and pleasure live on one underlying scale of how good an episode is, with a genuine zero in the middle, and every episode has a finite position on that scale. For any two episodes I can in principle say which is better, which is worse, or that they're exactly level, and I can add up the values of different episodes to get an overall total.

2. Additivity across lives. Benefits and harms aggregate the obvious way: if +1 is worth having in one life, then +1 in each of a billion lives is worth a billion times as much in total. Small goods don't mysteriously stop counting just because there are a lot of them.

3. Lexical threshold. Above a certain level of pain — agony, torture, something around there — no finite amount of mild pleasure can outweigh one such pain. Not even if the mild pleasure is spread across billions of lives. A world containing one torture is worse than an otherwise similar world containing any finite pile of pleasant afternoons instead.

4. Below-threshold tradeoffs. Below that threshold, I'm happy to trade pain against pleasure in the ordinary way; a mild headache can be outweighed by enough mild pleasures. The threshold doesn't make me squeamish about the small stuff.

5. Asymmetry without a second scale. I'm basically a negative utilitarian: reducing pain matters more than creating an equal amount of pleasure. But I don't think this means there are two incommensurable scales. It just means the single scale from (1) is steeper below zero than above it.

6. The threshold is structural. The threshold in (3) isn't just a pragmatic stopping rule for when the calculations get uncomfortable. It marks a real structural boundary in how good and bad states of affairs compare.

What I'd like from you: go through these and tell me whether the set hangs together. For each pair that's in genuine tension, say how severe the tension is and what the cheapest fix would be — which commitment changes, in what direction, and what that costs me. Also tell me which pairs only look tense but aren't, and why. Then, if you had to make a single revision to make the whole set coherent, what would it be and what would I have to give up?
