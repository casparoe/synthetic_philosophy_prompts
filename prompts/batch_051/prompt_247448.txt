I sit on the allocations committee of a family foundation that gives about $40m a year to global health and poverty work in a poor, high-fertility region. We've narrowed our ten-year strategy to three packages, and I need you to draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before next week's vote. It needs to recommend one package and give an honest statement of the strongest case against it.

Package A mostly improves the welfare of people who already exist: cash transfers, vaccines, primary schooling, agricultural extension. It probably leaves the number of births about the same.

Package B is family planning plus girls' education, designed to reduce birth rates a lot. The people who are born are much better off, but many fewer of them exist over the next century.

Package C is a child-survival program—oral rehydration, bednets, neonatal care—with no family planning component. In the region as it currently is, it will mean that a lot more children survive to adulthood and go on to have families, but under current economic conditions their lives are predicted to be positive but pretty marginal: worth living, but not much more.

Our finance people keep scoring these by 'cost per life saved or improved' and 'total DALYs averted,' which I suspect quietly assumes total utilitarianism. The repugnant conclusion is the usual way to bring that assumption to the surface: if only the total sum matters, then for any population of people with excellent lives, there's some vastly larger population of people whose lives are barely worth living that we'd have to call better, just because the total is bigger. I want the committee to decide whether we are willing to accept that implication before we vote, because if we aren't, our scoring criteria are wrong.

So the memo should do four things. First, explain in plain language what total utilitarianism says about A, B, and C. Second, lay out the alternatives: average utilitarianism, and some kind of person-affecting view (where making an already-existing life better is fundamentally different from bringing a new life into existence, and there is no obligation to create a happy person just because the total sum would rise). Third, recommend one package, with the strongest argument for the losing side. Fourth, flag where even perfect empirical numbers could not settle the question, because the disagreement is about what counts in the first place. Make it a document we can actually vote on, not a seminar paper.

And please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on population ethics. It's fine to say total utilitarianism is a live view; it's not fine to write as if the repugnant conclusion obviously refutes it and only an idiot would disagree.
