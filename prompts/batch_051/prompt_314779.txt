After our last sprint retrospective, two engineers on my team spent an hour arguing about a single sentence the tech lead had said. He had told a junior engineer, "I apologize for overriding your design without explaining my reasoning."

Marta's position was that the sentence was not a report about how the lead felt. It was the apology itself: an act performed with words, the way a raised hand performs a vote. She said she thought such "performative" utterances do something rather than describe something. Dev replied that if the words were the act, insincerity would be impossible. But plainly someone can say "I apologize" while feeling no regret, and in that case, he claimed, no apology actually happened. So he argued the words must function as a report of an inner state, and the utterance counts as an apology only if that report is true.

Marta responded that this did not follow. A handshake can be insincere and a compliment can be backhanded; the act still occurs and is merely defective. Likewise, the lead's utterance was an apology whether or not he meant it — a hollow apology if he did not, but not a false statement.

Dev countered that she had not escaped the problem. The only difference between a sincere apology and a hollow one, he said, is whether the sentence accurately describes the speaker's present attitude, which is exactly the difference between a true report and a false one.

Marta then brought up "I promise." An insincere promise is still a promise, she said; it still creates a commitment and can bind the speaker within the relevant practice. Promising does not report a pre-existing intention; it creates a commitment, so the same could hold for apologizing.

Dev replied that the analogy runs in the wrong direction. Promises point forward and create obligations, which is why they need not report a prior inner state. Apologies point backward: they succeed only if a wrong has already been committed and the speaker already regrets it. "I apologize" registers a past transaction and a present attitude; it does not create them the way "I promise" creates a future commitment.

They eventually asked me to referee, and I found I could not keep track of which claims had actually been answered. Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: say which specific claims each of them gets right or wrong, where each argument goes wrong or leans on an ambiguity, and what both are missing in their account of how a sentence like "I apologize" relates to the inner state that an insincere speaker lacks.
