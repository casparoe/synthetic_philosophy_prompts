I run a first-year undergraduate philosophy seminar of 18 students, with an 80-minute session available. I want a runnable classroom activity in which the students encounter, through what they do rather than through any preceding explanation, the pressure to separate what matters in survival from strict numerical identity. The material is Parfit's teletransporter and the nearby cases that come with it: a person is scanned, destroyed, and reconstructed elsewhere; or a brain is replaced part by part; or a procedure branches into two psychological descendants; or hemispheres are divided between bodies. I do not want Parfit, the teletransporter, or the phrase "personal identity" named until after the activity.

Design an activity built around small-group decisions with small stakes — points, tokens, or precedence among groups — so that the groups work through a sequence of cases, each close to the previous one, and discover only near the end where their own rulings have led. Good cases would be: Should the procedure be licensed? Should a contract or marriage survive the procedure? Should an insurer pay a claim on the person who wakes up after the transfer? The surface question should always be practical, never philosophical. Ideally the groups themselves generate each next case by modifying one feature of the previous case; if that is not workable, give them a sequence whose changes are small enough to reveal their decisions shifting over a threshold. The stakes should be tied to the internal consistency of their rulings and to whether they can state, at the end, a single rule that covers everything they ruled.

The outcome I want is not consensus but self-recognition: each student should notice that their own rulings came to treat survival as a matter of degree while their official language still insisted on all-or-nothing survival.

The debrief that follows should map the moments of reversal, embarrassment, or inconsistency in the game onto the distinction between numerical identity and psychological continuity, and onto connectedness versus continuity if the activity naturally reaches it. It should predict what often goes wrong when the exercise is run — for instance, groups treating the first case as obvious and only later realizing their rule has committed them, or groups refusing to take branching seriously — and say how the facilitator should respond without pre-empting the discovery. It should also say plainly which part of the idea the activity cannot produce on its own and must be supplied by the facilitator, such as the point that identity is normally taken to be one-to-one and all-or-nothing.

If this assignment rests on a confused picture of what the teletransportation cases are supposed to show, or if an 80-minute activity cannot honestly carry the distinction between survival and identity without smuggling in the conclusion, say so directly and tell me what a better version of the session would look like.
