Over the last few months I’ve been trying to get clear on what the rationalist-sphere actually inherited from Herbert Simon, and I keep running into a muddle I’d love a careful take on. On the one hand there’s the usual bounded rationality idea: real agents don’t have the cognitive capacity to maximize over all options, so in practice they run a search process, evaluate options one by one, and stop once they hit an option that clears some aspiration threshold — that’s satisficing. On the other hand there’s the move economists and some AI folks make, where you keep the optimization framework but fold the costs of search, information, or attention into the objective function, so the agent is still maximizing expected utility, just over an expanded choice set that includes computational actions. I used to think these were basically the same idea with different marketing. But now I think they’re not: satisficing seems to treat the stopping rule as psychologically real but not itself chosen by a higher-order optimization, while the bounded-optimizer story treats every rule, including stopping rules, as the solution to a larger maximization problem.

I’ve been arguing about this with a friend who does decision theory, and he keeps saying the bounded-optimizer story is trivially true if you pick the right costs, but that feels like it empties out the original insight. Is the distinction between satisficing and bounded optimization as sharp as I think, or is it mostly a matter of description? Which one actually do more explanatory work when you push on real cases like default effects, reliance on lexicographic heuristics, or the general phenomenology of settling for good enough? I’m less interested in who has the better label and more in which picture helps you make better predictions or build more honest models of what agents are up to.
