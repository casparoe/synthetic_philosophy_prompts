I've been meeting with a few friends to talk about language stuff—nothing academic, just people who get curious about why we talk the way we do. Last week we got stuck on how a word like 'pitch' can mean the sticky black stuff, a sports field, a throw, the highness or lowness of a sound, and the slope of a roof, and somehow nobody trips over it in normal conversation. One person in the group kept cutting the discussion short by saying 'context disambiguates everything.' It sounded profound and everyone moved on, but I think that sentence is doing a lot of lazy work. Sometimes by 'context' she meant the surrounding words; sometimes tone of voice or who was speaking; sometimes she seemed to mean the listener just makes a good-enough guess and we don't notice the misses. And I have a separate hunch she didn't know what to do with: maybe the interesting thing isn't just how we resolve ambiguity, but why languages let so much of it pile up in the first place, like its a resource and not just a mess.

I want to pin that one-liner down. What are the different precise claims 'context disambiguates everything' could be making, from boringly true to radical to probably false? For each, what exactly is 'context' and what exactly is the disambiguating supposed to be? Which readings actually explain how we live with homonyms like 'pitch' or 'bank', and which are just hand-waving dressed up as insight? And is there a reading that's about ambiguity being useful or tolerated, instead of just about it being fixed? I'd like it laid out like a menu I can bring to the group, with verdicts, so I can finally say which version she was probably using and whether it does the work she wanted it to do.
