I've been pushing a line in my decision theory seminar that I've been calling 'stakes realism.' The core idea is that the mugging setup cheats by letting the mugger name a payoff in a currency nobody has — utility numbers bigger than any state a life could actually contain. My proposal is that expected utility calculations are only well formed when every utility in them is anchored to outcomes you could in principle discriminate between, the way a just-noticeable difference anchors a psychophysical scale. Once a threat or promise goes off the top of that scale, it should be treated as undefined rather than as a very large number, and then the mugging can't get started. I also use the same move against infinite payoffs — infinity isn't a point on the scale. I know this smells like bounded utility, but I want a candid read on wether its actually distinct and worth the name. So: is it coherent, what does it get right, is it just bounded utility with extra steps, and where does it break? Think especially about a small chance of saving an enormous number of lives, or Pascal-style reasoning about existential risk.
