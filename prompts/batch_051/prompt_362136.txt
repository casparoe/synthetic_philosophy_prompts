I'm taking a grad seminar on population ethics and we've been stuck on Parfit's repugnant conclusion all week. The thing is, I keep thinking it's secretly a poem: start with a world of several billion genuinely flourishing lives, and the view says a vastly bigger world where every single life is only barely worth living would still be better, other things equal. No matter which escape hatch you try, the conclustion walks back in.

Could you write it as a villanelle for me? I'd like the two recurring lines to be the judgment that keeps coming back—maybe one line invoking the flourishing world and the other the barely-worth-living crowd—and each of the five tercets should stage one specific attempted escape: average utilitarianism, a lexical threshold below which lives don't count, a person-affecting view that says adding lives isn't the same as benefiting people, something like that. The ABA rhymes should make each failed objection feel like a stanza snapping shut, and the closing quatrain should land on the actual horror: not that the conclusion is ugly, but that every premise along the way looked almost impossible to deny. It doesn't have to be a flawless villanelle on a first pass, but it should be accurate enough that someone in my seminar would recognize the moves rather than wincing.
