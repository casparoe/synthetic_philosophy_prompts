Two people in my study group got into it about whether a mutable data structure is still "the same thing" after you change its state, and I honestly couldn't referee them. Arjun made y = [1, 2, 3], called y.append(4), and said it's obviously the same object afterward — same id(y), same slot in memory, and we'd never say a car becomes a different car because someone changed a tire. Bea said that's a dodge: at the level that matters for understanding the program, [1, 2, 3] and [1, 2, 3, 4] are different values, and a function that mutates its argument leaves the caller with a different value than it started with. Arjun said she's mixing up reference identity and value: identity is defined by the reference, not the contents, so mutation is exactly the operation that changes state while keeping the thing the same. Bea said that's the whole problem with mutation — it lets you say "same thing" by fiat while every downstream consumer reads something different, which is why reasoning about mutable state is so hard. Please adjudicate move by move: who is right about which point, where does each overreach, and what distinction would actually settle what they're fighting about?
