I teach a 75-minute seminar on phenomenology and embodied cognition to fourteen undergraduates who have not yet read Merleau-Ponty, and I intend this session to precede the reading. The topic recently stopped being merely textual for me. During rehabilitation after a wrist fracture, I found that physical-therapy instructions that specified my movement in joint angles and metric distances made the hand clumsy and self-conscious, while the therapist's instruction to pick up a cup the way I always do brought the whole gesture back before I could form any representation of it. I want my students to meet that contrast in their own bodies, and to feel, before it is named, the difference between the body as an object in neutral, measurable space and the lived body as the centre of a situation it already inhabits through its tasks and capacities, along with the reality of motor intentionality: the pre-reflective directedness of the body toward a task.

Please design a runnable 75-minute activity for that session, with a minute-by-minute timeline, simple safe materials, and no lecture before the exercise. The terms lived body, objective space, and motor intentionality should not appear until the debrief.

I want the exercise built around two contrasting experiences. First, a paired, blindfolded manipulation task: a sighted guide leads a blindfolded partner through the same simple action, for example finding and lifting a paper cup from a table and placing it at a marked spot, under two instruction regimes. Under the first, the guide may use only mechanical, third-person instructions (angles, distances, directions, joint rotations) and may not name objects or goals. Under the second, the guide may use only task- and affordance-language (what the object is, what it is for, reach for it, as if). After each condition, collect first-person reports: whether the hand felt located in a coordinate or aimed at something, where the movement flowed and where it froze, and whether there was a moment when the task suddenly became effortless. Put the two columns of reports side by side on the board before interpreting them.

Second, once the blindfolds are off, a short exercise in which an ordinary mastered movement, such as walking across the room, picking up a pen, or signing one's name, is performed first in the person's usual fluent way, and then while the person narrates or consciously directs it joint by joint, so that the group feels objectifying attention interfere with skilled action. Keep this brief and safe; the point is the felt difference, not physical strain.

The debrief should map precise observed moments onto the philosophical distinctions: the stiffness and brittleness of the mechanical condition onto objective, homogeneous space; the way a blindfolded hand aims at the cup under task-language onto lived, action-oriented space; the disruption of a familiar skill under deliberate self-direction onto motor intentionality, in which the body responds to the task without first representing its own position, and attention to the body as an object is a second-order achievement that can compete with that response. Students should leave seeing the lived body as a structurally different way of being in space: not merely feeling comfortable in one's skin, and not a rejection of objective space but an account of what objective space is built from.

Please also specify the rules needed to keep the exercise honest and safe, including how to keep the mechanical condition free of smuggled object names and how to manage blindfolded movement, and list all materials.

Finally, tell me (i) what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how to handle each failure: guides cheating by slipping in a noun, participants exaggerating clumsiness because the setup seems to ask for it, a pair treating the mechanical condition as a code to be cracked rather than an experience to be noticed, blindfolded navigation becoming unsafe, and the session overrunning. And (ii) which feature of Merleau-Ponty's position the activity cannot reproduce. My suspicion is that it cannot reproduce the clinical dissociation between abstract and concrete movement that the position draws on, and it cannot by itself establish the stronger claim that objective space is derivative from lived space rather than merely different from it; I want an honest note on what I will still need to supply in words, in the debrief or the following class.
