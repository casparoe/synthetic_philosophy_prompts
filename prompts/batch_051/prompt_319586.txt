I lurk on LessWrong and a few adjacent forums, and a mate who did a philosophy degree keeps insisting that physicalism is in much worse shape than the rationalist crowd thinks. He gave me a tidied-up version of Hempel's dilemma and I can't find the flaw, though I also can't bring myself to accept the conclusion. Take on the role of a professional philosopher of mind whose speciality is physicalism, think carefully (some brainstorming first is fine), then give me a verdict. Here's his argument:

(1) Physicalism is the thesis that everything is physical.
(2) 'Physical' has to mean either what current physics describes or what a completed, ideal physics would describe.
(3) If it means current physics, then physicalism is false: current physics is silent on conciousness, normativity, and plenty else, so those things fall outside the physical.
(4) If it means ideal physics, then physicalism is either trivial or empty. It's trivial because if ideal physics ended up positing phenomenal properties or abstract objects to explain the data, physicalism would just stretch to include them, so it couldn't be falsified. It's empty because we have no way now of saying what ideal physics will include, so 'physical' has no determinate content.
(5) So physicalism is either false, trivial, or empty, and in none of those cases is it a substantive thesis worth defending.

He says the only honest move is to drop the word 'physical' entirely and just state what you actually mean case by case. That sounds too strong to me, but I can't say exactly where the argument cheats. I have a vauge sense it's a false dilemma or a motte-and-bailey, but I can't make the objection stick. Is the argument valid? If not, which numbered step fails, and what would the strongest repaired version of that step be? If it is valid, which horn should a physicalist grab and why?
