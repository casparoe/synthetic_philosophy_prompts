A friend who has read some Marx offered me the following summary to explain what Marx means by exploitation and alienation:

"Marx's theory is that workers are exploited because they create the entire value of whatever they produce, yet they are paid only enough to survive. The difference — the profit the capitalist keeps — is unpaid labor, and that is what exploitation is: the gap between what a worker makes and what a worker is paid. Alienation is the inner, felt side of this same fact. Because the worker neither owns the product nor controls the work process, he experiences his work as meaningless and feels cut off from the product, from his own activity, and from other people. It follows that if workers were simply paid the full value of what they make, both exploitation and alienation would disappear. That is also why Marx's account only really applies to sweatshop conditions; in a well-regulated economy where wages are fair, exploitation is not occurring."

I suspect this is a mixture of insight and distortion, but I cannot reliably tell which parts are which. I am especially unsure about the step from "the worker creates the value" to "the worker is robbed of the difference," and about treating alienation as a subjective feeling. Please audit the summary: separate what a careful reader of Marx would accept from what is a popular simplification and what is simply wrong; bring in the distinctions that do the real work here (for example labor versus labor-power, and the early account of estranged labor versus the mature account of surplus value, so far as they matter); and say whether the two closing inferences — that paying the full value would end both exploitation and alienation, and that fair-wage economies escape the diagnosis — actually follow. Where the summary is unfair, state the more defensible version of the claim it is gesturing at. Keep the entire response to at most 600 words.
