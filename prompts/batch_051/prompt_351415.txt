I am a PhD student in the philosophy of language, and I have spent this term trying to reconstruct the implicit semantics behind my supervisor's verdicts on a run of declarative sentences. She classifies each as true, false, or neither, and she insists that 'neither' registers a genuine absence of truth and falsity rather than uncertainty about some fact that is already settled. Her recorded verdicts are these:

(1) 'The present King of Iceland is bald.' — neither.
(2) 'The present King of Iceland is not bald.' — neither.
(3) 'Iceland has no king.' — true.
(4) 'A man with no hair on his scalp is bald.' — true.
(5) 'A man with one hundred thousand evenly distributed hairs on his scalp is bald.' — false.
(6) 'A man with exactly four thousand evenly distributed hairs on his scalp is bald.' — neither.
(7) 'A man with exactly four thousand evenly distributed hairs on his scalp is not bald.' — neither.

The case I am trying to predict next is the following disjunction:

(8) 'A man with exactly four thousand evenly distributed hairs on his scalp is bald or is not bald.'

Please give me a committed prediction of her verdict on (8), not a survey of the available positions. What general rule about the behaviour of truth-value gaps inside compound sentences is the best fit for (1)–(7), and what does that rule force her to say about (8)? If you think (1)–(7) genuinely underdetermine her answer, be explicit: identify the candidate rules that remain live, say what each would predict for (8), tell me which verdict you nonetheless expect from her and why, and specify the further sentence I should put to her next week to eliminate the remaining options. Whatever you conclude, commit to a predicted verdict for (8) and to the principle that verdict would commit her to.
