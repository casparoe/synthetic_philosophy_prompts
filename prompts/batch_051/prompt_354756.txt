Ok so I've been stuck on a variation of Reid's brave officer case and I'd like a careful, step-by-step analysis of it. For background, Locke's memory criterion says, roughly, that a person at one time is the same person as a person at an earlier time just insofar as the present consciousness can extend back to the earlier thoughts and actions (I'm paraphrasing, not quoting). Reid's objection runs like this: a boy is flogged for robbing an orchard; as a young officer he remembers the flogging; as an older general he remembers taking the enemy standard as the officer but has completely forgotten the flogging. By Locke's criterion, the officer is the same person as the boy (because the officer's consciousness reaches back to the boy's action), and the general is the same person as the officer (because the general's consciousness reaches back to the officer's action). So by transitivity the general is the same person as the boy. But on the criterion applied directly, he isnt, because the general's consciousness doesn't reach back to the flogging. That's the standard contradiction and I think I get it.

Now the variation I actually want to work through. Add a fourth stage. Years later, the general has retired and is an old man, and he spontanously recovers a vivid, first-person memory of the boyhood flogging — the orchard, the sting, the shame of it — but by now he has completely lost the memory of taking the standard as the officer. So we have: boy, officer (remembers boy), general (remembers officer but not boy), old man (remembers boy directly, but not officer). And at every step it's the same human animal. What should a Lockean, or the most defensible neo-Lockean descendant of the view, say here? In particular: is the old man the same person as the boy? Is he the same person as the officer and the general? Does the fact that he recovered the boyhood memory after a gap mean the general-stage man now turns out to have been the same person as the boy all along, even though at that stage his consciousness didn't reach back to the flogging? Is that a coherent result, or does it amount to letting later facts retroactively fix earlier identity facts? And if that's unacceptable, what rule would you add to rule it out — and does that rule smuggle in biological continuity rather than memory doing the work? I'd rather have you defend one clean position than list every option, but do say which fix seems least ad hoc to you.
