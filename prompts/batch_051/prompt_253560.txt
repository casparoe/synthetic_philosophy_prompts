I'm a PhD student in cognitive science working on analogy-making, and there's a thing I keep going back and forth on. I've got a little copycat-style letter-string system that works in a tiny microdomain: three-letter strings, with a base pair like abc => abd (replace the rightmost letter by its alphabetic successor). The target xyz => ? is deliberately ambiguous because z has no alphabetic successor, and my system doesn't give one answer. Across runs it sometimes outputs xyd, sometimes wyz, and occassionally just stalls. The way people in my lab talk, the system's concept of 'successor' is slipping: under pressure the concept gets re-encoded as something like 'the literal letter d travels across' or 'reverse direction and take the predecessor on the left.' But I honestly can't tell whether that's true or whether the system just has a handful of fixed rules with different weights and 'conceptual slippage' is a nice story we tell about rule competition.

So here's what I want. How would you test the claim that this system really has a fluid concept that slips from one thing to another under pressure, rather than merely having several static rules that compete? Give me a concrete experimental design for this microdomain (or a clean variant of it): the manipulation, the dependant variable, the pattern of results that would push you toward the 'the concept slipped' reading and the pattern that would push you toward the 'it's just rule competition' reading. Then say what even a clean positive result would not settle, and whether that residue is a limitation of our experiments or a defect in the idea of having a fluid concept at all. Please don't just lay out options; commit to the one design and the one interpretation you'd actually bet on, and tell me where your confidence gives out.
