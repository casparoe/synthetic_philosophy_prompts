For as long as I have been doing failure analysis in an industrial plant, the moment that has never sat right with me is when two anomalies get written up as "the same mechanism." Two pumps fail a year apart. Two batches of product crack in the same place. Two control loops drift out of range overnight. One person will say these are the same failure mechanism; another will say no, the resemblance is superficial and the two events have different internal histories. The phrase is doing real work in those conversations: it determines whether we hunt for one root cause or two, and whether a fix that worked once should be expected to work again. And yet I cannot say what observation, short of somehow watching the causal history from the inside, would settle who is right.

The claim I want examined is this: when someone groups two anomalies as "the same mechanism," they are asserting something about the world that could be right or wrong, not merely choosing a convenient filing scheme. Set aside the weak version of the claim, where the phrase just means the two anomalies share observable features. I want to know how one would test the strong, substantive claim.

Design the best test you can. In a concrete case — say, two failures that look identical to every instrument we currently have — what intervention, measurement, or pattern of variation would you look for, and exactly which result would favor one mechanism over two mechanisms that happen to look alike? Then say, honestly, what a confirming result would and would not establish. If part of the claim resists testing no matter how good our instruments get, tell me whether that is a limitation of measurement or a sign that "same mechanism" is not fully defined — and whether any of that matters for the practical decisions that ride on the phrase.
