We run a wiki-style fact site where volunteer authors write short "answer cards" about animals, health, food, stuff like that. We keep tripping over generic sentences: "ducks lay eggs" gets marked true by our current checker, but "mosquitoes carry malaria" sometimes gets marked false, and it drives the authors nuts because both sentences feel true in some way even though only female ducks lay eggs and only a handful of mosquito species transmit malaria to humans. Im just a backend dev, not a language person, but I got asked to think through a new rule and I could use a second brain. I dont want to push a design that backfires.

The proposed mechanism: every sentence the parser flags as a bare-plural generic ("X do Y" about a whole kind) has to ship with a prevalence tag. The author states roughly what percentage of X actually do/are Y. If the number is under 50%, the sentence gets auto-rewritten to "some X do Y" unless the author spends reputation to check one of two boxes. Box one is "characteristic of the kind" — the trait follows from the kind's normal life cycle or way of working even if only some members show it, like ducks and egg-laying. Box two is "striking" — the trait is rare but dangerous or otherwise salient, like mosquitoes and malaria. Checked boxes cost reputation; users can challenge a checked box, and if the challenge wins the author loses more reputation and the challenger keeps it. A checked "striking" box also makes the sentence render with a small warning icon.

Walk through how this would actually play out in practice, not just whether it sounds reasonable. What does it incentivize authors to do, what does it incentivize readers/challengers to do, and what does it do to our moderation team? Where does it get gamed first? What kinds of claims get mangled or pushed underground? Are the failure modes fixable without killing the whole idea? And any chance the whole thing makes people worse at reasoning about generics over time?

Keep it under 700 words, and write it in American English.
