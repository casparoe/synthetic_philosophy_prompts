I spent my career in physics and only started reading political philosophy after I retired, so forgive me if I've got some of the details wrong. I keep coming back to Hobbes's argument that the sovereign cannot wrong its subjects in the sense of committing injustice against them, because when a person joins the commonwealth they authorize the sovereign to act on their behalf, so all its acts are in a way their own acts, and no one can be unjust to himself. In the seventeenth century you could at least see how that story was supposed to go: there was a king or a visible assembly, and authorization was something you could imagine actually happening. But the modern state is nothing like that. Nearly everything it does to people is done by administrative agencies under statutes that delegated broad authority decades ago, by officials nobody elected, following guidance documents nobody voted on, and increasingly by automated systems where no particular human being actually made the decision. A disability claim denied, a child taken into care, a drone strike — the person on the receiving end never authorized any of it in any recognizable sense, and a lot of the time neither did the minister who is nominally responsible. So what I'd like to know is this: does Hobbes's authorization argument survive that development, does it need to be repaired, or is it just dead? Be specific about which premise the modern administrative state puts pressure on — is it the premise that authorization actually occurred, or the premise that the sovereign is a single will whose acts can be attributed back to each subject, or something else — and say whether there's a repaired version that's still true and does real work. If there is, what does the weakened version still manage to justify, and what has it had to give up? And I really do mean the fate of the argument, not a survey of the current law of sovereign immunity; I can look the law up myself. Please write your answer as plain continuous prose, the way you'd write me a letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables.
