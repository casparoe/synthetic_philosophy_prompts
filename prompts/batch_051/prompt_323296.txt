In the AI safety discussions I read, I keep seeing the same analogy put to real argumentative work: a model's honesty or deceptiveness is treated as the same kind of property as a human's. A model that outputs a falsehood while the true answer is represented in its training data is said to be lying; a model that gives evasive answers under interrogation is called a dissembler; a model that flatters its user is diagnosed as dishonest in character. The analogy is then used to license the conclusions that deception can be read off outputs without peeking at internals, that a deceptive model deserves something like moral blame, and that evasiveness in low-stakes evaluation now predicts dangerous dishonesty later. I would like an audit of how far this analogy actually holds. Give the correspondence table first: what plays the role of a person's belief, communicative intention, act of assertion, desire that the hearer adopt a false belief, and the trust one normally extends to a speaker. Then divide the disanalogies into those that matter for this use and those that don't. The obvious candidates: outputs are sampled rather than asserted; there is no persistent self across contexts; the training objective is next-token prediction, not a desire to mislead; and the words have no referential intent. Which of these break the analogy, and which are merely different implementations of the same functional role? Finally, state whether the conclusion — that we are justified in calling models deceptive in the human sense and holding them to honesty norms as we would a person — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and give a better metaphor or framework if one is available. End with \answer{...} containing exactly one of: survives, survives with modifications, breaks.
