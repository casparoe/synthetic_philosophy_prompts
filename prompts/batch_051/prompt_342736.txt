I spent my working life in a lab, and I can't shake the habit of treating a logged measurement as settled. If an instrument recorded something at 3 a.m. while nobody was in the building, we still later wrote it up as what happened; it never occured to anyone to say the record was neither true nor false until a person looked at it. But nobody I worked with would treat a prediction that way. If I said on Tuesday that the apparatus would read 2.7 volts on Friday, they'd say that's not yet true or false, just something that might happen. In retirement I've started reading philosophy, and the sea battle argument has dug its way into me. The standard contrast is: 'There will be a sea battle tomorrow' is neither true nor false right now, while 'There was a sea battle yesterday' is true or false even if every trace of it has been destroyed and nobody will ever know. Both sentences point to a time other than the present, and both may be completly beyond our knowledge when we utter them. Yet we give the past one a determinate truth value and deny it to the future one.

Is there actually a principled difference that justifies this asymmetry, or is it just the psychology of remembering in one direction and anticipating in the other? If the world is deterministic, the future ought to be as fixed as the past; if it isn't deterministic, I don't see what makes the past so special. I'd like an honest verdict: keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or throw it out — and if you keep it, defend it against the determinist's objection.
