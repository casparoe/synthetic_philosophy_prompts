I should say at the outset that I have no training in philosophy or law; this is just a set of views I've arrived at by thinking on my own, and I want to know whether they actually fit together. Here is where I've landed on statutes of limitations and the moral significance of time passing.

1. The passage of time does not change how wrong a past act was. If someone committed a wrong in 1980, that act is exactly as wrong in 2025 as it was the day after he committed it. Time moves us farther away from the deed, but it does not slowly dissolve the wrongness itself.

2. I think the justification for punishment is partly backward-looking and partly forward-looking. The offender deserves it, and the punishment also does something going forward: it deters, it incapacitates, and it expresses public condemnation. I would not want to give up either side.

3. I also believe that people can change in a way that makes late punishment troubling. A person who did something serious at twenty and then lived a decent life for forty years is not, in a meaningful way, the same person we would now be punishing. This intuition feels important to me, but I don't know how far it extends.

4. I approve of statutes of limitations for ordinary crimes—including many serious ones—and I take their main justifications to be about evidence and finality: memories fade, witnesses die, records are lost, and people need a point at which the state's file is finally closed.

5. But I think murder should have no time limit. The wrong is so serious that the state's duty to condemn and punish it should not expire simply because time has passed.
