I've been trying to figure out what I actually mean by "function" in biology and I keep going around in circles, so I want to stress-test my gut verdicts against the two standard accounts. The etiological (selected-effects) story says the function of a trait is what it was selected for, what explains why it's there. The causal-role story says the function is just whatever it currently does that contributes to some capacity of the larger system. Here's where I land on a bunch of cases.

Normal adult heart—pumps blood, selected for pumping blood: function is to pump blood. No help, both accounts get it.

Heart destroyed by an infarct, no longer pumps: I still want to say its function is to pump blood and it's just broken. A heart that can't pump is malfunctioning, not a heart whose function changed. That feels like a win for the etiological account.

Lab-grown heart from the patient's own stem cells, grown in a bioreactor, transplanted; it's never been through natural selection, it was designed and built to pump: function is to pump blood, obviously. But now the etiological account has to count "designed" as the relevant selecting process, and I'm not sure that isn't special pleading.

Recurrent laryngeal nerve in a giraffe: it's a poor-design leftover from fish ancestry that detours way down around the aorta and back up to the larynx, but it currently innervates the larynx. I'd say its function is the current job (laryngeal innervation), not whatever it did in some fish ancestor. Point for causal-role.

A neutral substitution that drifted to fixation and slightly increases a protein's folding stability, with no detectable effect on fitness: I wouldn't call the extra stability a function; it's just a byproduct. Feels like a problem for causal-role, or at least causal-role needs a filter it doesn't obviously have.

Spandrel in the jaw—a chunk of bone that exists only as a side effect of jaw growth, never selected, but later gets co-opted as an anchor point for a chewing muscle: I want to say its function now is anchoring that muscle, even though it wasn't selected for it. Feels like causal-role carrying real weight.

Ostrich wings—can't fly, plausibly used now for balance, display, thermoregulation: I don't think their function is flight anymore, and I'm honestly torn between "current minor roles" and "no function at all."

Benign tumor that secretes a hormone the patient happens to need because their gland failed: I really don't want to say hormone secretion is the tumor's function. But the causal-role account looks dangerously close to being forced to say exactly that, unless we restrict it to proper parts of the system, whatever "proper part" is supposed to mean.

Carburetor in a car: its function is to mix fuel and air, and it was designed to do that. So artifact functions feel like they fall out of the etiological account if I'm allowed to count design as a form of selection.

So cases 2 and 9 pull me etiological, 4 and 6 pull me causal-role, 3 and 8 are trouble in opposite directions, and 5 and 7 I genuinely don't know. Is there one principled account—probably a hybrid with a clause or two—that captures most of these without turning into a gerrymander? State it as precisely as you can; tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and wether you'd overturn them; and tell me what it says about a case I havent listed: a gene selected for making one protein, whose protein later turns out to have a second, never-selected function that now keeps the organism alive. Also give me your honest take on whether carburetors and hearts even have the same kind of "function", or whether I should stop trying to unify them.
