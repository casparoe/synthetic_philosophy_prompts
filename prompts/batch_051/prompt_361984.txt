Two friends of mine got into a long argument at a barbecue last weekend, and I'm still not sure who was right. The concrete case was a beach town that wants to build a seawall, and the only feasible route runs straight through one owner's property, so the town would have to seize the land and tear her house down. Without the wall, a storm surge could flood about a hundered other homes. The owner refuses to sell; the town is moving to take the property and pay her market value.

Ren said the taking is obviously justified. (1) He argued that property rights exist because they make people's lives go better overall, and here the balancing clearly comes out in favor of the town: one house lost, a hundred saved. Treating the owner's right as something that blocks that calculus, he said, is fetishizing a boundary we only invented for convenience.

Alba answered that (2) this is exactly what a right is for: it's a constraint on what you may do to a person even when the aggregate outcome would be better. The whole point of calling something a right, she said, is that you don't just rerun the cost-benefit calculation at the moment of temptation, because if you do the right dissolves whenever a big enough crowd wants something.

Ren replied that (3) no one actually treats property rights as absolute — the owner couldn't run a meth lab on the land or keep the fire department out — so the real disagreement is just about how high the bar is before the right gives way, not about whether rights are different in kind from interests. Alba said that (4) is where he's blurring things: there's a difference between overriding a right to protect another right (or in an emergency that isn't about maximizing welfare) and overriding it because the numbers look good on a spreadsheet.

Ren said (5) that once she admits any exceptions at all, she owes us a principle for which ones count, and any principle she offers will turn out to be a disguised version of "violate the right when enough welfare is at stake." Alba said (6) the principle isn't disguised maximizing — it's that you may stop people from making others worse off through their own choices, but you can't use a person as a means to improve third parties' situation. Here, she said, the neighbors aren't being harmed by the owner; they want a benefit from land they have no claim to.

At that point I got lost. Someone else at the table said the whole "rights" vocabulary was doing no work and we should just ask what the town is permitted, required, and forbidden to do. I later found out people call these things rights as trumps, side constraints, or instruments, though no one at the barbecue used those terms.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. Rate each of Ren's and Alba's six moves as clearly mistaken, defensible but overstated, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say who got the better of the underlying case, what each of them was missing, and whether the person who wanted to drop "rights"-talk was right that it's a mere relabeling.
