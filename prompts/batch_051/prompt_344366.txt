I work on the applied side — mostly evals, not research — and lately I keep tripping over the claim that when a language model "thinks out loud," its chain-of-thought text is a report of its own reasoning. That is: the trace is causally connected to how the model actually reached the answer, not a plausible-sounding rationalization it produced after the fact.

I want to know how you'd actually test that claim. Not "what do the papers say" — suppose you had a model and a budget for experiments. What would you measure, what would you vary, and what pattern of results would make you say: yes, the trace is at least partly faithful to the computation that produced the answer? And what pattern would make you say: no, this is basically confabulation with nice grammar?

Then the harder half. Say you run the best version of that test and it comes out positive. What exactly have you shown, and what would still be open even in principle? I can imagine a model passing an intervention test while the trace still misses most of what happened, or the trace being accurate for the wrong reasons. I want the limits stated as carefully as the test itself.

And when you answer, please seperate what people who work on this mostly agree on from what's still genuinely contested. I hear confident claims in both directions — "chain of thought is mostly faithful" / "chain of thought is mostly made up" — and I can't tell which parts of that fight are settled. Don't smooth it over into a consensus if it isn't one.
