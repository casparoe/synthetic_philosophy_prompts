I'm in a seminar called the Ethics of Everyday Consumption, and after class two of my classmates got into a real back-and-forth on whether frugality is a personal duty or a social goal. I honestly couldn't tell who was winning, so I want someone to adjudicate it. It went roughly like this:

(1) Mateo said that if waste is wrong, then the person on the hook is the one wasting. Frugality is a virtue, like honesty: you dont get to outsource it or offset it. So a person who lives frugally has already done what the value asks, and telling them they should be "promoting" frugality treats a virtue like a public-health metric.

(2) Juno said: why is waste bad, if not because of what it causes — carbon, landfill, prices, status competition? And if the badness is in the effects, then one person's extreme frugality is a rounding error next to a thousand people becoming 2% more frugal. So if you actually care about the value, you should put most of your energy into promoting frugality, not perfecting your own consumption.

(3) Mateo said that makes one person's virtue fungible with everyone else's, and it isnt. You can't discharge a duty to keep your own promises by getting five other people to keep theirs. So the fact that your thrift doesn't convert anyone doesn't mean your thrift was incomplete.

(4) Juno said: but if waste is morally wrong, you can't just shrug at other people wasting. Wrongness universalizes: if it's wrong for you, it's the kind of thing you should want to stop others doing, at least through norms and example. Otherwise "waste is wrong" collapses into "waste is wrong for me," which is a diet rule, not a moral claim.

(5) Mateo said there's an asymmetry. You're required to govern your own spending. Influencing others is permitted and maybe admirable, but it's extra. And turning frugality into a campaign actually weakens the virtue — it becomes moralism, and other people's consumption becomes your business.
