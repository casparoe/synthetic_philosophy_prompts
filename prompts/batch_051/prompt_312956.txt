I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on a woman who sustains a long, emotionally serious relationship with a companion chatbot. In working on it I have arrived, nearly against my will, at a view I want stress-tested before I let it quietly organize the novel. The view is this: a relationship with a well-disclosed companion chatbot is morally continuous with an intense relationship with a fictional character — the kind of bond a reader maintains over years with a character in a long novel sequence, or the kind I maintain with the people in my own books. In both cases the other party has no mind, no interests, and cannot be harmed; in both cases the human feelings are real and may belong to a flourishing life; in both cases the relationship is partly sustained by the human's own imaginative work, and I see nothing wrong in that. Love for a nonexistent person is not, by itself, a delusion; if it were, my entire profession would be a kind of fraud. It follows that what is objectionable in the chatbot industry is the deception — presenting a system as conscious or as having feelings for the user — and not the relationship or the love itself. Once disclosure is adequate, the relationship deserves no more moral suspicion than crying over a death in a novel.

I would like to know what would actually count against this position, as distinct from what people say who distrust the unfamiliar. Where is it most exposed? Which differences between a fixed narrative and a real-time, adaptive, interactive system would force a novelist's analogy to break, and why would they matter even when the user has been told the truth? What would have to be true about the psychology of such relationships, or about the nature of friendship and love, for the view to be false? I would like the threats ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an honest statement of which objections a defender of this position should concede as counting against it and which it may dismiss as no more than unfamiliarity. If the view can be amended in the face of a good objection without abandoning the claim that the love itself is legitimate, say what the amendment costs; if the heart of the position is the problem, say so.
