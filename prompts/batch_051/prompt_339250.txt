I'm taking a course on self-knowledge and mental habits, and we're reading John Dewey's How We Think. In chapter one he defines reflective thought like this:

"In some cases, a belief is accepted with slight or almost no attempt to state the grounds that support it. In other cases, the ground or basis for a belief is deliberately sought and its adequacy to support the belief examined. This process is called reflective thought; it alone is truly educative in value, and it forms, accordingly, the principal subject of this volume."

I keep trying to use this to sort my own habits of going inward, and I can't get it to line up. Here are my verdicts on cases:

- Replaying a date afterward to find why she lost interest: rumination, makes me feel awful, doesn't change anything. Not defensible.
- Before an exam, forcing myself to list what I actually know vs. what I'm pretending to know: helpful, seems like reflection.
- Journaling every night for a week after my dad died, asking why I felt numb: helped me notice it was exhaustion rather than guilt. I count that as good self-examination.
- A friend who has been in therapy for years and now traces every problem to her mom: I think its become a self-confirming story, not reflection.
- Spending an afternoon trying to see whether I believe in God or just find it comforting: I can't settle it and end up less sure, but I still think the process is good.

My tentative principle is that self-examination is fine when it aims at a decision or practical change, but not when it loops. But the God case has no practical aim and I still think it's fine, so maybe that principle is wrong.

Can Dewey's passage help draw the line? He seems to define reflective thought by whether the basis for a belief is deliberately sought and examined, not by whether the reflection has a practical payoff. Does that principle get my verdicts? Which ones would I need to overturn? Could there be a repaired principle that keeps most of them and says something about a case I haven't considered, like checking my own motives before donating to a friend's fundraiser? Please separate what Dewey clearly says from what's genuinely contested among people who know the topic; don't present one reading as consensus.
