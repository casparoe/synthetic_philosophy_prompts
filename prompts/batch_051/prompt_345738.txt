Last night I spent several hours arguing with a close friend about conceptual engineering, and in particular about whether fixing concepts is genuinely distinct from analysing them, and whether the practice is compatible with a plausible picture of how meaning works. The argument left me with a set of commitments I had never tried to state together, and I would like a consistency audit before I present them in a seminar. I am not asking for a defense of the package as a whole; I want to know which parts of it pull against which, how badly, and what the cheapest repairs would be.

Here is where I landed:

(1) I hold a broadly externalist metasemantics. What a term or concept means in a public language is settled by historical and social patterns of use and by the parts of the world those patterns are anchored to, not by any individual speaker's private intentions or explicit stipulations.

(2) I nonetheless think conceptual engineering is a real and distinctive method. When philosophers argue that we should revise our concept of consent, or race, or marriage, they are doing something more substantive than announcing a stipulative redefinition for the purposes of a paper.

(3) I am drawn to the ameliorative picture: the valuable question is not only what our concepts happen to be, but what they should be for us, given the work they actually do. I think this project is legitimate only insofar as it is answerable to the existing concept's actual social function. An ameliorative proposal that simply ignores what the current concept does — whom it harms, which distinctions it enables, which inferences it licenses, which practices it stabilizes — is not engineering the concept; it is replacing it with a new one that happens to share a word.

(4) I do not, however, think one needs a complete analysis of the target concept before engineering it. A partial grip on central cases, core functions, and known defects is enough to begin, and sometimes enough to finish.

(5) I think conceptual engineering is best understood as directed at terms and the linguistic-inferential practices around them, rather than at "concepts" understood as mental or abstract objects that get swapped out one for another. I am suspicious of talk of changing the concept itself.

(6) I think engineering can succeed locally. If a revision of the legal concept of marriage or the clinical concept of addiction is adopted within a legal or clinical community and improves inquiry or practice there, that can be a genuine engineering success even if ordinary speakers never adopt it.

(7) I suspect the sharp line between conceptual analysis and conceptual engineering is overblown. Analysis always involves a normative choice about which strands of usage to systematize and which to treat as noise, so the descriptive question of what a concept means and the normative question of what it should mean are entangled from the start.

Please audit this set. I would like to know which commitments are in genuine tension with which, which pairs are only apparently in tension, and why. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as all equally serious; for each ranked tension, say what would need to be given up or reformulated and what the reformulation would cost philosophically. I do not want a bibliography or a survey of the literature; I want to know where the actual strain is in what I have written down.
