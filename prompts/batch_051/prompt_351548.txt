I am a novelist, not a professional philosopher, and I have been reading Wittgenstein's On Certainty while drafting a book about a narrator who gradually loses confidence in ordinary certainties — that the past is real, that other people remain in existence when nobody observes them, that the world was not created ten minutes ago. A friend who is a graduate student in epistemology introduced me to the thought that all doubting and checking depend on commitments that are not themselves being doubted or checked; in those late notes, some certainties are described as hinge-like, because they are what stays fixed while the rest of inquiry turns around them. She then put the following argument to me, and I cannot find my way out of it.

(1) Any inquiry can proceed only against a background of commitments that are not at that moment being put to the test, for if everything were tested at once there would be no standard left by which to test anything. (2) Call these commitments hinges: they are placed outside the reach of doubt not because they survived scrutiny but because scrutiny is possible only on the basis of them. (3) If hinges are not answerable to evidence, then no particular content can be rationally disqualified from being held as a hinge; being beyond evidential appraisal is precisely what a hinge is, so from an epistemic point of view one hinge is as good as another. (4) It follows that a person who holds an ordinary, checkable claim — that a former lover was unfaithful, that a political order is illegitimate, that a rival has been scheming against her — in the same unshakeable, evidence-exempt way is making no epistemic error; she has merely added another hinge. (5) But that cannot be right: paranoia, propaganda, and wishful thinking do not become intellectually respectable merely by being held fast, and if they did they would be beyond criticism from within any inquiry they helped to constitute. (6) So either the picture in (1) and (2) is false, or there is no epistemically meaningful difference between the framework certainties that make inquiry possible and any stubbornly held, evidence-resistant belief. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would most like to keep (1) and (2), and (5) also seems to me plainly true; my worry is that rejecting (3) or (4) forces me to invent some non-evidential standard for appraising hinges, and I cannot see what such a standard could be without granting the very relativism the argument exploits.
