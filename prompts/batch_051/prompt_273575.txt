In my school district, every incoming ninth grader is assigned a graduation risk score produced by a machine-learning model, and students above a cutoff are placed in a mandatory tutoring and mentoring program. The district defends this policy with an external audit that makes two claims. First, the score means the same thing no matter a student's racial or ethnic background: among students with the same score, the proportion who actually graduate on time is about the same in every group. Second, the model is equally error-prone for every group: the rate at which it wrongly predicts that a student will graduate, and the rate at which it wrongly predicts that a student will not graduate, are about the same across groups. And yet the program ends up enrolling about three times as many students from one group as from another, which has made the school board fight over whether that disparity is itself a form of unfairness. I am trying to work out what the district's defense actually commits it to. What conception of fairness does this practice presuppose? For example, does the district's position treat fairness as a matter of the procedure being equally accurate for each individual, or does it quietly rule out the view that fairness requires the burdens and benefits of the program to be distributed equally across groups? Is the implicit view coherent, and is the district's practice actually forced by it, or could several different theories of fairness license the same policy? Finally, I would like to know whether a single scoring system could satisfy everything the district claims at once — scores meaning the same thing across groups, equal error rates, and equal group enrollment — or whether some of those criteria are in tension with one another, and what would have to change about the policy if the district adopted a different conception of fairness.
