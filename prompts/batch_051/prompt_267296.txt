In his discussion of the origin of government in A Treatise of Human Nature, Book III, Part II, Section VII, Hume contrasts two neighbours who can easily agree to drain a meadow they share, because each sees that if he fails to do his part the entire project collapses, with a thousand persons who cannot agree to the same action, because each looks for a pretext to shift the trouble and expense onto the rest. In the same discussion, he writes: “The consequences of every breach of equity seem to lie very remote, and are not able to counter-ballance any immediate advantage, that may be reap’d from it.” The moral usually drawn from this is that large-scale cooperation needs government or artificial rules, because our passions are biased toward what is near and immediate.

But I am interested in a narrower question about the individual action. If I free ride on a large, already-established public good—say, a public bus service or a collectively maintained irrigation system—my failure to contribute makes no detectable difference to whether the good continues. The harmful consequences Hume mentions are not merely remote; in my individual case they may be causally negligible. Yet the charge of being a social parasite still seems apt. I want to know exactly what that charge amounts to. Should we say the wrong is still a violation of Hume’s rule of equity, only with the bad consequences spread thinly across many people? Or does calling someone a parasite pick out a different fault—such as treating the contributors’ willingness as a resource one is entitled to, making an exception of oneself to a rule one would want others to follow, expressing contempt for a practice one quietly depends on, or something about dishonesty or coercion? And do these different diagnoses matter for how I should feel when the public good is poorly managed, or when the benefit is unsought but unavoidable? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
