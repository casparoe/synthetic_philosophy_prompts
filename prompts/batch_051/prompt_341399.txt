I hold that backward induction gives the uniquely correct account of rational play in finite perfect-information games, and that the centipede game's conclusion — that the first player should stop at the first node even though every player would be better off if play ran to the end — is a result to be accepted rather than a paradox to be dissolved. Tell me what would count against this. I want the candidate defeaters ranked from decisive to merely awkward: formal arguments that backward induction requires more than common knowledge of rationality; experimental behavior in centipede-like games; alternative principles that predict and justify continued play (other-regarding preferences, team reasoning, bounded rationality, a small chance of irrationality); and internal objections such as the charge that the view conflates a decision procedure with a criterion of correctness. For each, say whether it would show the view false, show only that it has a narrower domain than claimed, or merely show that actual agents fall short of its demands. Conclude with an honest assessment of whether the view, once qualified to absorb these cases, has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a serious defect in a normative account of rational play.
