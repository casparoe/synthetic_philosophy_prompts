I have been working through the recent debate about insect welfare, and I have found myself drawn, somewhat against my initial instincts, to a version of the scale argument that I now want stress-tested. The position is this: if one assigns even a small probability — say, between one and five per cent — to the claim that insects are subjects of experience, then the sheer number of insects alive at any time, which by any credible estimate exceeds the human population by many orders of magnitude, makes the expected number of morally considerable lives at stake enormous; so enormous that, if one guides one's moral concern by expected value at all, insect welfare should dominate concern for other non-human animals and may even compete with concern for humans. On this view, a small probability of sentience is not a reason to set the matter aside; it is precisely the scale of the insect population that turns a modest probability into something morally explosive. I find the inference difficult to resist, but I suspect I find it difficult to resist mainly because expected-value reasoning is doing a great deal of quiet work, and because I have not been shown a clear account of where the argument might fail.

What I would like is a map of what would count against this position. I do not mean the practical objection that we would not know how to help insects, nor the psychological point that insects fall outside ordinary sympathy; I mean the places where the argument itself could come apart. Which empirical findings — for example, about how much central neural integration a creature needs in order to suffer, about the structures singled out by global-workspace or integrated-information theories of consciousness, or about the evolution of nociception without affective experience — would undermine the inference? Which results in decision theory concerning tiny probabilities, fanaticism, risk-weighted expected utility, or unbounded value bear on it, and how should a defender respond to them? Which conceptual points — about whether one can sensibly hold a graded credence in a binary property of this sort, about whether sentience comes in degrees that do not multiply in the straightforward way the argument assumes, or about whether the probability and the moral weight are being conjured from the same thin evidence — count as genuine defeaters rather than mere unease?

I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. In each case, please say whether a committed defender of the scale argument would themselves concede that it counts against the position, or would dismiss it as question-begging or as a confusion of levels of analysis; and say which defeaters require abandoning expected-value reasoning altogether rather than merely qualifying the argument. Finally, I would like an honest verdict on whether the position, after absorbing all the qualifications that are intellectually legitimate, has been insulated into unfalsifiability — and, if it has, whether that is a problem for a position whose whole point is to guide action under uncertainty, or an acceptable feature of a claim of this kind.
