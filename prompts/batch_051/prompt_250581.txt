I have been reading G.E. Moore's 1942 reply to his critics in Schilpp's The Philosophy of G.E. Moore, and I want to check an idea against it. Moore there considers a case of the following kind: someone says of himself that he went to the pictures last Tuesday, and in the same breath says that he does not believe that he went. If I understand him, Moore says the proposition could easily be true, yet there is something absurd about someone producing that string of words about himself; other people describing the same person in the same way need not be absurd at all. I am paraphrasing from memory, but the point I want to work with is that Moore locates the oddness in the act of asserting, not in any contradiction in the content.

I design a reporting pipeline with two independent components. One component reads raw telemetry and emits factual statements, for instance that the cache was flushed at 03:12. The other component reads the current state of a predictive model and emits statements about what that model's internal state supports, for instance that the model does not believe the cache was flushed at 03:12. The pipeline can concatenate these into a single line: the cache was flushed at 03:12, but the model does not believe the cache was flushed.

If I understand Moore's standard analysis, the absurdity in the original case comes from the fact that the same speaker both asserts the first clause and, in the second clause, gives expression to a state of not believing it; the second clause does not work like a factual report, even though it looks like one. In my pipeline, by contrast, neither component does both things. The telemetry component asserts p but has no beliefs, and the model-state component reports a belief without asserting the world-state claim. The conjunction is an artifact of concatenation. Does the Moorean absurdity survive this change, or does the standard analysis predict that it should disappear? I would like to know exactly which structural feature of Moore's original case is doing the work: the grammatical first-person, the fact that a single agent both asserts p and expresses a state inconsistent with believing p, or something about the audience being entitled to read the whole utterance as coming from one rational subject. Once that is identified, what does the answer say about whether my generated incident reports are making something like the same speech act that Moore found absurd?
