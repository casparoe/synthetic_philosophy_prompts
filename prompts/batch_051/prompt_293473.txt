In a philosophy of language seminar we have been working through Grice's account of conversational implicature, and I find myself stuck on a dilemma about its status. Grice is clear that semantic content and speaker meaning are different: when I say Some of the students passed, the sentence itself is supposed to put only a lower bound on how many passed, while the further thought that not all of them passed is something I as the speaker implicate, and my audience must recover it by assuming I am observing the Cooperative Principle and its maxims. But that recovery looks extremely predictable — a competent hearer arrives at it almost automatically, apparently by reasoning that if I could properly have made the stronger claim and did not, then I must be using something like the maxim of Quantity to communicate its denial. If the inference is that routine, why isn't the stronger claim simply part of what the utterance means in context? And from the opposite direction, when a speaker cancels the inference — Some of the students passed; in fact, all of them did — Griceans take that as evidence that the stronger claim was never part of the sentence's meaning. That leaves me with what I suspect is a false choice: either implicatures are regular enough that they collapse into semantic content, or they are so defeasible that there is no determinate fact about what was implicated when the speaker spoke. I assume Griceans reject both horns, so I want to know where my picture of calculability and cancellability is distorting the view.
