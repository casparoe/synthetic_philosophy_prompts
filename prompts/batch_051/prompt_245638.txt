Something I keep tripping over in a reading group on the Reformation: the debate between the classic Protestant line and the Catholic/Orthodox line about how you know what Scripture means seems to have been stuck in the same place for five hundred years. The Protestant says the text is clear on the essentials and is its own interpreter, so appealing to the church to settle readings just pushes the problem back a step — who interprets the church's interpretations? The Catholic side says that's exactly backwards: without a living teaching authority you end up with every reader as his own pope, and the proof is that the Protestant world splintered into thousands of readings while still claiming the text was clear. What I find odd is that both sides see the same regress — an interpretation needs an interpreter, whose interpretation needs an interpreter, and so on — and each just says the other side's stopping point is the problem. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is there a hidden historical question about what the early church actually did, a disagreement about whether interpretive authority can ever be non-institutional, a divergence in what the two sides mean by calling a text binding on a reader, or a bedrock difference about whether meaning has to be applied by persons at all? I have a hunch it's less a first-order argument about verses and more a second-order disagreement about where an interpretive regress is allowed to stop and why. But I'd rather you diagnose the structure of the stalemate than pick a winner, and if you think my hunch is off, say so. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
