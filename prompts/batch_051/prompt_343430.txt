Consider a semantic claim about generic sentences. A generic such as *ducks lay eggs* or *mosquitoes carry malaria* can be accepted as true even though only a minority of the relevant individuals have the asserted property: most ducks do not lay eggs, and very few mosquitoes actually carry the malaria parasite. One influential way of explaining this is that the truth of a generic depends not on the number or proportion of members with the property, but on whether the property is suitably connected to the kind itself—whether it is biologically normal, kind-defining, characteristic, or underwritten by the kind's nature or causal role. Purely statistical facts about prevalence, on this view, are at best a fallible cue to that underlying relation.

Your task is to turn this conceptual claim into something empirically testable. Design a judgment experiment using invented or unfamiliar kinds so that prior beliefs about real species do not do the work. Vary independently at least two factors: (i) the stated prevalence of property P among members of kind K, including both very low and very high rates, and (ii) whether P is presented as kind-characteristic—for example, tied to reproductive function, normal development, or the kind's ecological role—rather than accidental, unusual, or encountered merely by chance. Build in conditions that would also separate a third position: a hybrid view on which prevalence must cross some minimal threshold, after which factors such as danger, strikingness, or practical importance decide whether a low-prevalence property supports a true generic.

Specify exactly what judgments you would collect (for example, truth-value judgments or naturalness ratings for sentences of the form *Ks are P*), what pattern of results would support each of the three views, and what the strongest version of this test would and would not settle—including what even a clean result favoring the kind-characteristic view would leave open about the underlying semantics and about what it is for a property to be "characteristic" of a kind.
