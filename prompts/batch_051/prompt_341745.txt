You are a philosopher who works on the ethics of killing and letting die, and on the nature of the doing/allowing distinction itself. I would like you to help me reverse-engineer a colleague's implicit classification rule.

Professor R. has spent years recording her own verdicts on whether particular cases are instances of killing or of letting die. She insists that the distinction is morally weighty, and that it is not the same as the distinction between blameworthy and blameless conduct: she will readily describe a gross dereliction of duty as a letting die. She also does not think that "killing" automatically means "impermissible," though she treats a classification of killing as carrying a much heavier burden of justification. From her file, here are six settled verdicts:

1. A nurse injects a lethal dose of potassium chloride into a patient's existing intravenous line, intending to end his suffering; the patient dies moments later. Verdict: killing.
2. A hiker hears calls for help from a ravine where a climber lies injured but walks on without summoning help; the climber dies of exposure. Verdict: letting die.
3. A physician, caring for a debilitated patient with a do-not-resuscitate order who develops pneumonia, decides against beginning antibiotics because treatment would merely prolong the dying process; the patient dies of pneumonia. Verdict: letting die.
4. A son smothers his unconscious father with a pillow. Verdict: killing.
5. A lifeguard on duty becomes absorbed in a shouting match and fails to notice a swimmer drowning twenty metres away; the swimmer dies. Verdict: letting die, though she regards the lifeguard's inattention as seriously blameworthy.
6. A hospital administrator, during a shortage, denies the last available ventilator to a patient who cannot breathe independently and allocates it to a patient with better prospects; the first patient dies of respiratory failure. Verdict: letting die.

The case now before her, on which she has not yet formed a verdict, is this. A man on a dock extends an oar to an exhausted swimmer who is struggling to stay afloat. The swimmer grips the oar. Some minutes later, annoyed by her shouting, the man yanks the oar free and walks away; the swimmer drowns. The man did not push her under, and it is not his doing that made the water dangerous; but he did remove the thing that was keeping her alive at the moment of death.

Please work through this in stages. Begin by brainstorming at least three distinct rules that are consistent with all six of Professor R.'s settled verdicts, drawing on whatever features you think are most likely to be doing the work: the agent's bodily movements at the fatal moment, the source of the lethal threat, the interruption of ongoing aid, the presence or absence of a role-based duty, the difference between initiating and continuing aid, or anything else you regard as live. For each candidate rule, state how it would classify each of the six settled cases and the dock/oar case, and check explicitly that it fits all six. Then deliver your best prediction of how Professor R. will classify the dock/oar case, give a number for your confidence in that prediction, and say which candidate rule your prediction implicitly favors and why. Finally, tell me which feature of the case your prediction is most sensitive to, and propose one further case—ideally one she has not already classified—whose verdict would do the most to eliminate at least one of the remaining candidate rules; state what each candidate rule predicts about that further case.
