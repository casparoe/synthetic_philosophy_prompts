I write about the science of ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the past several months I have been preparing a long profile of a researcher whose work sits between neuropsychology and philosophy of mind. Her central concern is what it takes for one human organism to house exactly one subject of experience, and she is known for issuing definite verdicts about individual cases while refusing to commit in advance to a single criterion. From her interviews, lab notes, and a recorded graduate seminar, I have assembled what I take to be her record so far.

She counts an ordinary awake adult with an intact brain as one subject, and someone in dreamless slow-wave sleep as no subject at all, because she says there is no stream of consciousness present to unify. She counts a person who had one cerebral hemisphere removed in infancy and developed normally as one subject. Most relevant to my profile, she counts a split-brain patient whose corpus callosum was surgically sectioned as one subject while he moves through an ordinary morning routine of making coffee, reading, and talking, but she counts the same patient as two subjects of experience in the classic lateralized experiment in which the two hands act on conflicting instructions at cross-purposes; in that case she speaks of two centers of consciousness that are not co-conscious with each other.

I am about to visit her laboratory to observe a new case with the same split-brain patient, and I would like to know what verdict to expect before I ask her about it. In the new task the two hemispheres are given simultaneous but non-conflicting jobs: the left hand has to sort a set of tiles by texture while the right hand copies a dictated sentence. The patient performs both accurately and without interference, looking to an observer like any ordinary person multitasking, but afterward he can give a full verbal report only of the sentence task; he cannot say what the left hand was doing and can indicate the sorting result only by pointing with that hand. Would she most likely say there is one subject here or two? I am less interested in the label than in the rule her earlier verdicts already commit her to, the other rules that fit the record equally well, and whether the new case actually favors one of them; if the record underdetermines the verdict, tell me what further case would most cleanly separate the candidate rules.
