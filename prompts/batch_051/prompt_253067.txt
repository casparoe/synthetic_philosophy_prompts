I just spent two hours arguing with my friend about whether anyone "deserves" a spot in a selective program, and we went in circles: his endpoint was "no one deserves anything, because every talent is luck," and mine was "fine, but I have to run a selection commitee next month and the word 'deserves' is going to get tossed around a hundred times." He also kept saying the word "meritocracy" started as a dystopian joke, which I never actually checked.

So help me run that committee. I direct a small post-bac fellowship for public-university graduates: roughly 400 applications for 12 spots, once a year, and I chair the selection committee. The people in the room are mostly working professionals, not philosophers, and they will read a two-page protocol at most. We get maybe ten minutes of deliberation per borderline candidate, but the decisions are high-stakes for applicants (money, mentorship, a career line), and I have to sign off on every final decision. Every cycle the same claims come up: "she deserves it, look at what she overcame," "he doesn't deserve it, he was handed every advantage," "it's unfair to reject her for a bad GRE score she didn't choose."

Write me a reusable decision procedure for when the committee may legitimately say a candidate deserves a place, built from whatever the philosophy of desert and merit actually distinguishes. I know there's a difference between deserving something in a moral sense, being entitled to it under our published rules, and being qualified for it; I want that difference to survive. Include the question of what the desert base is — effort, achievement, need, improvement, something else — and the worry that effort and talent are themselves partly shaped by luck. And separate rewarding past performance from investing in future promise, because my committee collapses those two constantly. Give me the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops at a definite no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three invented candidates. Amara: excellent numbers, very priviledged background. Ben: weaker numbers, worked 30 hours a week through college in an under-resourced school. Third, a case you design where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like — reject someone I'd probably have admitted on vibe, or admit someone I'd have written off. Finally, say what the procedure cannot settle, how confident you are in it overall, and single out the one step or point you're least sure about — the specific spot where it's most likely to send a committee wrong, not generic hedging.
