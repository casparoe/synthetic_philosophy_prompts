I'm TAing an applied ethics discussion section on manipulation vs. legitimate persuasion, and honestly the students keep using "manipulative" as just a vibe — they can all point to cases they hate, but nobody can articulate what actually separates a manipulative sales pitch from a fair one. The assigned reading gestures at the idea that manipulation works by bypassing rationality, but then someone always brings up a truthfull car salesman who anchors you with a crazy high first price, and the group splits all over again. So I want to turn this into a sorting instrument rather than another pros-and-cons discussion.

Please write me a decision tree (or a "which view do you actually hold?" quiz with a scoring key, whichever works better) that classifies someone's position on where the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion falls. I want the branch questions to be about concrete cases and testable claims, not about whether the person self-identifies as a hardliner about autonomy. Work in at least these cases somewhere: the anchoring car salesman (every number he says is true, and the buyer knows he's a salesman); a friend who says "I'd do it for you," and means it, to get you to help him move; a cookie banner with a big green "accept all" button and a gray "reject" button hidden behind a settings menu; and a doctor who says "90% survival rate" rather than "10% mortality rate" when both are true, to get a patient to consent. Have the splits track things like: whether manipulation requires an intention to bypass the target's rational capacities or just has that effect; whether the problem is deception or the method itself; whether it matters if the target would endorse the method if it were explained to them; whether emotional appeals (flattery, guilt, fear of missing out) are a separate category; and whether the influencer's motive — helping vs. exploiting — changes the verdict for the very same tactic.

The leaves should at least include the rational-bypass view (wrong because it routes around the person's capacity to reason), the hypothetical-endorsement view (the test is whether the target would accept the influence method if they understood it), a motive-based view (same tactic, different verdict depending on whether the influencer is trying to help or use the person), and the "there's no principled line, it's all just influence" view. Also note the unoccupied combinations of answers and diagnose whether each is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored, and flag any two leaves your tree can't geniunely separate. Aim for something a student could take in five minutes and come out with a label plus a sense of what commitment that label actually costs them.
