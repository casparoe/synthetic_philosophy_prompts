I keep noticing an asymmetry I can't shake, and I want to know whether its defensible or just a habit. We rank the seriousness of individual wrongs without any queasiness: a judge gives a longer sentence for a premeditated murder than for a drunk-driving death, and nobody thinks the judge has committed an extra wrong by putting two crimes on one scale. Friends compare who treated who worse in a breakup and its just conversation. But the moment the same scale is applied to historical mass death — was the Holocaust worse than Stalin's famine in Ukraine, was the Armenian genocide worse than the killings in King Leopold's Congo — many of the same people say the ranking itself is obscene. Not that the numbers are uncertain or the answer is hard, but that the question shouldn't be asked at all, because weighing one atrocity against another turns suffering into a competition of victims and adds a second insult to the dead.

So the asymmetry is: ranking wrongs is treated as perfectly legitimate when the wrongs are individual, and as morally unacceptable when the wrongs are collective and historical, even though both involve exactly the same operation of putting terrible things on a single scale of badness. Is there any principle that generates both verdicts, or is the taboo at the collective level just an emotional reaction to scale, no more rational than refusing to add up two large bills? I want the strongest justification for the asymmetry you can build, stress-tested against awkward cases like a professional historian comparing two genocides in a scholarly book to explain why one killed on a larger scale, or a tribunal comparing two massacres to set reparations. And give me an honest verdict at the end: should we keep the taboo, abandon it, or confine it to some contexts but not others?
