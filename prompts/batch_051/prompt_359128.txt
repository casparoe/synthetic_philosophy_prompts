For the closing session of my seminar on old age and the good life, I want to give the students a short poem that functions as a structural diagram of the argument in Cicero's dialogue on old age. Please write a sonnet whose volta falls at the ninth line. The octave should state, in order, the four complaints against old age that Cato answers: that it withdraws a person from active affairs, that it weakens the body, that it strips away nearly every pleasure, and that it stands too close to death. The volta should enact the pivot of Cato's reply, in which each alleged loss is granted as fact and then revalued: the retreat from public business leaves the distinctively elder work of counsel and recollection; the body's decline is to be met by using, not lamenting, whatever strength remains; the cooling of the passions frees us from their tyranny and leaves the quieter enjoyments of conversation, study, and one's own land; and the nearness of death is no evil, since death is either simple extinction or a change to something better. Let the closing couplet compress the governing thought that the worth of an old age depends on the character one brings to it, not on the body's accounts. The poem should be accurate to the shape of the argument—a reader who knows the dialogue should be able to trace each complaint and its answer line by line, while a reader who does not know it should still receive a faithful version of the consolation—and it should not quote or imitate any particular translation.
