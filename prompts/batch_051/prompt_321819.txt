Language-model researchers often ask whether a model's chain-of-thought output is 'faithful', but at least three distinct targets get run together under that heading. There is causal faithfulness: the tokens presented as reasoning are the process that actually produced the answer. There is report faithfulness: the chain of thought truthfully describes reasoning the model did in some form, whether or not that reasoning caused the answer. And there is oversight usefulness: the chain of thought, whatever its causal status, lets a human or another model reliably detect when the answer is wrong. Consider a lab deciding whether to treat a model's self-reported reasoning as an audit trail in high-stakes settings, with a team of six researchers funded for two years. Which of the three targets should that team prioritise first, and how should the remaining two be ordered after it? Defend the ordering by identifying which undetected failure modes would be most harmful in an audit setting, which currently available methods (e.g., interventions, fine-tuning on known-answer tasks, comparing stated with revealed confidence) could make progress on each target, and which empirical findings over those two years would most change your ranking.
