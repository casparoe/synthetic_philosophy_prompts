My mathematics teacher grades partly on whether a solution is well-defined, and she has built up a track record of rulings about how the roots of a polynomial may be labeled. I want to predict how she will grade one problem that is likely to appear next week. Her past rulings are as follows. She gave full credit whether a student listed the roots of x² − 5x + 6 = 0 as 2 and 3 or as 3 and 2, saying the order did not matter. She accepted a solution that named the roots of x² − 3x + 2 = 0 as r and s and then computed r + s = 3 and rs = 2, because those values do not depend on which root is called r. She accepted x = ±√2 as the solution to x² = 2, explaining that the radical sign picks out the nonnegative square root while the ± supplies both roots. She rejected as ill-posed a problem that named the roots of x² − 2x + 2 = 0 as r and s and asked for r − s, on the grounds that, absent a rule fixing which root is which, the answer is not determined. She also told a classmate that labeling the roots of that same equation by the lexicographic order on the complex numbers — smaller real part first, with ties broken by smaller imaginary part — was defined but arbitrary, and she declined to say whether that would make a problem well-posed. The expected problem next week is: let r be the lexicographically smaller root of x² − 2x + 2 = 0 and s the other, under exactly that convention, and compute r + 2s. Please predict her verdict, state the general rule about canonical choices and well-definedness that best coheres with her earlier rulings (for example, invariance under relabeling versus any explicit convention being sufficient, or something else), say why that rule fits better than its nearest competitor, and describe a further case that would settle which rule she is actually using.
