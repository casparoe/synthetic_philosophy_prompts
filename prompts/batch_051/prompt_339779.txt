I am a PhD student in philosophy working on epistemic value, specifically the Meno problem. I am trying to formulate criteria for the kind of true belief that is supposed to be more valuable than a merely true belief, and I want to test those criteria against a real but borderline case. My working criteria are: (1) the belief is true; (2) the agent can give an account of why it is true, rather than relying on an inarticulate hunch; (3) the basis would still be available across nearby possible cases, so the correctness is not accidental; and (4) the agent’s confidence is calibrated to the strength of that basis. Now consider a senior radiologist who has for many years detected early-stage pneumothorax from chest films at significantly above-average accuracy. When asked, she says she can see that something is off, but she cannot articulate the relevant pattern; she also becomes less accurate when forced to justify each finding. Does her diagnostic true belief qualify as the more valuable kind of true belief, or is it merely true belief? Does this case show that one of my criteria is not necessary, or that my list misses what actually does the work?
