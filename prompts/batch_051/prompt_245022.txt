I am a novelist, currently drafting a chapter in which a retired composer and a neuroscientist argue about whether a particular piece of music is sad in itself or only sad in its effects on listeners. The scene has forced me to notice that I keep writing sentences like "the second movement is sad" without knowing what, exactly, I am asserting, and I would rather settle the conceptual structure before I put further words in either character's mouth.

Before I read any more aesthetics, I want the desiderata for a theory of how sounds can be sad — or, more broadly, how music can possess emotional qualities at all. What must an adequate account of musical expressiveness get right? I am not looking for a bibliography or a survey; I want the requirements themselves, with the case or consideration behind each, and a clear marking of which are non-negotiable and which are only the preferences of one camp. I have half-assembled my own list and do not know which entries belong on it. Candidate requirements include: keeping apart the music's being sad from its making listeners sad and from its representing sadness; explaining why a listener can feel exhilarated by a passage she also calls desolate; accounting for the felt fact that the sadness seems to sit in the music rather than in the listener; saying whether expressiveness survives across unfamiliar idioms, and whether rhythm or timbre alone can carry it; making room for music that seems to express emotions for which we have no names; and respecting the way a single score can be performed expressively or coldly. Some of these may be genuine requirements, some may be parochial prejudices, and some may pull against one another.

Please give me the list with the motivating case behind each requirement, flag any pair that cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify the single requirement that does the most work against the standard theories. Then, briefly, score the arousal, resemblance, and persona accounts against the list. The purpose I care about is narrow: I need a theory good enough that the argument between my composer and my neuroscientist is a real disagreement rather than a muddle, and good enough that my narration of a listener's experience does not quietly beg the question.
