I am a novelist who writes literary fiction, roughly one book every two or three years, and I am trying to impose some discipline on the part of my practice that has always governed itself by mood. My trouble is not generating material; it is finishing. I oscillate between sending work out before it is ready and polishing long past the point of diminishing returns, at measurable cost to the next book. The decisions recur constantly: whether a given scene has been revised enough; whether a chapter that is competent but inert should be cut or reworked; whether a sentence's remaining awkwardness is a flaw or simply a texture I have stopped noticing; when to show a manuscript to my editor; and which of her notes to accept. My agent and editor eventually have to sign off on a submission, but I decide when the pages reach them, and I want that decision governed by something sturdier than fatigue or pride.

From what I understand, the literature on satisficing and bounded rationality describes my situation exactly: an agent with finite time and attention, faced with an open-ended search, does not optimize but instead fixes an acceptable threshold and accepts the first alternative that meets it, adjusting the threshold as search proves easy or costly. I want that observation converted into a practicable, reusable procedure for a working novelist. It should be written as a checklist or a flowchart in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure ends with done, where it sends me back for another pass, and where it must stop pretending and hand the decision to a person because the question is an aesthetic one rather than a procedural one. Be explicit about which unit the procedure operates on—sentence, scene, chapter, manuscript—and how those levels connect; about how the acceptance threshold is set, re-set, and protected from my own drift; and about the points where judgment cannot be eliminated. At those points the procedure should force a decision rather than disguise one.

Run the procedure on three cases: (1) a long-standing scene I love that reads smoothly but no longer does real work in the book; (2) a structurally necessary chapter that is competent but flat, and that my editor has not flagged; and (3) a manuscript that by my own written criteria is finished, which I have nevertheless kept rewriting for a month past the deadline I promised my agent. At least one of these should conclude in a way I will dislike. Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do for a novelist, and where following it would systematically mislead me.
