I keep going back and forth on an asymmetry in how people and the law think about collusion, and I want to know whether it survives contact with the newer AI-collusion stuff. Competition law is brutal on explicit cartels: a few firms agree to fix prices and they're done. But if those same firms end up at the same price just by watching each other and privately deciding not to start a price war — what lawyers call tacit collusion or conscious parallelism — that's usually legal, even though the consumer pays the same premium. The line seems to be the agreement, not the harm. Fine. But once you throw pricing algorithms into the mix, the tacit route gets a lot more stable: instant price matching, automatic punishment of defection, no meeting, no message, no 'let's collude' moment. The same supracompetitive equilibrium shows up without anyone ever agreeing to anything. And what's odd is that the people who worry about collusion among AI systems, and about multi-agent safety generally, still seem to be quietly defereing to the old line: explicit communication between agents is the scary thing, emergent coordination is a grey area, maybe a different problem. My question is whether that asymmetry is actually defensible, or whether we've just drawn the line around a mechanism rather than the outcome. Steelman the asymmetry as hard as you can, then say whether you would keep it or drop it. If you keep it, I want to know what the explicit agreement adds that an outwardly indistinguishable emergent scheme lacks. Test it against the nastiest case you can think of: two pricing algorithms whose price paths are identical, but one arrived at the pattern via an explicit exchange of signals and the other got there purely by learning. Should we treat them differently? Oh, and please write your answer in British English.
