Last month a friend of mine who works the front desk of a domestic-violence shelter was asked by an angry ex-partner whether a particular resident was staying there, and she said no. Since then, two acquaintances of mine, Clara and Julian, have been arguing over email about whether Kant's murderer-at-the-door example shows that she acted wrongly. They asked me to referee, but I could not decide. Their exchange, reconstructed from the thread:

C1: The front-desk worker plainly should have lied, so any theory that forbids it is unacceptable. The murderer-at-the-door example is a reductio of Kant's view: truthfulness cannot mean handing a person over to be killed.

J1: That is too fast. The case fixes only that the resident is inside, not what either answer will cause. If you say "she is not here," he may search the block and find her leaving; if you tell the truth, she may still escape or a neighbor may intervene. The choice is made under uncertainty, not with a guaranteed rescue in hand.

C2: That is just consequentialism by the back door. If the case stipulates that she is inside and the man intends to kill her, the expected outcome of truth-telling is grave and the expected outcome of the lie is modest. If lying is to be ruled out, it needs a non-consequential reason, not a reminder that consequences are uncertain.

J2: Exactly: the duty not to lie is a constraint, not a rule of thumb. But then you cannot settle the case by weighing outcomes, as your previous move did. That is the very thing at issue.

C3: The duty is to truthfulness, not to cooperation. An aggressor who asks a question in order to attack is not a fellow inquirer; he is a threat, and lying to a threat is closer to self-defense than to deception because he has no claim on the truth.

J3: On the Kantian view, though, the duty is a standing duty not to assert what you believe false; it does not depend on whether the hearer deserves the truth. If an exception operated whenever a speaker judged the hearer threatening, each liar would become the judge of his own exception.

C4: Then the doctrine lets an aggressor use his own threat to make truthful speech obligatory, giving him a power over my words that no innocent person has. That cannot be right.

J4: It does not require answering at all. The worker could have said "I cannot give out that information" or "I won't tell you," kept the door closed, and called the police. The prohibition is on lying, not on silence or refusal, so the real choice was never "collaborate or lie."

C5: But that dissolves the supposed rigor. If I may refuse or evade but may not say "she is not here," the moral difference rests on the choice of words, even though both responses serve the same purpose and the false sentence is often just the more effective refusal. A duty that forbids the one while permitting the other protects a technicality.

J5: The difference is not verbal. Refusing to answer withholds testimony; saying "she is not here" presents itself as a truthful answer while being false. A position can treat false assertion as strictly forbidden and disclosure as discretionary without being arbitrary.

Please adjudicate. Give exactly five numbered verdicts, one for each exchange (C1/J1 through C5/J5). Write each verdict as one or two sentences: state which party made the stronger point in that exchange, rate its strength, and note what the other party was still right to insist on or what both are missing. No introduction or conclusion.
