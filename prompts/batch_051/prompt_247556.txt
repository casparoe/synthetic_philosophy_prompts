My study group has been arguing about deterministic chaos — the double pendulum, the Lorenz system, the butterfly effect — and one member has produced an argument that seems to show the field's central claim, that deterministic systems can be unpredictable in some respectable sense, is confused. Every step looks innocent, but the conclusion seems too strong, and I cannot find the exit. The argument, as he numbered it:

(1) A system is deterministic just in case its state at any one time, together with the laws of its evolution, fixes its state at every later time.

(2) If a system's later states are fixed by its present state and the laws, then those later states are in principle computable from a complete specification of the present state and the laws.

(3) If a later state is in principle computable from a complete specification of the state and the laws, then it can be predicted with any desired accuracy, for any length of time, from that specification.

(4) The standard examples of deterministic chaos — say, the Lorenz system, a double pendulum, or the classical three-body problem — satisfy (1).

(5) Therefore those systems can, in principle, be predicted with any desired accuracy for any length of time.

(6) So the unpredictability chaos theory discusses is not a property of those systems; it is only a practical limitation of our measuring instruments and computers, and it would vanish for a sufficiently well-resourced predictor.

(7) Therefore chaos theory faces a dilemma: either its examples are deterministic, in which case they are predictable in principle and "chaos" names only our current practical ignorance, or they are genuinely unpredictable, in which case they are not deterministic and the theory's standard examples were misdescribed.

He says this shows you cannot keep both determinism and the claim that chaos is a real limitation on prediction.

Where, if anywhere, does this argument go wrong? I would most like to keep (1), (4), and the idea that something about chaos remains even for an arbitrarily careful and well-resourced finite predictor — that the unpredictability of a strange attractor is not just the same thing as being short on data. But I may have to give some of that up. Tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked correct, and what rejecting it costs for our understanding of the butterfly effect, Lyapunov exponents, and the distinction between a system's being determined and its being predictable. If the right move is to distinguish senses of "complete specification," "in principle," or "prediction," draw those distinctions and say exactly what remains of chaos theory once they are drawn.
