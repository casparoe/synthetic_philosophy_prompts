My uncle died last month, and I have been asked to give the eulogy. He was, until about eighteen months ago, a widely admired newspaper columnist who made his name denouncing corruption among local officials; privately he was exacting about integrity, generous to his family, and the person who first taught me to take arguments seriously. Eighteen months ago a leak showed that during his one run for office in the late 1990s he had quietly accepted illegal campaign contributions, and that he had then spent decades writing that taking such money was exactly the kind of act that should end a politician's career. He resigned, made a single brief public apology, and spent his last months mostly silent. The funeral will bring together family members who do not want the scandal mentioned, former colleagues and readers who admired him, journalists who covered the story, and at least two politicians whose careers he helped end on precisely the grounds he himself turned out to have violated.

I want a eulogy that comforts without lying. I have spent enough time reading the philosophical back-and-forth on blame to think a real distinction matters here: whether a criticism is true is one question, and whether the critic had standing to level the rebuke is another. His hypocrisy did not make corruption retroactively acceptable, and I do not want the speech to treat it that way; but I also do not want that distinction used as a polite way of laundering what he did or changing the subject from it. I would like the problem of standing to blame to do structural work in the speech: it should shape which things can be said truthfully about him, about the people he condemned, and about what the mourners are doing when they praise him. Please write the eulogy so that it lands as honesty rather than as a lecture or a tribunal, and so it can be delivered in about six minutes.
