I've been running a little philosophy interview series for undergrads, and this month's guest is a philosopher who defends a strict resultant-luck-skeptic line about blame and punishment. His view is that if two people act with the same intentions and the same degree of recklessness, but only one of them happens to cause a death — one drunk driver hits a child, the other swerves through an empty intersection — the two deserve exactly the same amount of blame, and in his view the same punishment, with any legal difference chalked up to evidence-gathering and deterrence rather than desert. I'm worried my own questions will just keep receiving the same reply: that all asymmetry is hindsight bias or legal pragmatics. Draft eight to ten interview questions that actually preassure the view from different directions. Include at least one question on each of the following, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give: (1) whether the victim or the victim's family can reasonably resent the driver who hit them more than they could resent the lucky one, and if not, what resentment is supposed to track; (2) whether the agent who realizes he has killed someone should feel more retrospective guilt than the one who got lucky, and whether our first-person asymmetry here can be dismissed as a simple mistake; (3) how far the view extends to constitutive and circumstantial luck — whether someone raised to be callous is just as blameworthy as someone who fought the same upbringing, and if he bites that bullet, what blame is left to mean. Maybe close with a question about what case or evidence would actually move him, since he tends to diagnose every counterexample as the same confuson.
