I've been trying to work out what a commitment to monogamy actually binds people to, and whether breaking it is a moral wrong or just going back on an arrangement the couple happened to adopt. Here are my gut verdicts on some cases, and I can't tell whether they hang together. A couple agrees before a year apart that each may sleep with someone else once; one does. I think that's fine. A married man kisses a friend at a New Year's party with no such agreement; I think that's wrong. A couple is sexually exclusive in practice, but one partner carries on a hidden months-long exchange with a former partner — long nightly messages, confidences about the marriage, declarations of love — never meeting and nothing physical. I think that's wrong, maybe as wrong as the kiss. A man still loves his ex and knows he doesn't feel for his wife what he felt for the ex, but has never said or done anything about it since marrying. I don't think he's wronged his wife. An otherwise exclusive partner does webcam sex work for income without mentioning it, insisting that it's just a job and that no one touches her. I think that's wrong. After an illness leaves one spouse unable to have sex, the wife tells her husband to go elsewhere; he does, and she's privately devastated. I can't call that wrongdoing, since she told him to. The same arrangement as the first case, but one partner agrees only after weeks of pleading and threats about leaving. I think that agreement wasn't real, and the sex was wrong. Is there one principle — about what exclusivity actually protects — that captures these verdicts? And does it cast the wrongs as genuinely moral, or just as breaches of whatever terms the couple set? If no single principle captures all of them, which verdict should I give up, and what would the principle say about a couple who adopt a "don't ask, don't tell" rule for outside partners, where keeping it secret from each other is part of the arrangement itself?
