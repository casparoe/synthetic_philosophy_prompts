Write a Petrarchan sonnet (14 lines; octave ABBAABBA; sestet in CDECDE, CDCDCD, or another defensible variation) about the two-factor theory of delusions, using Capgras delusion as your concrete case. In Capgras, a person recognizes a close relative's face but the usual autonomic and affective response is absent, so the familiar person feels unfamiliar, and the person concludes that the relative has been replaced by an impostor or a duplicate.

Use the octave to stage the experience from the inside: the face that is recognized and yet does not feel like the one it resembles, the world handing the person a contradiction that demands an explanation. Build the octave so that a reader would naturally take the delusion to be a perceptual illusion, with the anomalous experience doing all the work on its own.

Make the volta, at the turn from octave to sestet, the two-factor move. The anomalous experience is only factor one; by itself it settles nothing. Factor two is the failure of the belief-evaluation machinery that should have tested and rejected the impostor explanation before it took hold. Let the sestet work the resulting puzzle about whether the person really believes the impostor claim: it can be sincerely asserted while leaving much else in the person's life and behavior untouched, which puts pressure on what counts as belief here. Let the closing couplet say something precise about which factor carries the philosophical weight, or what the two-step structure changes about how we describe the person, without using jargon.

The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mind or cognitive neuropsychiatrist working on delusions would recognize the two-factor view and the Capgras example and find the poem true to them, while a reader who has never heard of either would still find it a good poem. Translate factor one, factor two, and the belief question into imagery, syntax, and the turn itself rather than naming them in the lines.

After the poem, give one section titled Commentary on the choices made (350–450 words). In it, explain where the volta falls and why; map the octave to factor one, the turn to factor two, and the sestet to the debate over whether delusions are beliefs; and flag any interpretive liberties, simplifications, or places where the poem cannot carry the full theory and an instructor would need to supply prose.
