Two moral philosophers are engaged in a good-faith adversarial collaboration on a single question: is novelty — variety, unfamiliarity, and the active pursuit of the new — a constituent of human well-being, or only an instrument for producing it? One party, the constitutivist, holds that novelty is itself an element of a good life: a life can be made better by containing variety even when that variety does not increase any other good, and a life can be made worse by the absence of novelty even when that absence does not reduce the other goods it contains. The other party, the instrumentalist, holds that novelty contributes to well-being only insofar as it sustains or delivers other goods — pleasure, achievement, meaning, desire-satisfaction, the avoidance of the distress of boredom — and that a repetitive life containing exactly the same total amount of those other goods would be exactly as good as a varied one.

Write the joint statement that these two philosophers could both sign after the collaboration. The statement should have the following structure.

(1) Agreed claims. A numbered list of substantive, non-vacuous claims about novelty and well-being that both parties accept — including claims about hedonic adaptation, the common preference for and pursuit of variety, the fear of a boring life, and the observations and distinctions the disagreement is not about. These claims should be neither empty nor question-begging.

(2) Disagreements. A table or numbered section recording each residual disagreement in exact form, with the constitutivist's position stated in terms the constitutivist would accept as accurate and fair, and the instrumentalist's position stated in terms the instrumentalist would accept as accurate and fair. Where a disagreement is merely verbal — for example, one about the extension of the words "novel" or "boring" — it should be identified as verbal and set aside.

(3) Cruxes. A section identifying the sub-questions an answer to which would move at least one party. For each crux, present: the sub-question itself; the concrete case, thought experiment, or observation that both parties agree bears on it; and each party's prediction about what that case or observation shows, along with why the other party is not persuaded by that prediction.

The crux section should address at least the following:

(a) Two possible lives identical in their total amount of every other candidate good — pleasure, achievement, meaning, desire-satisfaction — differing only in that one life packages those goods into a small number of frequently repeated forms while the other delivers them through many changing forms. Is the varied life the better life, or does the description of the case already smuggle in an assumption about whether packaging is a separate good?

(b) The hedonic treadmill. Both parties accept that repeated pleasant experiences tend to produce less felt benefit over time. Does that adaptation show that novelty is itself a constituent of welfare, or only that novelty is needed to keep the production of other goods from declining?

(c) An informed and experienced person who, without self-deception or subsequent regret, prefers an invariant routine. Is such a life missing a good the person has merely ceased to want, or does the settled preference settle the matter?

(d) An experience machine with a fixed, finite menu of scenarios. If the menu is enlarged while every other good — including felt quality — is held exactly equal, does the life inside the machine become better?

For each crux, do not adjudicate: the task is to state what each party predicts and to make clear what result would count against each position. The document should proceed by argument and case judgment, not by citation or authority, and should not require information beyond what is described here.
