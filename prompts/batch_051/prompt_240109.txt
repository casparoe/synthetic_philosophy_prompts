Something I've been chewing on: the classic argument against restricting campaign spending is that spending on political speech is itself speech, and the right response to bad or misleading speech isn't to suppress it but to answer it with more speech. That argument seems to assume the speech in question circulates in some shared public arena, so opponents, journalists, and voters can actually see it and push back. But a huge share of modern campaign money doesn't buy public ads anymore; it buys microtargeted, A/B-tested messages delivered privately to small slices of voters — sometimes with different promises made to different groups that no one else ever sees, because disclosure lags and the ads are basically unobservable to outsiders. Does the more-speech argument survive that shift? Or does it stop supporting unrestricted spending and start supporting something like mandatory disclosure or ad transparency instead? I'm asking about the structure of the argument, not the policy: which premise exactly does microtargeting break, can it be repaired, or is the argument just dead in that environment?
