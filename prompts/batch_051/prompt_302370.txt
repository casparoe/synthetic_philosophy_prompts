You are a professor of medieval Jewish philosophy with expertise in Maimonides. I have been working through the Guide of the Perplexed and have reconstructed the following argument about animal sacrifice. Please think through it carefully, beginning by identifying the strongest charitable reading of each premise and marking any hidden equivocations or suppressed premises before evaluating the argument.

(1) Maimonides holds that the commandments of the Torah are not arbitrary: each commanded practice has an intelligible reason.
(2) Maimonides holds that animal sacrifice is one of the commandments of the Torah.
(3) Therefore Maimonides must assign an intelligible reason to the practice of animal sacrifice.
(4) Maimonides assigns a historical-pedagogical reason: the ancient Israelites could not have abruptly abandoned the sacrificial worship prevalent around them, so God accommodated their condition by redirecting sacrifice away from idols and toward the worship of God.
(5) A historical-pedagogical reason of that sort explains why a practice was introduced for a particular people at a particular time, but it does not explain why the practice is intrinsically fitting or why it should remain binding once the historical condition disappears.
(6) Therefore Maimonides is under pressure to say either that animal sacrifice was only provisionally justified, which sits uneasily with the standard Maimonidean commitment to the Torah's abiding authority, or that the historical-pedagogical reason is incomplete and some further reason must be supplied.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails as stated? If it is valid, are all the premises true to Maimonides' view, or does he have a way of denying one of them? Finally, give the strongest response a defender of Maimonides could make.
