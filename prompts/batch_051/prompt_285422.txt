I am working out what principle governs my own choices between monetary gambles, and I would like you to test the pattern against the following recorded verdicts. Treat these as data about me rather than as claims about what is rational.

First pair.

Choice 1A: £1,000,000 for certain.

Choice 1B: an 89% chance of £1,000,000, a 10% chance of £5,000,000, and a 1% chance of nothing.

I choose 1A.

Choice 1C: an 11% chance of £1,000,000 and an 89% chance of nothing.

Choice 1D: a 10% chance of £5,000,000 and a 90% chance of nothing.

I choose 1D.

Second pair.

Choice 2A: £4,000 for certain.

Choice 2B: an 80% chance of £5,000 and a 20% chance of nothing.

I choose 2A.

Choice 2C: a 25% chance of £4,000 and a 75% chance of nothing.

Choice 2D: a 20% chance of £5,000 and an 80% chance of nothing.

I choose 2D.

Third pair, in which both options are risky.

Choice 3A: an 80% chance of £3,000 and a 20% chance of nothing.

Choice 3B: a 48% chance of £5,000 and a 52% chance of nothing.

I choose 3A.

Choice 3C: a 20% chance of £3,000 and an 80% chance of nothing.

Choice 3D: a 12% chance of £5,000 and an 88% chance of nothing.

I choose 3D.

Fourth pair, involving a mixture with a common branch.

Between a 50% chance of £100 and a 25% chance of £200, with nothing otherwise in both cases, I choose the former. Now suppose a fair coin is tossed first: if heads, I receive £50 outright; if tails, I play whichever of the two gambles I chose. The comparison is then between an option offering a 50% chance of £50, a 25% chance of £100, and a 25% chance of nothing, and another offering a 50% chance of £50, a 12.5% chance of £200, and a 37.5% chance of nothing. I choose the first, and the thought of switching merely because the common £50 branch was added seems absurd to me.

My first attempt at a principle was that certainty is doing all the work: in the first and second pairs I choose the safe option when one option removes all risk, and the riskier option when both are already risky. The third pair seems to refute that, since neither option there is certain and yet the same shape of reversal appears. My second attempt is that I weight probabilities nonlinearly, overweighting small probabilities and underweighting moderate-to-large ones; this seems to fit the first three pairs. But the fourth pair looks like the independence axiom holding exactly, and I do not see how a nonlinear weighting of probabilities could respect that.

Please take these verdicts as fixed unless you can show good reason to overturn one of them. Give me the best principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that it would yield a definite verdict on a new pair. For each clause or feature of the principle, say which of my cases motivates it. Then apply the principle to the following unsettled cases, and show the working.

Case E: a 40% chance of £3,000 versus a 24% chance of £5,000, with nothing otherwise in both cases; both have an expected monetary value of £1,200. Which side does the principle place me on, and does it give a principled crossover point between the two choice patterns in the third pair, or merely a fitted threshold?

Case F: take the first two options from the third pair and mix each with a fair coin that pays £50 on heads. The comparison becomes a 50% chance of £50, a 40% chance of £3,000, and a 10% chance of nothing, versus a 50% chance of £50, a 24% chance of £5,000, and a 26% chance of nothing. Does the principle preserve my preference for the first of these, as my fourth pair suggests it should?

Finally, tell me honestly which of my recorded verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate without further complications, and which verdict, or how much simplicity, I would have to give up.
