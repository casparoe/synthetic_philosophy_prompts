Exam question (20 points): "Moral considerability is all-or-nothing: either a being's interests count, or they don't." Give the strongest case against this claim and in favor of the view that considerability comes in degrees. Use at least one non-human example of your own choosing. Then raise what you take to be the most serious objection to the degree view, and answer it.

Student answer:

Degrees of considerability fall right out of the capacities that make a being matter. Nociception, working memory, behavioral flexibility — none of these are binary, and in the animal kingdom they mostly increase together: a rat has more going on than a bee, a bee more than a flatworm, and so on. Since these capacities determine how much a being can be benefited or harmed, how much the being matters should track that same gradient. The degree view doesn't force us to say absolutely everything matters. A thermostat detects temperature error and throws a switch, but that's a purely functional relation to a setpoint, not a felt one; with no affect there's no welfare, so it sits at zero on the scale. That shows the view can still draw a lower boundary.

The most serious objection is that a continuous scale has no natural joints, so any cutoff for protection will look arbitrary. But that's weak. Lots of usable concepts mark thresholds on continuous quantities — water doesn't turn to vapor gradually in the pot, it boils. Moral cutoffs can likewise be partly conventional without being meaningless; we can decide that creatures below some level get only limited protection while still being on the scale.

And once you accept degrees, you should accept proportional weighting. If a being has one thousandth of the welfare-relevant capacity of a human, then its interests should count one thousandth as much. That's the real payoff: a million such beings would exactly balance one human, which is counterintuitive but consistent in a way the all-or-nothing view can't be.
