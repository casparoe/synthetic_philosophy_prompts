I’ve spent the past several years reading a forum whose regulars argue about decision theory, akrasia, time-inconsistent preferences, and the kinds of self-binding contracts people actually sign with anti-charity escrows. One commenter, K, has left a trail of one-line verdicts on cases about precommitment and rational irrationality, and I’ve kept a file of them. Here are the recorded judgments, with the cases stated as I understand them; for this exercise, work from my descriptions rather than any canonical text you remember.

1. Parfit’s hitchhiker. A hitchhiker stranded in a desert can get a ride to safety only by promising to pay the driver once they reach town. Both parties know that, once safely in town, a purely self-interested hitchhiker will have no reason to pay. In the version K discussed, the hitchhiker has access to a reliable precommitment device: an irreversible escrow arrangement that will pay the driver and collect from the hitchhiker with legal force on arrival. K’s verdict: take the device. Refusing it because one’s later self would rather not pay is a mistake. If no device is available, the driver is right not to trust the promise, and the hitchhiker cannot honestly give it.

2. Kavka’s toxin. A billionaire will pay you tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink a harmless but very unpleasant toxin tomorrow afternoon. The money arrives before the time of drinking, and you will not need to drink to keep it. K’s verdict: a normal agent cannot simply resolve to have the intention, because she knows she will not drink once the money is already in her account. But if the agent first takes a pill tonight that will force her to drink tomorrow, the intention becomes possible, and taking the pill is rational exactly when the payment is worth more to her than the anticipated misery.

3. Ulysses and the sirens. Ulysses orders his crew to tie him to the mast and to ignore his later orders to be released while the sirens are audible. K’s verdict: binding yourself in advance against an anticipated preference reversal is rational, and the fact that the future self inside the episode wants to be released carries no authority.

4. A gambler enrolls in an irrevocable five-year casino self-exclusion. During a craving, his future self will want the exclusion lifted. K’s verdict: enrolling is rational, and the future craving self’s objection has no weight.

I can see several principles that fit these four verdicts, and I cannot tell which one K is actually running. That is what I want you to sort out for me by predicting a new case.

The new case. Lena is 30 and is currently a committed supporter of a particular cause. She has been offered a legally binding, irrevocable 30-year contract that will require her to give 20% of her pre-tax income to an organization working on that cause, with exit terms so punitive that attempting to break the contract would be materially ruinous. The contract is administered by an independent third party and would be enforced just as a court would enforce a settlement. Lena is not worried about weakness of will with respect to her current values. She is worried that her values themselves will change: she expects that by her forties she will be more focused on family, more risk-averse, and much less moved by the abstract considerations that now drive her giving, and she wants to bind her future self against those expected changes in evaluative outlook. She acknowledges that, from the standpoint of that later self, the contract will feel like an alien and coercive force producing a steady stream of losses.

What would K tell Lena? I am less interested in the one-word answer than in the rule that produces it, the other rules that fit the four recorded verdicts just as well, and an honest statement of whether those four cases genuinely constrain the prediction.

I would like the response as a single JSON object with the following schema:

- “candidate_rules”: an array of two or more objects, each with:
  - “label”: a short name for the rule (for example, “ex ante expected utility”).
  - “statement”: a precise one-paragraph statement of the rule.
  - “prediction_for_lena”: the verdict this rule yields on Lena’s contract, and why.
  - “fit_notes”: how well the rule fits each of the four recorded verdicts, including any place it has to strain.

- “best_guess”: an object with:
  - “prediction”: one of “approves the contract as rational”, “rejects it as rational”, “approves only with amendments”, or “underdetermined by the four cases”.
  - “confidence”: a number from 0 to 1.
  - “closest_analog”: which of the four earlier cases is the nearest precedent and why.
  - “reasoning”: a paragraph explaining the best guess and whether the four recorded verdicts truly decide Lena’s case.

- “further_discriminating_case”: an object with:
  - “description”: a new thought experiment of your own, stated in a few sentences, designed to tell the candidate rules apart.
  - “what_it_tests”: which candidate rules it separates and how.
  - “expected_verdicts”: an array of objects mapping each candidate rule’s label to the verdict that rule would give on the further case.
