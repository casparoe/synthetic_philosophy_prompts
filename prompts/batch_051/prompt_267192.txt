As a professional philosopher of mind with a background in cognitive science, work through this carefully; before committing to an answer, brainstorm the main candidate principles and stress-test them against nearby cases.

Ordinary judgment is asymmetric in the following way. We unhesitatingly ascribe minds — perceptions, goals, memories, even something like deliberation — to individual animals with tiny brains, such as a single ant. Yet we do not ascribe a mind to an ant colony, even though the colony as a whole tracks environmental changes, remembers the locations of nest sites and food, makes collective foraging and emigration decisions, and builds large, regulative structures. Is there a principled distinction that justifies treating the individual as a minded system and the colony as mindless, or is the asymmetry merely a prejudice in favor of nervous systems and familiar bodies?

Consider the strongest candidate rationales — unified consciousness, the possession of a single central nervous system, representational capacity, functional organization, evolutionary individuality — and test each against hard cases such as split-brain patients, animals with decentralized nerve nets, swarms, and human institutions. Then give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, abandoned, or replaced with a graded or functional notion of mind. Throughout, separate what is reasonably settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers and scientists, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
