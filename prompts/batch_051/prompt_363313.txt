i've been lurking on the neuro-rights corner of rationalist-adjacent twitter for a while and people keep using "mental privacy" and "cognitive liberty" as if they're two seperate columns on a checklist, but nobody ever shows the joint. i want the distinction stress-tested before i keep it in my own framework. so: build me a matched pair of near-future thought experiments, one paragraph each, different mechanisms (not the same case with one dial flipped). first, a case where mental privacy is violated but cognitive liberty is untouched. second, a case where cognitive liberty is violated but mental privacy is untouched. constraints: no decoded inner speech, no reading propositional thoughts off an fMRI, nothing further out than a few years—consumer EEG, workplace neural monitoring, closed-loop implants, remote firmware updates, mood inference from wearables, that sort of thing. in the first case the harm has to be exhausted by unwanted epistemic access: no intervention flows back and changes the person. in the second, nobody ever learns anything about the person's current mental states; the device can sense locally if it has to operate, but no measurement becomes available to any person or algorithm outside the loop. after the two stories, do the check for each one: whose mental state is at issue, whether it's being accessed or altered, by whom or what, and why mental privacy and cognitive liberty give the verdicts they do. then tell me which single feature of the pair is doing the work—my prior is that it's the direction of the interface, read versus write, outbound information versus inbound causal interference, but make me earn that or talk me out of it. then: what is the cheapest change to each case that collapses the distinction, and how would someone who thinks these are really one right (or that one reduces to the other) attack your cases? finally, say what would change your mind—which empirical finding about neurotech, or which argument about the nature of privacy, would make you conclude the distinction isn't load-bearing—and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
