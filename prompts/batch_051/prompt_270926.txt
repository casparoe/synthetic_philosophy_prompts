A colleague of mine argues that vagueness is not a matter of semantic indecision but of irremediable ignorance: for every predicate such as "is a heap" or "is red" there exists a precise, context-independent boundary between its positive and negative cases, and our inability to identify it is an epistemic limitation, not the absence of a boundary. I suspect this position is observationally indistinguishable from the rival view that such predicates simply have no sharp boundary in any mind-independent sense. Is there any empirical procedure that could get traction on the difference? Construct the strongest test you can. For example, you might have participants perform forced-choice classifications along a continuous series differing by one grain or one nanometre of wavelength, under monetary incentives for consistency and carefully manipulated anchors, or you might probe whether judgments aggregate around a stable inflection point that resists changes in framing. Tell me exactly what results would support the unknowable-sharp-boundary reading, what results would support no-boundary semantic indecision, and then explain what a positive result would still leave untouched. Can the two theories come apart in principle, or does every operationalization of "there is a boundary" collapse into an artifact of the measurement method? Be candid about where the residue lies.
