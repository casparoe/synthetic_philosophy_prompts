Suppose a theorist claims that satisficing is a distinct kind of practical rationality, and not simply maximizing expected utility with search and deliberation costs folded in. The difficulty is that the two hypotheses are often behaviorally interchangeable: a policy of taking the first option that clears an aspiration level can be redescribed as an optimal stopping rule once a suitable cost function or preference for quick closure is added, while an apparently optimal reservation price can be redescribed as an aspiration level set by the local economics of search. Suppose you set out to test whether there is a real difference between the two, rather than a difference of bookkeeping. Propose a concrete decision task, identify the variables you would manipulate and the behavioral measures you would record, and specify the exact contrast that would separate a genuinely distinct satisficing process from a cost-augmented maximizing process. Say what result pattern would favor each reading, and which result pattern would be uninformative. Then state, as precisely as you can, what even a clean positive result would not settle: whether the agent literally uses an aspiration level or merely behaves in the way a satisficing model would describe, whether any finite body of choice data can rule out a sufficiently elastic account of decision costs, and whether establishing that agents satisfice in fact would show anything about whether satisficing is the normatively appropriate standard for a bounded rational agent.
