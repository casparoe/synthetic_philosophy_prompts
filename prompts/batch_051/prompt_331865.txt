I've been trying to understand algorithmic approaches to induction, and I recently read a popular long-form article that attempted to explain Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity to a general audience. The author summarizes the field roughly as follows: "Solomonoff solved the problem of induction by assigning each hypothesis a probability equal to 2 raised to the power of minus the length of the shortest computer program that generates the observed data. This vindicates Occam's razor in one stroke: simpler hypotheses receive exponentially more weight, the shortest adequate program is thereby the best explanation of the data, and any rational agent is compelled to prefer it. Because the method effectively sums over all possible programs, each weighted by its simplicity, the resulting probabilities are objective and uniquely determined; two agents fed the same data must eventually arrive at the same posterior probabilities. The only remaining difficulty, the article says, is a practical one, namely that finding the shortest program requires more computation than current hardware can manage. Kolmogorov complexity is, in the end, the true measure of simplicity, and Occam's razor has been upgraded from a vague heuristic to a theorem."

I have a nagging feeling that this is a mixture of accurate claims, compressed simplifications, and overstatements, but I don't know enough to sort them out. Could you audit the summary piece by piece and tell me what a computer scientist or formal epistemologist working on algorithmic information theory would accept, what they would qualify heavily, and what they would reject? I am especially interested in whether the final verdicts—that induction has been solved, that Kolmogorov complexity is the uniquely correct measure of simplicity, and that Occam's razor is now a theorem—actually follow from the parts of the account that are correct.
