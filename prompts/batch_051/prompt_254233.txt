I'm taking a seminar on causation and final causes because I kept hearing people say Darwin killed teleology, and I wanted to actually read the primary material instead of just nodding along. I'm stuck on this passage from Aristotle's Physics, Book II, Part 1 (Hardie and Gaye translation), where he's distinguishing natural things from artifacts:

“Each of them has within itself a principle of motion and of stationariness (in respect of place, or of growth and decrease, or by way of alteration).”

The way I understand him, this is part of the setup for his later claim (around II.8) that natural things act for ends: an acorn grows into an oak because the source of that directed change is inside the acorn itself. But here's my problem. Modern biology seems to have relocated the directedness. Individual organisms don't really have a built-in principle of motion in Aristotle's strong sense; what looks directed arises from random variation plus selection over a population across generations. So the source of the regular outcome is external to the individual and distributed across the lineage, not internal to it. Does Aristotle's teleological argument survive this Darwinian change in where the directedness is supposed to live, or did it depend essentially on the principle being internal? Which premise in the quoted passage is actually doing the work, and is there a repaired version that keeps final causation without committing to an internal principle in each organism?
