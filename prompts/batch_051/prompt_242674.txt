I'm the compliance lead at a mid-sized development NGO that runs health and agriculture projects in a handful of countries, and I've been told to replace our anti-corruption policy because the current one doesn't survive contact with the field. The old rule basically says no employee may give anything of value to any public official, which sounds clean in an audit but in practice means our staff either underreport what they actually do or can't get permits and permissions at all. We took on a new donor last year with strict anti-bribery conditions tied to the funding, so now there's real legal exposure on top of the ethics. I want a reusable decision procedure, not another policy statement: a checklist a field manager can actually run in ten minutes when something comes up, for deciding whether a gift, cash payment, or favor to a local official, customary chief, or community leader is the kind of thing we must refuse and report, or part of ordinary respectful work. The people using it are project leads and country managers, not lawyers or philosophers, and some version of this comes up every few weeks. If the checklist says "escalate," they call the country director or me; refusal decisions get signed off by me and documented for the donor. The procedure should be grounded in the best thinking about what actually makes a transaction corrupt, not just "it's illegal here," because we work in places where the host-country law is thin and the donor standard is what we get audited against. I specifically want it to handle the murky territory where bribery shades into clientelism or patronage — local elites distributing access and favors through personal networks — and not just the obvious envelope of cash.

Then run the procedure on three cases we've actually argued about. (a) A district official tells our team the permit for a clinic won't be processed unless we pay an unreciepted cash "processing fee"; the permit is otherwise valid and ready, and we know that without the cash it will stall for months. (b) Our field manager is expected to put an envelope of cash in the collection at the mayor's father's funeral, as everyone does at funerals in the area, and not giving would be read as a serious insult and damage the relationship our projects depend on. (c) We're considering hiring the district chief's son as a community liaison, partly because he's genuinely qualified and partly because employing him would keep his father from obstructing our other projects. For each case I want the procedure's answer, including at least one where the answer is going to make someone angry, and a short note on what the procedure can't settle and where it's quietly taking the Western side.
