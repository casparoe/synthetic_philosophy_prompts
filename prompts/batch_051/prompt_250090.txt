Over the past several months, while reporting a feature on why the familiar claim that a table exists turns out to be so difficult to state precisely, I have found myself reluctantly convinced by mereological universalism: the thesis that for any plurality of things whatever, there is a further thing that they compose. The view is usually introduced as a curiosity — it entails that there is an object composed of the Eiffel Tower, the tip of my pen, and the third moon of Jupiter — but I take the serious case for it to be this. The special composition question asks when a plurality of things composes a further thing, and any answer short of 'always' has to draw a line between the pluralities that compose and those that do not. Every candidate line looks arbitrary, vague, or answerable only by appeal to our own interests. Universalism alone avoids that embarrassment, at the cost of populating the world with far more objects than common sense recognises.

I do not mean the familiar complaints — that the view fills the universe with gerrymandered objects, such as the thing composed of a trout and a turkey, or that it is ontologically profligate. I have heard those and do not find them decisive. I mean the deeper liabilities: which intuitions, possible cases, and results elsewhere in metaphysics — about causation, persistence through time, vagueness, the identity of composites, or the semantics of ordinary count nouns — would force a defender of universalism to revise or abandon the view? Which of these would a committed universalist concede as genuinely counting against the position, and which would they dismiss as bullet-biting?

I should like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, together with an honest judgement as to whether the view has been insulated against counter-evidence, and whether such insulation is, for a global answer to a constitutive question, a defect or simply what one should expect.
