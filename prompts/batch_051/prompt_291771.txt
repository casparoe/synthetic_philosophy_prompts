The usual moral people draw is that you can't make a rule of inference like modus ponens into an extra premise, because the tortoise will just demand another rule to get from the old premises plus the new premise to the conclusion, and so on forever; so rules of inference must be something non-propositional—licenses or permissions to infer rather than further facts believed. My argument is that this is right about the letter of the example but wrong about whats stopping the regress. I think you can rerun the tortoise on the non-propositional picture itself: let the tortoise accept modus ponens as a rule, but then ask what licenses the application of the rule to these particular premises. Any answer—another rule, a standing disposition, a "we just do"—either raises the same question one level up or refuses the question instead of answering it. So the premise/rule distinction is not doing the justificatory work; what blocks the regress is something about inference being a practice that can only be questioned from inside itself. Which means the quietists are right for the wrong reason, and the premise/rule people are wrong about what ther own example shows. The audience is a general department colloquium but tilted toward philosophy of logic and language: a few people who think the tortoise just draws a clean line between premises and rules and nothing deeper, two or three Wittgensteinians who think the whole regress is a bewitchment by language and dissolves once you describe the practice correctly, and one formal epistemologist who will insist the problem is an artifact of refusing to formalize the tortoise's acceptance with a validity predicate.
