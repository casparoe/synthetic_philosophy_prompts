I've spent the last few weeks down a continuity-of-government rabbit hole: designated survivors, presidential and ministerial lines of succession, emergency relocation sites, the question of whether a survivable communications backbone actually gives anyone command authority, and the weird corner cases where three officials each plausibly think they're the legitimate acting head of government. I'm not a lawyer or a public admin person; my contribution is conceptual, and I keep noticing that the questions that feel profound at first are often not the ones that would change a plan if you answered them. I also suspect the fact that an adversary can read your continuity plan before choosing a target does more work than most discussions let on.

Here's the actual ask. I've got about a year, probably me plus one collaborator, to produce a short memo for a legislative committee that's rewriting continuity rules for a medium-sized democracy (think Australia or Canada, not the US specifically). We can't do statute-reading or war-gaming; we can do careful conceptual triage. I've accumulated more candidate questions than we can handle: whether the designated survivor is a caretaker with intentionally hobbled legitimacy or a full president-in-waiting; whether the survivor's identity should be public and how you trade accountability against making them a target; whether protecting a handful of officials is really preserving the state or just preserving an unaccountable executive; whether decapitation-strike planning raises different ethical issues than pandemic or natural-disaster planning; what happens to popular sovereignty when the continuity plan installs someone almost no one voted for; and whether the plan should even try to restore the old constitutional order after the institutions that made it legitimate are gone. Which three or four of these—plus any you think I'm missing—should we actually prioritize? Rank them and defend the ordering. I care less about the list than the reasoning: make explicit which questions are upstream of the others, how tractable each is for someone doing philosophy rather than logistics or law, and which answers would really change what a government should build, rehearse, or keep secret. Also tell me which parts of this debate are overrated, where people are mostly arguing about Hollywood scenarios or pattern-matching to civics.

After the prose, give me a table with columns: rank, question, why it matters, tractability (high/medium/low), neglectedness (high/medium/low), and what would change the ranking. Cap it at six rows. If you think the whole framing is solving the wrong problem, say that before the table, but still give me the table for the best version of the project.
