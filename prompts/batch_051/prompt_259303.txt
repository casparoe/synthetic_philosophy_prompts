I work in a materials-science group that simulates alloy phase behavior with atomistic Monte Carlo and molecular dynamics, and a dispute over one sentence in a grant report has forced me to think hard about reduction. We wrote that our computed phase diagram follows from the interatomic potential. A postdoc in the group circulated an argument whose conclusion is that no higher-level theory anywhere in the physical sciences reduces to a lower-level one, including the cases everyone treats as paradigmatic. I think the argument proves too much, which means some step in it must be wrong, but I cannot find the bad step myself. His argument, reconstructed as carefully as I can, runs as follows.

(1) A higher-level theory reduces to a lower-level theory only if the laws of the higher-level theory are derivable from the laws of the lower-level theory together with bridge principles linking their vocabularies. This is the standard deductive conception of reduction.

(2) In every case where such derivability appears to succeed — thermodynamics into statistical mechanics, continuum fluid mechanics into molecular dynamics, chemistry into quantum mechanics — the derivation only goes through after one takes a limiting operation: the thermodynamic limit (particle number and volume sent to infinity with their ratio held fixed), the continuum limit (lattice spacing sent to zero), or the classical limit (Planck's constant sent to zero).

(3) These limiting operations change the mathematics rather than merely simplifying it. The finite-particle equations are smooth in temperature and particle number, and the underlying microdynamics is invariant under time reversal. The limiting theory, by contrast, delivers exact thermodynamic singularities — a discontinuous magnetization, a diverging susceptibility — and irreversible macrodynamics, none of which the pre-limit theory can deliver.

(4) Therefore what comes out after the limit is not a logical consequence of the pre-limit theory. A theorem cannot have features that the premises rule out or cannot formulate; in particular, no finite-particle theory has among its consequences exact singularities that exist only in the infinite-particle limit.

(5) Consequently, in each candidate reduction the higher-level laws are generated by an idealizing limiting operation, and the choice of that operation — which limit, and why that one — is underwritten by an independent modeling decision rather than by the lower-level theory alone.

(6) It follows that no candidate reduction satisfies the derivability requirement in (1). Hence no higher-level theory reduces to a lower-level theory, in what the postdoc calls the only precise sense of reduction we have.

(7) But this strips reduction of its paradigm cases: the recovery of the ideal gas law from molecular mechanics, the calculation of a phase diagram from a Hamiltonian, and the derivation of electrical conductivity from electronic band theory. If these are not reductions, then the word reduction does no work in science, and our grant report's claim was empty.

I would like a point-by-point diagnosis rather than a verdict. Please say which numbered step is the first to fail, or, if the argument is valid, say that explicitly and identify which premise is the least costly to give up. For each step that can be resisted, explain why it looked plausible in the first place and what a reductionist should say in response. Then price the alternatives: if the correct repair is to weaken (1), say exactly what that weakening costs the standard deductive picture of reduction; if the correct repair is to reject (3) or (4), say what that costs for our understanding of phase transitions and irreversibility. I would most like to keep (1) in some weakened form and to avoid accepting (7), but I want to know what I am paying for that.
