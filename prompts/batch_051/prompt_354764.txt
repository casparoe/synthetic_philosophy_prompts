I have been arguing about whether brain size is morally relevant, and the argument keeps collapsing because we cannot agree on what would even show that a feature like brain size matters morally. I want a working definition of "moral status" (or "moral considerability," if you think that is the better term) that gets the following cases right, and that leaves it open rather than settling it by stipulation whether nervous-system size matters. For the purpose of this exercise, take "size" to mean the mass or volume of the whole brain, not neuron count or brain-to-body ratio; those can be treated as separate claims.

- A healthy adult human.
- A human with profound microcephaly: a brain less than one-third typical mass, severe cognitive impairments, but intact sleep–wake cycles and organized responses to pain and comfort.
- A sperm whale, whose brain is several times the mass of a human's.
- A common raven: a tiny brain in absolute terms, but flexible, multi-step problem-solving that many people would not expect from an animal of its brain size.
- An adult house mouse, with ordinary mouse behavior and a brain a tiny fraction of a human's.
- A human who, after a childhood hemispherectomy, has only one cerebral hemisphere but converses, remembers, and forms relationships normally.
- A science-fictional human whose entire brain has been shrunk to half its size by a drug while every cognitive capacity, memory, and emotional response remains unchanged.
- An infant born with almost all of the cerebral cortex absent, possessing only an intact brainstem.
- A cerebral organoid kept alive in a dish, made of human neurons, showing coordinated electrical activity but having no sensory organs or body.
- An artificial system that behaves, communicates, and reports experiences exactly as a conscious adult human would, but has no nervous tissue at all.

For each case, the definition should say whether the entity has moral status, whether it has the same status as an ordinary human or some other form of status, and whether the verdict changes when brain size alone is altered. I suspect in advance that "moral status" does at least two jobs—one about whether a being has a welfare or a point of view, and another about whether it is the kind of being to whom things can be owed or to whom wrong can be done—and that brain size might matter for one job but not the other. If you agree, give me both definitions and say which cases move between them; if you disagree, defend a single definition and run it through all the cases. Where your definition gives the wrong answer or no answer, say so and revise it; where it gives a surprising answer, either defend that answer or revise the definition. The deliverable is the definition or definitions, not yet a verdict on the brain-size debate.
