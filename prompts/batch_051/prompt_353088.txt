Analyze the decision to launch the Space Shuttle Challenger on January 28, 1986, through the lens of Aumann's agreement theorem. Use the following established facts as the basis for the case: the shuttle broke apart 73 seconds after launch, killing all seven crew members; the proximate cause was the failure of an O-ring seal in one of the solid rocket boosters; on the evening before the launch, engineers from Morton Thiokol, the booster contractor, recommended delaying the mission because the forecast cold would reduce the ability of the O-rings to seal the joints; NASA managers challenged the recommendation; Thiokol management reversed its engineers' position; and the launch went ahead in unusually cold conditions. The subsequent investigation traced the accident to that O-ring failure and to flaws in the launch-decision process.

Begin by stating, in your own words, the version of Aumann's theorem you are applying: two agents who share a prior, receive information according to partitions and update by Bayes's rule, and whose posterior probabilities for an event are common knowledge cannot hold different posterior probabilities for that event. Be explicit about the idealizations built into that formulation.

Now give a disciplined mapping of the pre-launch teleconference and the surrounding decision process onto the theorem. Identify the parties (Thiokol engineers, Thiokol management, NASA managers), the shared evidence (the O-ring erosion and blow-by data from earlier flights, the cold-weather forecast, the absence of flight data at comparable temperatures), the options (launching in the cold versus delaying), and, with particular care, the object of the disagreement. Distinguish a disagreement about a single probabilistic judgment, such as the probability of joint failure at the forecast temperature, from a disagreement about acceptable risk thresholds, burden of proof, or whose uncertainty should govern the launch decision. If the theorem's assumptions held, what would it imply the parties should have concluded once their differing posterior judgments became public: not necessarily agreement on the decision itself, but agreement on the probability question, with any remaining difference traceable to stakes rather than beliefs?

Then assess which assumptions of the theorem plausibly held in the episode and which failed. Consider at least these candidates: common priors versus divergent institutional priors about the reliability of flight hardware and about how much weight anomalous test data should carry; private information that never became common knowledge across the management hierarchy; Bayesian updating versus reasoning under schedule pressure and asymmetric loss functions; and whether the parties' differing conclusions were genuinely common knowledge in the theorem's sense.

Finally, run the analysis in the opposite direction. What does the Challenger case show about what Aumann's theorem can and cannot explain in real institutional disagreement? Is the episode best read as a counterexample to the theorem, or as a demonstration of the theorem's diagnostic value, since it pinpoints exactly where the rationality or commonality conditions broke down? Say explicitly where the theorem is silent, such as about incentives, authority, the social filtering of information, and the difference between shared beliefs and shared decisions, and end with a clear verdict on what the case establishes about the theorem's scope.
