For my final paper in a philosophy of science seminar, I am evaluating whether the no-free-lunch theorems in machine learning amount to a rigorous new version of the problem of induction. The skeptical argument I want to answer runs as follows. The theorems show that if we average performance over all logically possible target functions, every learning algorithm has the same expected performance, so in that sense a standard inductive method is no better than random guessing. Any advantage induction has on actual problems therefore comes from a bias or prior, and the skeptic can claim that the bias is exactly what needs independent justification; induction is thus rationally unprivileged. I have collected five replies to this argument. Please rank them from strongest to weakest as answers to the skeptical challenge, and rate each reply on a scale from 0 to 10 for how completely it dissolves the skeptical worry. Then write one sentence for each explaining why it lands where it does.

The replies are as follows.

The wrong-measure reply: the theorems average over a uniform distribution across all possible target functions, but that distribution has no epistemic authority. A real learner is entitled to assess methods over the structured subset of environments she has reason to think she occupies, and induction's strong performance there is the only justification that matters.

The indispensability-of-bias reply: the theorems show only that no algorithm can do well everywhere, and therefore that every algorithm, including the skeptic's own heuristics, embodies an inductive bias. The result refutes the fantasy of a bias-free method, not the rationality of holding a particular bias for which there is independent empirical support.

The symmetry reply: under the same uniform averaging, random guessing and anti-induction fare no better than anything else, so the theorem provides no positive reason to abandon induction for any specific rival. It leaves the contest between induction and its rivals to be settled by other considerations than the no-free-lunch result itself.

The simplicity reply: the universal average is swamped by incompressible, chaotic target functions. Actual environments present a vanishingly rare, low-complexity subset of regularities, and under a prior that favors simpler hypotheses, Occam-style induction outperforms random guessing on that subset. The relevance of the universal average is therefore exactly what the skeptic has failed to establish.

The tracking reply: results in online learning show that a strategy that maintains a pool of candidate predictors and reweights them according to their accuracy on the data seen so far is guaranteed to do nearly as well as the best candidate in the pool across the actual sequence of data, without assuming a uniform distribution over all possible problems. This yields a modest but attainable form of optimality, doing well relative to the best available alternative, which does not require dominance over all possible environments.
