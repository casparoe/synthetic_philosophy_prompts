I'm an ML engineer who's spent the last year working on RLHF, and I have an ethics-geek friend who keeps telling me that a lot of moral disagreement is really a confusion between preferences and biases. Her favorite example: someone says they oppose more immigration because they value cultural cohesion, while their critic says that's just an out-group bias dressed up in nicer language. Both are looking at the same behavioral data. She says the philosophical distinction matters because preferences are the things that should be respected or weighed in moral deliberation, whereas biases are the things that should be corrected or discounted; I hear that and think its just signal versus spurious correlation, but apparently that's annoying of me.

In my world a preference would be something like the reward function or the revealed ordering over trajectories, and a bias is either an inductive bias (a modeling choice that shapes how I generalize) or a statistical bias in the data (spurious correlations, label noise, selection effects). Please translate the philosophers' preference/bias distinction into ML vocabulary — loss surfaces, reward models, the datagenerating process, distribution shift, regularization, maybe the reward-hacking literature — as faithfully as you can, using the immigration example at least once. Then give me the residue report: what translates cleanly, what you had to fudge, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary. I'm especially curious whether the first-personal side of values — the fact that an agent can, on reflection, endorse or disown a desire as not really their own — is expressible at all in my vocabulary, and whether its absence means my framework is missing something important or the philosopher's distinction is doing less work than it sounds like.
