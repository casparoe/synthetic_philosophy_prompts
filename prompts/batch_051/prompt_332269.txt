An organization I'm part of is considering a rule for decisions that hinge on contested quantitative estimates—a project's chance of success, an expected cost, a risk level. When two internal teams submit materially different numbers, they enter disagreement resolution. Each team states its estimate and the evidence and model behind it; each must write a substantive reply to the other's case; they iterate until neither moves. If a gap above a fixed threshold remains, both teams lose part of their quarterly bonus in proportion to the remaining gap, and the organization adopts the arithmetic mean of the two final estimates as its official number. The designers cite Aumann's agreement theorem: rational Bayesians with common priors cannot agree to disagree once each knows the other's posterior, so after full disclosure a persistent gap is treated as evidence of sloppiness or bad faith and is priced accordingly.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral question. What does it actually incentivize? In particular: what happens to the estimates teams first report, to how much they reveal early rather than late, and to the surrounding forecasting culture? How do teams game the averaging step or the bonus penalty? Where does it fail on its own terms—even granting the designers' reading of Aumann—and which failure modes could be fixed by, say, an ex post proper scoring rule, paying for information rather than agreement, or an adversarial red-team stage, without reducing the thing to a plain prediction market?
