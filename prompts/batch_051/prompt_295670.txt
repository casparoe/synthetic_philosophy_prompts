I cover science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and I am currently writing an essay about the fear of a monotonous life. In interviews, I keep hearing a version of the same worry: a life can be calm, pleasant, and exactly what a person asked for, yet still seem to lack something if it merely repeats itself. The easy dismissal is that this is a failure to notice that variety is almost always an instrument — it prevents boredom, and boredom is a felt state, so the whole phenomenon would fit neatly into a hedonic account of well-being. I want to test whether novelty might instead be doing independent work.

Please construct a thought experiment to specification. Set up two possible futures for one ordinary person, with no experience-machine premises, no pleasure pills, and nothing science-fictional — ideally a workplace or domestic choice a normal person could actually face. The futures should be equal in felt experience, with the same overall balance of pleasant and unpleasant states. They should be equal in desire-satisfaction, with the person's actual desires fulfilled to the same degree in each; the difficult part is to ensure that a latent liking for variety is not quietly driving the result. They should differ only in how much novelty and variety they contain. Then demonstrate that the case meets the specifications. State the verdict of a hedonist about well-being, the verdict of a desire-satisfaction theorist, and the verdict of an objective-list theorist who counts novelty as an intrinsic good. I want a case in which the first two theories judge the futures equal while the third judges them unequal, so that the case isolates novelty as a candidate component of well-being rather than smuggling in pleasure or preference-satisfaction. The verdicts should fall out of the setup rather than being stipulated. Finish by identifying which feature of the case does the work, what would happen if that feature were removed, and how a desire-satisfaction theorist would attack the case.
