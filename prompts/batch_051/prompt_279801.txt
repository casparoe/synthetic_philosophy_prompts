I'm the principal of a K-8 school in a district that has adopted an aggressively evidence-based stance toward every high-stakes decision about a student. The official position is essentially Cliffordian: no teacher is entitled to hold a belief about what a student can do beyond what the assessments and work samples support, and any placement, retention, or recommendation has to be defended with that evidence. At the same time, I have watched teacher expectations move student outcomes in both directions, and I suspect the rule sometimes functions as evidence-based resignation: treating the absence of sufficient evidence that a student can succeed as equivalent to acting on the assumption that she cannot. I want a reusable decision procedure for when a teacher is entitled, or perhaps obliged, to hold and act on a belief about a student's capacity that outruns the current evidence, and when doing so is exactly the professional self-deception the rule exists to prevent.

Build the procedure from the James/Clifford debate about the will to believe and the ethics of belief, and keep the real force of both positions rather than splitting the difference. Do not reduce James to a license for hopeful fictions: force his machinery — that the option be live, forced, and momentous, that the evidence be genuinely indecisive, and that the believing itself can help make the belief true — to do actual work in the steps. The deliverable must be usable by an instructional leadership team with no philosophy background, in about forty-five minutes per contested case, with me signing off on any recommendation it changes. These contested cases reach us several times a month, and each one can shape a child's next few years. I want: the questions in a fixed order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a no, where it hands the decision to a human because the operative question is a value judgment, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, with guidance for making those judgments responsibly.

Then apply the procedure to three cases, including at least one where it gives me an answer I will not like. Use these two: an eighth-grade student with flat standardized scores whose teacher believes she should still be placed in advanced algebra; and a third-grade student whose reading benchmark sits just below the promotion cutoff and whose teacher believes retention would be harmful and that he would catch up in the ordinary classroom. Choose a third realistic case that exposes the procedure's limits, and close by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do.
