I'm running next week's section for a second-year epistemology course, and the topic is Williamson's argument that there are no luminous conditions — where a condition is luminous just in case whenever it obtains you're in a position to know it obtains. The lecture set it up by way of the KK principle (if you know p, you're in a position to know that you know p), and then moved to the cold-at-dawn case: you're cold at dawn and the temperature rises so gradually that you go from clearly cold to clearly not cold across a long series of tiny changes, and the upshot is supposed to be that the last cold moment isn't one where you're in a position to know you're cold. My students have mostly read Descartes and some intro epistemology, so they walk in assuming that their own mental states are basically open to them — if they're cold, they can know they're cold, end of story.

What does the carrying example actually need to have, and what would a bad choice quietly smuggle in? And what's one thing I can ask at the very end that would tell me whether they followed the argument itself, as opposed to just going along with the conclusion?
