Pain research commonly distinguishes a sensory-discriminative dimension of pain (where it is, how intense it is, whether it is sharp or burning) from an affective-motivational dimension (how unpleasant it is, how strongly you want it to stop). A striking piece of evidence for that distinction is the clinical picture often called pain asymbolia: after certain kinds of brain damage, a patient stuck with a pin can locate the sensation, describe its quality, and rate its intensity, yet deny that it bothers them, fail to withdraw, and sometimes even laugh at the examiner. I have seen people use this case to defend a much stronger metaphysical claim: that the badness of pain is not part of the sensory event itself but a separable evaluative-affective response that normally attaches to it. I suspect the clinical case supports less than people think, but I also suspect some version of the strong claim is right, and I want to know how much of the gap between those two suspicions is empirically testable.

If you were designing a study to test whether the unpleasantness of pain is a dissociable component of the experience rather than an intrinsic, partly constitutive feature of it, what exactly would you do? I want (a) a concrete design: instruments, instructions to participants, what conditions or populations you would compare, and what response you would treat as a clean dissociation. You may use asymbolia-like patients, pharmacological manipulations, hypnotic suggestion, or any other lever, but say precisely what is measured. (b) The pattern of results that would favor the dissociable-component reading, and the pattern that would favor the view that a state with intensity but no unpleasantness is simply not pain, or is pain only by courtesy. (c) An account of why the two readings are hard to separate. When a subject rates intensity 7 and unpleasantness 0, are we measuring a component that has gone missing, a person who has altered what they mean by pain, or a person reporting a judgment about their state rather than a feel of the state? Is that ambiguity a defect in our instruments, a principled limit of all third-person evidence about experience, or a defect in the notion of a component of an experience? Finally, say what a clean dissociation would and would not settle.

Keep the answer honest about the state of the field: separate what is empirically well-established from what is philosophically contested, and do not present the dissociable-component view as the consensus if the people who actually work on this do not treat it as settled.
