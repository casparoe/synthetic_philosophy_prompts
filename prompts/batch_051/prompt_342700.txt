Here is an argument I constructed for a seminar on sovereignty and membership. I cannot accept its conclusion, but I cannot identify the step that fails.

(1) A state is sovereign only if it has final authority over its own affairs: no outside body may impose binding rules on it.
(2) Joining a customs union or a confederation subjects the member to common tariffs, harmonized rules, and dispute-resolution bodies whose rulings bind it even when it dissents.
(3) Submitting to binding rules made by a body one does not control is to transfer final authority over those matters, and a body that has transferred final authority over some matters is no longer fully sovereign.
(4) Therefore a state cannot both join such an association and remain the same fully sovereign state it was before.
(5) By the same reasoning, no sovereign state may sign any binding treaty, since every treaty imposes obligations the state cannot simply rescind at will.
(6) That is plainly false: sovereign states sign binding treaties constantly, and doing so is usually regarded as an exercise of sovereignty rather than its surrender.

Where does the argument go wrong, and what does rejecting the mistaken step cost? I would rather keep (1) and (6) and give up something in the middle, but I want to know what that commits me to about sovereignty being 'pooled' rather than lost.

Please answer in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
