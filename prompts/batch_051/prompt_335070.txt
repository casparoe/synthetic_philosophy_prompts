Here's a practice that's gotten so standard I barely notice it anymore. A biopic about a real dead person opens with a card saying the film is inspired by true events but that some characters and events have been changed or fictionalized for dramatic purposes. Then the film uses the person's real name, keeps the real dates and places, and happily invents private conversations, motives, sometimes whole incidents — all for someone who's life is being borrowed and who isn't around to answer back. Meanwhile the same film gets marketed as telling the true story of this very person, and the disclaimer is treated as the thing that makes the whole project okay.

I want to know what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes about what we owe the dead. Here's what I'm trying to figure out: does the disclaimer presuppose that a dead person has no claim that outlives them, and that the only possible wrong is misleading the living audience? If the wrong could instead be to the dead person themselves — a kind of false witness against someone who can't respond — then the disclaimer looks almost beside the point, because it tells the audience that some of this is invented while the dead person is still being misrepresented. Does the practice assume that once a life is over, it becomes raw material for anyone who labels the product accurately? And do the disclaimer and the true-story marketing presuppose different things, or is the tension only apparent?

If I'm misdescribing the practice or the disclaimer is doing some other job, say so plainly instead of forcing a unified theory. Also tell me where you're confident and where you're speculating.
