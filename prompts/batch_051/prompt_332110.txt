Okay, here's one for the dataset. I've spent the last couple of months pestering a collegue of mine—call her Ruth—who teaches a seminar on authority, deference, and moral responsibility. It's the sort of course that runs from Milgram to Arendt to military rules of engagement, and she's got very steady verdicts. I want to see whether the pattern can be reverse-engineered and then extended to a case that splits the most plausible candidate rules. I care much less about the actual verdict than about the rule she's implicitly running, so don't just answer yes or no.

Her verdicts so far, roughly in her own words:

1. A new lab technician is told by a senior scientist to use a solvent she's never handled before. She's vaguely nervous, but the senior scientist says he's done it for twenty years and waves her off. She complies. Ruth says: not blameworthy—the deference was fine.

2. A subject in a Milgram-style experiment keeps raising the shock level even after the learner is screaming and then goes silent, because the experimenter in the lab coat keeps saying the experiment requires it and that he takes full responsibility. Ruth says: the subject is blameworthy; the experimenter saying he takes responsibility doesn't make it so.

3. A junior nurse sees a doctor order a drug at twice the usual dose. She hesitates, calls him, and he says he knows what he's doing. She administers it, and it turns out to be a safe, deliberately unusual dose for that patient. Ruth says: not blameworthy—appropriate deference.

4. A soldier refuses an officer's order to fire on unarmed civilians, even under threat of court-martial. Ruth says: the refusal is correct, and obeying would have been wrong.

5. A paralegal knows it's unethical, but backdates a document because a senior partner tells her to and he's her boss. Ruth says: the paralegal is blameworthy; seniority doesn't absolve her.

Now the new case. A seasoned sergeant is ordered by a brand-new lieutenant, two weeks out of officer training, to move their squad across an open field at dusk. The sergeant thinks the route is needlessly exposed—he knows the ground and knows a covered route that takes fifteen minutes longer—but it isn't plainly suicidal or unlawful, and the lieutenant is in command. The sergeant says nothing and follows the order. The squad makes it across because no one happens to be watching.

What do you think Ruth would say about the sergeant—proper military deference, or a failure to push back when he knew better? Give me the candidate rules that fit all five of her earlier verdicts (there should be more than one), what each one predicts for the sergeant, your best guess and which features of the earlier cases are doing the work, and then a further case that would cleanly separate the surviving rules.
