I've been working through the argument that qualia should be "quined" rather than refuted, and I think I've gone wrong somewhere. As I understand it, quining means showing that the term is a philosopher's posit that cannot be cashed out: Dennett challenges you to find any feature of experience that is simultaneously ineffable, intrinsic, private, and immediately apprehensible, and his coffee-taster cases, Chase and Sanborn, are meant to show that the allegedly stable property doing the explanatory work dissolves once you interrogate what the tasters are actually comparing. I find that diagnosis persuasive in the abstract, but it collides with something I cannot shake in my own case. I cook a lot, and I can reliably tell that my aunt's tamarind broth is less sour than last year's; the sourness presents itself as an immediate feature of the tasting, not as an inference from evidence or observation, and I cannot state what, exactly, I am comparing. But here is where I am stuck: Dennett insists he is not denying that we are conscious, or that experiences have properties, or that things seem some way to us. Yet if qualia just are the what-it-is-like of experience, and that notion is incoherent, then how is saying "there is no such property" any different from saying "there is nothing it is like"? Once the quale has been quined, what is left of the experience for my report of diminished sourness to be a report of? I suspect I am forcing a false dilemma or collapsing a distinction Dennett needs, but I cannot see which one.
