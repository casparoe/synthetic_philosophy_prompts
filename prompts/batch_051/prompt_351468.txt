I'm in a decision theory seminar that has spent three weeks on the Allais paradox, and the two people who never stop arguing about it have been told by the professor to produce an adversarial-collaboration document instead of another round of the same argument. One of them thinks Allais just shows people get confused — once you lay the choices out properly, the allegedly inconsistent preferences pretty much disappear. The other thinks Allais exposed a real flaw in the independence axiom as a norm of rationality, and that a decent theory should weaken or replace it rather then explain the pattern away. Write the document they'd produce.

Assume the classic common-consequence versions: 1A is 1 million for sure; 1B pays 5 million with probability 0.10, 1 million with probability 0.89, and nothing with probability 0.01. 2A pays 1 million with probability 0.11 and nothing otherwise; 2B pays 5 million with probability 0.10 and nothing otherwise. The defender of the axiom — call them the fixer — holds that independence is non-negociable and treats the common pattern of choosing 1A over 1B but 2B over 2A as a framing effect, an editing error, or the product of an irrational certainty heuristic. The Allaisian holds that the pattern is robust and reasonable, that it reflects a genuine certainty effect, and that something like a rank-dependent or disappointment-averse model is the better normative picture.

The document should open with a numbered section of claims both can sign, including an exact statement of which choice pairs conflict with independence and which don't. Then include a disagreements table where each side's view is worded so that side would happily accept the wording, no strawmen. Then identify the real cruxes: I suspect one is whether making the common consequence explicit — so the two problems are seen as sharing the same 0.89 branch — makes the 1A/2B pattern disappear, or merely changes what subjects say afterwards; a second is whether the certainty effect survives when the "sure" option is replaced by an outcome that is merely overwhelmingly likely. For each crux, specify a concrete case or experiment both sides agree bears on it, and record what each side predicts in advance. End with a note on which parts of the dispute they now think were verbal — for example, whether they were using "rational" in different senses — and which parts remain substantive. The goal is a text both could actually sign, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than smoothed over.
