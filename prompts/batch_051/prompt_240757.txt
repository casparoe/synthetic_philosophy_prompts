I serve on the twelve-member elected negotiating committee of a tenants' association that is trying to agree on a collective response to a developer's buyout offer. Our deliberations reliably descend into commitment races. A member announces in a full meeting that they will never accept less than a particular figure, or will not sign unless some condition is met; anyone who later softens is treated as a sellout; the next faction ups the ante; and by the final vote the committee is locked into positions that many members privately think are worse than the likely alternatives. We are considering a bylaw amendment designed to change the incentives around public commitments, and I want you to analyze it as a mechanism rather than judge it as a moral matter.

Here is the amendment. During the negotiation period, any committee member who makes a public commitment about the negotiations must lodge it with the secretary within twenty-four hours. The lodgment must state the exact commitment, a bond chosen by the member equal to between one and five percent of the member's monthly income, and a retraction condition: a description of an observable future state of the negotiations that would lead the member to withdraw the commitment. The retraction condition must be specific enough that the secretary can verify after the fact whether it occurred, and it may not be entirely within the member's own discretion. A lodged commitment expires after twenty-eight days unless renewed, and each renewal doubles the bond and requires a new retraction condition. If a member withdraws a commitment before its retraction condition has occurred, the bond is forfeited and the withdrawal is recorded in the minutes as a walk-back. If the withdrawal occurs after the retraction condition has occurred, the bond is returned and the withdrawal is recorded as condition-triggered updating. Forfeited bonds accumulate in a pot that is paid out at settlement in equal shares, one per member, to every member who has recorded at least one condition-triggered update.

I want a walkthrough of how this would actually behave in a room of people who are skilled at using rules against one another. Tell me who becomes more cautious about committing and who becomes more reckless; how the drafting of retraction conditions itself becomes strategic; what happens to the credibility of the committee's commitments with the developer, who will know the rule exists; and where the system is gamed first, whether in the form, the renewal clock, the dividend, or by moving commitments into settings the amendment does not cover. Track the dynamics over the course of a multi-month negotiation, including second-order effects on who runs for the committee and on what the association comes to admire in a negotiator. Then tell me which failure modes could be fixed by tightening the rule without losing its point, and which are baked into the very idea of making public commitments revocable.

After the walkthrough, give me a brief structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- incentives_by_party: an array of objects, each with party (a role on the committee) and incentive (one sentence on how the rule changes that party's behavior)
- gaming_tactics: an array of objects, each with tactic (name of the move), who_benefits (which party), and effect (what it does to the negotiation)
- failure_modes: an array of objects, each with mode (description of the breakdown), triggers (conditions that set it off), and severity (one of low, medium, or high)
- second_order_effects: an array of one-sentence effects on the association beyond the negotiation itself
- fixes: an array of objects, each with fix (a specific amendment to the rule), addresses (which failure mode it targets), and new_vulnerability (how the fix would itself be gamed)
- net_assessment: a short paragraph stating whether, on balance, the rule would reduce or merely relocate the commitment race, and under what conditions it would be worth adopting.
