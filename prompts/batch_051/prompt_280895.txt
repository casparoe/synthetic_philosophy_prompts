My dad died last winter and I've been rereading the Zhuangzi because it's the only thing on death that doesnt feel like a greeting card. Two passages keep nagging at me. One is the story where Zhuangzi's wife dies, Huizi comes to pay his respects, and finds him sitting with his legs sprawled, pounding on a tub and singing. When Huizi calls him out, Zhuangzi says that before she existed there was no life and no body; then the breath changed, there was a body and a life, and now there's been another change, like the four seasons, so wailing on would just show he doesnt get fate. The other is the skull story, where Zhuangzi finds a skull by the road, uses it as a pillow, and then dreams the skull tells him not to bother trying to bring it back to life: among the dead there's no ruler above and no toil below, and maybe our certainty that life is what you should cling to is the real mistake. The first seems to say death is just another change of form and mourning is confusion; the second seems to undercut even that confidence, saying we can't know which side is better or whether the dead regret their old hunger for life. Are these two actually in tension, or does one just complete the other? If you can reconcile them, give me the candidate reconciliations, then say what has to be true about the text for each to work, and which repair is the honest reading and which is special pleading. Also tell me if you think my gloss of the skull story is already overreading.
