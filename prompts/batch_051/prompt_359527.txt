I am a PhD student in programming languages, and each autumn I serve as head teaching assistant for a graduate software-design course with about 140 students and eight TAs. I write the grading rubric, and the most persistent inconsistency across graders is how to treat mutable state: the same submission will pass with one TA, lose design points with another, and be returned for redesign by a third. I want a single decision procedure, grounded in why mutation is hard to think about, that I can fold into the rubric and reuse every term. A TA with only an undergraduate background must be able to run it in under ten minutes on a submission she is reading for the first time, without executing the code or reading the full specification, and I must be able to defend its verdict to the course instructor when a student appeals a deduction.

Please translate the underlying principle — that mutation becomes costly to reason about when the behavior or value of a piece of code stops being readable from the expressions and arguments in front of it, and instead depends on hidden state, aliasing, or the order of updates — into an operational sequence of questions, with the theory left implicit so the TAs do not need to learn it. The procedure must distinguish among at least the following: mutation of a local variable whose identity cannot escape the current activation; mutation of an object whose identity is shared through references and aliases; state that persists across calls and changes what a later call returns or does independently of its arguments; and mutation that alters only performance or memory behavior, not observable results. It must not collapse into a blanket preference for immutability: it should say when mutation is the cleaner design and exactly what a student must supply to justify it.

Each step should end in one of four verdicts — accept; accept but require a written justification; deduct points as a design smell; or return for redesign — and the procedure should state what evidence moves a case from one verdict to the next. Mark clearly which steps are judgment calls rather than mechanical tests, and say what a grader should do when she cannot answer a question confidently. State plainly what the procedure cannot detect and where it will misfire.

Then run the procedure on three representative submissions: (a) a divide-and-conquer algorithm whose recursive helper uses a local mutable accumulator; (b) a module-level memoization cache that persists across calls but is never exposed or aliased; and (c) a global configuration flag read deep inside nested helpers and mutated between test cases. Make at least one of these a case where the procedure returns a harsher verdict than a typical grader would have given without it, and explain why that harsher verdict is correct.
