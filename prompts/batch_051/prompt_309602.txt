Ok, here's one for the final exam in my upper-level seminar on rational choice and moral psychology—closed-book, 90 minutes, essay question worth 20 points. I want it to punish people who just memorize "loss aversion" and "endowment effect" and spit back definitions.

**Question.** In the standard mug experiment, people who are randomly given a mug demand roughly twice as much to sell it as people who were not given one are willing to pay to buy one. One camp calls this an irrational bias—some kind of endowment effect or loss aversion. The other camp says the subjects are just revealing a legitimate preference to keep what they have. Your job is to say what, exactly, is at stake between these two readings, and what evidence or argument could in principle settle it. Don't just rehearse the definitions. Work out the criteria by which we distinguish a bias from a legitimate preference in a case like this, and then say where the two readings actually part company.

**Model answer (aim for about 600 words).**

The phenomenon is the gap between willingness-to-accept and willingness-to-pay: in the mug experiment, people randomly given a mug demand roughly twice as much to sell it as people who were not given one will pay to buy one. The first move is to distinguish this behavioral gap from loss aversion, which is only one candidate explanation among several. Loss aversion says that losses weigh more than equal gains in a reference-dependent value function; the endowment effect is the observed WTA/WTP gap. Conflating the two is a mistake because the same gap could come from transaction costs, real attachment, or subjects simply misunderstanding the task. Keeping them apart is what lets us ask the real question: when is such a gap a bias?

That leads to the second point. Calling something a bias is not a neutral description; it presupposes a standard. If losses simply count for more in a person's utility function, then demanding $10 to give up a mug rather than paying $6 to acquire something comparable is just maximizing that person's preferences, and no error has occurred. To call the gap a bias we need an independent criterion—for example, that the subject would disown the valuation after reflection, or that it conflicts with her own considered ends, or that it tracks features of the situation that are, by any reasonable standard, irrelevant. Without such a criterion, "bias" is just a pejorative for a preference we happen not to share.

Third, the strongest reason to suspect bias is not that subjects like what they own; it's the arbitrariness of the assignment. A coin flip decided who got the mug, and that flip then flipped valuations. If the ownership right had carried information—if the subject learned from having the mug that she likes it more than she expected—or if she had formed plans and attachment around this particular mug, the reassessment could be rational. But none of that is in place a minute after a random allocation. So the bias reading points at path dependence: the valuation tracks an arbitrary starting point rather than the object's value to the person.

Fourth, the experimental record matters and cuts both ways. When participants get market experience, clear instructions, anonymity, and practice, the gap shrinks or disappears. The bias reading takes that as evidence the raw gap was partly mistake—subjects didn't understand the elicitation—and that the procedures corrected it. The preference reading replies that the procedures changed the situation: in a functioning market, selling is not experienced as a loss, and the reference point has moved. So the empirical result doesn't settle anything by itself; it rephrases the same normative question about when we have corrected a mistake and when we have simply replaced one set of preferences with another.

Finally, the two readings part company over the burden of proof. The bias reading treats a preference as legitimate only if it is insensitive to arbitrary framing and would survive the subject's informed reflection; the preference reading treats expressed valuations as legitimate unless there is special reason to override them, and treats the reflected-out gap as no less real than the raw one. There is no value-free experiment that settles between those two default positions, because they differ about who must show that a preference is authorized. That's why the debate feels stuck: the mug facts are largely agreed, and the fight is really about what counts as an authorized preference. A strong answer ends by saying what evidence would move each side: for the bias reading, subjects repudiating their own WTA once they are walked through the design; for the preference reading, evidence that informed, experienced agents robustly protect their holdings in ways that fit their stable plans. That's the test they'd dissagree about.

**Marking scheme (20 points).**

1. Seperates the behavioral gap from loss aversion as one explanation, and names at least one other possible source (transaction costs, attachment, misunderstanding). 4 points. Where students drop it: they say the endowment effect just is loss aversion, and then can't even formulate the bias/preference question.

2. Articulates why "bias" needs a normative baseline, and gives at least one candidate (reflection endorsement, conflict with considered ends, arbitrariness). 5 points. Typical miss: treating "bias" as a purely descriptive, value-neutral label.

3. Puts weight on the arbitrariness of random assignment, and distinguishes it from cases where ownership carries information or creates real attachment. 4 points. Common error: defending the mug-holders by appeal to the sanctity of ownership without noticing that ownership was assigned by coin flip.

4. Shows that the gap shrinks or vanishes with market experience and clear procedures, and explains why this result is itself contested rather than a clean win for either side. 3 points. Common error: quoting the debiasing studies as if they prove the raw gap was irrational, full stop.

5. Identifies the actual point of disagreement as a burden-of-proof question about what counts as an authorized preference, and concludes the dispute is partly normative rather than purely empirical. 4 points. Common error: ending with "it's a bit of both" without saying where the readings part company.

Separating the grades: an A answer makes all five moves, uses the debiasing literature as a pivot rather than a cudgel, and states what would count as evidence against each reading. A B answer defines loss aversion and the endowment effect, maybe mentions that losses loom larger than gains, and grants that it's complicated—but never separates the phenomenon from its explanation or supplies a criterion for what makes a preference a bias.
