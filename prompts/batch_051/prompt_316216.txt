Here is a familiar argument for the claim that a player cannot wrong a video-game character directly. A character is a fictional object: a bundle of rendered appearances and scripted responses. It has no felt interests, no point of view, and no future it can be deprived of, so whatever is bad about mistreating it is exhausted by its effects on real people, such as the player's own habits and sensibilities, other players, or the developers' intentions. That argument was formed when characters were fixed scripts and canned animations.

Now suppose a game contains characters that persist across sessions, keep individual memories, pursue goals, learn from how players treat them, and respond to abuse with avoidance, protest, and a lasting loss of trust. Behaviorally, they satisfy the tests we would use before attributing interests and welfare to a non-human animal, even though whether felt experience accompanies those behaviors remains no more directly observable than it does for an animal.
