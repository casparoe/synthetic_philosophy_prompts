My partner and I are expecting our first child in a few months, and we keep stalling in the same argument: she was raised in a close-knit religious community that shaped nearly every hour of her childhood, while I was raised by secular parents who went out of their way to keep my future options open, and we cannot agree on how much we are allowed to decide for our daughter before she can decide for herself. I run a biweekly discussion group for new and expecting parents, and I would like to turn this private disagreement into something the group can actually use: a sorting instrument rather than a manifesto.

Please write a "Which view of parental authority and the child's open future do you actually hold?" quiz together with a scoring key. The quiz should sort readers by their judgments about concrete cases, not by which label appeals to them; no question should simply ask whether parenting should be licensed or whether children have a right to an open future. The audience is educated new and expecting parents without formal philosophy training, so jargon should be avoided or defined in a clause. Do not include citations, footnotes, or a reading list; the instrument should stand on the cases alone.

Build the instrument from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of positions: first the questions, then the positions located in the space of answer patterns. The following five positions must appear as leaves:

1. Open-future liberalism: a child's major life options are protected in trust for the future adult, so parents may not deliberately foreclose important options, and the state may intervene to protect option rights, not only to stop abuse or neglect.
2. Revocability: parents may shape a child's values, culture, and practices only while that shaping remains revisable or reversible by the child later; the state intervenes when the shaping is severe and effectively irreversible.
3. Family-integrity: a thick, identity-forming upbringing is a good in itself; a fully open future is an illusion; the state should defer to parents except in clear cases of physical or psychological abuse or neglect.
4. Licensing welfarism: childrearing is a high-stakes competence, like medicine or driving, so the state should screen prospective parents and may deny or revoke a license to raise a child where the child's serious interests would be put at risk.
5. Contractarianism: parental authority arises from voluntary undertaking and agreement; third parties may enforce the terms of parenting agreements and genuine exit rights, but there is no general state license to become a parent.

The eight questions must use, one per question, these eight cases:

1. A Deaf couple deliberately selects a sperm donor with congenital deafness because they want a child fully at home in their language and Deaf culture.
2. A child raised from birth inside a closed religious community that forbids secular higher education, smartphones, and dating outside the faith; at eighteen the child may leave, but doing so would mean losing family ties and communal support.
3. A state proposal requiring every prospective biological or adoptive parent to pass a competence assessment and hold a renewable license, with renewal review triggered by any child-welfare complaint.
4. A loving but illiterate couple unschools their twelve-year-old, who cannot read, though the child is healthy, fed, and securely attached.
5. A couple uses embryo selection to choose the embryo with the highest polygenic score for educational attainment, partly for the child's sake and partly for family pride.
6. Parents decide from infancy that their child will be a professional violinist and schedule six hours of daily practice from age four, allowing no other hobbies and no choice in the matter.
7. Two adults sign a surrogacy and co-parenting contract specifying the child's religion and schooling, with financial penalties for deviation; the commissioning parents later want to change the schooling over the surrogate's objection.
8. A child-protection agency removes a child from parents who are loving and materially adequate but apply relentless psychological pressure for the child to become a professional athlete, and the child is openly miserable.

Each question should present its case in two or three sentences and offer two to four mutually exclusive options, phrased so that a reader can answer from the vignette alone and cannot tell from the wording which position an option loads onto. For scoring, assign each option a loading across the five positions (zero, partial, or full credit), give a rule for aggregating the scores, and specify which questions serve as tie-breakers for the two closest pairs of positions.

Structure your response in exactly these sections, in this order, with these approximate lengths:

1. Framing note (about 80 words): a short note to the quiz-taker explaining that the instrument sorts by case judgments and that ties and unresolved combinations will be reported honestly.
2. The eight questions (about 900 words total): each numbered, with its vignette and labeled options.
3. Scoring key (about 400 words): the loading table or point system, the aggregation rule, and the tie-breaker questions.
4. The five positions (about 600 words): a one-paragraph portrait of each leaf, including how that position answers the case it finds hardest.
5. Diagnostic notes (about 250 words): which answer combinations are unoccupied, and why (incoherent, unattractive given the cases, or an artifact of these particular questions); and flag any pair of leaves the instrument cannot reliably separate.
