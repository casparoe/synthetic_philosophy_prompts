So I spent my working life running simulations in statistical mechanics and physical chemistry—reaction-diffusion systems, cellular automata as toy models, that sort of thing—and since retiring I've been auditing a philosophy of science seminar at the university down the road. Next month they've asked me to give the talk. I want to defend the view that Wolfram-style computational irreducibility isn't only a limit on prediction but also a limit on explanation, at least on the kind of explanation scientists actually hope for: one where you can survey a derivation, grasp why the outcome had to be as it was, and compress the whole business into something shorter than the process itself. My argument is basically this: if the only way to get from the rule plus initial conditions to the phenomenon is to run every step, then a derivation exists but it doesn't explain in any non-trivial sense; it just recapitulates the phenomenon in smaller clothes. I know the seminar won't let me get away with running rule 30 and shrugging. The regulars are: (a) a covering-law person who thinks explanation is subsumption under a law and will probably say my whole position is a confusion about deduction; (b) a mechanist who works on explanation in molecular biology and will say I've chosen a description with no levels left to decompose; (c) a grad student who knows algorithmic information theory cold and will ask why irreducibility isn't just a representation-dependent artifact; and (d) the convener, a general philosopher of science who always presses people to say which account of explanation they're assuming before they're allowed to say it's been limited. Give me a prep sheet for the question period, ordered by how likely and how damaging the objections are, phrased the way these four would actually phrase them. For each one, tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. And name the objection I most hope nobody asks, because its the one I've been quietly avoiding in my own head.
