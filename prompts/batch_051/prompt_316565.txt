I'm a TA gradding a midterm for a political philosophy course, and I want to calibrate the grading on one question before I do the rest of the stack. The question I set was:

"Early in J.S. Mill's The Subjection of Women (1869), Mill writes:

"Again, in practical matters, the burthen of proof is supposed to be with those who are against liberty; who contend for any restriction or prohibition; either any limitation of the general freedom of human action, or any disqualification or disparity of privilege affecting one person or kind of persons, as compared with others. The à priori presumption is in favour of freedom and impartiality. It is held that there should be no restraint not required by the general good, and that the law should be no respecter of persons, but should treat all alike, save where dissimilarity of treatment is required by positive reasons, either of justice or of policy."

Explain as precisely as you can what principle Mill is relying on here to place the burden of proof on defenders of sex-based legal subordination. Then evaluate whether that principle, taken by itself, can explain why some grounds of legal classification (like race or sex) are normally treated as 'suspect' while others (like age, income, or educational attainment) are not. If it can't, say exactly what's missing. (30 points)"

Here's one student's answer, copied out with their typos and all:

"Mill's principle is that the law has to treat everyone alike unless there are positive reasons of justice or policy for treating some people differently. Any disqualification or disparity aimed at one 'kind of persons' is therefore presumed wrong, and the state, not the person on the receiving end, has to produce the reasons. So when women are excluded from a profession, it's not up to women to prove they're qualified; the state has to prove the exclusion is required by the general good. On this reading, a ground of classification becomes suspect when distinctions based on it usually come from mere custom or prejudice instead of from positive reasons. Race and sex are suspect for that reason: the historical exclusions were backed by stereotypes about natural inferiority, not by actual arguments from justice or policy. Age, income, and educational qualifications are not suspect because there are real policy reasons for treating people differently along those lines—children can't vote or sign contracts, means-tested benefits single out the poor, and you need a medical license to practice medicine. So I think Mill's principle basically gets the right result, with one addition: some grounds carry so much stigma that even plausible-sounding government reasons should attract special suspicion, like the strict scrutiny branch of American equal protection law. The only weakness I can see is that Mill doesn't say how strong the positive reasons have to be. As written, 'reasons of justice or policy' would let almost any paternalistic rationale count, which makes the principle too permissive."

Can you grade this one out of 30 and give me the comments you'd write on it? I want a point-by-point assessment, not a general vibe, and I want you to catch the subtle stuff a hasty grader would miss.
