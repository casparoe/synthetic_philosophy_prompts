Start by laying out the standard case, then take the modified case step by step, and be explicit about where the standard analysis changes.

In the standard game, a fair coin is tossed until heads appears; if the first heads is on toss n, you are paid 2^n dollars. The usual calculation says the expected payout is infinite, even though almost no one would pay more than a few dollars to play. The familiar responses appeal to the fact that an extra dollar matters less as you get richer, or to the fact that no real bank can pay out arbitrarily large sums.

Here is the variation. Keep the coin fair and keep the 2^n payout, but make the timing explicit. Suppose there is one toss per day, and a run that ends on the nth toss pays out on day n. The player discounts future money at a constant daily rate: a dollar received t days from now is worth δ^t dollars today, with 0 < δ < 1. In the usual presentation, time of receipt is left out of the picture, which amounts to setting δ = 1; the variant is to ask what happens once δ is strictly less than 1.

Specifically:

(a) What is the expected present value of a single play as a function of δ?

(b) Does this just attach a clock to the original paradox, or does a positive daily discount rate actually dissolve it? In the modified game, is a player who maximizes expected present value still unable to settle on a finite fair price, or can they now name one precisely?

(c) What happens as δ climbs toward 1? Should the original infinite expectation be understood as the limiting case of the discounted game, or as a boundary idealization that misleads us about ordinary decisions?

(d) Once the payouts have been discounted in this way, does the older response that money has diminishing marginal utility still do any independent work, or has discounting already tamed the infinite expectation by itself?

Please don't just recite the standard resolutions. I'm interested in what the timing-and-discounting change specifically does, and whether it counts as a solution to the paradox or a change of subject.
