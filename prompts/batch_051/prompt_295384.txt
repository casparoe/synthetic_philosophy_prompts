In the Questions of King Milinda (Milindapañha), as translated by T. W. Rhys Davids, Nāgasena gets Milinda to accept that the person is only a convenient designation for the parts:

“Then thus, ask as I may, I can discover no Nâgasena. Nâgasena is a mere empty sound. Who then is the Nâgasena that we see before us? It is a falsehood that your reverence has spoken, an untruth!”

I keep seeing commentators say both that kamma is a strict moral-desert relation across lives and that there is strictly no owner of kamma. Before I read more such accounts, I want the specification. Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of karma as moral desert: what it must explain about cross-life justice, distributive inequality at birth, moral luck, and the difference between causal consequence and desert; what distinctions it cannot collapse (identity versus continuity, desert versus retribution, the agent’s perspective versus the observer’s); and what no-self commitments it cannot quietly abandon. Give the reason behind each requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are school-preferences. If some subset cannot be jointly satisfied, say that directly—that is the part I most want to see. Then briefly score three broad strategies against the list: a real-but-non-Cartesian person-continuant, a reduction to a momentary causal series, and a view that spreads desert across a biographical or karmic field without any owner.
