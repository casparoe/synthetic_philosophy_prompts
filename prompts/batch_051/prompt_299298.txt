Question: Does Searle's Chinese Room thought experiment show that a digital computer running a program could never genuinely understand anything? Explain the thought experiment, state what it is supposed to establish, and consider whether the systems reply undermines it.

Student answer: Searle imagines a monolingual English speaker alone in a room with a rulebook. Chinese symbols come in through a slot, and the rulebook tells him which Chinese symbols to send back out. From outside it looks exactly like a competent conversation in Chinese, but the man has no idea what any of it means. Searle's point is that syntax is not sufficient for semantics: manipulating symbols according to formal rules does not by itself produce understanding. Since a computer program is just that kind of formal symbol manipulation, Searle concludes that a computer running the program would be in the same position as the man — zero understanding of Chinese, even while its output is indistinguishable from a native speaker's. The systems reply objects that, although the man doesn't understand Chinese, the whole room — man, book, symbols, and all — does. Searle dismisses this because understanding is the kind of thing that has to happen in a biological brain, so saying the whole room understands is a category mistake.
