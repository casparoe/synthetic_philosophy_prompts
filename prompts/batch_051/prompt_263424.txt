A philosopher I know runs a seminar on choice without preference, organized around Buridan's ass and its modern descendants, and I am trying to reconstruct the principle behind her rulings from the ledger of cases she has judged. She has so far refused to rule on the original ass case, calling it underdescribed. Her extant verdicts are these.

1. The equal postdocs. A student has two postdoc offers she regards as exactly equal in every respect she cares about; she flips a coin and accepts whichever it selects. Verdict: rationally permissible.

2. The alphabetical shopper. A shopper faces two brands of canned tomatoes, identical in price and in every property she notices or values; she applies her standing policy of buying the alphabetically earlier brand in a grocery tie, and does so. Verdict: permissible.

3. The costly ditherer. A job candidate has two offers she regards as exactly equal but refuses to break the tie by any arbitrary means, because she wants a real reason to prefer one; both offers lapse while she waits. Verdict: rationally defective — not because she lacked a preference, but because she failed to make a decision once one was required.

4. The costless suspender. A reader choosing between two equally appealing novels, under no deadline and with no reason to prefer either, puts the choice aside and reads something else until a consideration emerges. Verdict: permissible.

5. The manufactured aversion. A renter deciding between two apartments she finds exactly equal notices that one has a stairwell she faintly dislikes, deliberately cultivates that reaction into a genuine aversion, and takes the other. Verdict: permissible.

6. The delegated pick. A tourist deciding between two identical souvenirs can find no difference between them, asks a stranger to point, and buys the one indicated. Verdict: permissible.

Next week's case is the bare drifter. A shopper with a full cart approaches two checkout lines that are, as far as he can tell, exactly equal in length, speed, and distance; he is in no hurry, has no preference between them, has no standing tie-breaking policy, flips no coin, and reports no inclination either way. Without stopping to deliberate, he simply angles his cart into the right-hand line.

Begin with the bottom line in your first sentence: the verdict you predict she will give. Then give the reasoning. In doing so, work through the candidate principles that fit her ledger — suspensionism about preference gaps (never select until a genuine reason to prefer appears), picking-permissivism (bare arbitrary selection is fine once the options are tied), tie-break proceduralism (a tie must be broken by an adopted procedure or manufactured difference before one selects), or something in that vicinity — and say which of these her earlier verdicts already rule out and what the survivors predict about the bare drifter. State the principle you think she is really running, and why the best guess goes that way. Then specify the one further case you would run next to settle whatever residual ambiguity remains, and say how each surviving principle would classify that case. Do not preface the answer or restate the setup.
