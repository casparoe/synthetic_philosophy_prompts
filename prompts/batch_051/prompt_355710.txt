I cover science and ideas for a living, so my working vocabulary is statistical rather than philosophical: priors, likelihoods, posterior updating, long-run calibration, sampling frames, and the difference between a population and the particular observation one happens to be inside. I want the Sleeping Beauty dispute restated in exactly that vocabulary, along with a report on what does not survive the translation. As I understand the puzzle, a fair coin is tossed on Sunday and Beauty is put to sleep; if it lands heads she is woken once, on Monday; if it lands tails she is woken on both Monday and Tuesday, with an amnesia-inducing treatment between awakenings, so every waking is subjectively indistinguishable to her. Upon waking she is asked for her credence that the coin landed heads, and the halfer answers one half while the thirder answers one third. Restate the best form of each side in terms of likelihoods and conditioning events, a sampling frame over possible awakenings, and what a long-run tally of awakenings, rather than of whole experiments, would calibrate to. I am less interested in a final verdict on the puzzle than in a careful inventory of losses. Tell me which parts of the dispute translate cleanly, which parts you had to strain to fit, and which parts have no statistical counterpart at all, and then say whether the residue exposes a real gap in the Bayesian toolkit's ability to represent an observer's uncertainty about her own location in an experiment, or whether it exposes a dispute that mostly dissolves once the frame is made explicit.
