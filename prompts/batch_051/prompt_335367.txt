I've been trying to get a clear grip on the claim that joint action isn't reducible to individual intentional states, so I first tried to steelman the strongest reductive account I could: interlocking individual intentions, each conditional on expectations about the other, with the conditions out in the open between them. Somewhere in that reconstruction I produced the argument below, which appears to show that the reductive account collapses into an infinite regress and therefore cannot be right. I don't trust it: it seems like one of those proofs-too-much where a term quietly shifts its meaning between steps, but I cannot pin down where. I want a precise diagnosis rather than a summary of the literature.

You are a professional philosopher working on collective intentionality and joint action, with a side interest in formal epistemology. Please think carefully before committing. Begin by brainstorming at least two independent ways to resist the conclusion, each identifying a different step or reading; state each in its strongest form, then evaluate it, and only then give your final verdict on which step actually fails — or whether the argument is valid after all.

The argument:

(1) If A and B are walking together, each of them does their part because they believe the other will do theirs.
(2) A belief that supports genuine joint action must be more than a private prediction: if A's belief that B will walk rests solely on evidence available only to A, then A and B are at best coordinating by luck, not walking together.
(3) So each participant's expectation must rest on grounds that are available to the other as grounds, and each must treat those grounds as their shared basis for acting.
(4) If each treats those grounds as shared, then each believes that the other has them; and since the same holds for the other's belief, each must also believe that the other believes that the first has them.
(5) The condition in (4) applies at every level: whatever grounds the second-order beliefs, it must itself be shared, and this generates a third-order belief, and so on without end.
(6) Finite agents cannot hold an actually infinite hierarchy of distinct beliefs.
(7) Therefore finite agents cannot satisfy the reductive account of joint action.
(8) But finite agents plainly do walk together.
(9) Therefore the reductive account is false, and joint action requires something irreducibly shared — a joint commitment or plural intentional state that is not decomposable into individual attitudes.

My question: is this argument valid? If not, which step fails, and why does it still fail on the strongest reading you can give it? If it is valid, which premise must a reductive account reject, and what cost does that rejection carry? I especially want to know whether the damage is done at step (5) — the move from shared grounds to an infinite hierarchy — or already at step (3), by requiring the grounds to be shared in the first place. Be explicit about the differences among common knowledge, mutual belief, and a shared basis, because I suspect an equivocation among those is doing the work.
