I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, and in my department's ethics working group a dispute about filial duties has run for two years without either side moving. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself rather than a verdict on which view is true.

One side holds the debt model: obligations grown children have toward their parents are grounded in what parents actually did — years of care, material support, and sacrifice generate duties of repayment or gratitude, whose content tracks the size and character of those past contributions. These obligations are backward-looking; they can survive cooling of affection, and they explain why even an emotionally difficult parent can retain some claim.

The other side holds the friendship model: there is no debt-like obligation of repayment at all. Whatever an adult child owes arises from the ongoing relationship and only insofar as that relationship is reciprocal, voluntary, and valued by both parties, as in friendship. The past matters, but only because it shapes the present relationship; it is not an independent ledger. Abuse, neglect, or estrangement therefore do not cancel a debt; they show that no relationship from which obligations could arise existed or remains.

The striking thing is that the two camps agree on nearly every concrete case, and each absorbs the other's favored examples. The debt theorist argues that if obligations rest only on the present relationship, a grown child could walk away from a loving parent for no reason and extinguish all obligation by ending the relationship — yet we think that is wrong. The friendship theorist replies that we also blame someone who drops a close friend suddenly and without cause, and that blame does not require positing a debt. Conversely, when a parent says that the child owes them after everything the parent did, both camps find something off: the debt theorist diagnoses a failure of proportion or tone, while the friendship theorist diagnoses the mistake of treating a relationship as an account balance. Each side concedes the other's cases and denies only that the other's explanation is fundamental.

My working hypothesis is that the dispute concerns whether the past can be a source of normative reasons independently of its presence in a present relationship — a dispute in the metaphysics of moral reasons rather than a first-order dispute about what children should do. On this hypothesis, the word "owe" is doing double duty: a ledger-like claim against particular past actions versus a normative relation that exists only within a live interpersonal relationship, so each side treats the other as misdescribing the shared cases rather than answering a different question. But it might instead be a substantive disagreement about whether gratitude or reciprocity is a distinct, non-derivative source of obligation. I want a diagnosis, not an adjudication. Is the disagreement verbal, substantive, or two positions answering different questions under one word? Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a new distinction, a test case engineered to separate the views, a burden-of-argument claim, or a recognition that the debate as framed cannot be settled — and why?
