I've been trying to explain deathism — not the abstract view that radical life extension would be bad, but the psychology of why otherwise sensible people keep saying they wouldn't want to live much longer even after you've answered their first three objections. I've been calling it the "deadline self." The claim is that a person's identity is partly constituted by their life having a terminus, the way a story is constituted by its ending. Not that death is good, and not that endless life would eventually get boring. The point is prior to that: if you remove the finish line you don't extend the race, you dissolve the runner. A life with no end can't be surveyed as a whole, can't be about anything, and for a lot of people being a self just is being the protagonist of one completable story. So radical life extension reads as a threat to who they are rather than to their plans. The person who wakes up in year 300 isn't them; it's the same body continuing, but the self — the narrative unit — is gone.

I know this sounds like I've relabeled the old immortality-would-be-boring argument, and I also know there's a family of views that say a person is the story they tell about themselves. I think mine differs in one respect: the boredom argument says an endless life would run out of things worth doing; my view says the damage isn't in the future content but in losing the ending itself. The ending is doing identity-work right now, not merely failing to provide more good chapters later. And I think the view makes a testable prediction: people whose self-conception is strongly narrative — they think of their life as having an arc, they plan their own funeral or write drafts of their own eulogy, they hate spoilers and unfinished things — should be more deathist, while people who don't think of their life as a story at all, or who genuinely expect an afterlife, should be less deathist, even controlling for fear of death.

Maybe that's to cute by half, and maybe "deadline self" is just a figure of speech dressed up as a mechanism.
