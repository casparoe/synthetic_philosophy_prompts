I'm a software engineer, and I follow a strict rule about what counts as evidence for a model or theory: if the evidence was already known when the model was proposed, I treat the model's agreement with that evidence as nearly worthless. At work, this means I judge a model by how well it generalizes to held-out data rather than by how well it fits the training set — no matter how reasonable the priors or how elegant the in-sample fit. In everyday reasoning, I apply the same rule: I'm unimpressed by economic or historical narratives that were reverse-engineered from the very events they claim to explain. The rule obviously guards against overfitting and post hoc rationalization. But it also feels too blunt — a theory that unifies a large and varied body of already-known data without making new predictions can hardly be worthless. Is my rule defensible? Under what conditions, if any, should old evidence count in favor of a theory, and can I state a principled version of my rule that keeps the protection without a blanket bias against explaining what was already known?
