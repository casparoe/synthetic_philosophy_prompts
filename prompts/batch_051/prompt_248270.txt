Take Descartes's evil demon, but change one parameter: instead of an all-powerful deceiver, it's a 90 percent demon. Most of the time it feeds you a perfectly coherent, normal experience, but about one episode in ten it slips — a chair looks square for a second and then round, a word on a page reads differently across two glances, a phone number you just checked changes when you look back. You know the demon works this way and you know the 90/10 rate, but you can never tell at the time which kind of episode your in. Does this version make Cartesian skepticism stronger or weaker? My first thought: stronger, because now you have positive evidence you're being deceived. Then I thought: weaker, because glitches can be tracked, cross-checked, and statistically quarantined — except Descartes's whole point was that if the demon can fake the world, it can fake your records and your cross-checks too, and that's basically the problem of the criterion in miniature: any method for sorting glitchy from reliable episodes is itself built out of possibly-glitched experiences. So where does that leave the argument? And how does Humean naturalism fit — the point that you can't actually live skeptical doubt, and nature just makes you believe? Is a 90 percent demon still a case where nature wins and you go back to daily life, or does falling short of omnipotence change the practical epistemology in a way that matters?
