Question. Historians of mathematics often warn against reading old texts anachronistically. In your own words, explain what anachronism means in this context, and use one example from the history of the concept of a function to show what an anachronistic reading can obscure.

Student answer. Anachronism is when you read modern concepts and modern notation back into a historical text as if the author already had them. For the function concept, a standard mistake is to see Euler's definition of a function as an analytic expression and treat it as a rough first pass at our modern idea of a function as an arbitrary mapping. But Euler wasnt groping toward our concept; he was working with a different object, in which a function was essentially something given by a formula. Treating his definition as a foggier version of ours makes the history look like a steady climb toward the current concept, and that hides what actually changed. A related point: Dirichlet completed the modern set-theoretic definition of a function, namely as a set of ordered pairs satisfying a uniqueness condition, and this is what finally made Euler's remarks precise. Of course you cant read any old text with no modern concepts at all, so the real danger is only when you forget that the translation into modern terms is your own.
