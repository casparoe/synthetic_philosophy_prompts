I'm an ML engineer and I work with Pearl-style causal models enough that I think natively in DAGs, do-calculus, counterfactuals, mediation analysis, and path-specific effects. I know the deontology side mostly from arguments on twitter, so go easy on the jargon. Here's what I keep doing: the distinction between what you intend and what you merely foresee looks like a graph property to me. If the bad effect lies on the path action -> bad thing -> good outcome, you're intending it as a means; if it's on a side branch, its merely foreseen. The terror bomber / tactical bomber pair fits that reading cleanly.

But then the paradox of deontology seems to show this is too clean. A deontologist says you may not kill one innocent person even when doing so would stop five other agents from killing; in causal terms you're allowed to weigh downstream killings when they run through someone elses do(.) but not when they run through your own intervention. That is weird from inside a structural causal model, because a graph doesn't mark any variable as me — every intervention is just a do(.) and the deaths in the model are the same deaths.

So I'd like a full restatement. Take the deontological package — the intending vs foreseeing distinction, the doing vs allowing distinction, and the agent-relative constraints that generate the paradox — and translate it as faithfully as you can into structural causal model vocabulary. Then give me the residue report. What maps cleanly onto DAGs and interventions, what had to be fudged, and what has no counterpart at all? In particular, can the causal formalism express why the path through my own action is specially impermissible, or does agent-relativity live entirely outside the graph? And is that residue a defect in my causal vocabulary or a sign that the deontologists were hiding a mystery inside words like ought and duty?
