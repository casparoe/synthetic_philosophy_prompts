I am a doctoral student working on the epistemology of adversarial institutions, and I have been trying to work out whether structured argument could be designed as an actual sport and what that sport would do to the practice of arguing. Suppose a well-funded nonprofit establishes a competitive reasoning league with the following format. In each match, two contestants are assigned by lot to defend one side of a live but publicly accessible proposition, an empirical, policy, or interpretive claim drawn from a standing list. They may not choose their side, and the draw is weighted so that no contestant defends the same side more than two-thirds of the time across a season. The match opens with a blind-scored steelman: each contestant writes the strongest version they can of the opposing position, and the quality of that restatement is scored before the argument proper begins. In the main rounds, contestants exchange timed arguments and replies under a rubric that awards points for accurately restating an opponent's claim before criticising it, for stating an explicit probability or confidence level for each of their own premises and conclusions and then updating it when presented with counter-evidence, and for formally conceding a sub-claim when the agreed record supports it. The rubric deducts points for equivocation, for shifting between a strong and a weak version of the same claim, and for piling up unargued objections. In the final round, contestants must switch sides and argue for the position they had been attacking, with penalties for any contradiction with their earlier statements relative to the same evidence. Judging is handled by a rotating panel whose inter-rater reliability is published each season, and judges receive a bonus when their rankings of a match agree with retrospective expert ratings of the same match produced a year later.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a moral or intellectual ideal. What would it actually incentivise among contestants once serious competitors begin training for the scoring system rather than for persuading a neutral observer? What would it select for — calibration, candour, strategic vagueness, performative humility, something else — and how would that depend on the details of the rubric and the judging bonus? Where is the scheme likely to be gamed first, and what second-order effects would it have on public argument if its matches became widely watched? Finally, which failure modes could be fixed without gutting whatever epistemic value the format was meant to have, and which failures are probably inseparable from turning reasoning into a scored competition? Please answer in British English.
