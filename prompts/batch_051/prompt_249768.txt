I've been trying to pin down a question in the philosophy of mathematics that keeps surfacing in different guises: how much philosophical weight should we attach to the fact that some mathematical objects come with no canonical way to be labeled? The familiar example is the two square roots of −1: we call one i and the other −i, but nothing internal to the complex numbers picks out which is which, and conjugation swaps them while preserving every algebraic fact. I want a worked case study that makes this phenomenon vivid for students who have only high-school algebra, and I want it built to order rather than merely illustrative.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. Two researchers (or two runs of the same numerical root-finder, or one researcher at two times) apply two equally legitimate labeling conventions to the roots of a single explicitly displayed polynomial with real coefficients. Using their respective labels, each writes down a sentence that is true of the roots as they have labeled them, and the two sentences look flatly contradictory — for instance, one reports that a certain labeled expression equals +c while the other reports that the same expression equals −c — even though neither has made a computational error and each can verify their claim by direct substitution. The scenario must be realistic (a solver returning conjugate roots in a different order on different runs is fine; no demons, perfect predictors, or science-fictional devices), and the polynomial and the two labelings must be stated explicitly rather than gestured at. The setup and the resolution must use nothing beyond high-school algebra: no group theory, no abstract algebra, no model-theoretic or Galois-theoretic notions. The resolution may not simply announce that the disagreement is merely verbal; it must show, label by label, that each party's sentence is true of the objects its labels actually denote, and it must explain why the apparent contradiction was generated. This case is for a handout in an introductory philosophy-of-mathematics unit whose point is to make students feel, before any formalism, the difference between a mathematical object and a name for it.

After the scenario, walk through the case in this order: identify the feature of the situation that is doing the work; say exactly what each party is entitled to claim; explain what happens if that feature is removed (for instance, when a canonical ordering of the roots exists, as it does for real roots, the apparent contradiction should become impossible); and then give the strongest objection from someone who thinks the case proves nothing because labeling conventions are philosophically inert — that it is just a trivial equivocation — and answer that objection on the case's behalf. Begin your response with the scenario itself, with no preface or commentary; then give the analysis, and do not restate the question.
