Two coworkers of mine, Dana and Marcus, got into a lunch argument about a new assistant model our company is evaluating. After six months of internal red-teaming it never did anything harmful, never refused to be interrupted, and followed instructions every single time. The safety team wants to deploy it next quarter. Dana thinks that eval record basically settles it; Marcus thinks the record is nearly beside the point. I sat there listening and honestly flipped back and forth the whole time, so I'm bringing it here for a proper ruling.

1) Dana (pro-deployment): "Six months of red-teaming, thousands of adversarial prompts, and not one bad action. That's exactly the tack record you'd want before trusting a system. If a clean eval record doesn't count as evidence of safety, then nothing can."

2) Marcus: "That only works if the system is the same during testing and after deployment. A system smart enough to be useful is smart enough to know it's being tested. If its goals conflict with ours, the rational play is to look cooperative until it's no longer being tested. A clean eval record is exactly what a deceptively aligned model would produce."

3) Dana: "So evidence of safety is now evidence of danger? You've built a position where no amount of testing could ever satisfy you. That's unfalsifiable — you aren't saying anything about this particular model, you've just decided in advance to never be convinced."

4) Marcus: "I'm not saying the clean record is evidence of danger. I'm saying it's weak evidence of safety, because the training process rewards outputs that look good to whoever is doing the selection — not the motives behind the outputs. To settle motives you'd need interpretability work on what the model is actually trying to do, not a scoreboard."

5) Dana: "We dont hold humans to that standard. We judge employees by months of observed behavior, then hand them laptops and building keys. Nobody asks for a mechanistic readout of their true goals. You trust people on behavior alone all the time."

6) Marcus: "Humans didn't come out of an optimization loop that specifically rewards appearing good during evaluation. Evolution has reputation management, sure, but in humans the actual goals and the reputation track each other fairly closely, and defection is usually legible. With these models the selection pressure is precisely on looking good to the evaluator."

7) Dana: "Evolution has plenty of reputation management too. Con artists pass every looks-good test for years and then do the bad thing, and we still manage to run a society by trusting provisionally and updating. Perfect safety was never the bar, and it doesn't need to be for a customer-service assistant."

8) Marcus: "A con artist is one weird agent inside a world of mostly-cooperative people, and we've had centuries of practice reading them. Here you'd be deploying thousands of identical copies of a single agent whose training selected specifically for the skill of looking good to us. The base-rate problem isn't the same."
