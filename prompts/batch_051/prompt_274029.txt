Two members of my reading group on collective decision-making spent most of an evening locked in an argument I ended up moderating, and neither would give ground. Elena opened with the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem: for deterministic single-winner rules in which at least three candidates are allowed, every profile of strict rankings is admissible, and no candidate is ruled out in advance, the only strategy-proof rules are dictatorial. So, she said, anyone who insists on designing a voting rule under which no voter ever benefits from reporting an insincere ranking is chasing an impossibility rather than solving a design problem. Marcus replied that the theorem's force depends entirely on a package of conditions that real institutions can and should relax. With only two candidates, sincere voting is a dominant strategy under simple majority rule, and many open contests collapse onto a de facto pair. With three or more candidates but preferences single-peaked along a shared left–right dimension, there are non-dictatorial and strategy-proof rules, such as selecting the median of reported peaks when the number of voters is odd. And once randomization is permitted, a rule like random dictatorship—draw one voter at random and implement that voter's top choice—is strategy-proof in the sense that no voter can improve her expected outcome by misreporting. The clean impossibility, he argued, is a theorem about a very particular set of assumptions, and the interesting flexibility lies in choosing which assumption to drop.

Elena answered that the two-candidate and single-peaked cases are precisely restrictions on the domain, and leave her original unrestricted three-candidate claim untouched. Random dictatorship, she continued, is not a counterexample to the deterministic theorem but a lottery over dictators, and is just as unacceptable as an answer to the original problem because it redistributes arbitrary power by chance rather than removing it. Marcus responded that this last move changes the subject: the theorem is a result about incentives, not about her preferred distribution of influence, and a procedure under which lying never helps is precisely a strategy-proof procedure, whatever one thinks of its normative credentials; dismissing randomization as beneath consideration imports unargued constraints into what had been presented as a mathematical limitation. At that point they turned to me for a referee's verdict. I need a point-by-point adjudication, not merely a winner: at each stage of the exchange, please say which claims are correct, which claims are overstated, and what both parties are overlooking about the relation between the theorem's assumptions and the ways one might genuinely escape them.
