In the advanced course on causal explanation that I teach to a mixed group of philosophers and statisticians, we spend several weeks on Pearl’s hierarchy and on what a structural causal model actually commits one to. The standard story my students learn is that causal claims come in three grades—associational, interventional, and counterfactual—and that moving from a lower grade to a higher one is never free: it requires importing assumptions, typically a causal graph, structural equations, independent exogenous disturbances, and the absence of omitted common causes. I have been trying to press on what would happen if a scientific community were made to pay a heavy and public price for each of those assumptions, rather than being allowed to leave them implicit in a paragraph of caveats.

Suppose the editors of all the major journals in epidemiology, economics, and quantitative social science announce that, beginning on 1 January 2030, no manuscript that draws a causal conclusion from observational data may be published unless it does all of the following: (a) states the claim on the correct rung of Pearl’s hierarchy instead of sliding from an associational estimate to an interventional or counterfactual one; (b) publishes a complete causal graph and explicit structural equations, with distributions for the exogenous errors, for every variable that enters the argument; (c) supplies a do-calculus proof that the reported quantity is identical to the causal effect claimed under the stated model; and (d) reports sensitivity checks for the identifying assumptions, including disturbance independence and the claim that all common causes are measured. Randomized experiments remain outside the rule. Manuscripts that fail any part are rejected on method, and the journals fund a five-year program to train methods reviewers in evaluating these materials. Apart from the rule and that training, nothing else changes: the data, software, funding streams, publication pressure, and day-to-day culture of the fields remain exactly as they now are.

Give me your best reasoned prediction of how these fields look fifteen years later, around 2045. I want the visible chain of mechanisms rather than a story: how research groups, graduate curricula, and journals adapt; how much of the rule’s pressure is absorbed by relabeling, evasion, or migration to lower-threshold venues; which sorts of causal claim quietly vanish and which survive intact; and whether the rule achieves what its designers presumably wanted. For each major prediction, tell me whether it is robust or fragile, and state the key assumption that would have to fail for you to be wrong. I ask because I want to use your answer as a foil in the seminar, not because I am seeking a ruling on whether the rule is wise.

Please structure the response as follows: (1) Immediate adaptation, roughly 200 words; (2) Second-order equilibrium, roughly 300 words; (3) Surviving and vanishing claims, roughly 200 words; (4) Robust versus fragile predictions, roughly 200 words; (5) Bottom line: did the rule work, roughly 150 words. Use confidence levels—high, medium, low—throughout, and make clear which links in your reasoning depend on contested assumptions.
