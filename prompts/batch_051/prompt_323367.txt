Here's a puzzle about the American tax system I've been chewing on. The federal estate tax is advertised as a tool for breaking up dynastic wealth — its exemption threshold is so high that only the very largest estates pay it, and politicians talk about it as if inherited fortunes where a problem for equal opportunity, since they give some kids a huge head start through no effort of their own. But at the same time, heirs get a step-up in basis: if someone dies holding assets that appreciated during their life, the gain is simply never taxed as capital gains. That means a fortune held as unrealized appreciation (grandpa bought stock in 1998 and never sold) can pass on without anyone paying income tax on the gain. And then there are dynasty trusts, which in some states can be set up so that, combined with the generation-skipping transfer tax exemption, the same pot of money skips estate taxation generation after generation while still supporting the family. So one part of the system announces that huge inherited windfalls are a problem, and another part quietly builds a tunnel around that problem.

What theory of fairness — or equal opportunity, or desert — does this whole setup actually presuppose? I don't mean what the politicians say; I mean the implicit view that would make the practice internally coherent. Reconstruct that view as charitably as you can, then tell me which parts the practice strictly needs and which are just optional gloss. Is it compatible with a bunch of different theories, or does it only make sense if you believe something quite specific about what makes a transfer of wealth okay? And what would the code look like if we swapped in the opposite view — say, that an inherited windfall should be treated like any other income?
