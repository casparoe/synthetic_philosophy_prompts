Suppose that, beginning in 2026, the legislature of a medium-sized, well-governed democracy—call it Meridian—amends the constitution to make it a fundamental objective of the state to reach first place in the World Happiness Report within fifteen years and to remain there. Meridian currently sits in the upper third of the table but outside the top ten. An independent Office of Wellbeing is created. Every bill before parliament must carry a certificate from the Office estimating its expected effect on the national mean life-evaluation score, with the underlying reasoning published; the Office may suspend any bill whose certified estimate is negative unless a three-fifths majority overrides it. The constitution instructs the Office to treat the national mean, rather than the spread of scores or the lower tail of the distribution, as the target, and the Office's own budget is set by a statutory formula indexed to the country's subsequent position in the ranking. The ranking itself continues to be compiled, as it is now, by an outside organisation: annually, a representative sample of residents is asked to place themselves on a ladder from zero to ten, countries are compared by their mean scores, and the report publishes a set of social and economic indicators alongside the league table. Meridian cannot alter the wording of the question, the sampling strategy, or the publication of its results, and it cannot lawfully exclude any low-scoring group from the survey. Assume no other country changes its behaviour in response to Meridian's new constitutional goal.

Give a reasoned prediction, step by step, of how Meridian's politics, public administration, media and ordinary social life change over the fifteen-year period. Throughout, keep two questions apart: which changes would raise the recorded score, and which changes would make ordinary lives go better? Set out the feedback loops by which each may drive or undercut the other. Identify which predicted effects are robust features of the incentive structure, and which depend on assumptions about how quickly citizens, officials and firms adapt to the measure. State what would have to be true for Meridian to top the table while becoming a worse place to live, and for it to fail to reach the top while becoming a better one. Indicate your confidence in each principal link in the argument. Conclude by saying, with reasons, how far a country ought in your view to want to top such a ranking at all.

Please write your answer in British English.
