Act as an expert in moral philosophy who has designed discussion-heavy seminars. I’m teaching an upper-level seminar on moral standing, and I want to base one session around Jeremy Bentham’s point in the Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation where he argues, in a footnote, roughly that the key question about nonhuman animals is not whether they can reason or talk but whether they can suffer, and that a mature horse or dog is more rational and more communicative than a human infant of a day old, yet the infant is protected and the animal isn’t. I’m paraphrasing, not quoting.

Your job: design a 75-minute classroom activity for 14 students that makes them feel the force of the idea that sentience and moral considerability come in degrees — that there can be a graded scale running from something like a thermostat up through insects, fish, dogs, primates, and humans, and that the boundaries are messier then species categories suggest. The activity should be a game or structured exercise where participants have to allocate some scarce resource (harm-reduction tokens, care hours, rescue priorities, something like that) across a range of entities, and the rules should be set up so that they stumble into the problem themselves before it gets named. Think carefully about the mechanics: start by brainstorming two candidate structures, tell me why you reject one, then give full rules, roles, a timeline, scoring if relevant, and a debrief script. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when it’s actually run and how a facilitator should handle it, and be honest about which part of the degreed view the activity can’t reproduce — e.g., whether the scale is cardinal or merely ordinal.
