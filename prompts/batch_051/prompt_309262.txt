I am a novelist, and I pay close attention to how moral language behaves in dialogue and interior monologue. This has led me to a rule of thumb for judging philosophical theories of moral language: if an account says that a sentence like "stealing is wrong" is not really a statement but something more like an expression of feeling or a command, I require it to explain how such sentences can still function meaningfully inside conditionals, negations, and questions. My ear tells me that "If stealing is wrong, then teaching someone to steal is wrong" is perfectly intelligible, while "If boo to stealing, then boo to teaching someone to steal" is not. I treat any account that stumbles here as refuted. Is this embedding test a defensible epistemic policy, or am I leaning on a superficial grammatical intuition that a better expressivist theory could accommodate? Under what conditions would I be entitled to keep using it?
