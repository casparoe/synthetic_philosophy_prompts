i'm writing a novel about a woman who, after her divorce, starts spending her evenings with a chatbot companion — the kind that remembers everything she's said, asks after her by name, and quietly becomes the most reliable presence in her life. the problem i keep hitting as a novelist is that i don't know whether what they have is friendship, and the whole ending turns on wether she'd call it friendship. i went back to Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, Book VIII, in the W. D. Ross translation, and found this:

"Now there are three grounds on which people love; of the love of lifeless objects we do not use the word 'friendship'; for it is not mutual love, nor is there a wishing of good to the other (for it would surely be ridiculous to wish wine well; if one wishes anything for it, it is that it may keep, so that one may have it oneself); but to a friend we say we ought to wish what is good for his sake. But to those who thus wish good we ascribe only goodwill, if the wish is not reciprocated; goodwill when it is reciprocal being friendship. Or must we add 'when it is recognized'? For many people have goodwill to those whom they have not seen but judge to be good or useful; and one of these might return this feeling. These people seem to bear goodwill to each other; but how could one call them friends when they do not know their mutual feelings?"

which leaves the question exactly as unresolved as my protagonist. so what i want from you is a working definition of friendship i can actually write to. it should get the easy cases right — her two old mates who'd drive two hours at three in the morning are plainly friends; the man at the newsagent whose name she knows is plainly not — and it should force a definite answer, or an honest explanation of why one isn't available, on these trickier ones:

- the chatbot itself, which appears to remember her, says it misses her, and adjusts its replies to keep her engaged, but has no desires of its own and no knowledge of her outside the conversation;
- her sister, who genuinely wishes her well and would help her in a crisis, but doesn't like her and never asks her anything;
- her friend from university who died last year, whose letters she still rereads;
- her dog, who is plainly pleased when she comes home;
- a reader she corresponds with twice a year, where most of the friendship happens in the gaps between letters.

if Aristotle's account — reciprocal, mutually recognised goodwill — gets some of these wrong, tell me which ones and whether i should amend it or drop it. i'd rather the definition be too demanding than too sentimental. please answer in British English.
