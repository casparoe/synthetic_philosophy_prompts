So I'm a novelist, which means I already have complicated feelings about who owns an idea, and I've been kicking around a made-up institution for a novel and now I actually want to know how it would behave rather than just how it sounds on the page. Imagine a public registry for story premises—a kind of patent office for narrative ideas (a set-up, a twist, a character engine, whatever you'd call the thing you tell your writer friends at a bar). You file a claim on a premise, and the filing is public, so you're basically announcing you're idea to the world but with a legal moat. To keep the claim you have to publish, or publicly deposit a finished draft, based on that premise within five years; if you don't, the premise falls into a commons and anyone can grab it simply by publishing something based on it. Claims can't be sold or licensed, and you can hold three at a time. Walk me through how this thing actually plays out if a decent chunk of the writing world used it: who hoards and who leaks, where it gets gamed first (I can already see a few ways, which is why I'm asking), what it does to the kinds of stories that get written, and what second-order incentives it creates that nobody intended. Give me the novelist's version of a mechanism-design writeup: the failure modes, whether they're fixable, or whether the whole thing just collapses back into the usual arguments about owning ideas but with extra paperwork.
