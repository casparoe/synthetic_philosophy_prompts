I'm retired now — spent my career in population ecology, counting things and fitting models — and only lately got into philosophy. I've been working through Sen's liberal paradox, and its one of those things that looks like a neat impossibility result until you try to say what it actually shows, and then everyone talks past each other. So here's what I'd like.

Write the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps on Sen's liberal paradox.

Camp A ("rights-first"): the right response to the paradox is to keep minimal liberalism intact — each person decisive over their own private choice — and to restrict the Pareto principle so it can't override a protected sphere. For them, rights are side-constraints, not preferences to be weighed against other preferences.

Camp B ("Pareto-first"): the right response is to keep Pareto unrestricted and to weaken or redraw the decisiveness condition, e.g. by excluding meddlesome preferences (preferences about other people's private conduct) from the social calculus. For them, the paradox shows that a protected sphere, naively drawn, lets externalities sneak in.

Context: the standard example has Prude and Lewd and one racy novel. Prude ranks nobody reading it above reading it himself above Lewd reading it; Lewd ranks Prude reading it above reading it himself above nobody reading it. If each is decisive over whether he himself reads, and Pareto holds, you get a cycle. Don't restate the theorem; assume I know it.

The document should have:

1. A numbered "agreed claims" section — claims both camps can sign about what the paradox does and doesn't establish, and about what counts as a resolution versus a change of subject. Make them substantive, not just "Sen proved a theorem" (and leave dates out; I don't care about them).

2. A disagreements table. Each row gives one precise point of disagreement, with the rights-first formulation in one column and the Pareto-first formulation in the other, each phrased so its own side would accept it.

3. A cruxes section: two or three sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, with a note on which side would move and how much.

4. For each crux, a concrete case or test both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction about that case written down in advance.

Two things I genuinely want the statement to wrestle with: (i) whether the Prude/Lewd setup is rigged by letting the meddlesome preferences — Prude's about Lewd reading, Lewd's about Prude reading — do the Pareto work, and whether a non-meddlesome version of the paradox is even possible; (ii) whether it matters that the cycle runs over who reads a single copy of a book, rather than over policies a goverment could actually implement. If you think part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why.

Write it as a document both camps could actually put their names to, not as a lecture. I'd rather have sharp agreed claims and an honest residual disagreement than diplomatic mush.
