I am leading a single eighty-minute first-year undergraduate seminar on the Mencius–Xunzi dispute over human nature. The students have read only the Mencius passage about the alarm one feels at seeing a child about to fall into a well and the Xunzi passage in which moral cultivation is compared to straightening warped wood with steam and clamps; most of them currently think Mencius is naive and Xunzi is a cynic about human improvement. Plan the session's explanatory arc so that the dispute appears by the end as a disagreement over what morality is—the cultivation of native tendencies (Mencius's sprouts) versus their transformation by artifice (Xunzi's craft)—rather than a difference in temperament. Set out, in order, the ideas that must be in place before either example can do its work, including what must first be said about the term usually translated as nature; the one carrying example you would build around and what it must show and not show; the misconceptions students are likely to bring or form and the point in the sequence at which each should be defused; what you would deliberately leave out at this level and how you would mark that boundary; and a final check that would show they have understood the disagreement rather than merely learned which label goes with which figure.
