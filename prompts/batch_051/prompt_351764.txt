I'm an ML engineer and in a planning meeting I floated using a "helpfulness" rubric as our north-star metric for evaluating a fine-tuned model. A colleague immediately cut me off with Goodhart's law — he said, and I think this is the standard phrasing, "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" — and concluded we shouldn't adopt any metric at all. I get the intuition, but I'm pretty sure that one sentence is doing several different jobs at once: some days it sounds like a boring fact about people fudging their dashboards, other times like a deep claim about measurement in general, and occasionally like the absurdly strong claim that no target can survive contact with an optimiser. We're not even mostly worried about humans gaming the rubric; we're worried about the model overfitting to it during training, which feels more like specification gaming and might not be what the slogan was ever about.

I'd like you to act as a philosopher of measurement and incentives. Don't give me a single interpretation — lay out the distinct precise theses this line could be expressing, from the trivially true to the wildly strong, and for each say whether it's a platitude, substantive and defensible, or false. Also say, for each, whether it would cover gaming by a non-human optimiser like a model or only by people with interests, and whether it would actually get my colleague his conclusion that the rational move is adopt no metric. Start by brainstorming candidate readings and then sort them out. I especially want to know what "a good measure" has to mean for the slogan to have any teeth, and what "ceases to be a good measure" has to mean for the no-metric-ever conclusion to follow.
