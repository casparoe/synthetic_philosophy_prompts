A quantitative social science journal is considering a new editorial rule for any paper that reports a Bayesian analysis. Under the rule, authors may not posit a prior distribution directly. Instead, they must state a set of constraints that they claim captures all and only their prior information about the relevant parameters — for example, that a parameter lies in a known interval, or that a mean or second moment has a particular value — and then adopt, as their prior, the distribution that maximizes entropy subject to exactly those constraints. Authors must pre-register this constraint set in a public registry before gaining access to the outcome data, and they must specify the coordinate system in which the entropy maximization is carried out, with a justification of why that coordinate system is the one in which their prior information is actually expressed. The editors adopt the scheme because they are aware that maximum-entropy priors are not invariant under reparameterization and depend on a choice of underlying reference measure; they want to force those choices into the open. Reviewers are instructed to reject papers whose pre-registered constraints look gerrymandered to yield a convenient posterior, or whose coordinate choice appears post hoc.

Walk through how this rule would actually behave in practice. What would it incentivize authors to do about the choice of constraints, the choice of variables, and the level of detail in what they pre-register? Where does the subjectivity the rule is meant to expel re-enter — in the constraint set, the coordinate system, the model specification, or elsewhere? Identify the points where the scheme would fail first, and assess whether pre-registration closes the largest loophole or merely moves the gaming earlier in the research process. Conclude with your judgment about whether the rule selects for honest, replicable statements of uncertainty or for a subtler way of laundering a desired prior into a paper.
