I run the philosophy club at my high school; we have fifteen regular members, ages sixteen and seventeen, and a single one-hour meeting to work with. I would like a session that makes the members feel the difference between an epistemic bubble and an echo chamber, rather than hear it defined, and I want the distinction to surface in their own decisions before the terms are introduced.

Please design a runnable activity rather than a discussion plan. It should center on a small-group decision problem with a determinate correct answer — a resource, route, or diagnosis problem would work. Include clear rules, roles or materials, a point schedule that does not involve real money, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the hour; the activity should require no internet access, phones, or purchased materials. Choose a self-contained version of the problem so that the exercise isolates the mechanics of information and trust instead of reopening political disagreements.

Build the activity around the following contrast. An epistemic bubble is an informational deficit: the person inside is missing relevant evidence or arguments, and when the missing material actually reaches them, they tend to update. An echo chamber is a trust structure: the person inside is not merely missing contrary voices but has been given reasons in advance to distrust those voices, so contrary evidence arrives and is discounted as unreliable or hostile. The difference should show up in behavior — for instance, two groups facing the same decision problem, one failing because it lacks a piece of information and the other failing because it has been taught to distrust the people carrying that information. Do not lecture this distinction up front, and do not name the two concepts until the debrief.

Make definite choices rather than offering me a menu of alternatives: the exact group composition, the content and wording of the materials, the scoring rule, and what the facilitator says at each transition. Defend each choice in a sentence or two by saying what it is designed to make participants do or feel. Begin your response with the bottom line in the first sentence: the name of the activity and the one-sentence insight it is engineered to produce, with no preamble and no restating of my request. Then give the full design and your reasoning.

Finally, tell me what is likely to go wrong the first time I run the activity and how I should handle it, and be candid about which feature of the echo-chamber/bubble distinction the exercise cannot reproduce, so I know what I must supply myself in the debrief.
