I'm a PhD candidate in social psychology working on the moral psychology of political disagreement, and in two weeks I'm giving a fifty-minute guest session in a third-year undergraduate political psychology seminar. The students have already read Haidt and can accurately describe moral foundations theory—the five (or six) foundations, the liberal and conservative weighting differences, and the standard illustrative cases. My job is to move them past the descriptive claim to the harder question on the seminar's syllabus: assuming the broad shape of moral foundations theory is right about how the moral judgments of left and right are organized, does that psychology tell us anything about which side is more likely to be correct?

I don't want to re-teach the foundations. I want the session to be about the inference—or the missing inference—from a descriptive moral psychology to any debunking, conciliatory, or relativistic conclusion. Don't explain the issue to me; plan how I should explain it to them. I need the explanatory arc worked out: what distinction has to be in place before the question even grips them, which later point depends on which earlier point, and the one concrete case that should carry the whole session, with each part of the argument drawn out of it. Tell me what that case must have and must lack. I suspect the obvious choice—a current issue that cleanly splits left and right—will backfire by teaching students to treat the foundations as team uniforms; if you think I'm wrong, say why, but commit to one example and justify it against that worry. I also need the misconception map. In my experience they bring three recurring shortcuts: that if each side has its own psychological foundations, political disagreement is just a difference in temperament and no one is right; that any psychological or evolutionary explanation of a moral belief automatically debunks it; and that moral foundations theory is itself a brief for conservatism because it treats loyalty, authority, and sanctity as moral concerns. Show me where in the sequence each of these will surface, how to head it off at exactly that point rather than swat it away, and how to make the session absorb the objection into the larger point. Also tell me what must be left out at this level and how to flag that without making the hour feel incomplete. End with one question I can ask them to tell whether the distinction has actually landed.

Make definite choices—one arc, one example, one closing question—and defend them rather than laying out options for me to choose among. Write the plan in American English.
