I keep noticing a weird asymmetry in how I talk about unpredictability, depending on whether it shows up in a physics paper or in my own code. When a meteorologist says the weather is chaotic and that a butterfly flapping its wings somewhere can change the course of a hurricane, I nod along and think "deep." But when a tiny difference in a timestamp or a floating-point rounding error flips the output of a deterministic simulation I wrote, my first instinct is to call it a bug — something fragile and broken, not something profound. Both are cases where a deterministic system is so sensitive to initial conditions that you can't know the outcome without running it. So why do I treat the atmosphere's sensitivity as a feature of nature and my program's sensitivity as a defect? Maybe the difference is that I could, in principle, pin down the inputs to my program, whereas the atmosphere has too many degrees of freedom to measure. But in practice I can't pin down my program's inputs either — that's the whole reason the rounding error got me. Is there a principled line between "chaotic system doing something mathematically interesting" and "deterministic system that's just pathalogically sensitive," or is it just a vibe based on which one I find annoying? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the case where the two are sensitivity-identical — same Lyapunov exponent, if you want to be annoying about it. And I want an honest verdict on whether I should start describing my flaky build as "chaotic" or stop being impressed by the butterfly effect.
