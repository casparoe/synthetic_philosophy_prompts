I'm a longtime reader of LessWrong, Overcoming Bias, and adjacent forums, and I've internalized the norms around adversarial collaborations: steelman the opposing position, avoid isolated demands for rigor, register predictions before the evidence is consulted, and separate verbal from substantive disagreements. I want you to apply those norms to the socialist calculation debate.

Write the joint statement that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between the Mises-Hayek camp and the Lange-Lerner camp could both sign. The Mises-Hayek camp holds that rational economic calculation under socialism is in principle impossible without private ownership of the means of production and genuine competitive markets, because prices for capital goods must emerge from actual exchange rather than from a planning board, and because the knowledge such calculation requires is dispersed, tacit, and not collectible by any central authority. The Lange-Lerner camp holds that a central planning board can set parametric prices and adjust them through trial and error in response to shortages and surpluses, thereby simulating a competitive market closely enough to achieve allocative efficiency without private ownership of the means of production, and that the information and incentive problems can be handled by suitable rules.

The document must contain the following:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These must be substantive enough to be worth signing, and both camps must be able to accept every sentence without reservation. Cover at least: what the dispute is and is not about; what would count as rational economic calculation; what prices are being asked to do; and what each camp is not claiming.

2. A disagreements table. Each row gives the issue in neutral terms, then states the Mises-Hayek position and the Lange-Lerner position, each worded the way that camp itself would accept, with no strawmanning and no asymmetric labels.

3. A crux section. Identify the sub-questions such that settling one would cause at least one camp to revise its view. Phrase each crux so both camps accept that it is a genuine crux. At least one crux must be formulated at the level of a mechanism or information flow — for example, whether successive parametric-price revisions remain unbiased once managers anticipate the revision rule and adjust their reports — rather than as a restatement of each side's overall conclusion.

4. For each crux, specify one concrete case, mechanism, or argument that both camps agree bears on it, and record each camp's prediction in advance, pre-registration style. Stylized settings are acceptable and preferred where they make the predictions specific enough that the two sides cannot both be comfortably right.

5. If any part of the dispute is verbal or definitional — for example, over the word 'market,' 'socialism,' or 'calculation' — say which part and why, and state the substantive remainder.

Write the document itself, not a meta-commentary about how it was negotiated. Do not include a bibliography, footnotes, page references, or citations to specific works; the claims should stand on the arguments rather than on attributions. Keep the whole document to at most 1,200 words.
