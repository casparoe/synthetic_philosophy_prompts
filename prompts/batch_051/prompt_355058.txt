After a career in physical chemistry, I have been running a small reading group on decision theory for colleagues who, like me, came to the subject late. We are currently working through the Kelly criterion, and I set the short-answer problem below about a week ago. One of the group members—a former statistician, so I want to be fair and precise—submitted the answer that follows. The question was: "A bettor with current bankroll W is offered a long sequence of independent, identical bets. On each bet she may stake any fraction f, with 0 ≤ f ≤ 1, of her current bankroll, the rest being held in cash. Each dollar staked is returned together with one dollar of profit if the bet wins (probability p > 1/2) and is lost if the bet loses (probability q = 1 − p). (a) Derive the value of f that maximizes the expected logarithm of her bankroll after one bet. (b) Say as precisely as you can what this strategy is supposed to achieve over many bets—in particular, whether it also maximizes expected terminal wealth, and why or why not. (c) Give one assumption under which the strategy is uncontroversially correct, and one circumstance in which it is not."

Her answer was: "(a) After one bet the bankroll is (1 + f)W on a win and (1 − f)W on a loss, so expected log-wealth is p log(1 + f) + q log(1 − f). Differentiating with respect to f gives p/(1 + f) − q/(1 − f); setting this to zero yields p(1 − f) = q(1 + f), hence p − pf = q + qf, so f = p − q. For p = 0.6 the optimal stake is 20 percent of the bankroll, and for p = 0.75 it is 50 percent. (b) Since the logarithm is a strictly increasing function, the f that maximizes E[log W'] is the same as the f that maximizes E[W']; the log is just an alternative scale on which to measure wealth. Over n independent bets, log terminal wealth is a sum of n independent one-period log-returns, so the law of large numbers implies that the Kelly bettor's terminal wealth exceeds that of any other fixed-fraction bettor with probability approaching one, and at any finite horizon it maximizes the probability of reaching any fixed wealth target. Thus Kelly loses no expected value; it is the policy with both the highest expected terminal wealth and the highest long-run growth rate. (c) The derivation plainly assumes that the bettor's utility is logarithmic in wealth. If utility is not logarithmic, a sufficiently risk-averse bettor could rationally choose a smaller stake, such as half-Kelly, so the criterion is not a universal law of rational choice; its force depends on taking long-run growth as the objective rather than ordinary expected utility."
