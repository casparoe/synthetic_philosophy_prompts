I find that I have firm but apparently conflicting intuitions about when I literally see something through an image, as opposed to merely seeing an image of it, and I would like to know what principle, if any, captures them. When I look in a mirror, I see myself. When I watch a live broadcast of a soccer match, I see the players, not just images of the players. When I look at an old photograph of my grandmother, who died years ago, I still want to say that I see her in the photograph, even though she is no longer there to be seen. But when I look at a painted portrait of her, I do not see her; I see a painting. My working explanation is that photographs are produced mechanically by light reflected from the scene, whereas paintings are filtered through the painter's beliefs and skill. Yet other cases unsettle that explanation. A false-colored electron micrograph of a virus: I want to say I see the virus, though the image is not formed by visible light and no unaided eye could see it directly. A heavily retouched portrait photograph: I am much less sure I see the person. A synthetic image of a scene that never existed: I see no scene at all, because there was none. But a deepfake of a real politician mouthing words he never uttered: I do not see him saying those words, despite a causal link to the real man. And a stacked astronomical photograph combining many exposures of a faint galaxy: I do want to say I see the galaxy, even though no single exposure would reveal it and the eye itself would see nothing at the eyepiece. Is there a single account of seeing through an image that delivers all of these verdicts? What does the most defensible account say about a security-camera image that has been algorithmically sharpened, or a live feed delayed by several seconds? And which of my verdicts, if any, does it force me to revise?
