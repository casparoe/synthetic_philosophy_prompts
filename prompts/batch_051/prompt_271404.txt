Since I retired from biology I've been working through some philosophy of religion, and a question has been bothering me about the old objection to original sin and inherited guilt. The objection as I understand it runs something like this: guilt is only ever a property of a person's own free acts, so the idea that it could be passed from Adam to his descendants, or from a wronged ancestor to later generations, rests on a conceptual confusion. That always seemed airtight to me.

Then I started thinking about the epigenetic results from the last couple of decades — the Dutch Hunger Winter cohorts, children of severely traumatized people, that sort of thing. We now think starvation or severe stress in one generation can leave biological marks in the next, through methylation and related mechanisms, not just through culture or upbringing. The marks are not guilt, obviously. But they do seem to be a way in which an ancestor's condition, and perhaps even an ancestor's wrongdoing, materially continues in the descendants. So I'm now unsure whether the philosophical objection survives intact. If its force depended on a clean separation between what an ancestor did and what a descendant is, the biology partially blurs that separation. Does the argument still work? Which premise is actually doing the heavy lifting, and is there a repaired version that draws a sharper line between inherited damage and inherited guilt? I assume epigenetics doesnt vindicate original sin, but I want to know whether it forces us to rebuild the argument against it, or whether it leaves the argument untouched.
