Please give me a single number: your credence that an agent cannot, while genuinely deliberating about what to do, coherently assign any determinate probability to her own eventual choice. The precise claim is this: for an agent facing a live choice between incompatible options A and B, who has not yet settled the matter either causally or by prior commitment, there is no rationally stable state in which she continues to deliberate and also holds a sharp credence such as "0.7 that I will choose A." On this view, the predictive stance and the deciding stance crowd each other out, so any sharp self-assignment is either a disguised partial decision, an illicit transfer of third-person or track-record evidence into a domain where the predictor is also the chooser, or a description of a choice that was never genuinely open.

Decompose your credence into at least these sub-questions, assigning a rough probability to each with a line or two of justification: (a) does sincere deliberation require the options to remain open in a way that a determinate first-person forecast would close? (b) could an agent have the right kind of evidence about her own not-yet-made choice, given that she lacks both a settled intention and the frequency data that only fits repeating or nearly automatic choices? (c) do everyday cases—accurately predicting that you will order the usual dish or skip dessert—show that self-prediction is unproblematic, or are they cases in which the choice is effectively already made and no real deliberation is occurring?

Show how (a) through (c) combine to yield your overall credence. Then identify which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, and state what concrete empirical result or conceptual argument would move that sub-question—and roughly how far it would move your overall credence.
