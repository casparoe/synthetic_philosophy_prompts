In my reporting on education and the behavioral sciences, I keep noticing a split in institutional practice that seems deeper than a mere difference of technique. The same school will run a dense token economy in the morning: students earn points for bringing back signed forms, lose points for calling out in class, and trade accumulated points for privileges, with no requirement that anyone explain or justify the rules to them. Then, in the afternoon, the same students sit in a Socratic seminar and are asked to state a claim, anticipate the strongest objection to it, and revise their position in front of their peers. The first practice appears to presuppose that a student is the sort of creature whose future conduct is selected by consequences, and that arranging the contingencies is sufficient. The second appears to presuppose that a student is a creature that runs candidate claims internally, weighs them, and discards the worse ones before acting—and perhaps thinks better by importing tools such as language, diagrams, and assigned roles. Some researchers sort minds into just this kind of ladder: Darwinian creatures, whose designs are revised only by selection across generations; Skinnerian creatures, which are shaped within a single lifetime by reinforcement and punishment; Popperian creatures, which test hypotheses in their heads before acting; and Gregorian creatures, which augment their thought with externally borrowed tools.

What I want to know is what a school is committed to when it runs both kinds of practice at once. Reconstruct the implicit theory of the learning mind behind each practice, distinguishing what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say whether a school can coherently be Skinnerian at 9 a.m. and Popperian at 11 a.m. If the school had to pick one picture of the student and follow it everywhere, what would it have to change in its grading, discipline, lesson design, and definition of what counts as a successful learner?
