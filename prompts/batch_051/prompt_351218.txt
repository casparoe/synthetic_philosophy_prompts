Design me a decision case where the inside view and the outside view disagree in the direction people find hardest to construct: the outside view yields the optimistic, ambitious forecast and favors the long-shot option, while the inside view yields the cautious forecast and favors the safe option — the reverse of the usual planning-fallacy pattern in which the inside view is the optimist and the outside view is the sober corrective. The case is for a paper arguing that the outside view is not intrinsically cautionary, and that an epistemic-modesty rule which defers to it can inherit optimism rather than correcting for it. So the reversal must be generated by the structure of the information, not by stipulating an unduly rosy base rate from an irrelevant class.

Constraints: the decision must be an ordinary professional, educational, or personal one — no lotteries, no terminal diagnoses, no financial markets, no startup-pitch clichés. The base rate must be genuinely informative, drawn from a reference class that is homogeneous and relevant to the particular case, and the person in the scenario must not be ignoring a statistic that anyone in their position would know. State the decision and the quantity being forecast; give the inside-view reasoning from the specific features of the situation; give the reference class, its base rate, and the outside-view reasoning that supports the long shot.

Then run the epistemic-modesty check: what would a rule that starts from the outside view as a prior and moves only on legible, surprising inside information recommend in this case, and does the case show that such a rule would inherit the overconfidence it is supposed to neutralize? Finish with an honest note on which structural feature actually produces the reversal, how a defender of the outside view would attack the case by redescribing the reference class, and what minimal change would collapse it back into a standard planning-fallacy scenario.
