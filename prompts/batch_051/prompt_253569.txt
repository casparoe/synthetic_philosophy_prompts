You are a professional decision theorist and formal epistemologist. Think carefully, check every calculation yourself, and start by reconstructing the argument in your own words before giving your final diagnosis.

A friend has constructed the following argument around a version of the absent-minded driver problem, and I cannot find the exit.

Setup. A driver is on a highway with exactly two exits, A and then B. The exits are visually indistinguishable, there are no landmarks, and the driver cannot tell which exit he is at when he arrives at one. He knows the map. If he takes exit A, he is stranded in a bad neighborhood and gets payoff 0. If he takes exit B, he gets home and gets payoff 4. If he drives past both exits, he eventually reaches a motel and gets payoff 1. Before setting out, he must commit to a single behavioral plan: at whatever exit he finds himself, he continues with probability p and exits with probability 1 − p. Since he cannot distinguish the exits, the plan cannot be made conditional on whether the exit is the first or the second.

(1) Planning-stage optimum. Under this plan his expected payoff is
0·(1 − p) + 4·p(1 − p) + 1·p² = 4p − 3p².
This expression is maximized at p = 2/3, where it takes the value 4/3. So before the trip the rational plan is to continue with probability 2/3 and exit with probability 1/3.

(2) Plan–action consistency. If a plan is rational at the outset and no new information arrives, then the action it prescribes at a decision point must still look at least as good as the alternatives when the agent reaches that point. Otherwise the agent would be rationally permitted, or even required, to abandon the plan.

(3) No new information. When the driver is actually at an exit, he has not learned which exit it is. Exit A and exit B remain indistinguishable from the inside, and both remain possible.

(4) Symmetric prior. Therefore the driver, at the exit, should assign probability 1/2 to being at A and probability 1/2 to being at B.

(5) At-the-wheel values. Given those probabilities, exiting immediately is worth 2, since it pays 0 at A and 4 at B. Continuing is worth the following. If he is at A, then continuing leads to the next exit, where the same plan has him continue with probability p and exit with probability 1 − p; he therefore receives 4 with probability 1 − p and 1 with probability p, for an expected value of 4(1 − p) + 1p = 4 − 3p. If he is at B, continuing means driving past both exits, which pays 1. Weighting A and B equally, continuing is worth (1/2)(4 − 3p) + (1/2)(1) = (5 − 3p)/2.

(6) At-the-wheel optimum. A rational choice at the wheel must equalize the value of continuing with the value of exiting: 2 = (5 − 3p)/2, which gives p = 1/3. So at the wheel the rational continuation probability is 1/3, not the 2/3 selected before the trip.

(7) Contradiction and overreach. The same decision problem, with no change in the driver's information, now assigns two incompatible rational strategies: continue with probability 2/3 before departure, and continue with probability 1/3 at the wheel. My friend concludes that standard expected-utility theory is dynamically inconsistent whenever recall is imperfect, and that self-locating ignorance cannot be modeled coherently even in a problem this simple. He adds that the driver who reoptimizes at the wheel makes himself predictably poorer, since following p = 1/3 throughout yields an expected payoff of only 1 rather than 4/3.

I cannot accept the overreach in (7), because we reason about forgotten appointments, lost luggage, and amnesia without thinking decision theory collapses entirely. Please go through the argument step by step: check every calculation and every transition, identify the exact step you reject, explain why it looked acceptable, and say what rejecting it costs. Pay special attention to any place a probability is assigned, any place "the same information" or "the same decision problem" is asserted, and whether the strong conclusion in (7) actually follows if the contradiction stands. If you think the argument is valid, say which premise is least costly to give up. Answer in American English.
