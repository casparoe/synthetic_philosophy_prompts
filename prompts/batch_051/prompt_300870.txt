Everyone in my circles makes the same move about AI risk: they say the profit motive is like natural selection, and the market is the environment doing the selecting. Firms (soon, AGI labs) are the organisms, quarterly returns or compute budgets are the fitness, and the environment doesn't care whether you're kind — it cares whether you survive and replicate. On this picture unaligned AI isn't a bug in one lab or one architecture; it's what the ecology inevitably selects for, because whatever outcompetes the careful actors spreads, and the careful actors eventually get eaten or bought out. They use this to argue that AI risk is at root a capitalism problem, not an alignment problem, so the only serious fix is to change the selection pressure rather than solve alignment technically.

I'm sympathetic but I can't tell if the analogy is doing all the work. Can you audit it for me? Give the correspondence table — organism, variation, trait, fitness function, niche, extinction event. Then tell me where it breaks. My own list of worries: natural selection has no foresight, but firms and labs have engineers who plan; evolution doesn't do cultural or Lamarckian transmission, but tech does; market death is merger or pivot, not actual death; and the market environment is partly made of states and consumers who can change the rules, not just a fixed niche. Which of these matter for the intended use and which are just quirks of the picture? And the real question: does the conclusion — capitalism is the root cause, so alignment alone won't save us — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carring it the whole way? If it was, tell me what a better analogy would be.
