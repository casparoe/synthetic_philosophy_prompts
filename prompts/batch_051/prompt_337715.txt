Over the past year I have been developing a distinction I call the directness slide, and I want a candid appraisal of whether it earns its keep. By the directness slide I mean the unnoticed transition from a causal claim to an epistemic one: from S's mental state M being caused by the world without conscious inference, to M's thereby carrying positive epistemic standing for the belief it produces, even if S can neither justify nor criticize M. Sellars's critique of the myth of the given, as I understand it, is precisely a refusal of that transition: a perceptual episode's being causally non-inferential does not make it epistemically self-justifying, because justification belongs to items that are answerable to reasons, not to brute causal episodes. My further claim is that the slide survives in theories that officially reject the given. It reappears whenever a position treats perception, intuition, or reliable subpersonal processing as non-doxastic and therefore exempt from the demand for reasons, while still allowing its outputs to function as default justifiers for beliefs. I call this residual exemption the given's immunity. The thesis I want tested is that the directness slide is a distinct and recurrent methodological fallacy, not merely a restatement of the myth of the given; that its persistence explains why anti-foundationalist theories keep importing foundation-like stopping points under new names; and that Sellars may have committed a version of it if observational reports acquire positive epistemic authority simply by being reliable non-inferential responses to the environment. Is the distinction coherent, or does it collapse into Sellars's own separation of justification from causation or into familiar objections to reliabilism? Where exactly is the causal-to-epistemic move legitimate, and where does my diagnostic overreach?
