I sit on the board of a small foundation that gives away a few million dollars a year, and I follow a standing rule: a program is eligible for funding only if I can state its expected benefit as a quantitative estimate backed by at least one rigorous evaluation, and when I cannot do that I assign the program an expected value of zero and move on. I know this treats not-yet-measured as not-valuable, and that it systematically favors interventions with short, legible causal chains—bednet distribution, deworming, cash transfers—over work such as movement-building, journalism, or institutional reform, whose effects may be large but are diffuse and slow to appear in any trial. My defense is that without the rule I would be choosing among optimistic narratives, and a narrow-but-measured allocation is still better than a narrative-driven one. Is this rule a defensible institutional policy, or is it the measurability bias that critics of effective altruism describe? Give me a definite verdict with your reasons, and state the strongest defensible version of the rule, including where exactly it should stop.
