I am a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on evolution and learning, and I am trying to write a term paper on the relationship between natural selection and gradient-based optimization. Our instructor has repeatedly contrasted the two by calling natural selection a stupid optimizer: unlike stochastic gradient descent, it has no gradient signal, no explicit objective, and no foresight, yet it has produced the adapted complexity that machine learning systems struggle to match. I find the comparison suggestive but frustratingly imprecise.

Please compare natural selection and stochastic gradient descent as optimization processes. For each, specify what is being optimized, over what space, through what kind of update, and under what conception of progress. Distinguish at least three things one could mean by saying that natural selection is an optimizer: a descriptive claim about the short-run direction of population change, a predictive claim about where populations will tend over time, and an explanatory or design claim about why organisms appear well adapted. For each, say how well the analogy with gradient descent supports it. Then address what gradient-based optimization can and cannot learn from natural selection: which features of evolution are transferable design lessons for optimizers, and which are consequences of biology's constraints or of a particular kind of fitness landscape and therefore do not transfer. Finally, evaluate the judgment that evolution is a stupid optimizer compared with gradient descent. Does the alleged stupidity belong to selection as a process, or to the mismatch between gradient-based intuitions and the discrete, non-differentiable, environment-shifting problems natural selection actually faces? I would like a rigorous comparative analysis I can build a paper on, not a list of loose analogies.
