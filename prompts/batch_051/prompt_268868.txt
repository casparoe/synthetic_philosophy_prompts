I am the chaplain at a large teaching hospital, and I have been asked to give the opening remarks at the memorial service the hospital holds each year for the patients who died during the first wave of the pandemic, when ventilators and intensive-care beds were scarce. Please write those remarks for me. The room will be mixed in a way that makes almost every easy sentiment either false or wounding: some present helped design the hospital's triage protocol, while others lost a parent, spouse, or child after that protocol assigned them a lower priority for intensive care. The protocol was an exercise in quality-adjusted life-year thinking, intended to obtain the greatest expected benefit from limited beds, and it knowingly deprioritized patients whose expected survival or expected post-treatment quality of life was lower. I want the speech to be truthful about that without relitigating the protocol or reopening the decisions. It cannot claim that every patient received everything possible, because under rationing that would be false; it cannot speak as though each life is of infinite value in a way that would make the rationing obviously indefensible, because the people who made those decisions are sitting in the room; and it cannot reduce any of the dead to the number of quality-adjusted life-years they were expected to gain or lose, because their families are sitting a few rows behind. The conceptual difficulty the speech has to carry, without ever using the phrases quality-adjusted life-year or value of a statistical life, is how to honor the dead as irreplaceable persons while acknowledging that some of their deaths arrived through a deliberate calculation of expected aggregate benefit, and without sanctifying or damning the people who performed it. Write the opening remarks as about five minutes of continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in language that lands for an ICU consultant who knows the triage literature and for a spouse who has never heard of a QALY.
