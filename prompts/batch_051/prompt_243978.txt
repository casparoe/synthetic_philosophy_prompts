I've come to accept the accuracy-first program in formal epistemology: rational credences are those that maximize expected accuracy, accuracy is measured by a strictly proper scoring rule, and the probability axioms are vindicated by the result that any non-probabilistic credence function is accuracy-dominated by some probabilistic one at every possible world. Play it straight: what would have to be true for this picture to be wrong? I want a map of its exposure, not another defense of it. Which assumptions does the dominance argument actually rest on — propriety of the scoring rule, additivity or separability of accuracy across propositions, the identification of epistemic value with accuracy, the per-world dominance criterion, the assumption that credences are the right object of evaluation — and which of these are live escape routes rather than merely logical possibilities? For each, what would a world or a case have to look like? Which results from nearby areas, such as epistemic value pluralism, the accuracy of full beliefs, or non-classical and imprecise frameworks, should a committed accuracy-firster concede as counting against the view, and which would they dismiss as changing the subject? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say honestly whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability — and whether that would be a defect in a normative theory of this kind. I would personally be most moved by a concrete case in which a scoring rule we would independently recognize as legitimate fails to be proper, or in which maximizing expected accuracy visibly licenses something irrational.
