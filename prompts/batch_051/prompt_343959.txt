I've been trying to get my head around accuracy-first epistemology by going case by case and writing down what I actually think before I look at what the formal results say. I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, so technical stuff is welcome, but I care more about the cases than about having a pretty scoring rule.

Here are my verdicts.

1. Rain city, no info. I've lived in a town where it rains on 30% of days. On a random morning with no further information, my credence in rain today is .3. Gut: rational.

2. Rain city, thunderclouds. Same town, but I'm looking out the window at black thunderclouds and the radar would clearly show a major storm. I post .3 anyway because "base rate." Gut: not rational. The .3 isn't saved by being the unconditional frequency, and I shouldn't ignore what's out the window. I can't say exactly what number I should post, but it should be higher.

3. Fair coin. Credence .5 in heads for a coin I know is fair. Gut: rational, no issue.

4. Forgotten gimmick coin. I know a coin is either double-headed or double-tailed, but I've forgotten which. My credence in heads on the next toss is .5. Gut: rational, but this case bugs me. I know the chance isn't .5; its either 0 or 1 depending on which coin it is. Yet .5 feels like the only defensible state. What I can't do is explain why .5 here is fine while .3 with thunderclouds out the window isn't — in both cases I'm in some sense ignoring something.

5. The wheel. A forecaster has a weather model she trusts that outputs .4, but she dislikes the number, spins a wheel, gets .7, and posts .7. Gut: not rational, even if .7 turns out to be what it does. This makes me think rationality is about how the credence answers to evidence, not just about the number posted. But expected-accuracy maximization — as I understand it — scores credences relative to worlds, and I'm not sure it cares enough about evidence-responsiveness.

6. The always-base-rate forecaster. Someone posts .3 for rain every single day forever. It rains on 30% of days that year, so she's perfectly calibrated. Gut: perfect calibration is a genuine epistemic achievement, but I still don't want to say she's rationally responding to evidence each day. I don't want a view on which calibrated means rational, and I don't want a view on which calibration is worthless.

7. The expert who's never wrong. In the no-info case I'm about to post .3, then I learn that a forecaster whose public track record is, as far as I can tell, perfect has posted .7 for today, with no reasoning attached. Gut: I should move to .7, or very near it. But I can't see why expected accuracy from my own perspective forces that; if my evidence doesn't include a model linking her number to the weather, maybe I can't integrate it at all. That strikes me as wrong, but my gut says deference is rationally required anyway.

8. Uniqueness. I have a strong pull toward thinking that if two agents have exactly the same total evidence, at most one of them is rational in assigning different credences to the same proposition. I also suspect "exactly the same total evidence" is doing too much work, since real agents never share all evidence. I can't tell whether the accuracy-first picture commits me to uniqueness, or whether I want it to.

My working principle is roughly: a credence is rational iff it's the one that maximizes expected accuracy relative to the agent's total evidence, where accuracy is measured by a strictly proper scoring rule — I don't much care whether it's Brier or log. But that principle seems strained by cases 4, 6, and 7, and maybe silent on 5.

So: what's the best principle, or small set of candidate principles, that captures all or almost all of these verdicts? State it precisely enough that I can see which clauses handle which cases, say which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and tell me what it predicts about two agents with the same total evidence who post .2 and .8 to the same proposition: both possibly rational, exactly one, or is the question itself confused?
