Article 12 of the 1978 Covenant of the Greenwell Fellowship — a small intentional community, founded in 1974, whose members hold voluntary simplicity as a shared ideal — reads:

"No Member shall keep within the bounds of the Fellowship more than fifty separate possessions, counting every article that may be held in the hand; and whatsoever is not used in the space of one year shall be given away, sold, or burnt."

The article has never been amended. The founders drafted it before any member owned a personal computer.

Daniel, a current member, owns one laptop and otherwise only his clothing and bedding; his physical possessions number twenty-nine. On the laptop he keeps roughly four thousand purchased e-books, most of them unread and bought in occasional bursts; a photograph archive of about ninety thousand images he no longer opens; an email archive of every message he has received since 2009, which he never searches; and a set of digital collectibles whose prices he checks a few times a week but which he does not otherwise use. He says he keeps twenty-nine physical possessions, all within the limit, and that none of the files is a possession at all, because no file is "an article that may be held in the hand." Another member, Ruth, replies that Daniel has become exactly the sort of hoarder the covenant exists to prevent, and that a reading of Article 12 which counts a shelf of forty-nine books against the limit but not four thousand books on a hard drive has mistaken the letter for the spirit of the rule.

I serve on the Fellowship's council, and we must decide whether Daniel may remain in good standing under Article 12. I have spent enough years in rationalist-adjacent forums to suspect that my own framing may be doing hidden work, so please push back where it is warranted.

Give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the result the plain words, read literally, most naturally yield for each of the article's two clauses; (2) the result the purpose of the two clauses, taken together, points to; (3) what the founders would most plausibly have said if the case could have been described to them when they wrote the article, and how much weight that should carry; (4) the result a reasonable member reading Article 12 today would reach; and (5) your verdict on which interpretive approach should control and whether, on that approach, Daniel still satisfies the covenant's standard. If the task is misframed — for example, because Article 12 is genuinely underdetermined here and no honest reading can declare Daniel in or out — say that outright rather than forcing the five-part answer.
