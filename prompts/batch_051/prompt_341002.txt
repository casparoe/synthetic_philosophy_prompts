I've been reworking the opening lecture for my philosophy of law course — the one on legal obligation — and there's a tension in the doctrine I can't resolve cleanly. Consider how a criminal court treats a defendant who didn't know what the law required. On one hand, the system runs on the maxim that ignorance of the law is no excuse: a person can be convicted of an offence buried in a statutory instrument amended last year, something only a specialist would plausibly read, on the fiction that everyone is presumed to know the law. On the other hand, the same system refuses to enforce laws that were never published, strikes down statutes as void for vagueness because they didn't give fair notice, and in some circumstances will excuse a defendant who acted on a mistaken statement of the law issued by an official body. So the law appears to say both that a rule which could not be known cannot bind, and that a rule which could not be known by this particular defendant can bind them anyway. That looks like a contradiction, or at least like the two halves run on different engines.

What I want you to do is reconstruct the theory of legal obligation, or fairness to rule-subjects, or responsibility — whichever you judge is doing the real work — that this practice presupposes. Does the no-excuse rule commit the system to the view that citizens have a standing duty to inform themselves about the law, or that they tacitly consent to whatever is duly enacted, or that their own ignorance is just a risk they bear? Are those commitments consistent with the fair-notice side of the practice, or does the practice as a whole presuppose two incompatible theories? And then say what would actually have to change if we replaced the presumption that everyone knows the law with a genuine epistemic condition on criminal liability. I don't just mean that more appeals would suceed; I mean the underlying theory that would have to be adopted, and whether the remaining fair-notice doctrines would become redundant or would instead have to be strengthened.
