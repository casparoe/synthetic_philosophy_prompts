I'm taking a seminar on Aristotle's De Anima, and I keep coming back to a puzzle about phantasia. The account we are given is that phantasia is distinct from both perception and thought: it is a movement produced by actual perception, it persists after the perceived object is gone, and it supplies thought with phantasms — Aristotle is often read as saying the soul never thinks without them. On the standard reading in my seminar, a phantasm is something like a stored percept or a mental image.

I've been testing a different interpretation that I call the workspace reading. On this view, phantasia is not fundamentally a faculty that produces picture-like items; it is the capacity to keep perceptual residues available off-line and to make them manipulable by thought. The defining feature of a phantasm would not be image-likeness but availability: it is whatever format allows content originally given in perception to remain in play when the object is absent, so the intellect has something to combine, divide, and inspect. Thought would need phantasia, on this reading, not because thinking is a kind of seeing-in-the-mind but because thought has no content of its own to work on and needs a sensory-derived medium. This seems to handle two things well. It explains why Aristotle assigns phantasia such a broad role — memory, dreaming, anticipation, deliberation — without making all these activities literal imaging. And it makes mathematical cognition less puzzling: I can think about a triangle determinately enough to prove something about it without calling up a finished picture, because the phantasm on this view is just the perceptual residue being manipulated, not a vivid image.

But I worry the workspace reading is too tidy. Three problems seem serious. First, falsity: Aristotle treats phantasia as something that can be true or false and ties its name to light and appearing. A buffer does not by itself misrepresent, so I would have to locate the error in what gets stored or how it is reactivated, and I am not sure the texts support that. Second, abstract and self-reflexive thought: if phantasms are residues of perception, what is the phantasm when I think about a universal as such, or when thought thinks about itself? Saying "there is some phantasm" feels like relabeling the difficulty. Third, originality: I gather there are already readings on which phantasmata need not be literal images, so my "workspace" may just be that idea with a more computational-sounding name.

My question is whether this reading can be made to cohere as a reading of Aristotle, or whether it collapses under pressure. What does it get right, and where exactly does it break? What are the strongest objections, and are any of them fatal rather than merely uncomfortable? And if the reading fails, what should a better account of phantasia's role in thought preserve?
