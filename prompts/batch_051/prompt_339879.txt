After a long argument with a friend about whether rational agents can persistently disagree, I have landed on the following view. Standard game-theoretic results that seem to rule out persistent disagreement or speculative trade — Aumann's agreement theorem, the no-trade theorems, and backward-induction solutions — draw their force almost entirely from the idealization that the agents' rationality, common prior, and the structure of the interaction are common knowledge. Weaken that idealization to high-but-finite-order mutual belief or to bounded iterated reasoning, and the prohibitions largely dissolve. So I now think common knowledge of rationality is not a harmless approximation but the engine of these results, and that disagreement, trade on private information, and coordination failure are far less diagnostic of irrationality than the field usually suggests. What would count against this view? I want the arguments, evidence, and formal results that should move me, sorted by how decisive they are, and an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated by loose phrasing — and, if it has, whether that is a defect or the right conclusion to draw. Please write your answer in American English.
