I sit on the undergraduate curriculum committee of a mathematics department, and I must circulate a short written opinion before the committee votes next week on the required lab sequence for our second-year course on geometry and curves. I would like you to draft that opinion.

The course has a fixed fourteen-week structure with two hours of laboratory work each week, and the registrar will not approve any additional lab time. We must choose between two five-week lab sequences; the one that is not chosen will, in practice, drop out of the required course.

The existing sequence teaches classical straightedge-and-compass construction. Students construct lengths and regular polygons, learn which magnitudes can be produced by a finite sequence of the two classical operations, and work through the algebraic characterization of constructible lengths, including the way it explains why certain classical construction problems are impossible. Its organizing idea is that a point or curve earns its status as a legitimate object of geometrical study by being constructible, by being something we can actually exhibit through a finite procedure.

The proposed replacement sequence studies curves that are specified by equations, algorithms, or limiting processes rather than by compass-and-straightedge generation, including curves defined by transcendental functions that cannot be produced point-by-point by the classical operations. Students would write programs to generate and approximate these curves, study their defining equations, and establish their properties by algebraic, analytic, and computational means. Its organizing idea is that a mathematical object is properly given when a finite rule specifies it, even if no classical construction can exhibit it.

A further wrinkle is that writing a finite program to generate a curve is itself a kind of construction. One can therefore see the proposed sequence either as abandoning constructibility as a requirement or as generalizing it from manual operations to algorithmic ones. Much of the argument between the two camps turns on how much weight this observation should carry.

Last night I had a long argument with a friend who defends the existing sequence. His sharpest point was that students who never make objects by hand come to treat curves as pictures emitted by software, and lose the habit of asking whether a mathematical object has actually been exhibited or only named. I believe he is right to identify that risk but wrong to conclude that the classical construction lab is the only defense against it.

Please draft the short opinion I will circulate to the committee. It should recommend a concrete decision about which sequence becomes the required lab and what, if anything, is preserved from the losing sequence. It should give reasons rather than merely describe the two options, and it should engage the strongest version of the constructibility view, including my friend's objection, without dismissing it as merely old-fashioned.
