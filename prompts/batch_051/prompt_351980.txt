Lately I've been going back and forth about the grit and resilience stuff, and I can't get the two halves of my own view to settle. On one hand, I'm attracted to the idea that grit is basically a domain-general trait—something like a trainable tendency to keep working at a goal when it's hard or unrewarding in the short run—and that, as a trait, it's value-neutral. The same persistence that gets someone through a dissertation can also keep a fraud scheme running; the capacity itself doesn't care whether the end is good. That seems clean, and it would also let 'grit' do explanatory work without me having to moralize every time someone applies it.

But I also have this intuitions that won't budge. When people praise 'resilience' in schools or workplaces, it very often seems to function as an ideological move: you take an underfunded school or an absurd workload, and you reframe the problem as a deficit of character in the student or the employee rather than a defect in the situation. And, separately, I have a gut-level sense that the stubborn persistence of a whistleblower and the stubborn persistence of a fraudster aren't literally the same psychological thing pointed in two directions—the ends are partly constitutive of the thing. Fortitude in the older virtue sense was never a free-floating skill; it was always indexed to what you were enduring and why.

So I'm torn between a theory that says grit/resilience is a unified, trainable, value-neutral capacity, and a pair of firm intuitions that say the concept is partly ideological and not nearly as unified as it looks. I don't want a 'both sides have a point' ending. I want to know what it would cost to keep the theory and debunk the intuitions, what it would cost to let the intuitions force an amendment or abandonment of the theory, and whether there's a formulation—say, distinguishing the trait from the rhetoric surrounding the trait, or redefining fortitude as inherently end-indexed—on which both survive. What should give way?
