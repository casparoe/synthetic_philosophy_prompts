I spent my scientific career fitting models to noisy data, and since retiring I have been reading the philosophy of history. I am struck by a parallel between the standard warnings about historical analogy—Munich, Vietnam, the Thucydides trap—and the phenomenon of overfitting. The structure of the mistake seems to be the same: a new situation is classified by its similarity to one or two salient training examples along a few selected dimensions; disanalogies and the base rate of outcomes are ignored; and the resulting lesson fits the remembered case beautifully but generalizes poorly. This looks like a nearest-neighbor classifier run in a high-dimensional feature space with a sample size of one. I would like to know whether this is a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion. If it is genuine, do results from statistical learning transfer—regularization, cross-validation, the bias-variance tradeoff—to the methodology of drawing lessons from history? And where exactly does the mapping fail? What would have to be true of historical processes for the formal results to carry over, and what does the parallel predict that turns out false?
