I'm a PhD student in linguistics (mostly morphophonology), and I've somehow become the de facto writing-system consultant for a community language committee where I do fieldwork. The commmittee — mostly teachers, elders, and a couple of people from the regional literacy bureau, none with formal linguistics — meets every month or six weeks to approve orthographies for languages that have barely been written down, and once they decide, the spelling gets baked into primers and adult literacy classes. Revising it later is basically impossible politically, so the first decision is the one that sticks, and the committee (with the literacy bureau) has final sign-off either way. I can't be at every meeting, so I want to leave them a procedure they can run in a single afternoon workshop whenever a new propossal comes in, without me in the room.

What I want is a reusable decision procedure that turns the idea that a writing system is a kind of theory of the language into actual steps: when should a script commit to surface sounds, when should it commit to underlying or morphophonemic forms, when should it preserve morphemes or whole words, and how phonemic does an alphabet actually need to be when those pull in different directions? For each step, say what question to ask, what counts as a good-enough answer to move on, where the procedure should stop with a "we can't decide this yet" (for instance because the sound system hasn't actually been worked out), and where it should pass the decision to the committee as a values call rather than a linguistic one. Then run the procedure on three worked cases: (a) a language with word-final devoicing, where spelling the surface forms would make the same root look different across singular/plural pairs; (b) a tone language where every syllable is high, mid, or low, but marking every tone makes the primers look intimidating and most tone contrasts are recoverable from context; and (c) a case you make up yourself where the procedure gives an answer the committee will probably hate — I'd rather it be honest than flattering. Format it as numbered steps with a one-line rationale each, then the three worked applications, and close with a short paragraph on what the procedure cannot settle. Hard cap: 1,200 words.
