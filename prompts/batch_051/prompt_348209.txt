I cover psychology and politics, and I keep hitting the same stalled argument—in the literature, at conferences, in arguments online—about what, if anything, moral foundations theory shows about which political side is right. The descriptive claim, roughly, is that self-described left and right lean on different moral foundations like care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and sanctity, with liberals loading up on care and fairness while conservatives spread themselves more evenly across all of them. One camp reads that as evidence that conservative morality has a broader structure and that the left's judgments aren't the neutral baseline they're often taken to be; the other camp says the measure is biased, the differences are smaller than advertised, and anyway a difference in which intuitions feel salient cant settle which side is actually right. What confuses me is that the two sides mostly agree on the descriptive findings well enough to keep arguing, yet nobody moves, and I've started to suspect the actual disagreement is over whether "who is right" is even the kind of thing descriptive moral psychology could answer—with some people quietly using the findings as a debunking argument against one side, others as a vindication of the other, and a third group using them as grounds to declare the whole question ill-posed. I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not a survey of the studies: is this a hidden empirical disagreement, a verbal one, a difference about what counts as a moral reason, or a fight about moral epistemology in disguise? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a distinction, an experiment, a different question? Also tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis and single out the point you're least sure of.
