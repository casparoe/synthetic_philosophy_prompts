Two people in my group chat got into a long fight over deplatforming, and I honestly couldn't referee it. Here's roughly how it went.

(1) Ben said when a social media platform permanently bans someone for offensive speech, that is punishment, full stop: they're deliberately making the person worse off in response to something the person did. So the platform should face the same kinds of fairness requirements we put on punishment, like a chance to apeal and some kind of proportionality.

(2) Priya replied that this is confused. A private company isn't a court; it's just refusing to keep hosting someone. Declining to associate isn't punishment. If I stop inviting a rude neighbor to my parties, I'm not punishing him — not even if it stings.

(3) Ben came back: of course it's social punishment. Ostracism and shunning are older than state punishment, and they're punishment because the harm is meant to express condemnation. The platform even says as much when it calls the ban an enforcement of it's rules. And the "private company" point is a dodge: if an entire industry deplatforms someone at once, you can't call that one host declining a guest.

(4) Priya said scale doesn't change the concept. What's missing is standing. Punishment, properly speaking, requires an authority with the right to judge and sanction. A platform has no such authority over its users; it only has a contractual power to end the service. Calling it punishment is a way to sneak in demands for due process that don't apply to ordinary private decisions.

(5) Ben said that's exactly the sleight of hand. The platform acts like an authority — it writes the norms, investigates alleged violations, and publicly announces a permanent ban as a verdict. It does all the expressive work of punishment and then hides behind "we're just a business" to dodge the responsibilities.

(6) Priya responded that even if deplatforming has a punitive flavor, not every hard treatment that follows wrongdoing needs trial-type protections. We shouldn't force a whole spectrum of social reactions into the criminal-punishment box; maybe deplatforming is more like ending a friendship than a prison sentence.

They asked me who won, and I genuinely couldn't say. You're a philosopher of law who works on theories of punishment and social sanction. Start by laying out the distinctions you'd need — punishment vs. mere refusal to associate vs. social sanction, and the role of standing or authority — then adjudicate this move by move. For each of (1)–(6), say what's right, what's overstated, and whether it actually advances that person's case. Then give an overall verdict: is deplatforming punishment in a morally interesting sense, and does Ben's fairness demand follow from it? Also say where the two of them are talking past each other.
