I've been thinking about a four-rung ladder of minds: Darwinian creatures, whose patterns of behavior are mostly fixed by their biology; Skinnerian creatures, who learn by blind trial and error out in the world; Popperian creatures, who can try actions out in their heads and let their mistakes die before they act; and Gregorian creatures, who can absorb words, tools, and designs from other minds, so their learning doesn't have to start from scratch. I'd like to reread Mary Shelley's Frankenstein through this ladder, because the creature seems to climb it: first driven by sensation and impulse, then learning by painful experience, then planning his revenge, and finally taking in the language he overhears from the cottage family and the books he later finds. I want a reading that runs both ways. Where does the ladder genuinely illuminate the novel, and what does it make visible about why the creature becomes articulate and why his borrowed learning is so closely tied to his estrangement? Where does it miss what Shelley plainly cares about, such as whether he was born good, what Victor owes him, and whether he has a soul? And does reading the novel as a climb up the ladder expose a gap in the classification itself, something a mind can have that the rungs don't capture?
