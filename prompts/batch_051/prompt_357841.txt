Stanley Milgram argued that the destructive obedience observed in his experiments is not best explained by cruelty, moral weakness, or unusual sadism. Rather, entry into a hierarchy can bring about what he called an agentic state: the person comes to see himself as an instrument for carrying out the wishes of a legitimate authority, and responsibility for his actions appears to him increasingly to lie with that authority rather than with himself. In Milgram's original scenario the authority was a human experimenter, present in the room, visibly issuing instructions and capable of owning them. Now consider a development Milgram did not face: in medicine, welfare administration, and military targeting, consequential instructions increasingly come from automated decision or decision-support systems, with no human author present at the moment of action. Does Milgram's explanation of obedience survive this development? Identify which premise the change puts pressure on — the perceived legitimacy of the authority, the possibility of transferring responsibility upward, the felt presence of a superior, or something else — and say whether the argument still goes through, needs repair, or is dead. If it survives only in repaired form, state the repair precisely and explain what the repaired account implies about when deference to an impersonal system can be a virtue.
