I'm a novelist, and I've agreed to write a long, essayistic piece for a magazine about deepfakes and the sense that we're losing some shared anchor for what counts as evidence. The draft is half-formed and I want comments before I send it to an editor. I'm not trained in philosophy, so please don't just encourage me — tell me where the thinking is off.

The essay hangs on one idea I'm flirting with, which I've been calling "the backstop isn't the image, it's the queue." The thought is that when people say deepfakes erode our trust in images and video, they point at the wrong object. The real epistemic backstop — the thing that lets an image settle an argument, or anchor a belief, or stand as the last word in a dispute — was never the pixels. It was the social and institutional traffic the image traveled through: a known source, a chain of custody, a newsroom that could be embarrassed, someone who could be cross-examined, an archive that logged when and how the file was handled. Deepfakes don't destroy that backstop by making the image lie; they destroy it by making the provenance of any image cheap and deniable. Once "it's synthetic" is always a live defense, even a genuine leak or a genuine recording can be waved away, because the defense costs the accused nothing. My essay's actual claim, then, is that the deepest damage isn't to images but to the queue around images: the reusable path from recording to public uptake. And the fix isn't mainly detection or watermarking; it's rebuilding the queue so that the path an image traveled is expensive to fake. But I'm worried this is half-true, and possibly just a fancy restatement of "provenance matters," which everyone already knows, and I can't tell wheter the essay has an engine beyond that.
