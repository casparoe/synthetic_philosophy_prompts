Panpsychism's combination problem has always looked to me like the preference-aggregation problem from social choice theory wearing a philosophy-of-mind costume. On one side you've got a bunch of micro-subjects (or at least micro-experiences) and you need to get from them to a single unified macro-subject; on the other you've got a bunch of individual preference orderings and you need to get from them to a single "social" preference ordering. Both are cases where the ingredients are unproblematic, the output is unproblematic, but there's no obvious summation operation that preserves the properties you care about. So my question is whether this is a deep structural identity or just the word "aggregate" doing all the work.

Here are the spots where it feels deep to me. First, Arrow's theorem says no procedure for turning individual preference orderings into a social ordering can satisfy a short list of innocent-looking axioms all at once. Is there anything analogous on the consciousness side — something like a proof that no "combination relation" can preserve all the features you'd want a composite subject to have, where the features play the role of Arrow's axioms? What would those axioms even be, and which one would a panpsychist be forced to give up? Second, both fields seem to cope by naming the gap rather than closing it: panpsychists posit a "phenomenal bonding relation" to do the summing, and social choice has ad hoc social welfare functions that just assert a rule. Is that the same failure mode or a different one? Third, some social choice theorists respond by denying that there's any group-level preference to aggregate in the first place, which sounds suspiciously like the illusionist or holist moves that deny the combination problem rather than solve it.

But I can also see reasons the parallel might be superficial. Individual preferences can in principle be put on a ballot and counted; micro-experiences can't be introspected, individuated, or read off from the outside, so the "inputs" to the aggregation are not given in the same way. And social choice usually assumes the preferences are already complete and transitive and then asks how to add them up, whereas panpsychism arguably never gets a well-defined notion of a micro-subject's experience in the first place. So maybe the real work on the consciousness side happens before aggregation, and the social choice comparison only becomes available after the hard part is already done.

Is there any result from social choice theory that would actually transfer over and tell us something new about the combination problem, or the reverse? Is there anything analagous to Arrow's impossibility result hiding in the combination problem, or is that just a fantasy of formalism? And if I've got a confused picture of either side, just say so plainly — if the parallel is mostly pattern-matching, I'd rather hear that than have the analogy flattered.
