I've been slowly teaching myself model theory this year (after work, so its slow going), and there's one thing I keep going around in circles on: Skolem's paradox. Here's how I understand the setup. ZFC is a first-order theory in a countable language — really just the membership relation. Cantor's theorem is a theorem of ZFC, so ZFC proves there are uncountable sets, like the powerset of the natural numbers. But the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem says that any consistent first-order theory in a countable language with an infinite model has a countable model. ZFC is consistent (I'm happy to grant that), so it has a countable model. And here's what bugs me. From outside, that model's domain is countable — I can enumerate everything in it. Yet inside the model there's an object the model considers the powerset of the naturals, and the model satisfies the sentence saying that object is uncountable. How is this not a flat contradiction?

I don't need proofs of either theorem. What I want is the conceptual resolution: which step in my description is doing the misleading work? When I stand outside and enumerate the whole domain, what exactly am I enumerating, and why doesn't that enumeration show up as an object inside the model? What does "uncountable" amount to inside a model, such that an outside observer can count the entire model while the model is still, in some sense, correct that something in it is uncountable? And if you could resist the one-line response that the required bijection simply doesn't exist in the model — I've heard that and it hasn't landed — and instead walk me through where my inside and outside vocabulary are sliding around, that would help a lot.
