David Hume argued that a larger stock of money eventually raises prices without increasing a nation's real wealth, even though the first injection can temporarily stimulate trade before prices adjust. Suppose he were shown a modern central bank conducting quantitative easing: it credits banks with newly created, interest-bearing reserves and uses them to purchase long-term government bonds and, in some cases, private assets, in a fiat-money system with a floating exchange rate and short-term rates near zero. Reconstruct the considered view Hume would most likely defend about whether, when, and through which channels such a policy creates wealth rather than merely altering liquidity and rearranging claims. Distinguish which of his principles carry over unchanged, which require modification (his price-specie-flow reasoning presupposed convertible metallic money and fixed exchange rates, for instance), and which questions his commitments leave open. Then state the empirical finding about a modern economy that would most likely cause him to revise that view, and explain how.
