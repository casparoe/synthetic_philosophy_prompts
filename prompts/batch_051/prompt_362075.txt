New rule for presidential succession: instead of the president privately tapping a designated survivor for the State of the Union, the survivor gets picked by a public lottery among the Senate-confirmed cabinet secretaries on the morning of the speech—not the vice president, who has to be in the chamber. Here's the twist: as a condition of taking the job, every cabinet secretary has to keep on file a sealed, two-page plan for the first hundred days, updated each January. If the lottery winner actually becomes president, the plan gets published unchanged before they take the oath—no revisions, even if the actual emergency looks nothing like what the plan assumed. Walk me through what this does. What do secretaries start writing in their sealed plans once there's a real (if tiny) chance they inherit the presidency tomorrow morning? How do presidents and party leaders game the cabinet lineup to shift the odds without being obvious about it? Where does it break first, is any of it fixable, and does the pre-published plan make a lottery-picked successor more legitimate or just more constrained?
