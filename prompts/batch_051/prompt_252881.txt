I've been worrying at a parallel and can't decide whether it's a real structural identity or just me pattern-matching on the word core. In Lakatos's picture of a scientific research programme you've got a hard core that the people inside the programme never test directly — by a kind of methodological decision — surrounded by a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses that gets adjusted to soak up anomolies. A programme is progressive while its belt-changes keep anticipating new facts, and degenerating when they only accommodate known anomalies after the fact. Common law seems to do nearly the same thing: a founding precident or doctrine is treated as effectively unrevisable most of the time, and courts distinguish it, add exceptions, add multi-factor tests, rather than overrule it. That pile of distinctions is the protective belt. A line of cases feels healthy when each new rule keeps telling you how the next case will come out, and degenerating when courts just bolt on exceptions that explain the case in front of them and nothing else.

If the isomorphism is genuine, two things seem to follow. One, Lakatos's criterion of novel facts would transfer to law — maybe a doctrine is doing real work when its new distinctions predict cases not yet litigated, rather than redescribing the last decision. Two, the analogy predicts something that looks false to me: Lakatos says the hard core never gets touched within a programme, but courts do overrule precedents. Is that a switch of research programme, or does it snap the analogy? Also, a legal rule partly brings future cases into existence by changing behaviour and what gets litigated, whereas nature doesn't care what hypotheses we hold; does that break the predictive symmetry?
