I've been on our department's citation-policy committee and also co-editing a journal, and I keep running into something about names that I can't get straight. In my field we treat the family name as the serious identifier: alphabetical order, indexes, how we mention authors in prose — the given name shrinks to an initial while the surname does all the work. It looks as though the convention presupposes that the family name is the real, stable public name and the given name is decoration. Then I hit the cases that don't fit. My collegue from Iceland doesn't have a family name in that sense; her second name is her father's first name plus -dóttir, and Icelandic phone books and library catalogs alphabetize people by given name, not by the patronymic. Our university library, meanwhile, won't merge the records of an author who changed her surname at marriage, so the same person sits in two places; but the journal I co-edit just retroactively changed the name on all the old articles of an author who transitioned, with no "formerly published as" note, as if the earlier papers had always been by the new name. The more I look, the less I can see what these practices think a name is.

What I'd like is a reconstruction of the implicit theory of proper names that would make sense of the surname-first convention, plus a verdict on whether the Icelandic, marriage-name, and transition cases can peacefully coexist with it. Does treating the family name as the canonical identifier really presuppose that surnames are more essential to the person, or more referentialy stable, than given names are? Which rival views of names does the practice quietly rule out, and where is it just underdetermined? And what would our cataloging and citation practices have to change if we replaced that presupposition with, say, a view of names as mutable labels, or as descriptions a person has to satisfy, or as rigid tags that pick out the same person no matter which string of letters sits on the title page?
