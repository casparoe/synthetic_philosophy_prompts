I have been turning over a kind of situation that I do not have a settled name for. A group of people will all be affected by some action, and yet any one of them, acting alone, can take that action before anyone else can object, deliberate, or stop them. The action might turn out to be a large benefit, or it might turn out to be a quiet disaster. A few researchers might each be able to publish a result that would be dangerous if wrong; a few engineers might each be able to ship something that cannot be recalled; one person with a large platform might be able to commit a whole community to a course of action overnight. I keep watching people argue about such cases one at a time, insisting that they themselves would or would not go ahead. But it seems to me that the prior question is what any adequate account of acting unilaterally on behalf of others would have to get right. That is what I want from you.

Please write the requirements list for a theory of when someone is justified in acting unilaterally on a choice that will be imposed on many others. Judge adequacy by two purposes: helping the actor decide in the moment, and letting the group and outside observers assess afterward whether the decision was wrong. I want the full specification: the cases the theory must handle and the verdicts it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the facts about individual and group judgment it cannot ignore, with the reasoning behind each requirement. In particular, your list should address at least the following, though it should not be limited to them:

- the fleeting opportunity that disappears if the person waits;
- the irreversible action with a small chance of a catastrophic downside and a larger chance of a modest benefit;
- the case in which every member of the group would act on the same private signal, and the combined result is worse than if only one had acted;
- the case in which the actor privately gains while the group bears most of the risk.

It should also mark distinctions the theory must not collapse, for example between acting for yourself with effects on others and acting as a self-appointed agent of the group; between a decision, an experiment, and a public statement of intent; and between reversible and irreversible moves. And it should note which features of real group judgment an adequate theory cannot wave away, for instance that a group's central or average judgment is often more reliable than a lone person's, and that a single decider is prone to overconfidence about how much he or she knows.

For each requirement, say whether it is load-bearing or negotiable, and flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Then tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding the easy answers.

As a benchmark, briefly score these three simple positions against the list: (a) act whenever your honest judgment says the expected value is positive; (b) never act unilaterally, whatever the stakes; (c) act only when you can reasonably guess that most affected people would consent. I am not asking for citations, reading lists, or bibliographies; I want the structure of the problem, not the literature. Please write your answer in American English.
