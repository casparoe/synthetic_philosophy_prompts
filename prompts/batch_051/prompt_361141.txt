I keep hearing this label "the unilateralist's curse" thrown around in groups I'm in—the whole setup where any one person can do the thing, and it's good if exactly one person does it but bad if a bunch do, and people can't coordinate before acting. Every time I try to explain my take it comes out as a pile of case judgments rather than a rule, so I'm trying to get straight what I actually think.

You're a professional decision theorist who works on group judgment and moral responsibility. Could you think this through carefully with me? Start by brainstorming a few candidate principles, then test them against my cases below before settling on the best one.

Here are my gut verdicts:

1. A 40-person faculty listserv. Someone posts a take I think is importantly wrong. If one person posts a careful correction, that's great; if forty people pile on, the thread turns into a harassment-looking dogpile and the original poster leaves the list. Before replying I refresh the thread and see zero replies. I post my correction. Verdict: fine, maybe even the right thing to do.

2. A private bug report to a maintainer. I find a security bug in open-source software. If one person files a clear report, the maintainer fixes it fast; if many people file, the queue floods and some reports leak into public issue threads. I check the tracker, see no report, and email a private one. Verdict: fine.

3. A drowning swimmer at a crowded beach. Maybe 80 people can see it. I dial 911 immediately and don't waste a second checking whether anyone else is already calling. Later it occurs to me that five others probably called too. Verdict: obviously fine; I'd do it again and I don't care about the duplicates.

4. A loose tile in my 12-unit apartment building's walkway. Anyone could tape a note to the landlord's door. One note gets it fixed; twelve notes get it fixed plus the landlord thinks we're all passive-aggressive. I notice the tile, don't ask around, and tape a note. I'd guess a lot of neighbors noticed too. Verdict: fine, though mildly awkward.

5. A grant where a lab can submit only one note of intent to a funder. I'm on a lab Slack of 25. I check the channel, no one has said they're on it, so I draft and submit. If three other people had also submitted, the lab would look disorganized to the funder. Verdict: fine, though I did feel a little spooked.

6. A field-building organization. I've been in a small research community that needs an org to do a certain thing. The field can absorb one, maybe two; if five people start rival versions, funders get confused, talent splits, and all of them underperform. Over a few months, three different people have told me, independently, that they're launching their own version. I still launch mine because I think my plan is the best and "somebody has to do it." Verdict: that one wasn't fine. I was piling on, and I knew it.

7. A panel submission. Me and two friends each independently come up with the same panel idea for the year's big conference. The form says duplicates waste reviewers' time and we should coordinate. I know the other two are considering submitting theirs. I submit anyway because I worry that if I don't, they'll both flake and the panel won't happen. Verdict: fine. I feel a little guilty about the reviewer load, but not enough to not submit.

The case I keep going back and forth on is a wiki that corrects a widely used database. Any registered user can submit a correction to a record. If exactly one person submits a fix to a wrong entry, the editors review and fix it quickly. If, say, nine people submit the same fix, the queue blocks and none of them get reviewed for weeks. I find an error in an entry and know two colleagues have found the same error and are "planning to submit something." If I submit and they do too, I've caused the blockage I was trying to avoid; if I don't and they also don't, the error stays for months. What should I do, and what does that say about my other verdicts?

So: is there one principle that gets all of 1–7, or close? If a simple principle forces me to drop a verdict or two, which ones should go? And what does your best principle say about the wiki case? Be honest if my verdicts can't all be saved, rather than gerrymandering to preserve them.
