I've been a software engineer for about a decade, no philosophy background at all unless you count arguing about naming things in code review, and I've got a homegrown framework for what happens when a metric goes bad that I'd like someone to actually push back on. I know Goodhart's law roughly as the idea that once a metric becomes a target, it stops being a good metric. I think people use that slogan for three very different failure modes, and conflating them is why a lot of metric fixes at my jobs have made things worse.

Here's my taxonomy, names are mine:

1. Remeasurement. The number moves because you quietly changed what counts toward it, not because the underlying reality improved. Example: our daily-active-users KPI went up after we broadened the definition to include anyone who landed on the homepage via a redirect, even if they bounced immediately. This is mostly a bad definition; pin the metric down and its fixed.

2. Proximal slip. The metric and the real target were correlated before anyone optimized, but optimizing the metric severs the correlation because the metric is a lagging or downstream proxy. Example: a support team measured on median resolution time starts closing easy tickets in seconds and letting the hard ones age; the number looks great, actual customer experience gets worse. This is what I'd call real Goodhart.

3. Residual squeeze. The measured thing genuinely improves, but unmeasured dimensions quietly rot as a side effect of the same effort. Example: we cut API latency a lot by dropping retry logic and leaning on a stale cache, so latency actually did improve, but correctness and freshness got worse and those weren't on the dashboard. It isn't gaming in the usual sense; its just optimizing a list that's shorter than the list of things you actually care about.

My two claims: (a) these are genuinely different mechanisms, not one law wearing three costumes; and (b) management almost always diagnoses proximal slip and tries to fix it with more metrics, but in mature systems the common one is residual squeeze, and more metrics can't fix it — you either stop optimizing the target so hard or keep a human in the loop who can feel the rot and has the authority to say stop.

I'm pretty sure this maps onto something in the overlap between economics and ML safety that I don't have the vocabulary for. Tell me: does the taxonomy hold up or collapse when you push on it? Is it already a thing under better names? Give me the strongest counterexample to each of the three categories and the strongest objection to claim (b). Be honest about whether my category 3 is actually distinct from category 2 or whether I've just gotten attached to a distinction because it fits my last incident report. And tell me how confident you are in what you say, and which point you're least sure of.
