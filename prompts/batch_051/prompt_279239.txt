I have been a teaching assistant for several years in a moral-responsibility seminar whose instructor uses a fixed set of vignettes and records, in a running document, a verdict on whether each agent is blameworthy together with a sentence of rationale. His settled track record is as follows. (1) A man is hypnotized into acquiring a powerful urge to embezzle, the session is erased from his memory, and he experiences the urge as alien to his own values but eventually acts on it: not blameworthy, because the motive was installed by hypnosis and never became something he identified with. (2) A woman is raised in a community that deliberately and systematically teaches that outsiders may be cheated without wrong; as an adult she reflects, wholeheartedly endorses the teaching, and defrauds an outsider: blameworthy, because she has made the teaching her own through reflection. (3) A man hands over a stranger's wallet at gunpoint: not blameworthy, because immediate coercion deprives the act of the kind of control blame requires. (4) A woman who gambled voluntarily for years becomes addicted, now regards the urge to gamble as alien to her considered values, but sometimes acts on it anyway: not blameworthy, because the action does not express her considered values. (5) A man with no unusual formative history carefully deliberates and spreads a damaging rumor about a rival to advance his career: blameworthy, because he acted on his own reflectively held values under ordinary conditions.

This year we are adding a sixth vignette: a woman is covertly hypnotized into acquiring a disposition toward ruthless business negotiation, and the session is erased from her memory. Over the following years, away from the hypnotist, she reflects on the disposition, comes to judge that ruthlessness serves the values she now wants to live by, and wholeheartedly endorses it; in a later negotiation she acts on it in a way that clearly wrongs her counterpart. If the instructor is to remain as consistent as possible with his own recorded verdicts, should he call her blameworthy or not? Identify the candidate principle or principles that fit the five standing cases, say which standing case is the closest analog under each candidate principle and what each principle dictates for the sixth case, give your best judgment about which verdict is the more defensible extrapolation and why, and specify one further vignette—not already settled by the standing five—that would cleanly separate the live candidate principles, recording what each principle would predict for it.
