I keep hitting a problem in my actual work that I think is secretly about moral philosophy. In fiction you have to pick a point of view: omniscient, first person, close third nailed to one character. And I've started to think the whole universalization tradition — Kant, Hare, Singer, the logic of "what if everyone did that?" — quietly picks omniscient and nobody notices. They all imagine the world where everybody does the act and ask whether you can will it, but they imagine it the way a novelist imagines a scene from above, seeing every character at once. My theory: the test only really counts if it's run in first person, from inside each life, but you can only run it one character at a time. I call this "focal universalization": a maxim passes if it's acceptable from each first-person point of view, taken one by one, the way a novel moves through focal characters.

Here's the catch I can't get past. The order you focalize through can change the verdict. The first character you visit sets the frame, and the later ones only look acceptable or unacceptable relative to him. So if the test has to be serial, it's not symmetric anymore, and if it skips back to omniscient, it's testing something no actual person can perform. I have a half-worked example about a broken promise where one of the lied-to characters ends up fine iff you visit the liar first and let him frame it as a favor, but if you visit the resentful one first the whole maxim collapses. I'm a novelist so I trust narrative order more than most people, but that might be the problem.

Tell me honestly: is focal universalization a coherent idea or just a metaphor I've overextended from craft? Is there a name for this already in the literature — am I accidentally rebuilding an ideal observer theory, or something Hare already handles with his fanatic, or something worse? And where does it actually break? Give me the strongest objection and tell me if its fatal or just annoying. dont pull punches, I'd rather kill it early than build a book on a bad idea.
