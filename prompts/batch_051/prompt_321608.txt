Over the last year or so I've been keeping a mental list of things that seem to actually get better over time versus things that just... don't, and I wrote down my gut verdicts. Coding, obviously: you write a function, it errors, you fix it, and a year later I'm way better than when I started. My dad always says five-day weather forecasts now are as good as two-day ones were when he was a kid — the feedback is just one number (did it rain or not) and its delayed, but forecasters clearly improved. Medicine definitely got better even though the feedback is really messy: a patient recovers and you don't know for sure why. Chess has exploded in skill level mostly because of engines, which is like instant feedback on every move. Debate club made me improve fast because the judge tells you exactly which argument lost them the round. And I'd say strength training programs are better now than in the 1970s, even though there's a ton of bro science, because velocity trackers tell you immediately whether a rep was fast or a grind.

On the other side: astrology never gets anywhere even though astrologers produce new readings every single day. Macroeconomics feels like the arguments from 1970 are still the arguments now, even though there is oceans of data. Literary criticism of the same poems just seems to spin out more and more interpretations and none of them ever get ruled out. String theory has been going for over forty years with amazing math and zero tested predictions, and it feels stuck. Nutrition science flips around — eggs were bad, then fine, then bad again — and I can't tell if it's progressing or just churning. And poetry writing in English class: I get graded and the teacher leaves comments, but I don't think I actually got better at poetry the way I got better at code, even though there was feedback.

My first guess was "speed of feedback," but weather and medicine break that, and so does the weightlifting versus nutrition comparison. My second guess is something about whether the feedback can actually tell you a specific claim was wrong. But then chess isn't really like that — it's more like making errors and correcting them. I'm going in circles. Can you give me a principle (or a couple candidate principles) that captures which of these fields improve over time, say what it predicts about cases I didn't list like climate science or clinical psychology, and tell me which of my verdicts I'd have to drop to make the principle clean?
