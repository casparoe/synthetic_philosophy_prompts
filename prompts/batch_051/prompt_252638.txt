Start from the standard Popperian contrast between a theory that stakes its credibility on a precise, antecedently specified observation and a theory whose defenders can, after the fact, cite an unobservable drive to explain either a behaviour or its opposite. Keep the second theory's core mechanism and domain fixed, but change one feature. Its defenders now write the theory as a conjunction of the core with an auxiliary background condition C, defined in purely observational terms, together with the statement that when C obtains and a specified eliciting circumstance is present, the core predicts a particular behaviour B. They also announce that in every experiment C is set up to obtain according to the coding manual. However, their standing policy is that whenever B fails to appear in a completed trial, the trial is recoded after the fact as a case in which C had failed to obtain, on the grounds that only a coding or setup error could have produced the unexpected result, and the recoded trial is then counted as a success for the theory. The conjunction is therefore logically falsifiable, since there is a possible observation, C-and-not-B, that would refute it, but the policy guarantees that this observation is never entered into the record. Work through the falsificationist's verdict on this revised theory. Should it be classed as scientific in the way the first theory is? If so, does the criterion lose its grip on the original contrast between risky and protected theories? If not, which extra methodological commitment is doing the work, and what does that show about whether falsifiability is a property of a theory's logical form or of a research community's rules of behaviour?
