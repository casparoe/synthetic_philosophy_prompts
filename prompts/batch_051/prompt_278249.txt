You are a moral philosopher with expertise in the ethics of historical memory and comparative judgments about mass atrocity. I am a philosophy professor preparing a seminar on whether historical evils can, or should, be ranked against one another.

I am compiling a list of arguments for the position that ranking historical atrocities — for instance, saying that one genocide was worse than another, or constructing a comparative scale of mass suffering — is either impossible in principle, morally objectionable, or both. The arguments I have collected so far are these:

(1) Incommensurability of goods. Different atrocities destroy different kinds of goods — human lives, bodily integrity, cultural continuity, basic trust, political institutions, the possibility of future flourishing — and there is no privileged weighting among these goods, so any ranking must impose a metric that the subject matter does not supply.

(2) Non-quantitative wrong-making features. Any ranking will be driven largely by scale — numbers killed, displaced, enslaved, starved — but the moral character of an atrocity also turns on features that resist a single measure: intention, betrayal, humiliation, the deliberate destruction of a way of life. A single ordering flattens exactly what is most significant about the wrong.

(3) Documentary asymmetry. Our knowledge of different atrocities is systematically uneven, because it depends on who could leave testimony, whose records were preserved, and whose destruction was counted. A comparative judgment will therefore partly track the accident of the historical record rather than the underlying evil.

(4) Instrumentalization and equal worth. Making the victims the data for a comparison treats them as material for a scholarly or political exercise rather than as singular moral claimants, and it adds a further wrong by arranging the dead in a hierarchy of greater and lesser victims, in tension with their equal moral worth.

(5) Competitive victimhood. Rankings are predictably captured by present-day politics, in which groups compete to have their suffering certified as the gravest in order to claim standing, resources, or immunity from criticism. Comparison thereby corrupts the memory it claims to serve.

(6) Standpoint and standing. No one occupies a neutral position from which to rank, since every judge is situated — as descendant, beneficiary, survivor, or outsider — and no person or institution obviously has the standing to determine which people suffered more.

(7) Historical singularity. Each atrocity is embedded in a particular historical conjuncture — particular technologies, institutions, meanings, and alternatives — and abstracting the event from that conjuncture in order to slot it into a comparative scale falsifies the thing being judged.

(8) The fitting-response objection. The appropriate response to atrocity is mourning, memorial, and acknowledgment rather than calculation. Comparison substitutes a detached, quasi-scientific stance for the response the events call for, and this substitution is what makes the exercise feel obscene.

Each of these strikes me as a distinct ground for the anti-ranking position, and as far as I can tell they are the standard moves in this debate, so I already know them. I am now looking for one further argument for the same broad conclusion that is genuinely different in structure from all eight — not a rewording of any of them, not a special case or application of one of them, and not a combination of two or more of them.

Please proceed as follows. First, brainstorm at least three candidate arguments in a sentence or two each, and flag for each whether it collapses into one of the eight above. Then take the strongest candidate that survives, develop it carefully, explain precisely what it adds that the eight listed arguments do not, and give your honest assessment of whether it is ultimately a strong argument or a weak one.
