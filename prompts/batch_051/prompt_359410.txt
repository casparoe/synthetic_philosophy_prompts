In statistical learning, the value of simplicity is usually instrumental rather than aesthetic: among models that fit the observed data acceptably, the simpler one often predicts new data better, and a model that becomes sufficiently complex begins to fit noise rather than signal. The bias-variance trade-off makes this precise in a narrow way: every extra parameter buys a better fit to the sample at some cost in generalization to new data.

I notice a structurally similar dialectic in ethical theory. A moral theory is expected to fit our considered intuitions about cases. When it does not, its defenders either revise the theory with qualifications and exception clauses or pressure the intuition. The more qualifications the theory accumulates, the better it fits the intuitions, but the more it looks as if it is being fitted to whatever data are put in front of it. Critics of such theories often object that a simpler, stricter theory is preferable even at the price of counterintuitive verdicts. What I cannot decide is whether the parallel with statistical model selection is deep or superficial.

If we treat moral intuitions as the data and moral theories as models, then a principle with many exception clauses looks like a high-variance model, and parsimony looks like a guard against overfitting the theory to a particular set of cases. But in statistics, parsimony gets its value entirely from a target that is independent of the sample: out-of-sample predictive error. In ethics, there is no obvious analogue of a held-out test set or of a distribution of moral truths from which intuitions are drawn. A theory's verdicts about cases and our intuitions about those same cases do not stand in the relation of prediction to observation; they are closer to two sides of a reflective-equilibrium negotiation.

Please assess this parallel for me. State the mapping that would be needed for results to transfer from one domain to the other, work out what would have to be true on the ethics side for that mapping to hold, and identify at least one thing the parallel seems to predict that turns out false once the mapping is made precise. In particular, address whether it makes sense to say that a parsimonious but counterintuitive ethical theory would generalize better to cases we have not yet considered, and what that could even mean when those cases are precisely the sort of thing our intuitions are meant to adjudicate. In the end, say whether treating simplicity as a virtue in ethical theory is best understood as the same kind of virtue it is in statistical modeling, as a different virtue justified on different grounds, or as a false analogy.
