I'm a novelist, and I've been chewing on something I noticed while revising a scene that might turn out to be nothing. You know the forecasting distinction between the inside view and the outside view—inside view is planning from the particulars of your own case (our people are talented, our plan is sound, we've already made progress), outside view is ignoring all that and asking what actually happens to projects like this in general. It struck me that this is almost exactly the machinery of point of view in fiction. In close third or free indirect discourse, the reader gets locked into a character's inside view: the plan, the reasons, the certainty that thier case is special. The scene's irony comes from the reader or narrator holding an outside view at the same time. Except in fiction the outside view isn't a base rate—it's the fact that the reader knows what kind of story they're in, or the narrators tone has already told them it won't work, or it's chapter three of a tragedy. Both cases feel like the same thing: the particular view is vivid and seductive and wrong; the abstract view is colder and, usually, righter.

Here's what I want to know: is this a real structural identity, or am I just pattern-matching on inside=character and outside=narrator? Take the role of a professional philosopher who has a good feel for narrative theory, and think carefully—brainstorm a bit before you answer if that helps. Give me the mapping explicitly and push on it. Does anything transfer between the two fields? For example, fiction deliberately shows the inside view in maximum detail so that when the outside view's verdict lands it hurts as tragedy rather than as statistic—does that explain why forecasters cling to the inside view, or is that a different thing entirely? And from the other direction: if the outside view almost always wins in forecasting, does that mean fictional narrators are doing something like reporting base rates, or is dramatic irony a different mechanism because the ending is already fixed, not merely probable?

Where's the first place the analogy breaks? My own suspicion is that fictional irony is fatalistic rather than statistical—the reader doesn't know that most such plans fail, they know that this one will, because the author has already decided it. But maybe that's exactly what makes the comparison interesting rather than a reason to drop it. Tell me whether the parallel is deep, superficial, or somewhere in between, and name something the parallel would seem to predict that turns out false.
