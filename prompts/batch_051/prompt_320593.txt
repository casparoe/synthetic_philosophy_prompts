I work in educational measurement, where a large part of my job is equating score scales across test forms: choosing a function that maps raw scores on one form onto the scale of another so that the scores can be used interchangeably. Equating is possible only because the design shares something across forms, typically common items or equivalent groups of test-takers. A recent argument with a colleague in economics about how to weigh students' academic growth against other outcomes pulled me into the literature on interpersonal utility comparisons, and I was struck by how much comparing my utility with yours looks like equating test forms in the absence of anchors. Even when a utility function is cardinal, its zero and unit are arbitrary until something fixes them; making one person's benefit commensurable with another's seems to require exactly the kind of cross-scale link that anchor items supply in psychometrics. The extended-preference strategy — asking a judge to rank alternatives such as 'being person i in social state x' over 'being person j in social state y' — strikes me as precisely an anchor-item design in which the judge provides the common items. I cannot tell whether this is a genuine structural identity, one that would let results from equating theory (about what is identified under each linking design, or when anchors drift) transfer to welfare economics, or whether I am just pattern-matching. Set out the mapping as precisely as you can, say what would have to be true of utility for the transfer to work, and name something the parallel predicts that turns out false — for instance, whether unit comparability without a common zero has a real equating analogue, or whether extended-preference judgments are vulnerable to the same drift that invalidates anchor items.
