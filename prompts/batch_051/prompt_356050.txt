I head the enforcement division of a tax authority. Roughly forty team leaders make daily decisions about which cases to audit, how much discretion to give inspectors, and which results count as good performance; the stakes include fines, livelihoods, and corruption. My predecessor ran the division on character — recruiting people of integrity, running ethics training, trusting senior staff — and gaming and minor extortion crept back. I have been persuaded, in outline, by Han Feizi's Legalism: a well-ordered institution cannot rest on the hope that officials will be virtuous, and the ruler should govern through clear public standards, positional authority, and the two handles of reward and punishment, checking claimed performance against actual results rather than relying on intentions or reputation. But I am not a philosopher, and my deputies find the Legalist answer cold and, in its pure form, impossible in a large modern office.

Write me a reusable procedure, grounded in that Legalist position, for deciding for any given role or task in the division whether to govern it through incentives tied to observable outputs or through selecting and cultivating character, and for recognizing where even the Legalist answer says incentives alone are not enough. The procedure must be usable by a deputy with no background in philosophy and no time for theory: an ordered checklist or set of tests, with the distinctions that must survive the translation from political theory to office practice kept, the rest dropped, and judgment calls marked as such. Then apply the procedure to these three cases, including the parts where it forces a calculation I will not like:

- an inspector judging whether a declared import value is genuinely plausible, a judgment we cannot directly observe;
- a team leader whose unit meets every quota but who is widely known to trade leniency for small gifts;
- a division-wide rule against accepting gifts that staff uniformly ignore.

Say explicitly what the procedure cannot settle, and where I as head cannot abdicate a judgment of my own.
