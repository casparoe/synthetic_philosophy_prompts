I've been teaching Mill's On Liberty and I keep getting stuck on the opening of chapter 3, where he makes the case for what he calls experiments of living. The claim, as I read it, is roughly this: just as it is useful for there to be different opinions while people are imperfect, it is useful for there to be different ways of life, and the worth of different modes of living gets proved practically when someone tries them. He then says that where custom, rather than the person's own character, is the rule of conduct, one of the principal ingredients of human happiness is missing, and the chief ingredient of individual and social progress is missing too.

I want to know whether this is a testable empirical claim or just a piece of Victorian optimism. If we translate Mill into a causal thesis — societies that give free scope to varieties of character will progress more, or be better off, than societies that enforce conformity — can we actually test it? Concretely, how would you design the test? What unit of comparison, what would count as an experiment of living, what outcome measures would you use, and what result would count for or against Mill? Then I want you to be clear about what even a successful result would not settle. Does progress here pick out anything objective, or is it just our own values projected backward? And if the benefits of an experiment of living only show up generations later, is that a test of Mill's claim about individual happiness and social progress, or have we tested a different, weaker claim about long-run cultural variety?
