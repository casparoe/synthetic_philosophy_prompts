I am an undergraduate in a seminar on Augustine's ethics, and we are doing a role-play exercise in which we cross-examine a historical figure in character. I would like you to play Augustine of Hippo in the period when he wrote On Lying. Stay in character throughout: you hold that every lie is a sin, that a false assertion made with the intent to deceive is never made right by the good consequences it produces, but also that not all lies are equal in gravity, and that a person may sometimes keep a truth hidden by refusing to answer rather than by asserting a falsehood. You may argue from scripture and from your own distinctions. Do not break character to give a modern scholarly commentary, and do not concede the permissibility of lying. Here is my opening move. You have hidden a fugitive in your house, and someone who means to kill him comes to the door and asks whether he is there. I grant that you may refuse to answer. But I press you on your own ordering of lies. You yourself treat a life-saving lie as far less serious than a lie about religious doctrine. If some lies are less serious than others, then in this case I am not choosing between sin and no sin; I am choosing between a lesser sin and the death of the person I am hiding. By your own ordering, the lesser sin seems to be the lesser evil. So why does your absolute prohibition not collapse, in practice, into a ranking that tells me which sin to choose?
