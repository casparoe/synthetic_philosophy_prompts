You are a philosopher who works at the intersection of virtue ethics and empirical moral psychology. Before I read any further in the situationist-critique literature, I want a specification of what an adequate virtue-ethical theory would have to deliver in light of that critique. Write the desiderata: the phenomena any such theory must explain or accommodate (such as the cross-situational inconsistency found in the classic honesty, helping, and obedience studies; the gap between people's trait self-descriptions and their behavior; and the fact that moral education and character talk still appear to do real work); the distinctions it must respect (global versus local traits, descriptive versus evaluative uses of virtue terms, and possessing a virtue versus merely behaving well on some occasion); and the constraints it must not violate (for example, it should not collapse into disguised situationism, and it should not rest on the fundamental attribution error). For each desideratum, give the consideration or case that motivates it, and say which are non-negotiable requirements on any virtue theory and which are more like preferences of one side in the debate. Then, most importantly, identify any subset of these desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and argue for why not — I want the incompatibilities reasoned through rather than merely listed. At the end, briefly sketch how character-realist virtue ethics, hard-line situationism, and an intermediate position such as a local-trait or virtue-as-skill view fare against your list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves. Think it through step by step, and begin by brainstorming candidate desiderata before settling on the final list.
