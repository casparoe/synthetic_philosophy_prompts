I've been going back and forth on how to price some weird gambles, and I want a single principle that fits — or at least to know which of my instincts have to go. These are all fair coins flipped until the first heads; let n be the flip the first heads lands on, so n has probability 1/2^n.

1. St. Petersburg: win 2^n dollars. I'd pay about $20, no more then that, even though the naive expected value is infinite.

2. Negative St. Petersburg: lose 2^n dollars. I won't play for any positive price. Maybe if someone paid me a lot to take it, but honestly I'd probably still refuse.

3. Pasadena: win (−1)^(n−1)·2^n/n dollars, so the sign alternates. The naive sum is 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + … = ln 2 ≈ .69. I'd pay about seventy cents.

4. Pasadena plus a dollar: win 1 + [the Pasadena payoff]. I'd pay about $1.70. Shifting everything by a dollar just shifts my price by a dollar; that seems right.

5. Negative Pasadena: win −(−1)^(n−1)·2^n/n, i.e. flip the sign on the Pasadena payoff. I'd need to be paid about seventy cents to take it.

My working rule has been: sum the expected payoffs in the natural order; if the series converges, conditionally or not, that's the fair price; if it diverges to ±∞, fall back on a rough risk-averse number. That fits all five, but it feels like curve-fitting.

So: is there a principled version of that, or a better principle, that gets these? I want you to actually commit to one and defend it, not lay out options. What does it say about a game that pays 2^n/n with only positive signs (so the sum is Σ 1/n, diverging), and about one with the same magnitudes but signs +, +, −, +, +, −, …? And which of my verdicts, if any, should I overturn? In particular, tell me whether the conditional convergence in the natural order is doing real work, and why the natural order should matter rather than some reordering.
