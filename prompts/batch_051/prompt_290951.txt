I spent four decades in the physical sciences before turning to philosophy, and I have carried over a taste for theories that are parsimonious, internally coherent, and willing to follow their commitments to the end. Classical theism attracts me for precisely that reason. The doctrine that God is omniscient, wholly immutable, atemporal, and possessed of a single perfect act of will — and, in its stronger forms, simple rather than composite — strikes me as the tidy version of theism. It refuses to make God a larger, longer-lived human agent, and it supplies a principled stopping point for questions about what God could learn, regret, or be moved by.

But that doctrine collides with a practice I cannot bring myself to write off. Petitionary prayer — asking God to heal a sick child, avert a disaster, soften a hard heart — persists in nearly every theistic tradition, and those traditions enjoin it rather than merely tolerating it. When the need is close and urgent, the act does not feel like a vestige or a piece of anthropomorphic theatre; it feels like a request that could matter. Yet on the classical picture it is difficult to see how it can. An immutable God cannot be moved from one intention to another by my asking. An omniscient God already knows my need before I ask. A God whose will is settled from eternity cannot divert his plan because I prayed at three in the morning: if the plan already included what I requested, the outcome would have occurred without the prayer; if it did not, the prayer asks for nothing. The theory that struck me as elegant now seems to purchase its elegance by making a central practice of the religious life incoherent, or at best a private exercise in adjusting my own attitudes.

I am not yet sure which side should yield. Present each resolution at its strongest: (a) the case for debunking the intuition, treating the felt force of petition as anthropomorphic, parochial, or spiritually immature, and recommending that the practice be deliberately re-educated into something else; (b) the case for amending or abandoning the classical attributes — for example, moving to a God who is in time, takes risks, and can genuinely be moved, so that petition makes strict causal sense; and (c) the case for reconciliation, in which prayer neither changes God nor reduces to mere self-edification — perhaps because God's single eternal act includes the asking itself as the instrument through which certain goods come about, or because the primary effect of petition is on the pray-er's alignment with God. For each option, tell me what would actually justify the move, not merely that some tradition has made it. And throughout, distinguish what is settled among those who work on these questions from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present one school's resolution as if it were the consensus.
