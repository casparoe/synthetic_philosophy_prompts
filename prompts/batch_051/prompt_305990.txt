I cover science and ideas for a living, and I keep noticing that the same test seems to do boundary work in two fields I report on. In defamation law, the line between actionable fact and protected opinion is drawn, roughly, by whether a statement is capable of being proved true or false: a pure evaluation or prediction is usually treated as opinion, while a statement is actionable when it carries or implies a verifiable factual assertion, whatever its surface form. Ratings and forecasts—credit ratings, product scores, epidemiological projections—are the cases where this line strains most, because they present themselves as measurements while courts often treat them as opinion. The logical positivists used a version of the same criterion, verifiability, to sort genuine factual statements from pseudo-statements and emotive utterances: what cannot be verified is not fact-stating at all. So the same test appears to decide, in the legal case, which speech exposes a speaker to liability and, in the philosophical case, which speech counts as meaningful. I cannot tell whether this is a real identity of structure, a loose analogy, or just two fields borrowing the same fact/value vocabulary in ways that only look alike. If the parallel is real, I would expect results to transfer between the two; if it is superficial, the two uses of verifiability should come apart somewhere sharp. In particular, I want to know what the law's struggle with ratings and forecasts tells us, if anything, about the epistemology of the fact/opinion boundary, and where the parallel breaks. Please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
