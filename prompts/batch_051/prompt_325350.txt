My friend who trades for a living has this analogy he won't shut up about: the market is basically a dispersed committee, and prices are its stated opinions. Every executed trade is someone getting up to speak and putting money behind what they say; size is how loud they are; the bid-ask spread is how far apart the committee members still are. When volume dries up he says you shouldn't read that as "nobody cares" but as "the meeting just ended and everyone has agreed." He ties it to some no-trade result he half-remembers from grad school: if everyone is rational and knows everyone else is rational, and no one wants to trade at the current price, then no one has any private information that contradicts the price—so by the theorem the committee has literally aggregated all the private info into a single number. His punchline is that any individual with a hunch the price is wrong is just failing to update on the collective wisdom, unless they can actually find someone willing to take the other side.

I find this deep and suspicious in equal measure. Can you audit the analogy and the theorem it's carrying? Give me the mapping first: what corresponds to the committee members, the speeches, the agenda, the vote, the consensus. Then go at the disanalogies: noise traders, people selling because they need cash, index funds, differing priors, disagreement that's about values or interpretation rather than facts, and the fact that a market has no agreed question it's trying to answer. And importantly, tell me whether the no-trade theorem can actually carry the weight my friend puts on it, or whether he's quietly importing common priors and perfect rationality and then congratulating the market for being rational. Does his conclusion survies once the disanalogies are in, and is there a better analogy for what a thin market with a stable price actually tells you?
