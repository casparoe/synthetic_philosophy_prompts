I've got a habit I use whenever someone makes a bare plural claim — the ones with no 'all' or 'some,' like 'immigrants commit crimes,' 'pit bulls are dangerous,' 'mosquitoes carry malaria.' I silently translate the statement into a statistical one and treat it as true only if a substantial fraction of the group actually does the thing, and I mostly discount arguments that lean on such claims when the fraction is small. My friend who does linguistics says I'm butchering the semantics: 'ducks lay eggs' is true even though only some female ducks lay eggs at any given time, and 'mosquitoes carry malaria' is true even though in many places only a small minority of mosquitoes are carrying it. He says these statements aren't head counts — they say something about the kind. But I'm not trying to do semantics, I'm trying to decide whether to cross the street or support a policy, and for that I want base rates. Is my translation a defensible epistemic habit, or is he right that I'm systematically mishearing what's said? And if there's a bit of both, is there a restated version of my rule that keeps the protection without the distortion?
