I am a retired physical scientist who has come to philosophy late, and the puzzle that has most unsettled my habits of thought is the dogmatism paradox. If I know something, then any evidence against it must be misleading, since it points away from a truth; so when an objection arrives, I can seem entitled to set it aside without serious examination. But the reasoning makes no first-person distinction between what I know and what I merely believe I know, so it threatens to make everyone a dogmatist. For the last meeting of my epistemology reading group I would like a nineteen-line villanelle in the voice of someone who has just dismissed a serious-looking objection to a belief he holds. Let one refrain be the dogmatist's move, some version of the thought that evidence against what one knows is by its nature misleading, and let the other refrain be the worry that returns against it, that the move proves too much. The refrains should do structural work: someone who knows the paradox should recognize it being reasserted until the two moves tangle, and someone who does not should still feel why the position cannot settle. Keep the paradox unresolved, with the final stanza bringing the two refrains together. Please write in American English.
