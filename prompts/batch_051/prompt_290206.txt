ok so i'm trying to sort out my views on whether an eternal afterlife is actually desirable, mostly because i keep finding myself agreeing with both sides of the Williams/Makropulos argument and i want someone to poke at the whole set before i present it in a seminar. here's where i've landed: (1) Williams is onto something real when he says a human life stretched out indefinitely would eventually go stale or stop feeling as if it is going anywhere, and the woman who has lived for centuries and just wants it to end is a genuine possibility; (2) but i don't think that's a deep fact about immortality itself, only a fact about the psychology we happen to have, so an omnipotent god could rekindle our desires or redesign us so tedium never sets in; (3) personal identity over a long afterlife has to be carried by continuity of the things we actually care about, not just memory, so if god has to keep replacing my final ends wholesale to stop me getting bored, the later person might remember being me without being me in the sense that matters, which would mean my eternal life wasn't really mine; (4) seperately, i hold a broad desire-satisfaction view of wellbeing: a life goes well for the person living it to the extent that the desires they actually have at the time get satisfied; (5) i also think achievement and growth matter in themselves, and achievements are partly constituted by the possibility of failure, so a guaranteed outcome that could not have gone wrong is less valuable as an achievement than one that could have; (6) despite that, i don't think heavenly bliss has to be about achievement at all — it might be closer to being absorbed in music or beauty, which has no success condition, though i'm not sure whether that just means (5) doesn't apply to heaven or whether something else is going on; (7) and i have a lingering worry that boredom is a functional signal, the thing that tells a mind to stop doing what isn't worth doing, so a mind redesigned never to feel bored would be unable to tell which activities are worth continuing, and that sounds more like a loss than a gain. what i'd like is a proper consistency audit: which of these are genuinely in tension, which only look as though they are, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and say for each what would have to give or be weakened and what the cheapest revision is, then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. don't presume (1) is my fixed point — i honestly don't know which one i'd drop. please write it as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, the way you'd write a letter, in British English, and at the end say how confident you are in the audit overall and which specific point you're least sure about.
