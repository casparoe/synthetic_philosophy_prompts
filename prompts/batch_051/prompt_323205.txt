Last night a friend and I went back and forth for two hours on a question neither of us could settle, and I'd like a third view that isnt just a restatement of the same two positions. We were arguing about what ought to give way when a principle we're fond of comes into conflict with a robust batch of intuitions about concrete cases. His line was that the principle is doing the real work: case intuitions are hopelessly context-sensitive, they get nudged around by framing, and they mostly reflect whatever we happen to have been trained to notice. I replied that every principle we actually trust got there by surviving a long run of awkward cases, so treating the principle as the fixed point looks circular, and invoking simplicity at that point just sneaks an aesthetic preference in as evidence.

The concrete place we got stuck was transitivity. I said I can honestly imagine someone with considered pairwise preferences that run A over B, B over C, and C over A, formed after reflection, with the descriptions rich enough that none of the options is quietly changing between comparisons. My friend said that is simply what a bug in case judgments looks like: any decent account of rational choice treats transitivity as an axiom rather than a result, so the case judgments lose by default. I ended up around 0.6 that the case intuitions should count as at least as much evidence as the principle in that kind of clash, and he was around 0.2, and it turned out most of the argument was downstream of that gap.

Give me your actual credence in the following claim: when a robust pattern of considered case judgments is intransitive in the way I described, that pattern should lower a rational person's confidence in transitivity as a requirement of rational preference at least as much as it lowers their confidence in the case judgements themselves.
