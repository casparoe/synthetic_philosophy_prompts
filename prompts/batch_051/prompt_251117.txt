Please describe the most important implications of the problem of old evidence for how I should think about postdiction. I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years and I'm comfortable with Bayesian talk, but I keep running into a practical version of this: someone proposes a clever theory that fits a bunch of data we already had, and nobody can agree how much credit the theory deserves for that. The problem of old evidence says, roughly, that if the evidence was already known before the theory came along, a Bayesian can't straightforwardly say its confirmed by it, because the evidence already has probability 1 for you. But I'm not sure what that actually implies for a real person trying to update. Does it mean postdictions should get no weight, or near-zero weight, or that we're just using the wrong formalism, or something else? What are the most important implications for pre-registration, for how much to trust theories constructed after the fact, and for whether 'it fits the data we already had' can ever be evidence for anything? Please write the answer in British English, and go beyond the toy version: I want to know which implications actually survive contact with messy human reasoning and which are just artefacts of treating an agent as a perfect logician.
