I am a graduate student TA for an upper-level undergraduate course in the philosophy of mathematics, cross-listed with mathematics. In two weeks I have to run a fifty-minute problem session for students who have completed linear algebra and one semester of abstract algebra: they know polynomial rings, field extensions, and the definitions of ring and field homomorphisms and automorphisms, but they have not seen Galois theory, and I am not allowed to build the session on it. The assigned topic is the conceptual question of whether it matters how you label the roots of a polynomial, introduced through the familiar fact that the two square roots of −1 are distinct but complex conjugation swaps them while fixing every real number. My own first exposure to this topic produced exactly the confusion I want to avoid: I was told that the choice of which root to call i was merely conventional, and I overcorrected into believing that i and −i are the same number and that any definition that chooses a root is illegitimate.

Don't explain the material to me; plan how I should teach it. I need the sequence of components in the order they must be introduced and why that order — what depends on what, and where the idea of an automorphism of the complex field fixing the reals must already be in place before the payoff. I need a single carrying example for the whole session; it should let the students feel why the two roots are distinct yet indiscernible by any statement built from the field operations with real parameters, and it should make vivid that the move of taking the one with positive imaginary part merely presupposes an orientation of the imaginary axis, which is the same choice being made. Give me the likely misconceptions in the order students will form them — I expect at least the pair of opposite errors I fell into, plus the belief that well-definedness requires the chosen object to be unique — and for each, the exact stage at which it should be headed off and how. End with one prompt I can put to the section that would show whether they have grasped the distinction between two objects being distinct but symmetric under an automorphism, and a definition's failing to be well-defined, and specify what a good answer contains and what each characteristic wrong answer indicates. Finally, say what must be left out at this level because it needs Galois theory, and what I should say to a student who asks.

Return the plan as a JSON object with these fields. session_goal: a two-sentence statement of what students should understand by the end. sequence: an ordered list of objects, each with step, component (the idea introduced), depends_on (earlier components it presupposes), why_here (why it comes at this point), and minutes (out of fifty). carrying_example: an object with example, what_it_must_have, what_it_must_lack, and why_alternatives_fail. misconceptions: a list of objects, each with misconception, where_it_surfaces, stage_to_head_off, and how_to_head_off. check_for_understanding: an object with prompt, correct_response_contains, and what_each_wrong_answer_indicates. deferred: a list of objects, each with topic, reason_to_defer, and what_to_tell_a_student_who_asks.
