At a hospital ethics committee meeting, a transplant surgeon closed off a discussion about donation after circulatory determination of death by saying, "The dead donor rule settles it — organs may be removed only from the dead." Since then I have seen the same phrase put to what look like incompatible uses. One colleague invokes it to defend the neurological criterion: a patient who meets the brain-death standard is dead, so recovering organs does not violate the rule. Another invokes it against the very DCD protocol our hospital uses — after cardiac arrest, absent circulation for five minutes, on the assumption that autoresuscitation will not occur, death is declared and retrieval begins — on the grounds that such a patient's heart could in principle be restarted by intervention, so the patient is not dead but dying. A third uses the rule to mean something narrower: the act of organ retrieval must never itself be the cause of death, whatever criterion determines that death has occurred. It seems to me that the rule appears to settle these disagreements only because different people pack different content into "dead," "irreversible," and "before removal."

I would like you to lay out, as a menu of distinct readings rather than a single interpretation, the principles the dead donor rule could be expressing in these debates. For each reading, specify what it commits one to about the neurological criterion, the circulatory criterion, and the distinction between permanent and irreversible cessation — permanent meaning that circulation will not resume because no attempt to restart it will be made, irreversible meaning that it could not be restored by any intervention. Then say of each reading whether it is a near-tautology, a substantive and defensible moral claim, or a claim that should be rejected. Finally, say which readings would actually license or forbid the donation-after-circulatory-death protocol and donation after brain death as I described them, and whether the rule, once disambiguated, settles anything.
