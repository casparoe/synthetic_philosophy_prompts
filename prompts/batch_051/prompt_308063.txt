Over dinner, a friend of mine — a mathematician who likes to treat practical philosophy as an extension of decision theory — flattened my ambivalence about the next election into what he called the settled account of the ethics of voting. I want to know how much of it is fair before I repeat it to anyone. His account, as close to his own wording as I can reconstruct it:

'The rational-choice result is that an individual vote is practically never pivotal; the chance of breaking a tie is on the order of one in a million even in a close election. Because the expected benefit of your vote is that tiny probability multiplied by whatever difference the election makes, it is almost always swamped by the cost of getting to the polling place. It follows that there is no defensible duty to vote — a duty with no causal effect is just superstition. Once you drop that superstition, the only remaining reason to vote is expressive: you vote the way you wear a T-shirt, to say who you are. So the ethics of voting is just the ethics of self-expression — a vote cannot be right or wrong except insofar as it sincerely expresses your values and shapes your own character. From there, lesser-evil reasoning is unavoidable if you are in the booth at all in a two-candidate race. A third-party vote is just another statement, and its content is "I would rather feel pure than help the side I claim to prefer" — which is materially indistinguishable from endorsing the worse candidate. So the only defensible vote in a competitive race is for the lesser evil, full stop.'

Like I said, I'm inclined to accept that final move about a close race, but he seems to get a sweeping conclusion about duty from a merely probabilistic premise, and I'm not sure "expressive act" can bear all the weight he puts on it. Which parts of his summary are fair to the positions he's drawing on — the paradox of voting, expressive theories of why people vote — and which parts are overstatements, subtle conflations, or standard objections passing as refutations? Would a careful defender of a duty to vote have to deny the rational-choice premise, or could they accept it? And does the lesser-evil conclusion survive once the earlier steps are corrected? Keep your answer to at most 1,000 words.
