I am a health economist and decision modeler, and until recently I would have said that any serious question about whether to create a life could be settled by comparing expected QALYs across the available options. That confidence has been shaken, for a personal reason: my partner and I are deciding whether to have a child, and the philosophical writing I have encountered on the procreation asymmetry — most centrally, the claim usually associated with David Benatar that a life that never begins has no one to be deprived of its absent pleasures, while its absent pains count as a positive — seems to me to turn on exactly the kind of modeling choices I make at work, but with the signs reversed. I would like you to do a careful translation of this debate into my professional vocabulary, and then report what did not survive.

Please restate the procreation-asymmetry debate and its main neighboring positions in the vocabulary of decision analysis and health-economic evaluation: acts, states of nature, outcomes, utility functions, counterfactual outcomes, expected utility, ex ante versus ex post assessment, dominance, the zero point and scale type of a utility or QALY measure, person-level versus population-level aggregation, and the choice of which counterfactual branches count as outcomes in the model at all. I am highly fluent in this vocabulary and a careful but not formally trained reader of philosophy, so keep the target-language rendering precise rather than introductory.

For the source language, include at least these landmarks and map each one onto the target vocabulary: (1) the four-part asymmetry itself — present pain is bad, present pleasure is good, absent pain is good, absent pleasure is not bad; (2) Benatar's stronger anti-natalist conclusion and the distinction between an axiological version of the asymmetry and a deontic version that could ground an obligation not to procreate; (3) narrow person-affecting views, on which one outcome can be better or worse only for someone who exists in it, and wide person-affecting views, on which comparisons may consider people who exist in only one of the alternatives; (4) the non-identity problem as it bears on whether "the child" is even the same person across the conception and non-conception branches; and (5) the neutral-existence or zero-comparator view, on which never existing can be neither better nor worse because there is no subject for the comparison.

Then give me the residue report. I want to know which disagreements translate cleanly into choices among utility functions, zero points, or restrictions on which outcomes enter the calculation; which can be reproduced in the target vocabulary only by something that looks like an ad hoc modeling restriction; and which cannot be expressed in the vocabulary at all. In particular, I want your judgment on whether the asymmetry can be represented as an internally consistent utility assignment over the four cells, or whether my framework's pressure to assign a single utility to the "no child" branch simply begs the central philosophical question. Do not smuggle in the target framework's assumptions and call the result a translation: show me exactly which stipulation does the philosophical work. Conclude by telling me whether the residue reflects a limitation of decision-theoretic vocabulary, a sign that part of the philosophical debate is less precise than it looks, or some of each. I am not looking for reading recommendations or citations; I want the translation itself and your diagnosis of what was lost in it.
