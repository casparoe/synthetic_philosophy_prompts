I have been reading the debate in democratic theory about the boundary problem, and I want a diagnosis of why it has stalled rather than a verdict on who is right. The question is familiar: before one can ask what a democratic people should decide, one has to know who belongs to that people — yet any rule for drawing the boundary seems itself to require a decision that already presupposes an answer to the question.

The main positions are well developed. One holds that all and only those whose interests are affected by a decision should have a say in it: the all-affected criterion. Another replies that affectedness is far too wide, since in an interconnected world nearly everyone is affected by nearly everything in some respect; it therefore proposes an all-subjected criterion, on which the demos is constituted by those who are subject to the binding laws or coercion of the decision at issue. A third position rejects both as attempts to answer from outside a question that can only arise from within: on this view the boundaries of a demos are given by a prior historical, cultural, or national community, and no universal membership rule is available.

What puzzles me is that all three sides already acknowledge the central circularity and have absorbed it without moving. The all-affected view treats the over-inclusiveness objection as an epistemic difficulty about predicting who will be affected, not a reason to revise the criterion. The all-subjected view treats subjection to coercion, rather than diffuse causal influence, as the only non-arbitrary membership relation. The self-determination view treats the other two as confusing a question that presupposes an existing demos with a question that would settle one. Everyone agrees that one cannot first fix the demos and then democratically decide who belongs to it; they disagree about what follows from that fact.

My working hypothesis is that the three positions are not rival answers to one question, but answers to three different questions — who is affected, who is coerced, who constitutes a people — that have been subsumed under a single heading. A competing diagnosis is that there is a single question, but it is recursively unsolvable, so every theory must ultimately rest on an unmoved starting choice. I would like you to carry out the diagnostic work properly: set out what each side is really asking, say which kind of disagreement this is — verbal, substantive-normative, or a disagreement about what would count as an answer at all — and identify whether the circularity is the real engine of the impasse or merely a surface symptom of a deeper clash of starting points. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves the problem, a reformulation of the question, an acceptance that it has no non-circular solution, or something else.
