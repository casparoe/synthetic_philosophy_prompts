A colleague in our group defends a view she attributes to Thomas Metzinger. On her account, the conscious self is a self-model: an internally generated, continuously revised representation of an embodied and persisting agent. The claim she returns to is that this self-model is phenomenally transparent in ordinary experience. She means transparency in a technical sense: a representation is transparent when a system does not experience the representation as a representation at all, but only the represented content, the way ordinary vision presents a room rather than presenting a visual model of a room. For the self, the claim is that I ordinarily find myself simply given as a bodily point of view in a world, and that the ongoing construction of that point of view leaves no trace in experience. The self-model becomes opaque, she says, only under pressure: in depersonalization, out-of-body episodes, or certain meditative and drug-induced states, where self-modeling itself begins to appear as a process rather than as a world.

I find this easy to say and worry that the view slides between first-person description and system-level architecture whenever it is challenged. She replies that it is also a functional claim about what a system represents and fails to represent about its own processing, and is therefore open to experiment. Concede her point for the sake of argument and treat transparency of the self-model as an empirical hypothesis. Design the strongest test you can: specify the manipulation or contrast, the dependent measures, and the pattern of results that would support the transparency claim, undermine it, or support only a weaker neighboring claim such as limited introspective access to certain bodily representations. Then be precise about the residue: what a maximally favorable result would still fail to show about whether the self really is a model, whether transparency is more than a redescribed absence of access, and what, if anything, remains beyond a third-person experimental framework.
