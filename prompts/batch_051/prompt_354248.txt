You are a professional philosopher with a serious grounding in ancient Stoic philosophy—especially Epictetus, the early Stoic theory of action, and the doctrine of preferred indifferents—and in contemporary work on control, responsibility, and cognitive-behavioral therapy. Take your time, and do not settle for the first familiar formulation that comes to mind.

I have spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and I absorbed the usual toolkit before I ever read the Stoics: expected value, goal factoring, noticing my own rationalizations, and a standing suspicion of claims that exist mainly to make their holders feel better. Stoicism reached me through that lens, mostly in the modern revival form that treats Epictetus's dichotomy of control as a practical algorithm: find the variables under your control, invest in them, and stop worrying about the rest. I have since read enough of the source material to suspect that the "control" framing does violence to the original, and my own verdicts on cases no longer fit either the naive modern version or, I think, the canonical ancient one. I want you to extract the principle my verdicts are approximating, or tell me which verdicts I should overturn.

Here are my concrete judgments.

1. Tennis. My footwork, shot selection, and quality of preparation feel unambiguously up to me. The match result is not unambiguously up to me, since it depends on my opponent and the conditions. I judge it correct to base my self-evaluation on my execution rather than on victory. So far the dichotomy fits. But I am not indifferent to winning: I enter tournaments partly because the possibility of winning is the point, and I do not regard wanting the result as a failure. I think of the result as a probability distribution over factors, some inside and some outside my command, and I still care about the right-hand tail of that distribution.

2. Job interview. My preparation, the quality of my answers, and my honesty are up to me; the committee's decision is not. Yet I do not accept the conclusion that I should do my best and treat the offer as an indifferent. The offer is the point of the activity, and I have substantial partial influence over it through how I frame my experience, the rapport I build, and credentials I spent years earning. I want to say the hiring decision is partly up to me, not simply not up to me.

3. Electoral politics. Whom I vote for is up to me; the election outcome is not. But I also knock on doors, and I take the right reason for that to be, in large part, that the candidate becomes more likely to win. I reject the suggestion that I should set only an internal goal such as acting consistently with my civic values and treat the external outcome as irrelevant. That looks like a roundabout way of refusing to admit that outcomes matter.

4. Insult and anger. When someone insults me, the first surge of anger is not something I choose; at that moment it is not up to me. What is up to me is whether I endorse the anger, ruminate on it, or act on it. But I also believe that years of practice make the first surge smaller and less frequent, so the boundary itself is trainable. I want to say the territory of what is up to me expands with habituation and shrinks when I am sleep-deprived or recently traumatized.

5. Chronic illness. After a diagnosis, the functioning of my body initially struck me as not up to me. But treatment adherence, diet, sleep, and seeking second opinions are up to me and materially change the long-run course of the disease. Classifying health as simply not up to me would, in my case, have been a costly error; health is partly up to me, with a large residual variance I cannot command.

6. A friend's grief. My friend's grief is not up to me; showing up, listening, and not flinching are up to me. I am comfortable with that split. But I should care about whether my friend recovers, not merely about whether I showed up, even though I cannot make recovery happen. The outcome is partly responsive to what I do without being under my command.

7. Belief. If a trusted source tells me something outlandish, I cannot simply decide to believe it. Yet judgment—the act of assenting to impressions—is the very thing Epictetus most insistently calls up to us. I can choose what to read, whom to spend time with, and what practices to adopt, and these choices shape what I will later find credible. Beliefs are not up to me in the moment, but they are substantially up to me over time.

There is a live tension across these cases: the canonical view identifies what is up to us with judgment, but my own experience of belief formation is far more success- and time-dependent than that suggests; and several verdicts treat the same thing—health, hiring, my friend's recovery—as neither fully up to me nor simply outside my control.

Please do the following.

State the best candidate principle, or small set of principles, that could capture these verdicts, precisely enough that the candidates make different predictions. For each candidate, say which of my seven verdicts it preserves and which it would overturn. In doing so, consider whether "up to us" is best read as a claim about causal control, about what the agent can count on as fully reliable, about what the agent is responsible for, or about what one's happiness should supervene on.

Then resolve the interpretive question: is the modern advice to focus on what you can control a faithful successor of the Stoic position or a distortion, and is my temptation to replace the dichotomy with a continuous control-share variable an improvement or a mistake?

Finally, apply the account you defend to two cases I have not settled: (a) whether to try to mend a family rift where my own conduct is under my control but the other person's response is not and may be driven by forces neither of us fully understands; and (b) whether a general should conceive of the aim of a campaign as winning the war, winning the battle well, or something whose conditions of success lie entirely in the general's own power.

Before you write your final answer, brainstorm several candidate principles, including at least one that preserves all seven of my verdicts by introducing more machinery than feels natural, and at least one simple binary principle that forces me to overturn several of them. I want the view you would actually defend, not a survey, and I want it to resist both the fatalist stereotype of Stoicism and the pop-psychology version that turns the philosophy into a productivity hack.
