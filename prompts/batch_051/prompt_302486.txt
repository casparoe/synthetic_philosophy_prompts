My friend and I just burned an hour arguing about mind uploading, and we couldnt even agree on what would count as evidence. His view is that upload-by-scanning is death — it makes a new person who thinks he's you — but replacing a brain one neuron at a time, with each artificial unit doing exactly the job of the cell it replaces and only switching on once it's tuned, lets the original person survive the whole procedure. His line is that there's never a break in the stream, and that a one-neuron swap can't turn the lights out, because your brain already replaces and rewires tiny parts of itself all the time without you going anywhere. So it's not a copy; it's just you on new hardware.

I said I don't see how the organism could tell those two histories apart at the finish, since you end up with the same pattern running on the same kind of device either way, so the only difference between death and survival would be how fast the pattern got installed. He said the difference is in the process, not the end state, and that if we actually ran it we could see it. I couldn't name an observation that would settle which of us was right, but I also dont think my blank means he wins by default.

So here's what I want walked through. Suppose we really could run a gradual replacement on a volunteer, plus a scan-and-copy on a second person and a sham procedure on a third, with everyone blind to who got which. What is the strongest set of measurements you would take before, during, and after to test the claim that the gradual-replacement person is the same person while the scan-and-copy person is not? Be concrete about what result would count as support and what would count as trouble. And then be honest about the part no test can reach: what would even a clean positive result leave open about whether anyone's actually still home in there, or whether process-continuity is just a story we tell ourselves to feel better about dying.
