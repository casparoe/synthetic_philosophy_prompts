I have been trying to think through the philosophical zombie argument on my own, and I keep noticing that it moves in a circle. We imagine a being that is molecule-for-molecule identical to an ordinary person but has no felt experience at all. The argument then says: because we can picture such a being, it is genuinely possible; and because it is possible, there could be two beings exactly alike in every physical respect yet different in consciousness, so consciousness cannot be wholly physical. But when I run through this, the conclusion seems to be just the original picture wearing a stronger word: conceivable turns into possible, and possible then does all the work.

I would like you to write a poem that makes this circular motion felt, not merely described. Please write it as a pantoum of four or five quatrains, the form in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, and the opening lines come back at the end, so the poem closes inside its own beginning. Let the recurring lines carry the argument's recurring moves: the proponent insisting how easily the zombie can be pictured, the critic objecting that what is conceivable need not be possible, and the quiet slide by which conceivable is upgraded to possible. Thread the two voices so the reader watches the conclusion feed the very premise it was supposed to support. The final stanza should catch the argument returning to its starting intuition rather than resolving it. The poem should be accurate enough that someone familiar with the conceivability argument would recognize each move, and clear enough that a reader who has never met the argument would still feel its pull.
