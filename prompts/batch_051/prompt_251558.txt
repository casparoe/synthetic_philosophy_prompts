I chair a standing budget committee at a public university. It meets monthly, and its votes bind the administration, which will not revisit a decision unless the process was defective; I have no professional facilitator, and the other members have not read Habermas. The members range from vice-provosts and deans to undergraduate and graduate student representatives, and they differ sharply in status, speaking experience, and the interests they are there to protect. I want to run the committee so that its decisions come as close as a real two-hour meeting can to the standard in Habermas's account of communicative rationality: a decision is legitimate only if it could be reached in discussion where no one is prevented from raising the relevant validity claims — factual, normative, or about a speaker's sincerity — and nothing settles the outcome except the unforced force of the better argument rather than status, threats, or bargaining. Turn that idea into a procedure I can actually follow before, during, and after each meeting, and that my members can understand without the theory behind it. Give me the checks in the order I should apply them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than procedure. Apply the procedure to two cases: first, a dean proposes reallocating maintenance funds to a lab renovation, and the discussion conspicuously avoids asking whether the affected units were consulted; second, a student-backed proposal to make a campus building more accessible is widely supported, but its factual premise about cost and construction constraints is asserted rather than tested, and no one in the room can verify it. Set up at least one application so that the procedure gives an answer I will not want. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say what the procedure cannot deliver — what part of legitimacy cannot be reduced to a checklist. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about the design: which objection to this picture of legitimacy, or which practical feature of real committees, would lead you to a different procedure, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
