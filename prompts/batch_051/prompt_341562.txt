I've had a weird few months, broke up, moved, started meditating mostly because I couldn't sleep, and I keep circling back to a view I once saw glossed as tranquilism: the idea that the only thing that matters non-instrumentally is the absence of craving and suffering. Positive stuff—pleasure, achievements, relationships—is either worthless in itself or only good insofar as it quiets wanting or pain. I can't shake it because, honestly, the moments that felt best recently were mostly moments where wanting just stopped, not moments where I got what I wanted.

I'm trying to turn this into a short essay and I'd like comments on my rough notes, not the prose. The working thesis is something like: tranquilism has a pursuit problem and an underdetermination problem, and the obvious backpedal doesn't fix both at once.

Notes:

1. The pursuit problem. If only the absence of craving matters, then wanting to become tranquil is itself a craving. So the only way to coherently pursue tranquility is to stop pursuing it. The view can't guide anyone who isn't already there; a tranquilist can't set out to become one, only stumble into it. So tranquilism is either self-effacing (you'd be better off not trying to follow it) or it has to hand out advice that says "don't act on this advice," which is an odd thing for a view about what ultimately matters to say.

2. The underdetermination problem. Absence of craving and suffering also describes dreamless sleep, a deep coma, maybe death, and a rock. Taken literally, then, the view ranks a tranquil awake person's state as no better than a rock's. Most people drawn to tranquilism would flinch at that. The standard fix is: "I mean the absence as had by a conscious subject." But then consciousness is doing value-relevant work, and it's no longer true that only the absence matters. You've smuggled in at least one extra good—there being a subject in the first place.

3. The backpedal I keep trying out for myself: maybe tranquilism is only a claim about how to rank conscious states, not a complete theory of value. Consciousness is a precondition, not a contributor. Does that dodge (2)? Maybe. It still leaves (1), unless you let second-order desires (wanting not to want) count differently from ordinary cravings. But then you need a principle for which cravings are the bad ones, and that seems like it gives up the clean "only absence matters" slogan.

So here's what I actually want: a committed set of comments on these notes. Specifically—does objection (1) go through, or can a tranquilist say the pursuit of non-craving isn't itself the kind of craving the view condemns? And is (2) a real problem, or am I just being sloppy about what "only" quantifies over? Don't lay out readings an leave it there; tell me which side you'd bet on and why, even if it's close.
