I sit on the committee of a small housing co-operative, and a dispute has begun to worry me. The building has one shared common room, and two members have applied for the same Sunday-morning slot in the coming term. The first proposal is a children's music class open to any family in the building. The organiser estimates that a dozen children would attend, and she points out that no other room is large enough and that many parents in the building have few affordable alternatives nearby. The second proposal comes from four residents who want the slot for a silent reading group. They would tolerate other quiet activities in the room, but not a music class. Their case, as one of them put it in writing, is that counting four against twelve misrepresents the choice: for each of them, the quiet of that particular room at that particular hour is a rare and important good, whereas the music class, however lovely, is a pleasant extra that children could forgo without much loss. We considered compromise, but the room cannot sensibly be split for a morning because the class needs time to set up and the readers say the benefit lies in an unbroken quiet stretch.

The disagreement on the committee is now philosophical as much as practical. One member insists that we ought simply to compare the total happiness each proposal would produce. Another replies that no such comparison is possible, because pleasure and benefit are not measured on any common scale across persons, and that every method we might adopt—counting heads, weighing intensity, asking how much each side would sacrifice to get the slot—smuggles in a moral assumption rather than measuring something objective. I find the second view plausible, but also paralysing, since the committee must decide next week. I am less interested in which group ought to get the room than in understanding the conceptual disagreement. Is one of these committee members making a mistake, or are we facing a genuine principled limit? If the latter, how should one reason about a decision that nevertheless cannot be avoided? I would be grateful for a reply in British English.
