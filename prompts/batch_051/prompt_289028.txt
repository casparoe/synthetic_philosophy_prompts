I did experimental physics for thirty years and only started reading philosophy of quantum mechanics after I retired, so forgive the amateur framing. I find the Everett picture almost irresistible as a theory: you keep the Schrödinger equation as the whole story, you drop the collapse postulate and the special status of measurement, and the definiteness of outcomes gets explained as observers in branches being uncertain about which branch they're in. The systematic virtues seem enormous. But I keep tripping on probability. If I prepare an electron in a superposition with unequal amplitudes, I don't just expect one branch or the other — I take it as an objective fact that spin-up is more likely, and the Born rule plus a century of lab work seems to back that up. On the Everett view every branch is real, so "probability" gets demoted to something about my uncertainty about which branch I'm in — and I don't see why I should be uncertain at all, given that all of them occur. I know there are decision-theoretic arguments meant to recover the Born weights, but they always feel like they're quietly assuming the thing I wanted grounded. Am I confusing an objective chance with a degree of belief, or is the theory asking me to give up something I'm right to insist on? What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and is there a reading of "probability" on which both survive?
