I have been reading my way through the philosophical debate about what an emotion fundamentally is, and after a while it began to look less like a debate than like three groups of people each talking about a different paradigm case while pretending they share one subject. The judgment camp says that an emotion, or at least its evaluative core, is a kind of judgment or belief: to fear a dog is, centrally, to take the dog to be dangerous. Recalcitrant emotions are the obvious problem — a person can judge, with full conviction and good evidence, that flying is safe, and still be terrified — and the proposed repairs are always variants on the thought that there is another, more stubborn judgment underneath. The perception camp handles recalcitrance beautifully by taking emotions to be perception-like states that present the world as dangerous, offensive, or lost without needing our assent, but then it has trouble with emotions that seem to require a developed conceptual repertoire, such as indignation at a subtle slight or regret about a decision one still thinks was correct. The feeling camp insists that an emotion just is a pattern of bodily feeling, which gets the lived character of fear right but seems to leave out what the emotion is about, and it struggles with emotions whose bodily signature is weak or absent, such as a cool, settled grief or an intellectual-sounding contempt. The same objections have been running for decades, and I have never seen anyone change camp.

What I want is not an adjudication of which account is right, and not a survey of them. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement. My working hypothesis is this: the three camps are answering different questions under the single word 'emotion' — roughly, one is asking what an emotional state consists in, another what its informational relation to the world is, and the third what it is like and how it shows up in the body. If that is right, is the apparent impasse mostly verbal or, more precisely, a mismatch of questions that could be dissolved by a division of labor? Or is there a substantive disagreement about a single target after all, and if so, which part of it is doing the real work — a hidden empirical question about what the sciences of affect will find, a conceptual question about what a state must do to count as an emotion, or a bedrock judgment about which examples are paradigmatic? Please engage what each camp actually says, and tell me what would count as progress under your diagnosis, whether that is a distinction, a kind of experiment or result, a reconfigured question, or the honest conclusion that no progress should be expected.
