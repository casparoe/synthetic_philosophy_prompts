I keep running into a particular move in the nuclear-strategy literature and in policy-adjacent philosophy seminars, and I want someone to actually audit it rather than nod along. The move is to say that nuclear brinkmanship is basically a game of chicken: two drivers heading at each other, the one who swerves first is chicken and loses, so the rational thing is to precommit to not swerving—by throwing the steering wheel out the window, if you could. It is then used to argue that in a confrontation between nuclear states, the rational strategy is to signal absolute resolve, make escalation credible, and avoid public hedging, because the moment you look willing to compromise you have already lost.

I want to know how far this analogy actually holds. What corresponds to what? Give me a table with the following columns: (1) Feature of the chicken game, (2) Nuclear analogue, (3) Correspondence strength—exact, strained, or false, (4) Assumption the analogy quietly imports, and (5) Whether that disanalogy undermines the resolve-signalling conclusion. Then after the table, two paragraphs: first, what the analogy gets right; second, a concise verdict on whether the policy conclusion survives, or whether a better analogy would be needed—and if so, what that better analogy might be.

One more thing: I do not want a literature review. Separate what is actually settled among people who work on deterrence from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. I am asking because I teach this stuff, and I have started to suspect the chicken picture is doing the argumentative work while pretending to be just a metaphor.
