I'm a novelist, and most of my books are lightly fictionalized lives—not quite biography, not free invention. For the next one I want to stop pretending I'm just telling a good story and admit that I'm doing something in the old Plutarchan line: using a real person's life as a kind of moral mirror. The trouble is, the minute I like a subject I start sanding away their worst parts, and when I don't like them I turn into a debunker. So I'd like you to write me a reusable procedure I can run on my pile of notes before I begin drafting: a checklist in words for deciding (a) whether a particular life can honestly carry moral instruction at all, (b) what the instruction actually is, and (c) whether I'm sliding into hagiography without noticing. This isn't a one-off; I run this with every subject, and the stakes are the difference between a book that sharpens a reader's moral sense and one that flatters them. It has to be something I can actually use over a long afternoon with index cards and uneven sources—some letters, some recollections, a diary maybe, plenty of gaps. Make it operational: ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a human because its a values call rather than a fact of the record. It should also say which steps are really just judgment wearing a checklist costume, and what the procedure cannot do. I don't want a syllabus or a theory summary; I want the steps themselves. Keep it reusable across very different subjects.

To keep it concrete, run the procedure on these two people I've been sketching. One is a doctor who ran a free surgical clinic in a poor port town for two decades and genuinely fought off a corrupt licensing board; nobody disputes the good. She also published a nurse's early ideas as her own without credit, and by every family account she was a cold, punitive mother. The other is a politician who took one brave, personally costly stand that almost certainly averted a massacre, and did it partly because he wanted to be remembered as a hero; he then spent years lying about the fact that the original plan came from an aide he'd fired. For each, tell me what the procedure says, where it balks, and whether I get to write the book I wanted to write. And make sure at least one of the applications gives me an answer I won't like—if a procedure for moral learning from lives never tells me to drop a project, I don't trust it.
