In my seminar on truthfulness and its limits, we have been working through Kant's case of the would-be murderer at the door: someone asks whether your friend is hiding in your house, and Kant holds that even here you may not rightfully lie. I want to see how much of that verdict depends on the particular features of the case, so I have been thinking through the following variant.

Change the questioner from a would-be murderer to a seven-year-old child, and change the question from the whereabouts of a friend to the existence of Santa Claus. The child has been told by both parents and by other trusted adults that Santa exists, is deeply invested in the associated practice, and would be disoriented and hurt by a flat denial; the parent regards the proposition as false. The parent can say the child will be shielded from the truth for now, or she can answer truthfully, or she can evade in a way the child will notice. No third party's life is at stake, and the apparent good of the falsehood is not preventing a killing but sustaining the child's trust in parental testimony and preserving a shared family practice. The addressee is, further, a child whose rational capacities are still developing.

Work through how the standard Kantian analysis of the murderer case applies to this variant. Specifically: (1) Is the parent's affirmative answer best classified as a lie, or as something else—pretence, playful speech, or a non-literal response to the child's underlying request for reassurance? (2) If it is a lie, does the absolute prohibition on lying yield the same verdict here, and are the grounds for that prohibition weaker or stronger when the addressee is a child whose trust in the speaker is still being formed? (3) What would a defender of Kant's no-lying verdict in the murderer case be committed to saying in the Santa case, and what additional principle would they need to avoid either treating all paternalistic falsehoods as permissible or collapsing the Santa case into the murderer case? (4) Reversing direction: if the Santa case is permissible, does its rationale transfer back to the murderer case, or does the structural difference—preventing a foreseeable killing versus preserving a relationship of trust—do all of the ethical work?
