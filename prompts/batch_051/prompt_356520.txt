Alright so I've been reading Mill's On Liberty, and this one passage from Chapter 2 has been stuck in my head:

"On every subject on which difference of opinion is possible, the truth depends on a balance to be struck between two sets of conflicting reasons. Even in natural philosophy, there is always some other explanation possible of the same facts; some geocentric theory instead of heliocentric, some phlogiston instead of oxygen; and it has to be shown why that other theory cannot be the true one: and until this is shown, and until we know how it is shown, we do not understand the grounds of our opinion."

I've been kicking around a project at work to flag "good faith" in online political discussion, and every time I try to define it I end up back at this passage. So before I write another line of code, I want the actual spec. What would a theory of arguing in good faith need to get right for Mill's standards to come out as an output rather than a vibe? Like, what cases does it need to handle — the person who cherry-picks but genuinely thinks the cherry is the whole pie, the person who has read the other side and can steelman it but still thinks they're wrong, the person who is open to evidence but only from outlets they already trust? What distinctions can't it collapse, for instance between actually having considered an objection and just being able to recite it? And does the theory need to be usable from outside — you can't read minds, so what observable behavior counts — or is that asking too much? Give me the requirements list with the case behind each one, mark which are negotiable and which aren't, and tell me which single requirement kills the most popular notions of good faith out there.
