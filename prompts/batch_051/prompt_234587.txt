I am writing an essay for my English class on Natalie Babbitt's novel Tuck Everlasting, and I want to connect it to a debate I have been reading about radical life extension. The novel asks us to take seriously the idea that living forever might be a curse: a family has become immortal after drinking from a spring, and the protagonist has to decide whether to drink from it herself. In the life-extension debate, advocates of treating aging as a medical condition often call the opposing outlook 'deathism,' meaning roughly the view that death is natural and that trying to postpone it indefinitely is misguided, immature, or a denial of what makes life meaningful. Tuck Everlasting would seem to be a perfect literary statement of that outlook, and I want to test that impression rather than assume it.

I would like a reading that treats the novel as a thought experiment rather than as a proof. Which anti-extension arguments does the book actually dramatize — that an endless life would become stagnant and repetitive, that mortality is what gives our choices and relationships their significance, that the spring is too dangerous to fall into the wrong hands — and which does it merely assert through atmosphere or a character's speech? Where does the novel stack the deck, for example by making the immortal family isolated and melancholy instead of showing someone who has used centuries to learn, build, or care for others? Conversely, is there anything in the text that suggests the Tucks' unhappiness comes from secrecy and isolation rather than from immortality itself? I want the essay to show where the novel genuinely engages the strongest version of the pro-longevity position and where it only appears to refute it, rather than simply saying the book is for or against death.
