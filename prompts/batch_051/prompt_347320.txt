Okay so my friend and I just spent like two hours going back and forth on innate ideas last night, and one thing kept bugging me. Pretty much everyone is fine saying a newborn has a built-in sucking reflex, or that a songbird has a built-in template for it's species' song, or that humans have some built-in fear-preparedness for snakes and spiders. Nobody treats that as weird or mystical. But the second you say a baby is born with an innate concept of number, or built-in causal reasoning, or some built-in constraints on what a possible human grammar looks like, people act like you've gone full Descartes and the whole thing is spooky. We treat inborn behaviors as boring biology and inborn ideas as epistemic woo.

I honestly can't tell whether there's a real difference there or whether we're just squeamish about the word 'innate' the moment it touches beliefs and concepts. The candidate lines my friend and I kicked around were: behaviors are just dispositions to move while ideas have content that can be true or false; or capacities are just how the machine runs while knowledge is software loaded in; or instincts do their job without representing the world while concepts are about the world. But each of those seemed to break on closer look — like, a snake-fear disposition sure seems to be about snakes in some sense, and a song template seems to encode information.

So here's the actual question. Pick the strongest principle you can find that separates the innate capacities and dispositions everyone accepts from the innate knowledge empiricists like Locke rejected, and tell me whether it holds up. If it doesn't hold up, who should give ground: the empiricist who wants innate capacities but not innate ideas, or the nativist who thinks innate concepts are supposed to be a big deal? Or is the whole capacity/knowledge line just a historical mess we've inherited? Please answer in American English.
