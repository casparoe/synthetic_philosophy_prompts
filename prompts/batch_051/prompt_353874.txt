I am the treasurer of my high school's student council, and our principal has proposed replacing the way we allocate money to student clubs. Currently the council spends several weeks comparing every funding request line by line, trying to find the fairest distribution of a fixed sum. The principal, who says he has been reading about bounded rationality and Herbert Simon's idea of satisficing, wants to replace this with a process he calls the good-enough grant. The proposed rule works as follows. Before each grant cycle opens, the school board sets two parameters: a viability threshold from 0 to 100 and a maximum number of proposals the committee may read in the single review meeting. Clubs submit proposals through an online portal, and the timestamp determines the order in which they are read. During the three-hour review meeting, two readers score each proposal against a published rubric. As soon as a proposal's average score meets or exceeds the threshold, the proposal is funded for its full request, up to a standing cap of $2,000, and the committee moves on. A proposal below threshold is rejected and may not be reconsidered or appealed during that cycle. The committee is explicitly told not to compare funded proposals with one another and not to hold money back in case a stronger proposal arrives later. Whatever remains at the end of the meeting returns to the district's general fund. The principal's rationale is that the current process asks volunteers to optimize under impossible limits on their time and attention, and that the new process would force every proposal to stand on its own rather than on how persuasive its advocates are.

I need to write a report for the council on this proposal, but I do not want to argue for or against it in the abstract. Please act as an expert in mechanism design and the logic of bounded rationality. Begin by brainstorming the distinct stakeholder groups and the levers each one can pull. Then analyze how the rule would actually behave: what it would incentivize clubs to do before and during submission, what it would incentivize the school board to do when setting the threshold and the reading cap, and what it would encourage the readers themselves to do. Pay particular attention to path dependence, threshold gaming, the information carried by the viability score, and whether the failure modes could be fixed without bringing back the costly optimization the rule was meant to eliminate. I would like the analysis to be as specific as possible rather than a generic debate about whether satisficing is a good idea.
