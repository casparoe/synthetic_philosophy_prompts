Ive been trying to work out what I actually think about the hard problem of consciousness by going on my gut about particular cases rather than general slogans. Here are my verdicts.

There's something it's like to be a bat. I dont just mean that bats behave flexibly; I mean that no matter how complete a story I got about echolocation circuits and auditory cortex, I'd still feel there was a leftover question about what the bat's experience is like. The same goes, even more strongly, for me listening to a chord: a perfect simulation of every neuron firing in my auditory system for two seconds still leaves me wanting to ask "but is the chord actually heard?" But a perfect simulation of one neuron doing its ion-exchange thing leaves no residual question for me. If the simulation gets the ions right, that's all there is to that neuron. And a perfect simulation of my liver during the same two seconds feels the same: no gap. Digestion is the kind of thing where, if you get the chemistry right, you've just got digestion.

I also notice the gap vanishes for some information-processing things. I don't think there's anything it's like to be my camera's autofocus, even when it's tracking faces. I don't think there's anything it's like to be a blindsight patient's visual system helping them guess where a shape is while they report seeing nothing. Yet I'm perfectly willing to say there's something it's like to be a newborn, or an octopus, even though I can't imagine their experience from the inside and their brains are nothing like mine. And I'd be genuinely stunned—in a way I wouldn't be about the liver—to learn that a friend under general anaesthetic had been experiencing the whole operation.

Notice the sticky pair: bat echolocation is as foreign to me as my camera's autofocus, yet I feel the first has an inside and the second doesn't. So is there a coherent principle behind where I draw the line, or is it just a jumble? It feels like it might be something about whether I'm disposed to model the thing's states from an "inside" point of view, but that can't be the whole story since I can't do first-person imagining of bat echolocation. If I adopt your best principle, which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: an artificial system that tracks its own internal states and reports them as reliably as I report mine, but has no evolutionary history, no body, and no feelings of warmth or pain—does something come on for it, or not?
