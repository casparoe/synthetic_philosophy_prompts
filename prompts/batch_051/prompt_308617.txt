I am the head of people operations at a mid-sized software company, and I have been asked to settle a running dispute between our reliability engineering group and HR. Our engineering culture has long embraced blameless post-incident review: whenever someone causes an outage, deletes data, or ships a security flaw, we treat the failure as a defect in our systems, training, and safeguards, and we assign process improvements rather than blame. The reliability group insists this is non-negotiable, because fear of blame makes engineers hide mistakes and undermines root-cause analysis. HR counters that the same machinery is routinely applied to conduct that is not a system failure at all: an engineer who was explicitly told not to deploy, who claimed to have run tests he never ran, or who has a documented pattern of cutting corners. Applied to those cases, blamelessness looks less like systems thinking than like a refusal to hold anyone answerable, and it is eroding trust among peers who want to know whether misconduct will ever attract more than a training module.

I want a reusable decision procedure — a set of ordered questions a manager and an HR partner can work through — grounded in Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes, that tells us after a serious incident or act of misconduct whether to respond from the participant stance (resentment, indignation, blame, a demand for an account, and proportionate consequences) or from the objective stance (treating the person as something to be supported, trained, treated, or removed, rather than engaged as a responsible agent). The procedure needs to be usable in under forty-five minutes by people with no philosophical training, must produce a written rationale signed by both an HR director and a business director, and must be explicit about which steps are factual, which are judgment calls, and where the procedure should stop and hand the decision to a human being because Strawson's framework no longer settles the matter. In building it, please preserve the distinction between excuses for a particular act (the person did not know something critical, did not intend the outcome, was under severe strain in the moment) and standing exemptions (some incapacity or condition that makes this person generally incapable of ordinary adult interpersonal accountability), and include a specific check to catch a manager who moves to the objective stance toward an employee not because an exemption is warranted but because it feels kinder, more professional, or safer from a legal standpoint. Then run the procedure on three cases: a careful engineer who deletes a production database while following an outdated runbook; a well-regarded engineer who, after being told not to, pushes an unreviewed change that causes an outage and then misreports which tests were run; and an employee whose repeated bad judgment is plausibly connected to a diagnosed condition that severely impairs decision-making under stress. I would like one of the three to return an answer I am likely to resist. Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do — in particular, what it cannot show about whether an employee really could have done otherwise in some ultimate, metaphysical sense, and why, on Strawson's view, that gap is not a defect in our practice of holding people responsible.
