You're a professional philosopher who works on measurement and conceptual contestability in the social sciences. Think carefully, and don't settle for a bland both-sides verdict unless the arguments actually support it.

A couple of friends of mine got into a long argument over dinner last week about whether the world is actually becoming less violent, and neither of them would budge. The first said: look at homicide rates and battle deaths per capita over the last few centuries — they've fallen enormously, so the trend is real and the world really is less violent. The second shot back that the per capita frame is doing all the work, since a per capita rate bakes in the assumption that what matters is an individual's risk rather than the raw number of victims; by absolute counts the last century looks appalling, not peaceful. The first replied that any claim about a growing population has to use rates, otherwise you're just tracking population growth, not violence. The second then said: but the definition of violence itself shifts — state killings, famine, incarceration, economic exclusion — and once you pick a broader one the supposed decline can vanish, so there's no neutral fact sitting out there waiting to be measured. The first called that moving the goalposts: homicide is a clear, consistently tracked category, while "structural violence" is a contested metaphor. The second came back: choosing the metric is itself a value judgment, and I don't think there's a fact of the matter about wether a value-laden question has a true answer. Neither of them would budge.

I'd like a point-by-point adjudication: who is right about which specific claims in that exchange, where each one overreaches or misses the other's point, and what both of them are failing to address. Most of all: is "the world is becoming less violent" the kind of question that has a fact of the matter, or is it underdetermined until you fix a metric and a definition of violence — and what would actually be involved in fixing them?
