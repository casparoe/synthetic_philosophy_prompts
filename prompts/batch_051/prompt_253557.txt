I've been arguing with a friend about whether Lewis's account of convention can explain how social norms harden into something people treat as morally binding, and we keep going round in circles with the usual examples (driving on the left, shaking hands, meeting at the same cafe). I'd like a case built to order rather than another recycled one.

Work with this rough version of Lewis's account: a convention is a regularity R in a population such that (i) nearly everyone conforms to R in the recurring situation; (ii) nearly everyone expects nearly everyone else to conform; (iii) nearly everyone prefers to conform given the others conform, because R solves a coordination problem; (iv) there is an alternative regularity that would have worked just as well; and (v) all this is common knowledge. Also work with a simple internalisation view of norms: a regularity has become a norm when people treat it as a rule, feel entitled to expect conformity, and respond to violations with blame or punishment.

Construct a two-stage thought experiment in which a regularity starts out as a textbook Lewis-convention in stage one, and then in stage two the underlying coordination problem disappears, or the pay-offs change so that each individual would privately prefer not to conform even when the others conform. Nevertheless the regularity persists, because everyone still expects conformity and treats defection as blameworthy. The transition must happen without anyone designing it, and the mechanism should be concrete — for instance children internalising the regularity as a rule, or third-party punishment emerging. Your case should be one where, in stage two, Lewis's account no longer counts the regularity as a convention, but the internalisation view does count it as a norm.

Then do the checks: state the original convention and the alternative regularity that was available; explain why stage one satisfies each of (i)–(v); identify precisely which of those conditions fail in stage two and why; and say whether the stage-two regularity is better called a convention, a norm, or something else. Finish by saying which feature of the case does the real work in turning a convention into a norm, what would happen if you removed that feature (would the case collapse back into a plain convention?), and how a defender of Lewis's account would try to resist your description of stage two. Please write the answer in British English.
