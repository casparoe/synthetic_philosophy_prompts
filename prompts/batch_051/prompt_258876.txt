Before I read another account of what makes a translation faithful, I want the specification of the problem in front of me. Take the case of translating a text whose form is not decoration but part of what is being said: verse under a strict meter and rhyme scheme, an acrostic, a sestina, or a piece where the author's having worked inside an arbitrary constraint is itself part of the meaning. What must an adequate theory of translation deliver for such cases? I want the desiderata: the cases any account must get right, including the one where a literal rendering is formally inert and the one where a formally dazzling version quietly changes the claims the source makes; the distinctions it cannot collapse, for instance between preserving the original form, building an analogous form in the target language, and conveying what it is like to encounter the constraint; and the consideration behind each requirement. Tell me which requirements are non-negotiable for any account and which are merely the preferences of a particular school, whether any pair of requirements cannot be jointly satisfied (fidelity to propositional meaning and fidelity to the formal constraint being the obvious candidate), and which single requirement does the most work in excluding familiar slogans about translation. Skip the scorecard of existing theories; the criteria themselves are what I want. Please write in continuous prose, as in a letter or an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
