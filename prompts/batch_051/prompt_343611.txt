I've gone back and forth on a question that shows up constantly on rationalist-adjacent forums, and I want to turn it into a "which theory do you actually hold?" quiz for my small blog. The question: does "Sherlock Holmes" refer, and whats going on when we say "in the stories, Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street" given that Holmes is not a real person? (If the quiz ends up showing that these are two different questions you can mix-and-match answers to, that's a feature, not a bug.)

You are a professional philosopher of language who works on fiction, empty names, and truth-in-fiction, and who's good at explaining this stuff to smart amateurs. Please think carefully. Before writing any questions, start by brainstorming the underlying yes/no axes in the logical space; I care much more about clean diagnostic questions than about getting the label names right.

Deliverable: an 8-to-10-question quiz, each question a concrete case with two or three answer options in ordinary words (avoid jargon in the answer options), plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. Build the positions from logical space, not from a list of names: lay out the questions and the answer-combinations they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space, then note any unoccupied combinations and say briefly whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or probably just unexplored. If two positions can't be separated by any question in the quiz, flag that explicitly rather than pretending the instrument discriminates them, and say what extra question would pull them apart.

The scoring bins need to include at least these:
(a) direct reference plus pretense: the name contributes only its referent, "Holmes" has none, so outside the game of make-believe "Holmes is a detective" is false or truth-valueless, and "truth in fiction" is a pragmatic matter of what the story prescribes we pretend;
(b) descriptive-content view: "Holmes" functions as a package of descriptive conditions (detective, lives at 221B, friend of Watson, etc.) and doesn't need a real referent to be meaningful;
(c) abstract-object creationism: authors literally create abstract objects—Holmes is an actually existing thing, just not a concrete person;
(d) nonexistent-object realism (Meinongian-ish): some objects have no existence but still have properties and can be subjects of true predications, so "Holmes does not exist" is true of Holmes;
(e) possible-object realism: Holmes is a possible but non-actual person, so the name refers to a merely possible individual;
(f) if the questions support it, a contextualist/pluralist bin where different uses of "Holmes" do different jobs and there's no single semantic story.

Please use at least these cases, reworked into quiz questions (you can add others):
1. Telling a friend "Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street" vs. flatly saying "Holmes does not exist."
2. "Arthur Conan Doyle created Sherlock Holmes"—is this like "Doyle created a chair," or not?
3. The famous inconsistency about whether Watson was wounded in the shoulder or the leg: is that a contradiction inside the fiction, a sign of two slightly different characters, or not a real problem because story-truth is just "whatever the stories say"?
4. A crossover fanfic where Holmes meets a character from a completely different series: is it still the same Holmes?
5. A historical novel about Queen Victoria full of flatly false details: does "Victoria" in the novel refer to the real queen, a made-up counterpart, or something else?
6. An alternate-universe retelling where Holmes is a banker who never detects crime: is it still Holmes?
7. "If Holmes had existed, he would have been cleverer than most real detectives"—true, false, neutral, or not even assertable?
8. "There is no such person as Holmes, but there is such a fictional character as Holmes"—is the speaker contradicting themselves, just tracking two uses, or is the second half literally true?
9. A story that says straight out "Holmes was not a detective" (against the canon): is that a false statement in the fiction, a different fiction, or evidence that "in the fiction" doesn't respect a canonical world?
10. One more question of your own, aimed at whichever bins the first nine leave tangled together.

One meta-level request: please seperate what's more or less settled in the field from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. If, say, "Holmes fails to refer" is mostly common ground while "what does the explaining instead" is wide open, say so in the scoring key. The quiz should sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they think sounds good, so no question should just ask "do you think Holmes is an abstract object?"

Write in American English, and skip citations and reading lists—I want your own best reconstruction of the terrain and a usable quiz.
