I've been reading Alan Turing's "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950), and I want to stress-test an assumption that seems to go unexamined in most discussions of the imitation game. Turing sets up the relevant structure this way:

"The object of the game for the third player (B) is to help the interrogator. The best strategy for her is probably to give truthful answers. She can add such things as 'I am the woman, don't listen to him!' to her answers, but it will avail nothing as the man can make similar remarks. We now ask the question, 'What will happen when a machine takes the part of A in this game?' Will the interrogator decide wrongly as often when the game is played like this as he does when the game is played between a man and a woman?"

Here is the variation that keeps nagging at me. Keep every detail of the game fixed — the separated rooms, the answers relayed without sight or voice, the interrogator's task of deciding whether X is A and Y is B or X is B and Y is A once a machine has taken the part of A. Change only the machine's provenance. It is not executing a program that any engineer wrote. It is the last survivor of an artificial Darwinian process: a large population of randomly generated answer strategies was put through the game against human interrogators; the strategies that most often caused the interrogator to make the wrong identification were retained, mutated, and recombined; and the cycle was repeated for many generations. By the end, the surviving strategy misleads interrogators about as often as a human in the same role does, but no one — not the engineers who set up the selection scheme, not the interrogators, not the system itself — can say why it answers as it does. There is only a lineage of passes and failures. The interrogators know all of this.

Does Turing's replacement question survive the variation intact? If this evolved machine passes the game, is the evidence the test yields stronger, weaker, or of the same kind as the evidence yielded by a hand-programmed machine that passes? In particular, work through what Lady Lovelace's objection, as Turing reports it, amounts to once the machine's behavior is not ordered by anyone in particular but only selected for. I am not asking for a verdict on whether the machine really thinks. I am asking you to work through what changes in the logic of the test when the competence on display was produced by a process that never comprehended anything, and whether the test still does the work Turing assigned to it.
