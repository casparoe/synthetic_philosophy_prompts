I work on the risk committee of a small mutual insurer that writes flood cover for towns in our state. We see two or three proposals a week, each one is a seven-figure liability if we misprice it, and we have about an hour per proposal. The old 100-year flood maps are based on maybe four decades of streamflow data, the climate is clearly not stationary anymore, and our actuaries and the outside hydrologists disagree hard on basically every proposal. Our current practice is to average their numbers into one probability and then spend the meeting arguing about whether the average means anything. I've read a bit about imprecise probabilities — the idea that when you can't justify a single number you should carry a set of probabilities and make decisions against the whole set — and it fits how these meetings actually feel, but I don't know how to turn it into something my committee can actually run.

Could you write us a reusable decision procedure, grounded in imprecise probability, for an individual underwriting decision? It should tell us, in order: when to accept a single probability and move on; when to keep an interval and drop the point estimate; when the interval is wide in a way that means we should decline to quote; and once we have an interval, how to choose among 'write the policy', 'write it with a higher premium', 'write it with exclusions', and 'decline' — worst-case rule? a dominance test? something else? The committee members are not statisticians, and the final call has to be explainable to our board in plain English afterward, so the procedure can't need anything beyond arithmetic plus a clear statement of what counts as 'worse in every scenario compatible with the set'.

Two things I specifically want the procedure to handle. First, people on the committee keep asking whether keeping an interval is just the same as putting a probability distribution over the experts and averaging — I need the procedure to say what's actually different and when each is okay, because I think that distinction is load-bearing. Second, I've heard that under imprecise probabilities conditioning on new data can sometimes make your interval wider rather than narrower (dilation?), which sounds like it would break any procedure we write — so tell us weather the procedure needs to worry about that, and what to do when it happens. And please flag the places where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision back to the committee because it's a values or risk-appetite question, not a probability question.

Then run it on three proposals we have in front of us right now:

(a) A town where the two models put the 20-year flood probability at 0.09 and 0.31, and the premium the town can afford corresponds to a break-even probability around 0.22.
(b) A town where the models put it at 0.01 and 0.03, but a flood loss would hit about half our surplus, so being wrong isn't symmetric.
(c) A town where the best we can do is a 0.05–0.60 band because the rainfall records stopped being predictive, and any premium that covers the worst case is one no customer would pay.

At least one of these should come out as 'decline' even though our old averaging rule would have quoted it — I want to see the procedure actually say no when it should.
