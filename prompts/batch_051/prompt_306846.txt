So I've been in this theology-and-philosophy reading group for a couple of years now, and the one argument we cannot make any headway on is what actually makes idolatry bad. We all agree on the stock examples — the golden calf, treating money or a career or a romantic partner as the thing your whole life is organized around, celebrity worship, nationalism, that sort of thing — but we keep going in circles about which feature of these cases is doing the wrong-making work.

The first camp says it's fundamentally an epistemic mistake: you're attributing a kind of ultimacy or finality to something that doesn't have it, like treating a finite good as though it were the ultimate ground of your life. The second camp says no, people usually know perfectly well that the thing is finite, so the problem has to lie in the structure of the attachment itself — a disordered love, an allegiance that is ordered wrong — not in a false belief. The third camp thinks we've over-psychologized the whole category: for them idolatry is a public, practical concept about what a community gives its ultimate allegiance to, and talk of an 'idolatrous heart' with no outward practice is just metaphor.

I've started to suspect that the reason this never moves isn't just stubborness; it's that we're not actually having one debate. The first two camps disagree about whether valuing has a cognitive component that can be in error, while the third camp is answering a sociological question and the others are doing moral psychology. But maybe that diagnosis is too easy, and the real problem is that "ultimate value" is an ill-posed notion that we keep treating as if it picks out a single thing. What kind of disagreement is this, actually, and what would count as progress — a distinction, a restated question, or should we accept that the concept is essentially contested and stop expecting convergence?
