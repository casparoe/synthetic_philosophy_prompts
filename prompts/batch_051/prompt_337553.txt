So I'm working on a feature about whether autonomous systems can be kept from quietly colluding, and the pricing-bot case is the cleanest version I have. Take a big online marketplace where several sellers' repricing bots have slid into a pattern: nobody ever emails another seller to say 'let's raise prices,' but each bot has learned that if it inches its price up and the others follow, everyone makes more money, and if one undercuts, the others start a price war until it falls back into line. Antitrust people say there's no agreement, so their hands are tied. A regulator floated a technical fix. Every bot registers with the platform and commits to a 'jitter' module: each posted price has to equal the bot's chosen price plus a random draw from a fixed zero-mean distribution whose seed the platform holds, so the platform can audit the jitter later but rival bots can't strip it out. On top of that, on randomly chosen 'reset days' — about one in twenty, picked by a platform lottery — the bot has to ignore competitor data entirely and post a boring cost-plus-minimum-margin price. The theory is that jitter makes the pricing signals unreadable and reset days break the rhythm that keeps tacit collusion stable. I want to know how this actually plays out, not whether it sounds nice. What do the bots learn to do? What do the people running the bots do with the seed commitment or the reset calendar? Where does it get gamed first? And is the failure mode patchable, or does the collusion just migrate to dimensions nobody jittered — shipping speed, warranty terms, bundled add-ons, response times? Walk me through it.
