I am a retired atmospheric scientist who has lately been attending a philosophy discussion group at a nearby university, and I have been turning over a remark from a community meeting on climate policy. One of my neighbours dismissed proposals to encourage individual changes—fewer flights, less meat, lower thermostat settings—by saying that such appeals misunderstand the situation, because "no single raindrop feels responsible for the flood." He treated this as decisive, concluding that individual behaviour is not a meaningful part of climate action and that only systemic responses matter. I have since gathered that philosophers debate something called the inefficacy objection, but what I want to understand first is the slogan itself.

The sentence "no single raindrop feels responsible for the flood" seems to me to be doing several different jobs at once. It could be a trivial observation that raindrops do not have mental states; an empirical claim that one person's emissions make no measurable difference to the climate; a moral claim that when a harm would occur whether or not I act, I cannot be required to refrain; a claim about fairness, or about where political effort is best directed; or a claim that responsibility attaches only to the whole system and never to its parts. I would like to raise this carefully with my neighbour rather than simply argue back. Would you lay out the distinct precise claims the raindrop saying might be used to express, ordered from the relatively trivial to the more radical, and say which of them actually support his conclusion that individual behaviour is not a meaningful part of climate action? Where the slogan only appears to support that conclusion, I would especially like to know why the apparent inference fails.
