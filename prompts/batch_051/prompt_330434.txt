I cover the science of how experts form and revise judgments, and I keep returning to a tension between a principle and a case. The principle is that epistemic rationality respects no boundary between first-order and higher-order evidence. Suppose a reasoner has excellent direct evidence for a proposition and then receives credible evidence that reasoners of her type, in her present condition, systematically overrate exactly that kind of direct evidence. She ought, on this view, to lower her confidence; rationality requires adjusting to the total evidence at every level, and refusing to adjust is a form of epistemic akrasia, in which one believes a proposition while one's own assessment of the evidence no longer licenses that belief. The view has the virtues of simplicity, generality, and a single procedure for every kind of doubt, including doubt about oneself.

Yet the case will not leave me alone. A forensic accountant has reconstructed an embezzlement scheme from ledgers she has transcribed and checked entry by entry, and she can point to the precise entries that make the fraud, on her reading, the only live one. She is then shown a rigorous actuarial study reporting that accountants who have been awake as long as she has, working under comparable public pressure on a case of this profile, are markedly overconfident in detecting exactly this pattern. She has no independent reason to believe she is the exception. She rechecks the crucial entries, finds the same thing, and retains her original confidence, while fully accepting the actuarial finding as statistically sound. I cannot bring myself to call her irrational. Her higher-order evidence is real, but it is a fact about a class to which she belongs, not a fact about the entries before her, and lowering her confidence in those entries because of a generalization that does not touch them feels more like a failure of nerve than an exercise of rationality.

I want to know, not which side wins, but what it would cost to abandon either. Present the strongest case for the principle and the strongest case for the intuition the accountant preserves, and then give a negotiated verdict: under what conditions should the intuition be debunked and set aside, under what conditions should the principle be amended or abandoned, and is there a reading on which the two are compatible? Structure the answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, covering in turn: the strongest case for the principle; the strongest case for the intuition; what would justify debunking the intuition; what would justify amending or abandoning the principle; and a final negotiated verdict on compatibility or which side yields.
