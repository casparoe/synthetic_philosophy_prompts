I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and over the past year I have been researching a book about two mathematicians who spend their lives quarrelling over a single sentence. In the course of that research I assembled a small theory of mathematical objects, and I cannot now tell whether it is philosophy, a private metaphor, or simply a mistake. I would like it assessed candidly rather than merely charitably.

I have come to think of mathematical objects as characters in a play. A character is exhaustively constituted by the part it plays: what makes Hamlet Hamlet is the web of relations to the other figures, the lines assigned to him, and what the script permits the action to do through him. There is no further ingredient, no hidden Hamlet-stuff waiting in the margins. For the same reason, I think the number 2 just is the part it plays — successor of 1, predecessor of 3, first prime, the addend that doubles to 4 — and there is nothing further to ask about what 2 is made of. It follows that when a student asks whether 2 is an element of 4, I want to answer that the question has no determinate sense until one fixes a production. In the dominant modern staging, where numbers are constructed as von Neumann ordinals, 2 is an element of 4; in a Zermelo staging, it is not. So far, I gather, this is close to what is called mathematical structuralism.

Where I may have gone beyond it, or fallen off, is a stronger idea I have been calling the repertory principle. A character recurs across productions: the same Hamlet walks through the Folio and through a heavily cut modern staging, played by different actors, with scenes altered. I therefore believe cross-structural identity claims in mathematics should be adjudicated the way recurring-character claims are. We are entitled to call the 2 of the natural numbers and the 2 of the real numbers, or the ordinal 2, the same character only when there is a traceable lineage of role-preserving moves — embeddings, standard inclusions, and the like — and not merely because the same numeral appears in both places. Pressed to its end, the principle predicts that for some pairs of structures the question whether this is the same 2 is not deep but badly posed, just as it would be for Hamlet in the Folio and Hamlet in a parody. I would like a candid verdict: is the repertory principle anything more than structuralism in theatrical dress, and if so, does the dress buy anything, such as a workable test for deciding when cross-structural identity questions should be answered rather than dismissed? Where does the comparison actually break down, and what does that failure tell me about whether the underlying view of numbers is right?
