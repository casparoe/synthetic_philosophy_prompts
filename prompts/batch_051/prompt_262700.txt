I spent my career in the natural sciences and have been spending my retirement working through the decision-theoretic literature on risk, and one topic I keep returning to is the micromort — commonly understood as a one-in-a-million chance of death — as a unit for comparing everyday risks. The familiar argument I have in mind asks whether driving is "worth it" by weighing the expected loss of life from a trip, computed from a stable, population-level per-mile fatality rate, against whatever benefit the trip provides. The comparison only works if the risk side can be treated as a roughly fixed number read off a table, so that the decision is a single trade-off between two stable quantities.

Now suppose a modest but real technological development: the same trip's risk is no longer a fixed table value. Telematics in the car log hard braking, speed, and late-night driving; a wearable estimates fatigue, recent stress, and sleep; the software combines these with weather and traffic to produce a personal, continuously updated probability of death for this particular journey, a number that can change by an order of magnitude from one day to the next. The quantity that the classic argument compares against the benefit of the trip is now unstable, partly endogenous to how one drives, and partly under the control of whatever model produces the estimate.

Does the "is driving worth it?" argument survive this development in anything like its original form, need repair, or collapse into a different question altogether — for instance, a question about how to act under rapidly revised personal probabilities rather than about comparing stable activities? Please identify the specific premise or premises of the standard micromort comparison that the development bears on, and say whether the argument can be restored by substituting personalized micromorts for population averages, or whether something deeper has shifted. I am after the conceptual architecture, not a survey of empirical risk estimates. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
