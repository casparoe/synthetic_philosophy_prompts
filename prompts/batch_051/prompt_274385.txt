For a graduate decision-theory seminar, take the Ellsberg three-color problem—an urn with 90 balls, 30 red and 60 black or yellow in unknown proportions—and change its information structure rather than its payoffs. The decision maker is told only that at least f of the 60 non-red balls are black, where f is an integer between 0 and 60. Payoffs remain $100 on the indicated event and $0 otherwise, and the two choices remain the usual two: red versus black, and black-or-yellow versus red-or-yellow.

Work through the following in order.

First, derive, as a function of f, the choice predicted by maxmin expected utility in each pair, where the set of priors is every probability distribution consistent with the stated information. Identify the critical value f* at which the first pair changes, and state the exact comparison at f = f*, not only the strict cases, including whether the resulting pattern is representable by subjective expected utility.

Second, show that subjective expected utility cannot reproduce the classic Ellsberg reversal—red preferred to black while black-or-yellow is preferred to red-or-yellow—for any admissible subjective probability and any value of f. Then characterize, for each f, the range of subjective probabilities for black that yield the consistent choice patterns subjective expected utility does allow.

Third, state the general condition on where the interval of possible probabilities for black sits relative to the known probability of red that determines whether the reversal survives. Distinguish the three regimes—interval wholly below the red probability, interval straddling it, and interval wholly above it—and say which of these regimes can be reached by varying f, and what alternative constraint would produce the remaining regime.

Finally, give a brief interpretation: what does the location of f* reveal about whether the Ellsberg pattern tracks ambiguity as such, or tracks a known probability being compared with an interval that stretches across it?
