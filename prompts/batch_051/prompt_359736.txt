Question:
In Chapter VI of Principia Ethica, Moore uses his method of isolation to identify what is good in itself. Explain how this method works, how Moore applies it to personal affection and aesthetic enjoyment, and how his conclusions about organic unities bear on the question whether the value of friendship can be decomposed into the value of its parts.

Student's answer:
Moore's method of isolation asks us to imagine a thing existing entirely by itself and then ask whether we would still judge its existence to be good. This is supposed to screen out things that are good only as means, such as money or power, because those would lose their value if they had no effects. Applying the test, Moore finds that the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects pass the test because they are pleasant states of consciousness. His view is therefore a refined hedonism: he ranks qualitative differences among pleasures rather than treating all pleasures as equal, but the underlying good is still pleasure itself. For friendship, what is intrinsically good is the pleasure it produces; the friend's actual character matters only as a causal source of that pleasure. Moore goes on to say these goods are organic unities, which means their value is greater than the sum of their parts. That is why the whole cannot be reduced to its components, though he does not deny that we could in principle measure it by adding the values of the parts plus some extra amount. In the end, he is right that the whole matters for value, but his talk of organic unities does not really prevent decomposition; it just adds a bonus term.
