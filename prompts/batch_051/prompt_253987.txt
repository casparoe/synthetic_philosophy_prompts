I'm grading an upper-division undergraduate bioethics exam that included the following passage from John Stuart Mill's On Liberty, Chapter V:

"The maxims are, first, that the individual is not accountable to society for his actions, in so far as these concern the interests of no person but himself. Advice, instruction, persuasion, and avoidance by other people if thought necessary by them for their own good, are the only measures by which society can justifiably express its dislike or disapprobation of his conduct. Secondly, that for such actions as are prejudicial to the interests of others, the individual is accountable, and may be subjected either to social or to legal punishment, if society is of opinion that the one or the other is requisite for its protection."

The exam question was:

"Using Mill's maxims from this passage, assess whether an advance directive signed by a competent patient should be legally enforceable when a later self with dementia contemporaneously resists treatment. Does the authority of the earlier self outweigh the expressed wishes of the later self, and on what grounds?"

This is the student's answer:

"Mill's two maxims set up a strong presumption against coercing someone for her own good. A treatment refusal is essentially self-regarding, so a competent patient's advance directive refusing future interventions falls under the first maxim and cannot be overridden merely because the later self has changed her mind. The later self's contrary request is likewise self-regarding, but the first maxim only says society may not punish or coerce self-regarding conduct; it does not require society to give equal weight to every later change of mind. Enforcing the directive is not paternalism toward the later self because it is not justified by what is now good for her, but by the fact that the earlier self's exercise of autonomy would otherwise be annulled. The second maxim is not triggered because no one other than the signer is harmed. The only relevant exception comes from Mill's discussion of voluntary slavery later in the chapter: a directive that surrendered all future agency would be invalid, but a limited treatment refusal does not do that. Therefore the earlier directive remains authoritative, except where the later self retains enough agency that overriding it would be required to avoid a new and distinct harm to another person."

The comments should identify what the student handles correctly, where the reasoning is underdeveloped, and any subtle errors a quick read might miss. I don't want a vague overall impression; I need the specific justifications for the grade.
