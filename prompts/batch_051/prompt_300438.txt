I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar and we read about pain asymbolia, and my friend (she's doing a neuroscience masters) made a claim I can't stop poking at. She says the condition basically proves that the badness of pain isn't part of the pain itself. Her line is: these patients can locate a pinprick and give it a number on a 0–10 scale, but they don't pull away, don't wince, and when asked they say it doesn't actually bother them — some even smile and tell the researcher to keep going. So she splits pain into a sensory-discriminative part and an affective/evaluative part, and says the second is what makes pain bad, and pain asymbolia shows they can come apart.

I'm not convinced. It feels like a handful of odd reports is being asked to settle something conceptual. "Doesn't bother me" might be a changed way of using words, or the lesion might have changed the pain itself instead of stripping off some extra layer, or maybe their 'pain' just isn't the same kind of thing as my burned hand. So here's the task I want you to take seriously: design the strongest possible test of the claim that the badness of pain is separable from the pain sensation itself, rather than intrinsic to it. What would the test look like — behavioral, neural, pharmacological, whatever — and what result would count for the claim and what result against it? And then be honest about what part of the claim even the cleanest positive result would leave untouched.
