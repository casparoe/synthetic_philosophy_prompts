I'm giving a presentation to the clinical ethics committee of a big teaching hospital next month, arguing that we should support a pilot for a regulated, government-run market in kidneys. The design is meant to avoid the standard objections: the government is the only buyer, the price is fixed, donors get most of the payment as a health-insurance credit and a pension top-up rather than cash, and there are no face-to-face donor-recipient meetings or directed donations. I need a Q&A prep sheet. The committee has five people: a transplant surgeon who is quietly furious because she watches patients die on the waiting list; a Catholic bioethicist who keeps circling back to the idea that an organ is a gift and that pricing it, even indirectly, corrupts the relationship; a health economist who thinks the fixed price is too low to clear the shortage but also thinks the pilot's framing papers over a labour-market problem; a former kidney recipient who got her organ from her brother and is uneasy about strangers being paid for theirs; and a palliative care nurse who thinks the whole proposal is a distraction from getting more people to register as ordinary dead donors. My argument has three planks: (1) the current waiting list is killing people and the shortage is getting worse; (2) a state monopsony with deferred, non-cash compensation avoids the most serious exploitation and corruption worries; (3) keeping the donations anonymous and the compensation non-cash turns it into a civic, publicly administered practice rather than a private sale. Write me the objections in the order they will actually come up, phrased the way each of those five people would phrase them, with a note on which have genuine force, what I should concede up front, where my reply is softest, and the one question I should be praying nobody asks.
