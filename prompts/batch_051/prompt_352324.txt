I am a PhD student in philosophy of mathematics, and I run a weekly 75-minute undergraduate seminar on infinity for roughly fourteen students who have each taken at least one course in logic or real analysis. I want a structured classroom activity through which the students feel the force of Thomson's lamp before hearing its name. My idea is that the class acts as an inspection board that must certify, in writing, whether a specified lamp is on or off at the end of a one-minute procedure in which its switch is toggled at the halfway point of the remaining interval, then at the halfway point of what remains, and so on. The activity should let them approximate the procedure in the room with a real lamp, a stopwatch, and assigned roles, and the rules should be arranged so that as they push the approximation closer to the advertised completion time their records become incoherent in a way that is not merely a failure of time or attention. Please give me a runnable design, including roles or rules, a timeline, the debrief, and an honest note on which feature of the mathematical case the physical activity cannot reproduce (for instance, the difference between an actually executed finite sequence and the mathematically specified infinite sequence). Say what will predictably go wrong when I run it and how I should handle that, and make sure the participants come away seeing that the procedure fixes every finite stage while leaving the final state not merely unknown but undetermined by the description.
