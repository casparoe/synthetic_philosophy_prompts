Three months ago I moved into the strategy and policy group at a frontier AI lab, and the question of whether our competitive behavior is a rational response to structural incentives or a costly failure of coordination has become my daily problem. I am about to present a working paper to the lab's mixed research seminar and need a preparation sheet for the question period.

The thesis I will defend is that Fearon's three rationalist explanations for war — private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and issue indivisibility — survive transplantation to AI development rivalries, provided the transplant is treated as an analogy rather than as a direct test of a bargaining model. In compressed form, my argument is this. First, the private information that matters is not mainly about resolve or military strength but about the rate and safety properties of a rival's technical progress, and developers have structural incentives to conceal capability jumps and safety-relevant incidents; unlike Fearon's crisis bargaining, there is no settlement moment, so private information leaks out mainly through demonstrations, deployment, or defection. Second, the commitment problem appears as the inability of a leading developer to credibly promise either to slow down or to share the returns from a decisive lead, and any restraint agreement becomes harder to enforce as the perceived prize approaches. Third, the indivisible issue is not the technical capability, which is continuous, but the social and regulatory position attached to being first: the power to set de facto standards, define the governance agenda, or lock in an architecture that others must build around. I will conclude that the three mechanisms are not alternative explanations, as the war literature sometimes treats them, but mutually reinforcing in the AI case — which is why the race cannot be dissolved by informing the actors better.

The audience is not single-disciplinary. It includes two formal international relations theorists who work on crises and bargaining and will resist any use of Fearon outside a crisis-bargaining setup; two AI governance researchers, one who thinks the phrase 'AI race' is an overstretched metaphor and one who doubts that state-competition models fit the behavior of labs; an economist of innovation, who will hear 'indivisibility' as what he would call ordinary winner-take-most competition; and a senior safety researcher who used to work inside a lab and believes my rational-actor assumptions are doing more work than the framework can bear.

Prepare me for the question period. Give the objections in roughly the order they are likely to arise, phrased as these particular people would phrase them, not softened versions. For each objection, tell me what to concede and what to hold, and identify where my reply is weakest so I do not overclaim. Mark the two or three objections that are both likely and damaging. Then identify the single weakest link in my overall argument — the claim I should have rehearsed a defense of rather than hope they miss. And end with the one objection I should be praying nobody asks, with the best fallback line I can give if it comes.
