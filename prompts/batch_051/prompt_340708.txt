I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and in three weeks I am leading a forty-five-minute methods session for my newsroom's early-career science writers. The topic is mind-talk in science coverage: how to avoid writing that an algorithm genuinely knows something in one paragraph and that it merely predicts in the next, or that the brain weighs options the way a person does. I have settled on the one idea I want the room to leave with: Ryle's concept of a category mistake, with the ghost-in-the-machine picture as the historical target. The practical aim is not philosophical culture; it is that a reporter, by the end, can see why a question like where in the brain is the knowledge is badly formed before they answer it, and can distinguish that from a compressed empirical claim that is merely loose wording.

Please do not teach me the idea. Plan how I should teach it to non-philosophers who write about science for a living and are quick with methodology but suspicious of philosophy they cannot use by deadline. The plan should give me: the order in which the components must come and what depends on what; the single carrying example or case to build the whole session around, and what that example must have and must lack — I suspect Ryle's university visitor, used alone, lets them file the whole matter under definitions, so tell me if there is a stronger one and why; the misconceptions this particular audience will predictably bring or form — my guesses are that this is just a verbal dispute, that Ryle was a behaviorist denying inner life, and that modern neuroscience has simply shown that the brain does decide — and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than argued with at length; what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and say so; and one final exercise or question that would show me whether they can catch a category mistake in unfamiliar examples from neuroscience and AI coverage, including a near-miss that is not a category mistake, rather than just repeat my illustrations back.
