I help run a weekend workshop on AI safety for roughly twenty participants, mostly undergraduates and early-career software engineers, and I have been given a two-hour block on the problem of scalable oversight. I want the group to come away understanding, in their bones rather than as a slogan, why it is so hard to judge claims you cannot verify yourself — and why a structured debate between two well-informed adversaries might, perhaps, let a weaker judge extract the truth that neither expert would reliably hand over on their own. The problem with our previous attempts is that they have either turned into an ordinary classroom debate or into a lecture. So the constraint I want to impose is strict: no framing talk up front, no mention of AI, oversight, or debate as a method until the debrief. The idea should show up first in what participants do and in the decisions they find themselves forced to make.

Please design a runnable classroom game for the two-hour slot. The game should assign participants rotating roles — at minimum a judge, a truth-teller, a deceiver, and spectators — and use a scoring scheme that needs no real money. The judge must be prevented from inspecting the ground truth directly, while the two 'experts' have seen it. The truth-teller scores only when the judge accepts the true answer; the deceiver scores only when the judge accepts a false one; and the judge scores only by being right. Crucially, neither expert should be constrained to tell the truth — the deceiver may lie freely, and the truth-teller may say whatever helps the true answer win — so that the judge cannot simply trust the honest-sounding party and must instead extract information through questioning, cross-examination, or requests for evidence. If possible, build in a moment early on where each participant experiences being the judge once and feels the pull of relying on a single, plausible-sounding source; then a later stage where two sources can be played against each other; and let the difference in the judge's position between those stages be the core of the felt idea. Please make the facts of each round rich enough that cross-examination is not trivial (a single hidden token is too thin), but simple enough that a judge who asks good questions can realistically win.

Structure the response in the following sections, in this order and with roughly these lengths:

1. Overview (~200 words): the game in one paragraph, what participants should come away seeing, and why it is suited to twenty people and two hours.
2. Materials and set-up (~150 words): what to prepare in advance, room layout, and how to split and rotate the group.
3. Roles, rules, and scoring (~350 words): exact instructions for the judge, truth-teller, deceiver, and spectators, the pay-off scheme, the judge's constraints, and how rounds end.
4. Predicted pressure points (~200 words): where in the game the intended feeling should arise, and what participants should be doing or saying when it does.
5. Debrief (~300 words): a script or sequence of prompts that maps specific moments in the game onto the parts of the scalable oversight and debate idea, and that names the idea only here.
6. What will go wrong and how to handle it (~200 words): the most likely failure modes on a first run and concrete fixes for each.
7. What the activity cannot reproduce (~150 words): be frank about which features of the real scalable oversight problem the game distorts or omits, and what the facilitator will have to say explicitly afterwards to compensate.
