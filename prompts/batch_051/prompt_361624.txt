As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I recently sat in on a workshop on autonomous systems. After a presentation about a robot that had learned to rearrange objects in a kitchen, someone asked why the system never seemed to represent the obvious fact that moving a mug does not change the position of the refrigerator. That question set off an exchange between two participants—Ben Okoye, a machine-learning researcher in robotics, and Anya Riedel, a philosopher of cognitive science—which I reconstruct from my notes below.

For background: the frame problem, in the form they were discussing, is the problem of how an agent determines which facts about its situation change, which persist, and which are irrelevant when it acts, without explicitly enumerating the indefinitely many non-effects of each possible action.

Okoye said, "The reason it doesn't reason about any of that is the reason I think the frame problem is no longer the obstacle it used to be. The old worry was that a logic-based agent would need frame axioms—statements that the refrigerator stays put, the walls stay where they are, and so on—for every action, and would have to labor through those inferences at every step. A learned system doesn't need any of that. It learns which aspects of a scene predict the next state and which aspects are irrelevant, and it simply doesn't represent the rest. The frame problem, in that original form, was an artifact of a brittle way of building planners."

Riedel replied, "That solves only the bookkeeping version. The deeper frame problem was never about avoiding the explicit deduction that the refrigerator stays put. It is the problem of determining, in advance of experience, which features might turn out to matter and which can safely be ignored. A learned model manages to ignore the refrigerator because its training signal or its architecture has already marked features of that kind as irrelevant. Step a little outside the training distribution—a refrigerator on casters, a fire behind the counter, a cord underfoot—and the system has no principle for noticing that a formerly inert feature is now relevant. That, not the deduction of non-effects, is where the problem was always meant to bite."

Okoye said, "But a domain-general principle of relevance is not something humans possess either. People are notoriously bad in novel situations; novelty is precisely when accidents happen. What humans have is accumulated experience, strong priors, and the ability to notice their own uncertainty and slow down. Those are things we can approximate in machines: out-of-distribution detectors, ensembles that report confidence, uncertainty thresholds that trigger asking a human. If the frame problem is defined as requiring a guaranteed relevance oracle, then neither humans nor machines can solve it. But an engineering problem does not have to be solved in that sense; it has to be managed."

Riedel said, "The handoff to the human is the tell. Either the human is supplying the relevance judgment, in which case the system has not solved the problem but delegated it, or the designers have pre-specified the conditions under which the system flags uncertainty, which just relocates the frame problem into the designer's choices. And more data cannot close the gap, because the space of features that might become relevant in a new situation is unbounded. No finite training distribution selects the right relevance scheme for distributions it does not cover."

Okoye said, "You are holding the system to a standard of performance under unbounded novelty that no safety-critical engineering has ever met. Bridges fail in unprecedented floods; that does not mean structural engineering failed to solve a conceptual problem. What one wants from an agent is not a proof that it will never overlook a relevant fact, but behavior that degrades gracefully: perform well on the intended distribution, flag novelty, and stop or ask for help when uncertain. Modern systems increasingly do exactly that. If the frame problem is defined so that nothing short of omniscience counts as a solution, the definition is doing the work."

Riedel said, "I am not demanding omniscience. I am making a narrower claim: a system that can flag novelty is flagging novelty relative to a fixed space of features that its designers or its training process deemed representable in the first place. The moment the dangerous property of a situation is outside that space—the downed power line that should be treated as an electrical hazard, not merely as an obstacle to avoid—the system's relevance assignment is not just fallible; it is blind in a way that matters. That blindness is not an implementation detail. It is the frame problem in its contemporary form."

Okoye said, "Then we are no longer talking about the frame problem as it was originally posed. We are talking about open-world recognition and out-of-distribution robustness, which are hard but active research areas with partial, improving solutions of exactly the engineering kind you seem to dismiss. My claim was narrower: the original problem of enumerating the non-effects of actions no longer blocks progress, because systems can learn transition models instead. If you want to call the remaining difficulty 'the frame problem,' you can, but you have not shown that it is an unsolved philosophical obstacle rather than a hard problem that is being chipped away at."

Riedel said, "I will grant your narrow point. Explicit frame axioms are no longer necessary, and the deductive formulation is mostly bypassed in practice. But the broader question—how an agent's relevance assignment is selected and revised when the world shifts—was always part of the philosophical discussion of the frame problem, not a new label. On that broader reading, you have not dissolved the problem; you have instantiated one particular relevance assignment and delegated every revision of it to a human or a designer. That does not refute my point. That is my point."

Okoye said, "But notice what follows from your concession. If explicit frame axioms are bypassed, then the obstacle that once defined the field's debate is gone. What remains—transfer to novel circumstances, detecting that something unmodeled matters—is the ordinary problem of generalization under uncertainty, which every serious engineer already treats as central. That is precisely my conclusion: the frame problem as a distinct philosophical obstacle has dissolved, and what is left is a hard but ordinary engineering problem."

I left the session uncertain how much of this was a disagreement about the current state of autonomous systems and how much it was a disagreement about what the frame problem is in the first place. I would like a step-by-step adjudication: what specific claims does each make, who is right about which claims, and where are they talking past each other? In particular, does the exchange equivocate between a narrow sense of the frame problem—the need to enumerate or deduce the non-effects of actions—and a broad sense—the problem of relevance and novelty—and do either side's conclusions survive once the two senses are distinguished? What, if anything, are both of them missing?
