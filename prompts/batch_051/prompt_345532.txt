I'm a novelist working on a character who's convinced nothing matters, and I've been reading around nihilism to get her voice right. A book for a general audience summarized the family of views like this: moral nihilism is the claim that there are no moral facts, so no action is ever really right or wrong; existential nihilism is the further claim that life has no meaning or purpose, so no project is ever really worth doing; and mereological nihilism is the weirder sibling that says ordinary objects—tables, persons, your own self—aren't really there, there are only simple particles arranged table-wise or person-wise, so the self you keep worrying about is an illusion. The book then says the three together add up to the conclusion that nothing matters, that no choice is better than any other, and that a nihilist can't even rightly condemn anything, because there are no facts of the matter, just arrangements of particles.

I suspect that's not quite fair but I don't know enough to say where it goes wrong, and the book is by a professional. Can you go through it and tell me wich parts a careful philosopher would accept, which are common simplifications, and which are just run-together ideas? In particular: are moral and existential nihilism really the same claim at different levels, does mereological nihilism actually pull any weight in the argument about meaning, and does anything in the accurate parts force the conclusion that a nihilist has to live as if every choice is as good as another? If the summary is unfair mostly in how it's bundled, what should a fairer one-sentence-per-view summary say? Please answer in American English.
