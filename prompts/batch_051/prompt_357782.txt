I have been going back and forth on the statue and the clay, and I want a considered ruling rather than a survey. On one side, the modal argument for distinctness feels close to a proof. The clay out of which a statue is made can survive being squashed into a ball; the statue cannot survive that; so the clay has a property that the statue lacks; so, by Leibniz's Law, the clay is not identical with the statue. Accepting the argument commits me to a clean and fully general principle: whenever a portion of matter of one kind comes to be shaped into another kind of thing, a second material object pops into existence, coincident with the first. That is a real theoretical gain—no arbitrary lines, no special cases. On the other side, I cannot make myself believe, outside the study, that there are two material objects on the table where the statue sits. Perception presents one continuous body; if I reach out I expect to touch one thing; ordinary counting and practical reasoning never consult a boundary between statue and clay. The theory says two; the intuition says one; I want to know which side should give, and what the surrender costs in each direction.

If the intuition wins, spell out exactly what the modal argument must abandon. Does rejecting distinctness force me to say that the statue can survive squashing after all, or that persisting through squashing is not the kind of property Leibniz's Law ranges over, or that "the statue" and "the clay" pick out the same thing under different descriptions but not under different sortals? Be specific about which premise breaks.

If the theory wins, tell me how I should regard the one-object intuition. Is it a harmless deliverance of ordinary language, a perceptual artifact I should debunk and drop, or a tracking of something real that the two-object ontology has failed to capture? Can I debunk it here without thereby losing my trust in every mid-sized object I see?
