For the past year I have been covering, as a journalist who writes about science and ideas for a general-interest audience, the effort to put ethical reasoning into machine systems. In three weeks I am giving a lunch seminar at a university artificial-intelligence institute, where I will defend a position I have come to through that reporting. The position is this: a machine that is genuinely doing ethical reasoning, rather than merely conforming to a behavioral specification, should reason through a retrievable and contestable collection of paradigm cases and analogies — a modernized casuistry — and should be required to surface those cases in its outputs. My claim is not that top-down moral principles are beyond machines in principle, nor that end-to-end learning from human preference data is necessarily unsafe, but that the case-based approach is the only one of the three that gives auditors, regulators, and the people affected by a decision the materials they would need to contest it.

My intended moves in the talk are these. Top-down principle application fails in implementation: maximally general rules stay vague exactly where they are safe and become arbitrary precisely where precision is forced on them. End-to-end preference learning produces behavior without reasons; when such a system fails, no one can localize the failure to a stated consideration, and the available remedies are more data and retraining rather than an objection to which the system or its operator must answer. A case-based architecture, by contrast, makes every judgment an auditable act of analogy: which past cases are treated as similar, and which differences are taken to matter, must be visible and revisable. I will concede that this approach reintroduces human judgment in selecting, annotating, and weighting the case library, and I will argue that this is a strength rather than a flaw, because it places the judgment somewhere it can be inspected, named, and disputed.

The audience will be precise. Most of the room will be machine-learning researchers and engineers who work on safety, alignment, and reinforcement learning from human feedback. They will judge the proposal by whether it can be specified, trained, evaluated, and scaled, and they will be wary of anything that sounds like a hand-coded wrapper around a black box that quietly reimports unaccountable human discretion. A few applied moral philosophers attached to the institute will also be present; they will press on whether casuistry without an underlying moral theory is anything more than pattern-matching with a historical label. One former clinician who now works on the regulation of medical algorithms will care about fast decisions, legal liability, and how the system behaves in a genuinely novel case.

I would like a question-period rehearsal, not a general defense of the essay. Identify the eight or so objections this particular room is most likely to produce, order them by some combination of likelihood and damage, phrase each one the way a member of this audience would actually put it, and give for each a reply I could deliver in two minutes, with a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Indicate where I should concede and where I should hold. End with the question I most hope no one in the room thinks to ask.
