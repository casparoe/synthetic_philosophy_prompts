I have been trying to pin down what I actually think morality runs on, so I wrote out how I respond to concrete situations before considering any general rule. Here are my honest verdicts. Lying to my grandmother, who has dementia and asks every few minutes where her sister is, by saying that her sister, who died years ago, is "just out shopping": fine. Telling a coworker her presentation will go well when I thought it was flat and she is about to walk on stage: fine. Telling a friend after his divorce that I always thought his ex was wrong for him, when in fact I liked her and thought he was the problem: not fine, even though it would comfort him. Writing on a loan application that I have no other debts while I still owe my brother money: not fine. A nurse telling a frightened patient "this won't hurt" just before a procedure that will hurt, to keep him still: fine, but it leaves me uneasy. A seller telling buyers the roof was replaced last year, which is true, while failing to mention the cracked foundation: not fine, even though the sentence itself is true.

I get the same uncomfortable pattern with promises and harm. Breaking a lunch date because my train was stuck for two hours: fine. Breaking the same date because I stayed up late and woke up exhausted: not fine, though I might do it anyway. Breaking a promise to help a friend move because my child woke up with a high fever: fine. Slapping someone in the face to stop them from slapping a child: fine. A surgeon cutting into an unconscious patient without consent to remove a tumor that will otherwise kill her within days: fine. The same surgeon making a cosmetic incision without consent: clearly not fine.

What unsettles me is that the same sort of fact—causing pain, deceiving someone, breaking one's word—seems to matter one way in one situation and another way, or not at all, in the next, and I cannot find a simple rule that fits without a stack of exceptions. I sometimes want to say the mistake is to demand a general principle in the first place: that moral judgment is just a skill for seeing each case as what it is. But that also sounds like giving up on explanation. So here is what I am asking. If there is a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures all or almost all of these verdicts, state it as precisely as you can, and for each clause tell me which of my cases pulls you toward it. Then tell me which of my verdicts the principle forces me to abandon, and what it says about the case I keep oscillating on: a teacher telling a parent that her son has a real gift for mathematics, to keep him in a tutoring program, when the boy is actually only somewhat above average. Finally, tell me whether the view that no such principle exists—that each case is judged on its own and the same feature can count for, against, or not at all depending on context—is a serious position rather than mere sloppiness. Please answer in American English.
