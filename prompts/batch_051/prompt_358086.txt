I have been unable to decide whether a parallel I keep noticing between two fields is a real structural identity or just a metaphor doing all the work, and I would like you to adjudicate it carefully.

On one side is the problem paleontologists face with preservation bias. The fossil record is a nonrandom sample of past life: an organism must be buried in conditions that allow fossilization, have parts that survive decay, lie in rock that survives erosion, and then be found and collected. The result is a systematic bias toward hard-shelled, shallow-marine creatures and against soft-bodied or upland ones. Researchers know this and must distinguish an absence in the record from an absence in the world.

On the other side is the problem that interests me in the study of archives and power. A person or event must leave a trace in a form an institution can keep, the trace must actually be kept, it must survive loss or destruction, and it must eventually be selected, catalogued, and made accessible. The result is a systematic bias toward the literate, the propertied, and the institutionally powerful, and a silence around people who left no record or whose records were not thought worth keeping.

The parallel I want tested is that these are, structurally, the same problem: inferring the shape of an underlying population from a sample that has passed through multiple correlated filters of production, preservation, selection, and discovery. The strong version of the claim is that the stages match up one-to-one and that methods for correcting bias in one field should have informative counterparts in the other. The weak version is just that both can be described with the same vocabulary of bias and silence.

The main reason I hesitate is agency. A trilobite does not choose to be preserved, but people and institutions do choose, or are pressured, to record some things, omit others, destroy files, and redact. I cannot tell whether that is a deep disanalogy in the structure of inference, or simply one more filter that differs in its cause but creates the same problem for whoever comes later.

Please structure your answer in four sections. In "The mapping" (~150 words), state the analogy precisely, stage by stage, and say what would have to be true for the strong version to hold. In "Where it holds" (~200 words), set out the respects in which the two inference problems genuinely share the same structure. In "Where it breaks" (~250 words), set out the respects in which they come apart, with particular attention to agency, intention, and whether silence means the same thing in both fields. In "Verdict" (~100 words), say whether this is a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion; name one concrete thing the parallel would predict that you believe is false; and say what the parallel is and is not good for. In the verdict, please also state how confident you are in the overall assessment and single out the specific point you are least sure about.
