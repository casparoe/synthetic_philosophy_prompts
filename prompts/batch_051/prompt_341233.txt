I spent most of my working life in the natural sciences, where computers were instruments for simulation, approximation, and data analysis; only in retirement have I begun reading the philosophy of mathematics. Before I go any further into that literature, I want the specification rather than the survey. Please set out the desiderata for an adequate theory of mathematical proof—adequate, that is, for a practising scientist who wants to know when a computer-assisted result may be treated as established—in light of the fact that some accepted proofs are too long for any single human being to survey.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that forces it. Your list should in particular address, though I welcome additions:

- the distinction, if it survives scrutiny, between a proof and a computation, and between a proof and an experimental or numerical confirmation;
- the distinction between the correctness of a derivation and its surveyability, and between a theorem's being true and our being warranted in asserting it;
- the distinction between checking each step of a derivation and grasping the proof as a whole, and whether either is necessary for mathematical knowledge;
- the computer-assisted proof of the four-colour theorem, the proof of the Kepler conjecture in sphere packing and its later formal verification, and brute-force finite case checks generally;
- the fallibility of hardware and software, and whether it should be treated differently from the fallibility of human checking;
- the fact that a machine-checked formalization certifies a theorem only relative to the correctness of the proof checker's kernel and the faithfulness of the encoded statement;
- the difference between rerunning the same program and independently reimplementing the algorithm, and what each can and cannot certify;
- the epistemic role of referees, journal acceptance, and trust in computational infrastructure: are these merely sociological, or do they carry epistemic weight?
- whether an adequate theory must preserve the idea that mathematics yields an a priori kind of certainty, or may countenance results known in a quasi-empirical way.

Then mark each desideratum as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and flag any subsets that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied—for instance, the demand that a proof be surveyable by one human while also serving to certify a theorem whose shortest known proof runs to tens of thousands of cases, or the demand that mathematical knowledge remain strictly a priori while also counting as known a result whose only route passes through fallible computation. I am less interested in a scorecard of existing positions than in the criteria themselves, and most interested in the conflicts among them.
