I am a senior conservation officer at a land trust, and I review proposals to alter habitat: wetland restorations, invasive-shrub removals, forest thinning, controlled burns, and letting old fields succeed to woodland. Up to now I have scored these projects with ecological criteria—species richness, native cover, water quality, carbon storage—but I have come to doubt that those criteria track whether the animals on the site have lives worth living. I want to replace my checklist with one built on the principle that habitat interventions should be judged by their net expected effect on welfare across all sentient animals affected, not by species richness or ecosystem "integrity" as proxies. I know that principle is contested; the procedure should expose where the contest changes the recommendation, not hide it.

The decisions come up fifteen to thirty times a year, range from half an acre to several hundred acres, lock in management for decades, and each requires a written justification to the board. My field staff are trained ecologists, not philosophers or welfare scientists. Three complications are central. First, the animals likely to dominate the accounting are r-selected—high fecundity, high juvenile mortality, short lives: insects, small rodents, amphibians—so the welfare total may be driven by enormous numbers of brief, largely painful lives rather than by the deer and birds my staff actually notice. Second, we almost never have direct measures of welfare, so the procedure must run on proxies such as abundance, reproductive strategy, predation load, body size, and whether a population is thriving or merely persisting. Third, habitat interventions change how many future animals come into existence, not just the average quality of a fixed set of lives, so the procedure must say whether adding a life of a given quality counts in favor of, against, or neutrally toward the decision, and how much the verdict depends on that choice. There is also live disagreement on my board about whether invertebrate suffering is real enough to count; the procedure should show how far the final answer depends on that and what to do when it cannot be settled.

Give me a reusable procedure, written as a checklist or an ordered sequence of tests that a field ecologist could follow. For each step, say what evidence it requires, what to do when that evidence is missing, and whether it is a mechanical test or a judgment call dressed up as one. Then apply the procedure to three cases: one where it approves a conventional restoration, one where it blocks a project that scores well on my present ecological criteria, and one where it returns no determinate answer. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, where it is most likely to misfire even when followed carefully, and which steps a person who rejected the underlying principle could most reasonably refuse to take.
