I've been going back and forth on updateless decision theory, and I've talked myself into an argument that seems to prove it collapses into Pascal's mugging, but I can't find the step that's doing the work. The anchor is the standard counterfactual mugging. A supernaturally reliable predictor tells you: I flipped a fair coin. If it had come up heads, I'd have given you $10,000, but only if you were the sort of person who would hand over $100 if it came up tails. It came up tails, so now I'm asking for the $100. You didn't get the ten grand; whether you did was settled when the coin landed. Causal decision theory says don't pay, since the $100 can't cause the heads payoff. But the policy of paying is worth $4,950 in expectation across both branches (half the time you get $10,000, half the time you lose $100), while not paying is worth $0. So it seems you should pay.

Now here's the slide that bothers me:

(1) In the counterfactual mugging, you should pay the $100.
(2) Whatever makes paying rational there can't be the causal effect of the $100, since by the time your asked the coin is tails and the payoff branch is no longer live. It must be that you're choosing a decision procedure, and you prefer the procedure that does better across branches, including the branch you now know you aren't in.
(3) That principle is perfectly general: if some action is what your procedure outputs, and the procedure has higher expected value than the alternatives across all the situations where it runs — including ones you've updated away from — you should output that action.
(4) But now consider a stranger on the street who says: "I flipped a coin. If it had come up heads I'd have given you $X, and I'd have given it only if you'd hand me $100 now. It came up tails, so pay up." If your credence p that the stranger's story is true times X/2 exceeds $100, the same procedure-level calculation says pay.
(5) But for any p, however small, there's some X large enough that pX/2 exceeds $100. So the reasoning seems to say you should pay such muggers even for arbitrarily tiny p and correspondingly huge X. That's just Pascal's mugging.
(6) Obviously you shouldn't hand $100 to a random stranger who merely tells you a big number tied to a story you have vanishingly little reason to credit.
(7) So either step (1) is false, or something in (3)-(5) has to go.

I'd much rather keep (1) and (2) than accept (6), since rejecting (1) feels like it pushes me back into causal decision theory and its predictable losses in Newcomb-style cases. But I can't see a principled way to stop the slide at (4) or (5) that doesn't just cap utilities by fiat or gerrymander priors. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it actually commit you to? Say how confident you are in the diagnosis and where you'd expect reasonable people to push back. If I've misdescribed the mugging or the argument trades on an ambiguity I didn't notice, tell me that straight instead of trying to save it; I'd rather have it taken apart than get a neat-sounding fix.
