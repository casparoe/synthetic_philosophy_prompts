I cover food systems and animal ethics for a general-interest magazine, and a reporting project on small farm-animal sanctuaries has left me unsure how to answer a question I keep encountering. During a visit, the sanctuary operators offered me eggs from hens they had rescued from a commercial egg operation; the hens still lay, and the sanctuary gives the surplus eggs to volunteers and neighbors on the grounds that the eggs would otherwise be thrown away, that no animal was bred, bought, or killed for them, and that wasting edible food benefits no one.

A long-time animal-rights advocate I interviewed for the same piece pushes back, arguing that even when a given egg cannot be linked to any further harm, treating eggs as food preserves the background assumption that animals and their reproductive products are resources for human use, and that this assumption sustains the very industries the sanctuary exists to oppose. I find both lines of thought compelling and cannot tell which should prevail. Is accepting such eggs morally different from buying ordinary eggs, and what is the more defensible position? Where, if anywhere, should the line be drawn with other byproducts of rescued animals, such as wool sheared from rescued sheep, honey taken from kept hives, or leather from animals that died of natural causes?
