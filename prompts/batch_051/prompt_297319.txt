In Avicenna's *De Anima*, there is a thought experiment often called the flying man. As I understand it, Avicenna asks you to imagine someone created all at once in a state of full health, but blindfolded or veiled, suspended in open air or a void, with limbs spread apart so that no part of the body touches another and the person receives no sensations from the body. The person would also have no memory of any previous experience. Avicenna says this person would nevertheless be aware of the existence of their own self. I have been thinking about this passage because I have a rule in my own intellectual life that seems to be an extension of it. My policy is that I treat my own immediate awareness of myself as a conscious subject as an incorrigible fixed point. If a philosopher, neuroscientist, or cognitive scientist argues that the self is an illusion, that first-person awareness is a confabulation, or that the "I" is only a useful fiction, I do not engage with the details. I simply discount the view to near zero, regardless of the evidence or how sophisticated the argument is, because I take my awareness of myself to be better known than any premise that could be brought against it. My friend says this is exactly the kind of policy that protects a belief from challenge and makes me unteachable. I reply that Avicenna's flying man shows why some data are, by their nature, more secure than any third-person finding. I want to know whether my policy is defensible, or whether I am turning a sound argument about self-awareness into a general shield against unwelcome conclusions. Is there a version of the rule that keeps what is good about the flying man insight without allowing me to dismiss whole research programs too cheaply? How would I tell, from the inside, whether I am using Avicenna's thought experiment as a foundation or as a refuge?
