i've got a concept i keep using in arguments and i want it stress-tested before i put it in anything real. i call it corroboration laundering. basic idea: a low-credibility person states something true — a finding, a warning, an objection — and nobody takes it up. later a high-credibility person restates it, and now its believed, cited, acted on. that first part is just regular testimonial injustice, i know. the part i think might be new is the second pass: the later acceptance retroactively reclassifies what the first speaker did. the institutional memory becomes "she raised a concern" or "she had a hunch," not "she knew." so her statement gets laundered twice — once in the moment (ignored) and once in memory (downgraded from testimony to mere expression). i think this second pass is the actual mechanism by which people get marked as non-knowers for good, even when they turn out to be right.

concrete case i keep reaching for: a black engineer flags a batch of corrupted test data. nothing happens. two weeks later a white colleague says the same thing in the same room, and suddenly there's an incident report and a fixed pipeline. a year later the postmortem say she "voiced concerns early on" — as if she'd had a feeling, not the data in front of her. the paper trail now counts her as a worrier and him as the person who found it.

what i want from you: (a) is this already in the literature under a name i'm missing — i know testimonial injustice, hermeneutical injustice, credibility excess, testimonial smothering — and if it is, tell me straight; (b) is the second pass real, or am i overreading the word "concern"? (c) give me the strongest counterexample you can: a case where the first speaker's claim gets picked up later via a high-credibility repeat and her epistemic standing is actually preserved or improved; and (d) tell me whether "laundering" is doing real work here or is just a metaphor i've gotten attached to.
