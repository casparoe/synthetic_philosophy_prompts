I've got a rule about disagreement and I can't tell whether it's defensible or just a bias dressed up in game theory. I once learned about Aumann's agreement theorem, which — as I understand it — says that two perfectly rational Bayesians who start from the same prior can't agree to disagree once each one's posterior probability is common knowledge between them. So my policy is: whenever a friend and I have read the exact same sources and hashed out our reasoning until we're just repeating ourselves, I split the difference between our probabilities. Last month a friend put a claim at 60% while I was at 80%, and after we each explained why, we both moved to 70%; I called it a win for rational updating. But now i'm worried I've built a dodge. If she'd been at 95% and me at 5%, would averaging to 50% really be sane? Does Aumann actually license averaging, or is that my own invention? Under what conditions — same priors, common knowledge of rationality, genuinely shared evidence — would my compromise rule be justified, and when is it just folding to keep the peace? I dont want to throw out the idea that a peer's disagreeing posterior should move me, but I also suspect "split the difference" is doing more work than the theorem can support. If the rule is broken, is there a nearby rule that keeps whatever is good about taking peer disagreement seriously without mechanically meeting in the middle?
