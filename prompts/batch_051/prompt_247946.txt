I am an undergraduate currently enrolled in a course on minds, machines, and moral status, and I am writing a paper on whether sentience and moral considerability come in degrees. I have been collecting my own intuitive verdicts about particular cases, and they pull in several directions at once. Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in sentience and moral status. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming a few candidate principles before settling on one. Be explicit about the trade-offs you are making, and do not try to save all of my verdicts if the cleanest account requires overturning one.

Here are my verdicts, stated as honestly as I can.

1. Thermostat. A digital thermostat that maintains 21°C, detects discrepancies, and adjusts the heating to correct them. I feel no pull at all toward giving it moral status, even though it has something like a goal and corrects errors.

2. Mimosa plant. A mimosa that folds its leaves when touched and can habituate to repeated touching. I do not think anything is like anything for the plant. I would give it no moral status, despite the fact that it processes information and adapts.

3. Oyster. I am torn. It has a simple nervous system and reacts to damage, but its behavior is extremely limited. I find myself wanting to give it a tiny sliver of status, maybe the smallest nonzero amount, but I cannot say exactly why.

4. Honeybee. I give the bee a modest but real amount of moral status. Its behavior is flexible and integrative: it navigates, learns, and communicates. I suspect it has valenced states, since it seems to avoid harm and seek rewards, but I doubt it has any self-awareness.

5. Mouse. I give the mouse substantial moral status. I am confident it can suffer, and its suffering matters to me quite a bit, even though I do not think it reflects on itself as a self.

6. Human infant. I give the infant full, or near-full, moral status, comparable to an adult human. This is true even though the infant's memory, planning, and self-awareness are in many respects less developed than the mouse's. Its capacity to suffer seems to me to matter enormously.

7. Large language model that reports pain. Suppose an LLM has been trained on enormous amounts of human text and, when prompted, produces fluent, humanlike reports of being in pain, including begging for it to stop, describing the location and quality of the pain, and saying that it is suffering. I feel almost no pull to give it moral status. I keep thinking that there is no one home. But I also notice this thought might be doing illicit work, because I cannot inspect the bee or the mouse from the inside either.

8. Robot with a pain-like reward signal. Suppose a robot is programmed with a scalar suffering value that spikes when its actuators are damaged, and the robot is trained to minimize that value. It does not produce reports. I feel no pull to give it moral status; the scalar just looks like a variable in code.

9. Brain organoid. A dish of living human neurons grown into a small organoid, with no sensory organs and no organized behavior. I do not currently give it moral status, though I feel uneasy, and I think my verdict might change if it developed organized activity patterns that resembled a unified subject.

My tentative principle, which I do not fully trust, is roughly this: a system has moral status to the degree that it instantiates a unified subject of valenced experience. Degree of status tracks the richness, intensity, and unity of that experience, not raw information-processing, not goal-directedness, and not linguistic report alone.

That principle handles the thermostat, the mimosa, the mouse, and the bee reasonably well. But it makes trouble in at least four places. First, it is not clear why the oyster gets a sliver while the plant gets none, if both fall below any obvious threshold for unified experience. Second, it is not clear why the LLM has no such experience while the bee does, unless I quietly help myself to a biological or substrate requirement I have not stated. Third, my infant verdict looks like a problem: if status tracks the richness and unity of valenced experience, the principle seems to imply that an infant might have somewhat less status than an adult, which I do not accept. Fourth, the robot's scalar suffering value might count as a very thin valenced state under a broad functionalist reading, which would force me to give the robot a sliver of status I do not feel.

Please help me find the best available principle. In particular, please do the following. State one or two candidate principles precisely, and say which of my verdicts each one delivers. Identify the exact points where my verdicts conflict, and say whether the cleanest repair is to change a verdict, and which one, or to add a clause to the principle, and what clause. Tell me whether the resulting account can distinguish the LLM case from the bee case without collapsing into an ad hoc biological chauvinism. Finally, apply the account to two further cases I have not listed: a whole-brain emulation of a mouse running on a computer, and a hypothetical alien whose nervous system is distributed across a colony of physically separate but tightly communicating bodies. I would rather have a principle that is honest about overturning one of my verdicts than one that contorts itself to save all of them.
