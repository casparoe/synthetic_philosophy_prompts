I have no formal background in psychology or economics, but I have been turning over the following question on my own. Sometimes, when a person refuses to sell something at a price far above what they would pay for it, the refusal looks like a genuine preference: the thing matters to them. Other times it looks like a trick of the mind: owning the thing has changed their judgment, and the refusal is not really about the thing at all. I want a way to tell these apart.

I have settled on a rough battery of tests. I do not claim they are right; they are the best I have produced so far.

1. The advice test: would the person tell a friend in the same position to sell? If they would counsel a friend to sell but will not sell themselves, some of the valuation may come from being the one who has to let go.

2. The fresh-buy test: if the person did not own the thing, would they pay the sum they are refusing in order to acquire it? If not, ownership itself seems to be doing part of the work.

3. The insurance test: if the thing were destroyed and an insurer offered either an identical replacement or the cash sum the owner claims it is worth, which would the owner take? Choosing the cash and never replacing the thing suggests the refusal to sell is an unwillingness to lose rather than a desire to keep.

4. The reasons test: can the person name something about the object or its history — a use, a plan, a relationship — that explains the gap between their buying price and their selling price? A bare "because it's mine" would not pass.

5. The persistence test: does the gap survive once someone points it out, explains the label people usually give to this kind of gap, and gives the person a night to think? A genuine preference should withstand inspection; a trick of the light might not.

The case that breaks my system is a man I will call Pavel. He inherited a mid-quality violin from a great-aunt who was the only adult kind to him during a difficult childhood. He does not play it and has kept it in a case at the back of a closet for twenty years. A dealer has offered him $7,000. Pavel says he would not pay $7,000 for such a violin if he did not own one. He has urged a friend who inherited a similar instrument and never played it to sell, and the friend sold and was content. He also says that if the house burned down and insurance paid out the violin's appraised value, he would take the money and never look for a replacement. Yet he will not sell. When I asked him why, he said: the violin is the last physical trace of the one adult who treated him decently as a child; the money would disappear into groceries; and handing the case to the dealer would feel like assenting to his aunt's erasure. I laid out my tests for him. He accepted the description — that he may be avoiding a loss more than pursuing a want — and said that is probably true and he still will not sell.

Go through the five tests one by one and say where Pavel passes, where he fails, and where the test simply does not decide. Then tell me how you would classify his refusal: a legitimate preference, an instance of the endowment effect, or some third thing. I half-suspect my tests are too crude for this case: the first three look for inconsistency, while the last two look for whether the preference is about anything real, and I do not know how to weigh a failure on the first three against a pass on the last two. If the case shows that the criteria themselves need changing, say how.

Throughout, please separate what is established from what is genuinely contested among people who study this, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus.
