Hume opens Of the Obligation of Promises (A Treatise of Human Nature, 3.2.5) by denying that any naturally occurring act of the mind could be what a promise expresses:

"The act of the mind, exprest by a promise, is not a resolution to perform any thing: For that alone never imposes any obligation. Nor is it a desire of such a performance: For we may bind ourselves without such a desire, or even with an aversion, declar'd and avow'd. Neither is it the willing of that action, which we promise to perform: For a promise always regards some future time, and the will has an influence only on present actions."

I want a working definition of "promise" that honors Hume's negative claim without reducing a promise to "whatever a convention happens to make binding." Test your definition against each of the following and give the verdict it yields:

1. A parent says to a child, "I promise we'll go to the zoo on Saturday."
2. An actor on stage says, "I promise I'll have the money to you by Tuesday."
3. A candidate says in a campaign speech, "If elected, I will cut the sales tax by half," and later repudiates it.
4. A mobster says to a shopkeeper, "I promise your windows will be broken if you don't pay by Friday."
5. Someone clicks "I agree" to a terms-of-service box without reading it, thereby committing to arbitration.
6. A person says to no one else, "I promise myself I'll quit smoking."
7. A patient in severe pain signs a form promising to pay for treatment after an orderly says only, "This will make the pain stop."
8. A friend says, "I'll try to make it to your party," and does not come.
9. A couple recites marriage vows before witnesses and a registrar.
10. A person says to a dying friend, "I promise I'll look after your dog," and continues to do so for years.

I take 1, 9, and 10 to be promises, 2 not to be a promise, and 7 to be a promise but not a binding one; I am genuinely unsure about 3, 4, 5, 6, and 8. Propose the definition, run it through all ten cases, and say where it gives the wrong verdict or no verdict; then revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. Finally, say what the surviving definition and the ten cases jointly imply about Hume's further claim that promises are human inventions whose force derives from convention rather than from nature, and identify which case tests that claim hardest.
