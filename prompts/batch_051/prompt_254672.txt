I have been defending a version of the contingency argument in a seminar, and a colleague pressed an objection that now strikes me as too strong to ignore but impossible to locate precisely.

(1) The contingency argument is driven by the Principle of Sufficient Reason: every contingent fact has a sufficient reason. (2) Now take the fact that God freely creates this world rather than no world, or rather than some other world. If that act is genuinely free, the fact is contingent. (3) By the Principle of Sufficient Reason, it therefore has a sufficient reason. (4) If that sufficient reason were itself a contingent fact, the same principle would demand a further sufficient reason, and the explanatory regress the cosmological argument was meant to block would simply reappear; so the sufficient reason must ultimately be a necessary fact, presumably a fact about God's own nature. (5) But if God's choice has its sufficient reason in a necessary fact about God's nature, then, given that nature, God could not have chosen otherwise. (6) So God's creative act lacks alternative possibilities and is not free in the sense the argument's defenders typically want to preserve. (7) And if God could not have done other than create this world, then, since God exists necessarily, this world exists necessarily as well. (8) Therefore the contingency argument seems to prove that the world is not contingent after all, contradicting the very distinction it set out to secure.

I cannot accept (8), but each earlier move looks forced, and I would prefer to hang on to the Principle of Sufficient Reason in some form while also keeping a robust sense of divine freedom. Which step or inference should I reject, and what does each available repair cost?
