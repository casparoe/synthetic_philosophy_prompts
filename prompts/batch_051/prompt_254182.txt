I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether the "extra" degrees of freedom in a gauge theory are real, and I've ended up defending the line that they're pure surplus structure. In electromagnetism the vector potential is just a redundant way of describing the same physical situation; in gauge theory generally, only gauge-invariant quantities—field strengths, Wilson loops, S-matrix elements, expectation values of gauge-invariant operators—are real. The gauge-variant stuff, like the value of A at a point or the local phase of a matter field, is descriptive fluff. Choosing a gauge is like choosing coordinates: different guages are just different notations for the same world.

But I want to know how exposed this position really is. Tell me what would count against it: which phenomena, arguments, or formal results would have to be taken seriously as counterevidence, and which are merely logical possibilities that nobody should lose sleep over. Things I'm already half-worried about include the Aharonov–Bohm effect (where the potential seems to matter in a region with no field), Wilson loops and holonomies, large gauge transformations and their associated charges, edge modes, Gribov copies, and the fact that the standard path integral seems to integrate over gauge-variant configurations I'd rather call unphysical. For each, I want to know whether a committed surplus-structure person would count it as a genuine defeater or dismiss it, and on what grounds—and whether it's fatal or just awkward. Rank the defeater candidates from "the view would have to be abandoned" down to "costs at most a shrug." Be honest about whether the view has insulated itself: if I've quietly defined "physical" so that nothing gauge-variant could ever matter, say so, and tell me whether that's a feature or a bug.

At the very end, give a machine-readable overall assessment as \answer{...} with a short label—something like \answer{well insulated}, \answer{moderately exposed}, or \answer{badly exposed}.
