I'm currently taking an upper-level course on Plato's ethics, and I have fallen into a habit while working through the "why be moral?" problem. To evaluate any proposed answer to Glaucon's challenge from Republic II, I mentally put the agent behind the ring of Gyges: completely invisible to other people and thereby free from all social reward, punishment, and reputation effects. If the offered reason to be just would cease to be available, or would lose its force, under those conditions—because it depends on being seen, trusted, reciprocated, or on an emotion that needs an audience—I classify it as a reason to appear just rather than a reason to be just, and I set it aside almost entirely. I now apply this filter almost automatically, even to arguments in the course readings: the view that justice is a kind of health or harmony in the soul, the view that wrongdoing damages one's prospects in the afterlife, the view that morality is a social contract. I have started to suspect the test is rigged, since it seems to build in from the start that no fundamentally social or instrumental consideration can count as an answer to Glaucon. Is the Gyges test as I have described it a defensible heuristic for separating genuinely moral considerations from merely prudential or reputation-based ones, or is it a bias dressed up as a method? If it is not defensible, what is the strongest revision of it that keeps the useful filtering without begging the question? And where would even the revised version most likely misclassify? Please separate what a careful reader of Republic II at this level should treat as settled from what is genuinely contested among moral philosophers, and do not present a contested reading of Glaucon as the consensus. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the question.
