I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I am trying to understand what it means to prove something in mathematics. I have been reading Henri Poincaré's *Science and Hypothesis*, and in the opening chapter, "On the Nature of Mathematical Reasoning," he compares proofs to an induction step: if a theorem is true for the number 1, and if we show that it is true for *n* + 1 whenever it is true for *n*, then we conclude it is true for all positive integers. He writes:

> But how can we know that all the steps of this long journey, which we can make in our minds in a single bound, are not merely possible but necessary? Because we see in it, by pure intuition, the affirmation of the power of the mind which knows itself capable of conceiving the indefinite repetition of the same act, once that act is possible. This is why, in the case of mathematical induction, the conclusion is not a mere convention or an experimental generalization; it is the direct consequence of our belief in the unity and simplicity of the self, in the power of the mind to think the indefinite repetition of an act.

That passage caught me, because I have always assumed that understanding a proof by induction means understanding why the step from an arbitrary *n* to *n* + 1 preserves truth, and that the principle of induction itself is just the background rule licensing the conclusion. Poincaré seems to be saying something stronger: that the proof's force comes from an intuition about the self's capacity to repeat an act indefinitely, not merely from the step-by-step correctness of the algebraic manipulation.

The questions should press the guest from several angles: whether this "power of the mind" intuition is doing any work beyond the formal induction axiom, whether it collapses into mere confidence in one's own algorithm, whether it explains why students can execute induction while still doubting the conclusion, and whether the account has anything to say about proofs that are not by induction.
