I'm a retired chemist — thirty-five years in a lab, so I'm new at this philosophy business and I apologize if I mangle the terms. I've been stuck on Boethius. In the Consolation of Philosophy Book Five he tries to reconcile God's foreknowledge with human freedom, and the part I keep going back to is his distinction between simple necessity and conditional necessity. If I read him right, his thought is that God's knowing something makes it necessary only in the conditional sense — the necessity belongs to the link between a condition and its consequence, like his walking example, where if someone knows a man is walking, then it follows necessarily that he walks, but the walking itself isnt made simply necessary. So far so good.

But I tried to lay the whole thing out as a formal argument, and now I don't know where it goes wrong. The conclusion feels false, but each step by itself looks okay. Here it is:

(1) Suppose God, in his eternal present, knows infallibly that I will do A tomorrow.
(2) If God's knowledge is infallible, then there is no possible world in which God knows that I will do A tomorrow and yet I fail to do A tomorrow.
(3) If God is omniscient by nature, then in every possible world He knows every truth; so in particular, if the proposition "I will do A tomorrow" is true, then God knows it in every possible world.
(4) From (2) and (3), it follows that in every possible world I do A tomorrow.
(5) If in every possible world I do A tomorrow, then my doing A tomorrow is simply necessary, not merely conditionally necessary.
(6) Therefore Boethius's distinction does not actually secure human freedom: God's necessary omniscience plus infallibility turns conditional necessity into simple necessity.
(7) So either I do not act freely, or God is not the way Boethius says He is.

I keep suspecting step (3) is doing something sneaky with the scope of "every possible world," but maybe the real fault is earlier, in (2), or even later, in (5). Is the argument valid? If not, which is the first step that fails, and why? And would Boethius's claim that God is outside time rather than knowing in advance change the formal points in any of the steps? I'd be grateful for an answer in American English.
