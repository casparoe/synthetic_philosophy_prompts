I am the senior planner for a regional planning commission that reviews, about once a month, proposals that change both how many people live in the region and how good each person's life is: subdivisions, apartment rezonings, annexations, transit packages, and proposals to preserve open space by refusing growth. We are repeatedly stuck on the same conceptual knot: some proposals add many people whose lives will be decent but below our current regional average, and other proposals keep the population smaller while making existing residents better off. Board members argue by analogy to families and cities — is ten people each at a seven better than five people each at a nine? — and we have no settled way to answer that. I want a written decision procedure my analysts can run before anything reaches the elected board.

The procedure should be grounded in the strongest defensible philosophical treatment of total versus average value and person-affecting concerns, but it must be usable by non-philosophers and free of jargon. My analysts can produce rough, interval-based estimates of effects on residents (commute time, housing cost, crowding, green space, air quality, a life-satisfaction survey), but they cannot produce precise utilities, and they typically have one working day per proposal. The elected board has to sign off, and it will reject anything that reads as intellectual game-playing rather than a practical tool.

Please provide:
1. The plain-language questions to ask, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each and where the procedure stops with a "no" or a "yes."
2. A rule for when the procedure should add across all residents (new and old), when it should compare averages, and when it should consider only the existing residents — plus a rule for how to treat the question of whether the affected people would have existed, or lived elsewhere, if the proposal were rejected.
3. Clear markers of the steps where the procedure must hand the question to the elected board because the remaining issue is a value judgment rather than an estimate, with the board's options stated honestly rather than hidden inside a formula.
4. Worked applications to three proposals: (a) a combined housing-and-transit package that adds 12,000 residents and slightly improves average wellbeing for everyone; (b) an annexation that adds 25,000 residents whose lives will be decent but below the current average, lowering the average while raising the total; and (c) a downzoning that blocks 8,000 new homes and raises the average wellbeing of existing residents by reducing crowding, while the would-be residents either live elsewhere in worse conditions or, in some cases, would not exist at all. Include at least one worked application in which the procedure returns an answer the board will not like.
5. An honest statement of what the procedure cannot settle and the kinds of proposals for which it is likely to misfire.

Be concrete enough that an analyst can execute it in a day with imperfect, interval-based estimates, and say explicitly where a rough estimate is enough and where the decision turns on getting a number right.
