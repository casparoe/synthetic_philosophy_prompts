There is a striking asymmetry in how we judge two ways of reaching out toward extraterrestrial intelligence, and I want to know whether it can be justified. Pointing radio telescopes at the sky and listening for artificial signals is a routine, publicly funded scientific activity that almost nobody calls reckless. But deliberately broadcasting powerful, targeted messages into space—sometimes called METI, or active SETI—is treated by many scientifically literate people as an irresponsible gamble, something that ought to require international consultation or even a moratorium. The asymmetry is stranger than the bland difference between looking and shouting, because the scenario everyone has in mind when they support listening—receiving a deliberate message from another civilization—requires someone on the other side to have decided to transmit; a person who supports listening while condemning transmitting is therefore, in that scenario, relying on aliens to perform an act they would forbid for us. I would like the strongest principle that delivers both judgments, and an honest verdict on whether it survives scrutiny. Does the rationale that listening is harmless and reversible while transmitting is causal and irreversible actually do the work, or does it only restate the act/omission distinction in new language? Does the position survive the case in which every civilization adopts the same listen-only policy, so that the galaxy stays silent even though all parties would have preferred contact? And if the objection to transmitting is that it reveals our location and technology, what should we make of the fact that we have already been leaking radio and radar into space for decades—is refusing to transmit the moral equivalent of locking the front door while leaving every window open, or is a deliberate beacon different in kind rather than merely in degree?
