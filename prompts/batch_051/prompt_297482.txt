I cover science and ideas for a living, and I've noticed that the people I interview in, say, climate modeling or protein folding talk about simulations as if they were experiments: you build a model, run it, poke a parameter, and you've learned something about the world, even thought no physical instrument ever touched anything. Then I talk to philosophers, and they run "experiments" too, except the apparatus is a story: Twin Earth, the trolley, the violinist, brains in vats, philosophical zombies. They seem to treat the results as data about concepts or morals or what's possible. A physicist friend summed up the attitude for me: a thought experiment is just a simulation run on the brain's own hardware, so it should inherit whatever epistemic credibility simulations have. That line is doing a lot of work in how he dismisses the weirder cases in philosophy, so I'd like it stress-tested.

Audit that analogy for me. Lay out what maps onto what: the model, the initial conditions, the run, the check against reality, the debugging and peer review of the code. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which breaks actually matter for the conclusion that a well-run thought experiment is evidence in the same way a well-run simulation is. Does your verdict change depending on the genre — thought experiments inside science (Galileo on falling bodies, Einstein chasing a light beam) versus in ethics (the trolley, the violinist) versus in metaphysics (zombies, Twin Earth)? And where does "intuition pump" sit in the analogy: is it the software the simulation runs on, the monitor the output is displayed on, or something else entirely?
