here's a thing i keep going back and forth on. i'm pretty convinced by the fair-play account of why free riding is wrong: when you accept the benefits of a cooperative scheme that other people maintain at some cost, you owe your fair share, and that's what makes taking without contributing wrong. it's one principle that seems to handle the bus, the strike, the shared kitchen rota, voting, even the climate case. but the intuition i can't budge is the one about no-difference cases. say the bus driver never checks and my two-dollar fare wouldn't change the service, the drivers' pay, or the fare-enforcement budget by even a cent; or the strike is already won or lost regardless of whether i cross the line. in those cases i can't make myself feel like i'm doing anything to anyone. the theory still calls it free riding and says it's unfair; my instincts say no harm, no foul.

i dont want to be told "just pay, it's simpler." i want to know what it would actually take for one of these to give. if the principle is right, is the no-difference intuition just me failing to scale up a real aggregate harm, or does it track something the principle shouldn't overrule? if the intuition is right, does the theory need to be rebuilt so obligations only bite at thresholds or kick in for dispositions rather than individual acts? and is there a reading where a causally inert free ride can still be unfair, or compatible with fairness, or do i have to demote one of them and, if so, wich?
