Last night I got into a long, slightly drunk argument with a friend about statutes of limitations, and I want to see it done properly. My friend's line was that they're just a practical concession to bad evidence: memories fade, witnesses die, physical traces disappear, and at some point a trial is more theater than fact-finding. I kept pushing back that there's something deeper about time itself mattering morally — that even with perfect evidence, punishing a seventy-year-old for what an eighteen-year-old did might not be the same kind of act, or might not be something anyone still has standing to do. He said I was smuggling in a mushy "people change" argument and called it a day.

I'd like you to be the professional here. You're a moral philosopher who works on punishment, responsibility, and the ethics of law. Start by brainstorming the strongest versions of the two positions — one that says the passage of time is only ever an epistemic/pragmatic problem for holding people accountable, and one that says time itself can morally erode what is owed or who may demand it. Then write a dialogue of about 800–1200 words between two equally competent characters, one defending each view. Have them actually engage: they should respond to the other's points, and at least one of them should be forced to revise or refine their view in the face of a good objection. Make sure they get into the genuinely hard cases — a wrongdoer who confesses forty years later with the evidence still solid, a victim whose grievance has never cooled, and the fact that some wrongs get no legal time limit at all while others expire quickly. And no strawmen; if one side loses a point, let it be on the merits.
