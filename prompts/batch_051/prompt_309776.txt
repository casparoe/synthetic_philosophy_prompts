I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar in practical ethics, and this term we are working through the problem of individual contributions to collective harms: the thought that one's own flight, burger, or vote is overwhelmingly unlikely to make any difference to the outcome, and the question of whether that fact undermines one's reason to refrain or to participate. My students grasp the standard free-rider analysis quickly, but I find they treat the inefficacy objection as a curiosity rather than as a live problem: they say that if everyone did it things would be terrible, and then move on. I want an activity that stops that move in its tracks.

Please design a 75-minute classroom activity for around sixteen students that makes participants feel the force of the inefficacy objection before it is named. The activity should have real structure: rules, materials, a scoring or pay-off scheme that does not require actual money, roles or asymmetries where they are useful, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the conceptual terrain. The conceptual targets are the distinction between (a) cases in which an individual act is genuinely inert because the harm is overdetermined or the relevant threshold has already been crossed or is out of reach, and (b) cases in which an individual act makes a small positive expected difference by raising the probability that a threshold is crossed; I would also like participants to feel the pull of the thought that if I do not do it someone else will, and the difference between asking what I should do and asking what we should do. The students should run into the problem through their choices and incentives, not through a lecture with discussion questions appended; the facilitator should name the idea only in the debrief.

As you plan, take on the role of an expert in experimental philosophy and game-theoretic pedagogy: think carefully about likely student behaviour, about the incentives you are creating, and about the points where the exercise might accidentally teach the wrong lesson. Begin with a brief internal brainstorm of possible game structures before settling on one, but present only the settled design in your answer. Structure the response under the following headings: "Design brief" (one short paragraph stating the learning aims and the one-sentence rationale for the mechanism you chose); "The activity" (roughly 400 words covering setup, materials, rules, roles, timeline, and the scoring scheme); "Debrief" (roughly 300 words giving the sequence of questions and explaining how the facilitator maps observed choices onto the conceptual distinctions); "Predictable failure modes" (roughly 200 words on what will go wrong the first time it is run and how to handle it); and "What the activity cannot reproduce" (roughly 150 words on which feature of the inefficacy objection must be supplied by the facilitator afterwards). Use British spelling throughout.
