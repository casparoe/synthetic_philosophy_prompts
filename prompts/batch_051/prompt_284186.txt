Here's something I've never quite managed to square. Sweden, Norway and, more recently, France run what's usually called the Nordic model: selling sex isn't prosecuted, but paying for it is — the buyer is criminalised precisely so the seller doesn't have to be. Germany and the Netherlands went the opposite way and treat sex work as a legal, regulated job: you can advertise it, do it in a licensed brothel, pay tax on it. And New Zealand more or less took sex work out of the criminal code entirely, on the model of any other personal service. All three regimes say the point is to protect sex workers, but they seem to rest on completely different answers to a very basic question: is paid sex a form of exploitation, an ordinary job, or something the criminal law should simply stay out of until someone is actually coerced?

I want you to excavate the philosophy each policy quietly presupposes. Start with the Nordic model and stay in the weeds: if selling is legal but buying is a crime, what does that presuppose about the seller's agency, about whether consent to paid sex can be valid, about what the buyer's desire is, and about where the exploitation is located in the transaction? Then do the same, a bit more briefly, for the German/Dutch legalised model and for New Zealand decriminalisation. For each, distinguish what the policy strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with, and say where it's philosophically underdetermined — where more than one theory of consent, autonomy or harm would justify the exact same law. Also flag where a single regime seems to presuppose inconsistent things (for instance, treating the seller as capable of deciding to work but as incapable of validly consenting to the payment).
