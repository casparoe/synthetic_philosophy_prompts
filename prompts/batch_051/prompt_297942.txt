Since retiring from a career in atmospheric physics, I have been reading the philosophical and economic literature on intergenerational justice and climate policy, and I find that I have drifted into a private rule for deciding which arguments I will take seriously. An argument about how much we should do now for the sake of future people — whether it concerns the social discount rate, a carbon price, or a mitigation schedule — must decompose its treatment of the future into three separate questions: whether future people matter less merely because they are later in time, whether they will exist at all, and whether they are likely to be materially better or worse off than we are. If a writer gives me a single number, or a general slogan about not mortgaging the present for the future, without telling me which of those three considerations is doing the actual work, I set the argument aside.

My reason is not that I distrust economics; it is that the three considerations pull in different directions, and an argument that quietly merges them has not yet told me where the moral weight lies. The rule survived until a former colleague, who now works on climate institutions, pressed me on it. He argued that the decomposition is artificial in just the cases that matter: extinction risk is partly endogenous to the policies we choose, expected future wealth depends on the same contested climate damage functions, and a demand for separated factors smuggles in a model in which the factors can be separated. He added that my rule is a physicist's prejudice for explicit parameters, and that there are cases in which the most honest statement is precisely that the components cannot be disentangled. I took his point, but I remain reluctant to accept arguments that leave their assumptions about remote future people compressed into an undifferentiated rate.

Am I right to treat decomposition as a condition on the acceptability of these arguments, or have I turned an analytical preference into an unjustified filter? If the rule is too strict, is there a defensible version of it — for example, requiring that a writer settle for an ordinal rather than a cardinal statement of which factor dominates, or that they flag where the entanglement prevents separation — that would keep the demand for transparency without insisting on a decomposition the subject may not allow?
