After a department talk last week, two people I know — Rhea and Tomas — fell into an argument about the Knobe effect. It lasted through dinner and ended with both of them asking me to settle it. I couldn't, and I won't pretend I did. Here is their exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it, prefaced by the case that set it off.

The background case. An executive is told that a proposed project will increase profits and will, as a side effect, damage a nearby river. She replies that she cares only about profit and approves the project; the damage comes about. In the canonical finding, most people who read this vignette say the executive damaged the river intentionally. If the vignette is altered so that the side effect benefits the river, and the executive makes the same profit-first reply, most people say she did not benefit the river intentionally. The only thing that differs between the versions is whether the side effect is harmful or helpful.

The exchange.

(1) Rhea: That is the cleanest evidence I know that the ordinary concept of an intentional action has a moral ingredient. If "intentionally" were a purely descriptive term — the agent knew the outcome would come about and went ahead anyway — the two versions would be treated the same. They are not, so the concept itself must be sensitive to whether an outcome is bad or good.

(2) Tomas: Too fast. The vignette only shows that English speakers use the word "intentionally" to do two jobs at once — describe the causal role of an outcome and register blame. In the harm case, denying intentionality sounds like making an excuse for wrongdoing, so respondents do not deny it. In the help case, asserting intentionality sounds like awarding credit the executive has not earned, so they withhold it. The underlying judgments could be identical; only the conversational stakes of one word differ.

(3) Rhea: You keep saying "only" the conversational stakes, but you have just described the ordinary conditions of application for the term. If whether speakers will apply "intentionally" tracks blame and credit, then blame and credit are part of the conditions under which the term applies — not a residue to be scrubbed off in search of a pure descriptive core. The word, in its ordinary use, is a responsibility-attributing term.

(4) Tomas: Maybe, but notice that your evidence is a single verb with a well-known blame flavor. Replace it with a blander phrase — "did the damage to the river come about as part of what she knowingly did" — and I would bet the asymmetry shrinks. If it does, the effect is a fact about the rhetoric of one word, not a fact about how people mark the boundary between intended and merely foreseen outcomes.

(5) Rhea: That bet assumes the point at issue: that the bland phrase and "intentionally" pick out the same property, minus the blame. "Intentionally" may be the more faithful guide to how people assign outcomes to agents. "Part of what she knowingly did" does not eliminate the evaluative dimension; it just relocates the question to which outcomes count as things she did.

(6) Tomas: Here is a cleaner worry. What the vignette manipulates is not simply good versus bad. In the harm case, the executive's stated indifference flouts a norm against treating damage to others as negligible; in the help case, the same indifference merely fails to be altruistic. So the effect could be tracking norm transgression rather than moral valence as such. People may mark norm-violating side effects as more intentional; that would be a less revisionary explanation, and the original asymmetry cannot rule it out.

(7) Rhea: But the norm you have just invoked — against treating harm to others as negligible — is itself a moral norm. You have not removed morality from the explanation; you have moved it one level up. If you want to show that the effect tracks deviance in general, not badness in particular, you need a harmful side effect that violates only a conventional rule, or a helpful side effect that violates a moral norm, and you need the judgments to follow the norm rather than the valence.

(8) Tomas: Exactly — we need those cases. That is my point. The canonical asymmetry is consistent with your moralized-concept story, with a purely conversational story, and with a general norm-sensitivity story. Treating it as a demonstration that the concept of intentional action is moralized is therefore the overreach. I do not need to show the effect is merely verbal; I only need it to be underdetermined by the data.

Please go through this exchange move by move. For each numbered move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say which specific points, if any, each person is right about, and what both of them are missing. Do not just pick a winner: I want a point-by-point adjudication of the arguments as actually made, including the places where each overreaches or concedes more than they should.
