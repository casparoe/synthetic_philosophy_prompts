Here it is. Pearl's causal hierarchy has three rungs: association (what he glosses as seeing), intervention (doing), and counterfactual (imagining). Rung two is the do-operator, setting a variable by surgery on the graph; rung three needs the full structural causal model, latent variables and all. Grammatical mood gives something eerily similar: indicative, imperative, subjunctive. The indicative reports how things are; the imperative is the mood of bringing something about; the subjunctive handles the hypothetical and contrary-to-fact. Seeing/doing/imagining against indicative/imperative/subjunctive — the glosses almost line up word for word.

If the identity were real, you'd expect the hierarchy's epistemic asymmetry to reappear in grammar: rung-one information normally underdetermines rung-two and rung-three claims, so you'd predict you can't derive imperatives or subjunctives from indicatives alone, and that a language without a distinct subjunctive should make counterfactual reasoning harder.
