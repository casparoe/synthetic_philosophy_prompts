I need a working definition of suffering that can do real work in debates about fish and crustacean welfare in aquaculture — not a survey of how the word gets used, but a single definition precise enough to classify the cases below and to reveal where it gives out.

Treat this as a test set. The first two items I regard as clear instances of suffering; the next two I regard as clearly not instances of suffering; for the remaining four I have no settled view, but I want the definition to force a definite verdict.

Clear instances of suffering:
1. An Atlantic salmon in a crowded net pen, heavily infested with sea lice, with open sores along its back, flashing against the net and not feeding.
2. A rainbow trout lifted from the water during harvest, held in air, and cut without stunning, which gasps and thrashes until it dies.

Clear non-instances of suffering:
3. A sea anemone that contracts when prodded and slowly expands again once the disturbance stops.
4. A bacterial mat in a biofilter, shifting its metabolism as an oxygen gradient moves through the tank.

Hard cases on which the definition should force a definite answer:
5. A prawn that receives a brief noxious chemical stimulus to one antenna and then spends minutes grooming, shielding, and rubbing that antenna against the substrate.
6. A Pacific oyster that snaps its shell shut when a shadow passes over it and remains shut for several minutes.
7. A fish kept under general anesthesia during a procedure that would otherwise be painful: its nociceptors keep firing while the brain activity associated with wakeful processing is suppressed.
8. A fish held for weeks in chronic crowding, with cortisol persistently elevated and growth slowed, but with no acute escape behavior or visible agitation.

State the definition, then apply it to all eight cases one by one. For each case, say which verdict the definition actually produces — suffering, not suffering, or no determinate answer — and compare it with the verdict I have flagged. If the definition gives a flagged case the wrong answer, either revise the definition or defend your surprising verdict. Then say whether suffering, on your definition, comes in degrees, and if so what does the grading. Finish by identifying the point where the definition is least stable.
