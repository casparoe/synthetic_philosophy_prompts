In Descartes’s Second Meditation, he considers looking from his window and seeing hats and coats pass along the street; he notes that what the senses strictly show him are hats and coats, which could cover artificial automata, and that his judgment that they are men is an act of the understanding rather than a sensory given. I keep returning to this passage because it seems to express, in a few compressed lines, the inferentialist view of other minds that I find theoretically compelling: my own mind is given to me directly, while every other mind is a posit I make on the basis of bodily behavior, an inference rather than an encounter. This view has the systematic virtues I tend to want from a theory: it is economical, it does not multiply immediately known minds beyond necessity, and it makes sense of the main-character feeling as a structural fact about first-person experience rather than a metaphysical accident. But I also have a firm intuition I cannot dissolve: when someone tells me they are in pain, or when a child cries, the other person is not an NPC. I do not seem to be running a quick inductive inference from behavior to a hidden mental state; I seem to be confronted by a mind as directly as I am confronted by my own thoughts.

I do not want to be told simply that solipsism is false or that the intuition is fine. I want to know what each side would have to give up in order to settle this clash. Should I treat the intuition as a built-in social reflex that is practically indispensable but epistemically second-class, so that the theory stands and the intuition is debunked? Should I revise the inferentialist picture so that it allows a direct, non-inferential awareness of other minds? Or is there a reading of Descartes’s passage, on which the judgment is not a fragile guess but an act of the intellect that discerns what the senses already present, that lets the theory and the intuition both survive? Give me the strongest version of each option, and say which move is best supported by the passage and why.
