I've been going back and forth with a friend for weeks about whether the propensity interpretation of fitness actually answers the tautology objection or just relocates it, and we keep ending up at the same impasse, so I'd rather read a sharper version of the fight.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers of biology, Priya and Tomás, who disagree about exactly this. Priya defends the dispositional view: fitness is a probabilistic disposition to leave offspring in a given environment, not a summary of actual reproductive output. On her view, an organism can be highly fit and still fail to reproduce because of bad luck, and that gap between disposition and outcome is what gives fitness empirical content, so the old worry that natural selection is just a tautology rests on a category mistake. Tomás thinks appealing to propensities mostly relocates the problem. If the propensity is inferred from actual reproductive success, it inherits the original triviality; if it's inferred from component traits like running speed or drought tolerance, then those traits are doing the causal work and the fitness propensity starts looking like a weighted summary rather than something that does explaining. Have them genuinely respond to each other's actual arguments rather than restating positions, make each of them concede at least one point, and push the dialogue toward the question of weither there's a real difference between being testable in principle and being testable in practice in messy natural populations. Don't let either character be a strawman — both are competent philosophers of biology who have read the same studies and data.
