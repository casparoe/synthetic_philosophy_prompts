I'm a novelist. My protagonist is a philosophy grad student who keeps a file of transcendental arguments — arguments that go from something supposedly undeniable about experience, thought, or language to the conclusion that some doubted thing is after all its necessary condition. So far she's carded these: Kant's transcendental deduction (the unity of consciousness as a condition of possible experience); Kant's Refutation of Idealism (outer experience as a condition of being able to place one's own states in time); Strawson's argument that identifying and reidentifying particulars presupposes a spatiotemporal world of bodies and persons; Putnam's brain-in-a-vat argument (the claim that one is a brain in a vat needs the representational hookup it tries to undercut); and Davidson's argument that a scheme wholly untranslatable into ours is no scheme at all, since interpretation has to find most beliefs about the world shared and true. I suspect these are the ones everyone reaches for. Can you give me one more? It should be genuinely different from these — not a paraphrase, special case, or combination of them — and it would help if you said how it sits alongside the entries she already has and whether you think it's actually a good transcendental argument. And please seperate the standard settled readings of these arguments from the points people who work on them actually contest (for instance, whether one of them even counts as a transcendental argument, or whether it succeeds).
