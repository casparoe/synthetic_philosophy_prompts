I've been trying to devise a decision rule for the unilateralist's curse, and I want a candid assessment. I understand the curse as the fact that when each member of some group can act alone to produce or prevent an outcome that affects the whole group, and members reasonably disagree about whether the outcome is good, the group tends to get whatever the most optimistic member does first—because that member crosses their private threshold and settles the question for everyone. I call my proposed fix the 'unanimity-in-imagination test.' Before acting unilaterally, an agent must ask: would I want every other member of the group to act unilaterally whenever their own judgment told them to? The thought is that a yes gives me a group-level rule rather than a private license, and a no exposes that I am just trusting my own take. Test case: five independent labs each can publish the same dual-use finding; they know of each other but are not coordinating. I want to say a lab passes the test only if it would still want publication in the world where all the publication-enthusiastic labs publish and the collective result is overexposure. But I suspect the test is only Kant's universal law with the group written in, and that it relocates the curse rather than lifting it. Is the test coherent, is it new, and where exactly does it fail?
