The Fair Hiring Practices Code of our state contains these two provisions.

Section 9(b): "A prima facie case of disparate impact in hiring is established if the complainant shows that the employer's overall selection rate for any protected group, computed across all applications acted on during the hiring cycle, is less than four-fifths of the selection rate for the group with the highest overall selection rate."

Section 9(c): "No prima facie case arises where the employer's selection rates, considered department by department, program by program, or other hiring unit by hiring unit, are no less favorable to the protected group than to every other group."

Meridian Engineering hires for two graduate trainee tracks in a single hiring season. Track A, Equipment Technician, makes offers to a large share of applicants; Track B, Design Trainee, makes offers to only a small share. The parties stipulate to the following figures for the most recent season:

Track A: 400 male applicants, 356 offers; 200 female applicants, 180 offers.
Track B: 100 male applicants, 6 offers; 1,900 female applicants, 124 offers.

Within each track, women received offers at a slightly higher rate than men. Across both tracks, however, the male offer rate was 72.4% and the female offer rate was 14.5%. No one alleges that Meridian steered applicants between the tracks by sex. A woman rejected from Track B brings a complaint, saying section 9(b) is plainly satisfied. Meridian answers that section 9(c) is plainly satisfied, so no prima facie case arises. Both sides agree the drafters did not anticipate that the pooled comparison and the unit-by-unit comparison could point in opposite directions.

Does the Code, properly interpreted, make out a prima facie case on these facts? Spell out what the text of the two provisions, the purpose of the statute, what a reasonable reader would take the scheme to require, and what the drafters would most plausibly have said if shown these numbers would each support. Then say which approach should govern here, and why.
