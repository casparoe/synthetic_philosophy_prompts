Ok so I'm working on a metaphysics paper about causation and I keep getting tangled up in the "New Hume" debate. You know the one: does Hume think there really are secret necessary connections in nature that we just can't know about, or is causation for him just regular succession plus our mind's habit — full stop, nothing more. I can never get the dialectic straight because the New Hume people make such a big deal about the exact wording of his two definitions of cause. They say something like, look at the second definition, where he says the cause is an object followed by another where, if the first hadn't existed, the second never would have — and the transition in the mind. They read that as a real counterfactual dependence, not just a feeling we project. And Galen Strawson or whoever says Hume is a "skeptical realist": he's not saying there's no necessary connection, just that we can't discover what it is, because all we can observe is the regularity. But then the other side says no, his whole empiricism rules that out — he literally says we have no impression of power or necessary connexion, so it's meaningless to assert it exists beyond our experience.

So here's my actual question. Suppose I grant the New Humes their textual reading — that Hume never denies there's an in-the-world necessary connection. What would his view actually amount to then, ontologically? Like, there's some glue binding cause to effect, but it's completely inaccessible to us, and all we ever get is constant conjunction. I don't see how that's not just a bare posit with zero content, and I don't see what distinguishes it from the atheist who says "fine, I allow there might be some unknowable something behind it all, call it God if you want." Does the label "skeptical realism" do real work, or is it a name for having it both ways? Help me with a deflationary version of this: how would a New Hume cashed out actually differ from the plain old regularity reading, apart from tolerating a we-know-not-what?
