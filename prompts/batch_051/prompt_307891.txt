I've spent years reading rationalist blogs, so my default picture of practical reason is expected utility: assign probabilities to outcomes, assign utilities to those outcomes, pick the act that maximizes. But I keep getting pulled toward the transformative-experience argument, and I can't make the two fit. As I understand it, for choices like becoming a parent, emigrating, or undergoing a procedure that changes what you can experience, you can't know in advance what the outcome is like from the inside, and the experience will change the very preferences you'd use to evaluate it. The standard machinery then has nothing to grip: you lack the phenomenal data, you lack a fixed utility function for the calculation, and there's no value-neutral standpoint from which to compare your current preferences with the ones the experience would install. That part feels close to airtight. Yet I also have a firm intuition that we can and do distinguish better and worse reasons for becoming a parent or emigrating: people reason well or badly about exactly these choices, and that doesn't feel like a comforting illusion. Deciding to have children isn't just a coin-flip or a leap of faith, and calling it an exercise in authenticity sounds like relabeling a failure of decision theory. I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that gives, what it would take for the decision-theoretic picture to give, and whether there's a reading on which both survive — for instance, treating the predicament as ambiguity rather than ignorance, or treating choice-for-a-future-self as a legitimate object of rational deliberation rather than a glitch in it. You are a professional philosopher of action and decision theory. Before settling anything, brainstorm the strongest version of each side; then give me a negotiated verdict: what would justify debunking the intuition, amending the theory, or dissolving the apparent conflict. Keep it rigorous, not padded.
