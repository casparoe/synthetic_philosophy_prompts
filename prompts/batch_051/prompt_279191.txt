I've been calling it the Ledger Principle. Here's the setup. The no-free-lunch results say that averaged over all possible data-generating processes, every algorithm that learns from past data has exactly the same expected error as random guessing. My friend says this is a boring result about optimization with no philosophical bite; I say it gives a precise form to the problem of induction, and not a comfortable one.

My claim: whenever somebody says a rule 'learned' from past cases and now beats chance, that's just selective bookkeeping. For every world where the rule outperforms guessing, there is another world where it underperforms by the same amount, and the two cancel. So the apparent success doesn't amount to any net competence; it amounts to choosing which worlds your willing to be wrong about while calling the worlds where you're right 'the regular ones.' Induction never gets validated by its success — it just relocates the failure to environments you've silently excluded. It's not just that we have no reason to think the future resembles the past; it's that the usual appeal to past success can't even distinguish a bias that tracks the world from a bias that is merely moving its losses off the books.

My friend's comeback is that I'm smuggling in a uniform prior over all possible worlds and then pretending that flat average is the correct measure of justification. But I don't see it. I'm not saying we should average over all worlds; I'm saying if your inductive bias only looks better than guessing after you've restricted to a favored class of worlds, then you've assumed the very thing you were supposed to earn — you haven't learned regularities, you've just changed the subject to the places where your bias happens not to fail yet.
