I'm a software engineer at a language-learning company, currently designing the interactive timeline module for our Italian course. The unit is built around a question that initially looked simple but keeps breaking the flow: when did Latin become Italian? I don't have any formal philosophy background, and I've realized the hard part isn't the historical facts — it's the conceptual structure underneath them. My first instinct was that there must be a date, a generation, or at least a well-defined set of criteria where one language "really" became the other, but everyone I've asked gives a different candidate (the fall of Rome, Dante, vowel changes, the standardization of the literary language), and none of them explain why the question itself resists a clean answer. I don't need a reading list or citations; I need to know, as a designer, what idea has to take hold in a learner's head before the question even makes sense, and what order the rest should follow.

Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in vagueness, classification, and social ontology, and enough historical linguistics to keep the examples honest. Think carefully and begin by brainstorming the hidden dependencies between the concepts you'd need to introduce; don't write the lesson copy itself, just the instructional blueprint. Then give me a plan for a roughly thirty-minute interactive visual explanation aimed at curious adults with no classics or philosophy background. The plan should specify: the exact order of ideas and what depends on what; one concrete carrying example — a word, a sound change, a text, or a pair of snapshots — and what it must and must not have so it doesn't quietly mislead the learner; the misconceptions I should expect at each stage and exactly where in the sequence each should be defused rather than merely listed as wrong. Some from our user testing: that Latin "died" when the empire fell, that Italian was "invented" by Dante, that the answer is a percentage of shared vocabulary or a mutual-intelligibility score, that there is a hidden objective answer linguists possess, and that language change is something that happens to words rather than to speakers' practices. Finish with one check-for-understanding question I can put at the end that would show the learner has absorbed the idea rather than just repeated that "change is gradual," and a short list of things I should explicitly refuse to explain at this level, with one line on where each would derail the lesson.
