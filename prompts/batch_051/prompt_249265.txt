I have been thinking on my own about a puzzle that will be familiar to many people: blackmail seems clearly wrong, yet it is assembled out of ingredients that seem, taken one at a time, permissible. A person may ordinarily reveal an embarrassing but true fact about someone; that same person may ordinarily keep it to himself; and a person may ordinarily ask to be paid for something of value. Yet when someone combines these into "pay me, or I will reveal it," the result is treated as a serious wrong. I have been trying to locate exactly where the wrongness enters, because all the obvious candidates — the disclosure, the secrecy, the desire for money — seem individually insufficient.

The view I keep returning to is this. In an ordinary exchange, the buyer wants something whose value exists before and independently of the seller's offer. In blackmail, the seller's threat is what first produces the thing being sold. Before the demand, the victim's secret is simply private. By declaring that he will reveal it unless he is paid, the blackmailer makes the silence valuable to the victim and then offers to sell it. The harm he offers to withhold is a harm that his own proposal has manufactured. On this account, blackmail is wrong because of the conditional structure of the demand — the creation and sale of protection from one's own permitted conduct — and not because secrecy should never be sold, nor because the truth is being used as a weapon as such.

I can already see an objection, and it is the reason I distrust the account. A settlement offer has a similar shape: "withdraw my lawsuit, or pay me." If settlement is generally permissible while blackmail is not, then either my account is too broad, or the differences I reach for (the litigant has a prior claim against the other party, while the blackmailer has none) are doing more work than the account admits.

I want help designing the best possible test of the claim that the wrongness of blackmail lies in the conditionalized creation of the very harm being sold — or, if the claim cannot be tested in any ordinary sense, an explanation of exactly why it cannot. By a test I do not mean simply asking people whether they disapprove; I mean whatever mix of constructed cases, contrasts with permitted practices, or real-world variation would genuinely discriminate between my explanation and the most plausible rivals — for instance, that the wrong is the commodification of secrecy itself, or that the wrong is exploitation of the victim's frightened state. I want to know what results would favor each view, and, just as important, what even a strongly favorable result would leave unsettled.
