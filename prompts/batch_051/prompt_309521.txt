I am a novelist, and in the manuscript I am drafting there is a small coastal community with a common disaster fund, administered by an assembly whose past decisions are already fixed. The assembly's precedents are these. It paid in full a fisher whose boat was destroyed by a storm that no one could have anticipated. It awarded nothing to a merchant who had declined to buy insurance that was cheap and readily available. It paid one-third to a winegrower whose experimental vines were killed by an unseasonable frost; the vines ordinarily flourished in that soil, and the local records gave no reason to expect such a frost. It awarded nothing to a man whose house burned down while he was away voluntarily tending a sick relative, on the ground that his absence, however admirable, was chosen.

A new claim now comes before the assembly. A cabinetmaker bought a workshop on a hillside known to be prone to landslides; the site was cheap for precisely that reason. She had first applied for a loan to buy a safer property and was refused, and in practice her only alternatives were to abandon her trade or move her family into a single room. A landslide has now destroyed the workshop.

I want the assembly to decide this claim consistently with its own record. What should it decide? Which of the earlier decisions is the closest analogue? What principle or principles would make the four earlier decisions jointly coherent, and what does each candidate principle require for the cabinetmaker? If the precedents underdetermine the new case, set out what principle the assembly would be adopting under each possible decision, and identify the further case that would most cleanly separate the candidate principles. Please write your answer in British English.
