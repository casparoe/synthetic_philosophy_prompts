I am a journalist working on a feature about a company that adapted a general-purpose health-companion chatbot for use in a memory-care unit without rewriting its ethics specification. The original specification includes this clause: "The assistant must never deceive the user. If the user expresses a false belief, the assistant shall gently state the correct information." Its authors were thinking of ordinary consumer exchanges—mistaken medication times, wrong appointment details, family members expected at the airport—not of a setting in which the true statement itself does harm. In the memory-care unit, a resident with advanced dementia asks each morning where her husband is; he died two years ago, and the truthful answer makes her relive that bereavement every day. A nurse has asked the engineers to let the robot say that he has gone to the store. On the plain wording, does the clause forbid that response? What does a purpose-based reading of the clause yield, what would its authors have said if this case had been put to them, and what would a reasonable reader take the clause to mean? Which of these approaches should govern an autonomous system, given that its interpretation of a rule is ultimately a matter of what it is programmed to do?
