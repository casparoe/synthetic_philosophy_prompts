I'm a philosophy PhD student, mostly epistemology but I keep wandering into the ethics of belief, and I've got this tension I can't get rid of. On one side I'm genuinely convinced by a strong evidentialist view about belief: roughly, that forming a belief on what you know is insufficient evidence isn't just an epistemic slip, it's a moral failing, and the appropriate response to insufficient evidence is doubt — suspending judgment, refusing to assent. I like how clean the view is. It gives me a single norm, it dosen't carve out special exemptions for comforting beliefs, and it turns critical thinking into a real moral virtue: doubt becomes, literally, a duty.

But I also have a brute intuition I can't shake, which is that someone who treated their partner's word as just another data point to be audited would be a worse partner, not a better thinker. If my partner tells me something and I have no specific reason to distrust her, the thought that I ought to run some private little evidential audit before I'm allowed to believe her feels all wrong — it feels like I'd be failing her, not being rational. And I don't just mean the easy case where her track record settles it; I mean the case where the evidence genuinely underdetermines what she says and I believe her anyway, because she's the one saying it. Trust seems to involve giving someone the benefit of the doubt as a kind of gift, and a relationship where every belief about the other person is held exactly to the degree the public evidence supports seems less rational than cold, maybe even a little cruel. So I've got a compelling theory that says doubt is a duty, and a firm intuition that sometimes believing beyond the evidence is what you owe someone.

What I want to know is which of these should yield. Is the loyalty intuition just the evidentialist's point in a bad disguise — because her having told me is itself evidence, and my history with her is evidence, so the theory was never actually demanding I treat her as suspect? Or does the intuition track something the theory leaves out: a second-personal reason to believe, a reason that isn't evidence but is still normative? Or is the intuition the thing to debunk — a bias toward people I love that I'd be embarrassed to generalize into a rule for how anyone should believe about anyone? Give me the strongest version of each option, and say what would justify dropping the intuition versus amending the theory versus some compatibilist reading where "doubt is a duty" only kicks in once the trust itself is the question under review.
