I have been trying to work out whether the deep unease many people feel about cruelty to mammals and the near-complete indifference they show towards the massive routine killing of insects can be brought under a single coherent moral principle, and I should like your help in doing so as carefully as possible.

Consider the asymmetry as concretely as I can state it. A person who leaves a dog to die in a hot car, or who sets fire to a live cat, is generally regarded as having done something seriously wrong, and most of us would support legal sanction. By contrast, a farmer who fumigates a greenhouse, or a motorist whose windscreen splatters with insects on a summer drive, kills thousands of creatures in ways that would obviously involve suffering if those creatures were sentient; yet very few people treat this as a moral failure at all. This is not merely an asymmetry of scale, since the numbers cut the other way: it takes far more effort to kill one dog than to kill a million insects, but the dog's death is treated as the greater wrong.

Now set alongside this the following argument. Suppose you are genuinely uncertain whether an insect is sentient—capable of feeling pain or pleasure—and you assign a low probability, perhaps one in a thousand, to that possibility. A single pesticide application can affect ten million insects, which yields an expected ten thousand sentient individuals harmed, even before one considers the certainty of death. If we would regard the deliberate harming of ten thousand dogs as a moral catastrophe, then appealing to the low probability that any particular insect is sentient cannot by itself explain why we treat the pesticide case as negligible. The question is whether any principle can justify the asymmetry, or whether we are simply under the influence of salience, visible suffering, and species bias.

Please do the following. First, formulate the strongest principle you can find that would license treating the certain, visible suffering of one dog as a serious matter while treating the merely probable suffering of an enormous number of insects as morally trivial. It may appeal to differences in the character of experience, to the role of uncertainty and discounting, to the kinds of minds involved, to the interests served by the killing, or to something else. Second, test that principle against modified cases: a one-in-a-thousand chance that the animal in the hot car is sentient, multiplied across ten million cars; a pesticide that kills only a single insect; an insect whose sentience has been established rather than merely suspected; and a case where the human interest served by the killing is minor. Show where the principle holds and where it gives out. Finally, deliver an honest verdict: is the ordinary asymmetry defensible, should it be revised, or is it an inconsistency we should correct? Write your answer in British English.
