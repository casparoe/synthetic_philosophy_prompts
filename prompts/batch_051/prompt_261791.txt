A philosopher of economics makes a claim I want help getting empirical traction on. Expected utility theory's axioms—especially the independence axiom—are usually presented as normative requirements of coherence, not as psychological regularities. On this view, a person who exhibits the classic Allais common-consequence pattern does not merely have tastes the theory fails to capture; their preferences are internally inconsistent in a specific way. The pattern is this: they choose a sure $1 million over a lottery that gives a 10% chance of $5 million, an 89% chance of $1 million, and a 1% chance of nothing, but they choose a 10% chance of $5 million over an 11% chance of $1 million. That pair treats the common 89% chance of $1 million as if its value changes when it is present versus stripped away, which no single assignment of utilities to outcomes can represent. The coherence argument adds that such a person is exploitable: under standard assumptions about dynamic consistency and reducing compound lotteries, they accept a sequence of trades each of which looks good at the time but which together produce an outcome they would have refused, or they become a money pump over repeated choices.

I can see two rival readings of these violations. On the incoherence reading, the Allais pattern is a seam in the person's preferences: something that would come apart under reflection, repetition, or real incentives. On the non-expected-utility reading, the pattern reflects a stable, subjectively reasonable way of weighting risk—rank-dependent weighting, regret aversion, or some other well-behaved non-expected-utility representation—and the alleged exploit only works because the experimenter redescribes or repackages the same act at different stages.
