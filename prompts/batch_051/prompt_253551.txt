I'm giving a department colloquium next month to a philosophy faculty that is mostly moral philosophers: a strong contingent of Kantians, several Ross-style deontological pluralists who are inclined to think obligations can genuinely conflict, and two or three moral realists who work on the semantics of "ought." My thesis is that genuine moral dilemmas are impossible at the level of all-things-considered obligation. The standard anti-dilemma arguments from agglomeration and ought-implies-can both go through once "ought" is kept firmly in its verdictive, all-things-considered sense — from "X ought, all things considered, to do A" and "X ought, all things considered, to do B," together with "X cannot do both," something has to give — but the dilemma literature is nevertheless tracking a real phenomenon: irresolvable conflict among contributory (pro tanto) obligations, with the familiar regret and residue. The delicate part of my talk is that I claim this real phenomenon should be redescribed rather than called a dilemma. I expect the Rossians to feel that "genuine" has been withheld from them by stipulation, the Kantians to suspect that my contributory obligations let Ross-style pluralism in through the back door, and the semanticists to challenge the verdictive/contributory ambiguity in "ought" on which the whole disambiguation depends.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet for that audience, not a generic defense of the position and not the objections I would get from deontic logicians or from specialists deep in the moral-dilemmas literature. Please structure it exactly as follows: (1) "Concessions up front" — the points I should grant early to keep credibility, around 150–250 words. (2) "The objections in order" — the six to nine objections I should actually expect from this room, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are; for each, phrase the objection the way a member of this audience would actually put it (a Rossian's phrasing should not sound like a Kantian's), then give a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, then say explicitly where that reply is soft, and close with a note on whether I should concede, hold, or redirect; around 200–250 words per objection. (3) "The question to dread" — the one question I should most hope nobody asks, stated in the questioner's voice, with around 200–300 words on why it is dangerous and what I might say if it comes anyway.
