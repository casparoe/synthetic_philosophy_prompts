I have spent the better part of a decade following, and occasionally participating in, a dispute in the philosophy of set theory that seems to me to have reached a complete standstill. Everyone in the room accepts the same formal facts: Cantor showed that the real numbers have cardinality strictly greater than that of the natural numbers; the claim that no set has a cardinality lying strictly between them is the Continuum Hypothesis; and Gödel and Cohen established that this claim can be neither proved nor refuted from the standard ZFC axioms. Yet from this single, uncontroversial independence result, the field splits into camps that no longer seem to be arguing with one another so much as past one another.

One camp holds that CH nevertheless has a determinate truth value. On their view the cumulative hierarchy of sets is a definite, mind-independent structure, and the independence proof shows only that ZFC is too weak, not that the question is unanswerable; the task is therefore to find and justify new axioms, most plausibly strong axioms of infinity, that will settle CH the way ZFC settles more elementary questions. A second camp draws the opposite moral: if our best canonical axioms leave CH undecided, then there is no fact of the matter, or at least no fact we are entitled to claim; "the" universe of sets is not a unique object but a family of equally legitimate models, and CH is simply true in some and false in others. A third camp, toward which I find myself drifting, suspects the real dispute is not about the mathematics at all, but about what makes a statement of set theory true, or about what counts as evidence for a new axiom; the camps agree on every theorem and every independence proof, yet assign different weight to intrinsic plausibility, extrinsic fruitfulness, and the conviction that set-theoretic truth outruns our axioms.

What I want, then, is a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on CH. Is this a substantive disagreement about the set-theoretic universe, a verbal disagreement about the words "true" or "exists," two different questions — one mathematical and one epistemological — being run together under the single heading "Is CH true?", or something else? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a new theorem, a widely accepted new axiom, a principled account of how axioms are justified, or an honest argument that the question should be dissolved? I am less interested in a survey of the positions than in an account of why the debate has the peculiar shape it does — stalled despite complete agreement on the formal facts.
