I'm trying to think through a decision between two paths that matter to me in very different ways: taking a research fellowship abroad, which would be excellent for my career and intellectually right for me, versus staying in my hometown, where I could be present for my mother, whose health is declining, and remain part of a community I love. A friend, listening to me go back and forth, finally said: "You can't compare apples and oranges." His point wasn't just that the two options are different; it was that any attempt to weigh them against each other is misguided, and that I should decide on a different kind of basis altogether — duty, or the kind of person I want to be, or what I would least regret. That sounds plausible, but the slogan is doing more work than I can track. After all, I can compare the options along particular dimensions: the fellowship is clearly better for my career, staying is clearly better for my family and community, and neither dominates the other outright. So "can't compare" can't mean that all comparison is impossible.

Since then I've been reading around in work on hard choices, and I keep meeting a tangle of nearby terms: commensurability (sharing a common measure), comparability (admitting of better / worse / equal relations), incommensurability in a stricter sense, and the idea of parity (two items being comparable without one being better, worse, or equal). I can't tell which of these, if any, the proverb is reaching for, or whether it's really a claim about the options themselves or a claim about what deliberation should look like.

I'd like you to lay out the distinct precise claims "You can't compare apples and oranges" could be making, from the trivially true to the radically false, and place each on that conceptual map. For each candidate reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false, and — crucially — whether it would actually support my friend's conclusion that I should stop trying to weigh the two options and decide on some other kind of ground. I want the kind of menu I could bring back to the argument to pin down exactly what he needs the slogan to mean, and to see whether he can have it.
