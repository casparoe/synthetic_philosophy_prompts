Ok so I'm an undergrad in a philosophy of mind course and we've spent the last two weeks on pain. I've gotten pretty convinced by the line that pain is intrinsically bad — the idea that pain isn't a neutral sensation that we then happen to dislike, but that the badness is built into the experience itself. The standard argument goes something like: you can't have a pain that isn't bad for the person in it, because the very felt character of pain is aversive — it presents the state as bad and says, in effect, make it stop. That seemed right to me.

But then we hit pain asymbolia, and now I dont know what happens to the argument. The cases I saw are patients with certain brain injuries who, when poked with a pin or exposed to some noxious stimulus, say they feel pain. They can locate it, rate its intensity, and tell sharp from dull. But they don't flinch or withdraw, and they insist it isn't unpleasant — it doesn't bother them, it isn't bad. So it looks like pain and badness can come apart.

What I want to know is whether the intrinsic-badness argument survives this. Does asymbolia just force a repair (for example, only the affective-motivational component of pain is bad, and the sensory-discriminative part is evaluatively neutral)? Or can the intrinsic-badness person hold the line by saying the asymbolic patient doesn't really have pain — they have a pain-like sensation without pain proper? Is that a fair move or just moving the goalposts? And if the argument does die, which premise exactly did asymbolia kill?

I'm not asking which theory of pain is ultimately correct; I want to know what happens to this particular argument when you feed it the asymbolia data. You're an expert philosopher of mind who also knows the clinical pain literature. Could you think it through carefully, maybe brainstorm a couple of candidate diagnoses before settling, and write the answer in American English? Thanks!
