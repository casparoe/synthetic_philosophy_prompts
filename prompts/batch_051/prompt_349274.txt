I keep coming back to the view that alignment is, in principle, a well-posed problem of the same kind as finding a program with property P. There's a target — roughly, do what informed, careful, non-coerced humans would endorse after enough reflection — and a system is either closer to it or not, full stop. I like this because it gives alignment a non-vibes success condition, and because solvable in principle then means something clean: there exists a finite or computable procedure that hits the target within any tolerance you set, independant of our current engineering limits. The theory even tells you the hard part is just specifying the target at the right level, not the concept of a target itself.

But I can't shake the intuition that the target is path-dependent in a way that breaks the analogy. Building the systems, especially the first ones capable enough to matter, will change what we notice, what we endorse, maybe what we take wanting to mean at all. So the thing being approximated is partly fixed by the attempt to approximate it. On this view, solvable in principle is a property of a problem statement, not of a problem, and here the statement loops in the solver and the target.

Give me the strongest version of both. Then walk me through what it would cost to debunk the intuition as a scope confusion or a disguised complaint about measurement, what it would cost to drop the well-posed-target picture, and whether there's a reading of in principle on which both survive.
