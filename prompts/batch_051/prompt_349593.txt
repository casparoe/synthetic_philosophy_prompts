I hold an epistemic view of markets, roughly Hayekian in spirit, and I want it stress-tested. The position is this: the strongest argument for letting allocative decisions be made through competitive prices is neither an incentive argument nor a liberty argument but an informational one. Prices are the best mechanism we know for aggregating local, dispersed, often tacit knowledge that no single agent or central planner could assemble. Each trade transmits information from someone who knows something particular about time, place, or circumstance, and the resulting price vector functions as a public summary of knowledge that no one individual holds in full. On this view, the important failures of markets are failures of this informational channel, and the relevant comparison is not between real markets and an idealized planner but between the informational performance of actually available institutions.

I know the standard market-imperfection objections — externalities, public goods, asymmetric information and adverse selection, monopoly — and I set them aside, because I do not claim markets are perfect, only that their informational function outperforms the alternatives.

What I want from you is a map of the deeper defeaters, especially those arising from no-trade results. As I understand it, the no-trade theorem says that if agents share a common prior, are commonly known to be rational, and are commonly known to have different private information, then they should not agree to trade purely on the basis of that private information: the very willingness to trade at a proposed price would reveal the information behind it, and the price would adjust until the gains from trade vanish. If this is right, an informationally efficient competitive market looks like one in which no one can profit from private information — which seems to undercut the idea that real-world trade is how private information gets into prices in the first place. I need to know whether this result is a decisive objection to the epistemic view, a limitation of a particular formal model, or a paradox that itself needs diagnosis before it can be used against anything.

Please do three things. First, sort the candidate defeaters into those that should actually move a defender of the position and those a committed defender may rightly dismiss, and say which concessions defenders themselves would make. The candidates include: the no-trade theorem and its apparent implication that fully information-revealing prices are inconsistent with a market equilibrium; the related paradox that if prices perfectly aggregated all available information, no one could be compensated for the cost of acquiring information, so the informational claim may undermine its own mechanism; the possibility that markets sometimes aggregate correlated noise, herding, or common sentiment rather than independently held information; empirical episodes in which prices stayed far from any plausible fundamental for too long to blame on transaction costs; and the conceptual worry that a price is a single number that discards the semantic content of the knowledge it supposedly aggregates, so talk of prices as aggregated knowledge is metaphor rather than mechanism. Second, rank the ones that survive scrutiny from fatal to merely awkward, and state what the position predicts that rival views do not, so I can see where the view genuinely risks being wrong. Third, tell me honestly whether the position, as I have stated it or as it could be qualified without losing its Hayekian character, has been insulated into unfalsifiability — and whether that insulation is a problem for a claim of this kind or an appropriate modesty about what can be established in economics.

One constraint on your answer: I would find most persuasive defeaters that target the informational claim itself, rather than ones that concede it and shift to a different criterion such as welfare or liberty; those change the subject. What I want to learn is whether the claim about markets as information-aggregating institutions survives, and where its soft spots are.
