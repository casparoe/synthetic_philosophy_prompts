I have kept a record of a friend's verdicts about which departures from the real world she can willingly accept as stipulated facts within a story. Her record so far:

- She has no difficulty accepting a story that opens by stipulating that no object denser than water can float.
- She accepts a story in which the dead are buried face-down and everyone regards this as the only safe practice.
- She accepts a story in which greeting someone requires a bow of exactly ninety degrees and those who fail are mocked, as long as the story presents this as local custom.
- She resists a story whose narrator stipulates, in the narrator's own voice, that killing a stranger for an inheritance is morally right.
- She resists a story whose narrator stipulates that a country's institutions of slavery are just and humane.

I am now drafting a story set in a world where passing a salt shaker directly into another person's hand is a serious moral wrong—not merely a breach of etiquette—and the narrator states this in squarely evaluative terms. Should I expect her to resist this stipulation or to accept it? In your answer, set out the candidate rules that fit her record, say what each rule predicts for the salt-shaker case, give your best guess and explain it, and name the further case that would cleanest distinguish the live rules.
