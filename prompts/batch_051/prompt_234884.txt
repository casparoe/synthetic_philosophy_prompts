I cover consciousness and cognitive science for a general audience, and I've ended up halfway convinced of something that used to strike me as obviously nuts: that conscious experience is a user illusion. Not just that the self is a story, but the stronger thing — that the whole notion of private, qualitative feel, the what-it's-likeness, is a kind of interface the brain presents to itself, and introspection is systematically the wrong instrument for checking that picture. I know the weak version (experience is real, we're just wrong about its structure) and the strong version (there's nothing it's like, full stop) pull apart, and I'm not sure which one I'd defend under pressure.

What should actually move me? I want a ranked map of what would count against this view, and against its strong version in particular. Which experimental results, clinical cases, or arguments would be fatal rather then merely awkward? Are there predictions a defender of the view is stuck with that they'd rather not have to make — and have any of those already happened? I'd also like an honest answer to whether the strong version has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and if so whether that's more or less damning for this kind of view than it would be for an ordinary empirical claim. And where the weak and strong versions come apart: what evidence would finish off the strong version while leaving the weak interface story untouched, and the reverse?
