I'm a novelist, and I keep replaying an argument I sat through at dinner the other night between two friends. One designs combat systems for a big online game; the other is a lawyer. The designer's guild had just watched the city they'd spent two years building get wiped by a rival guild using a bug, and he was still genuinly raw about it. The lawyer thought he was being ridiculous. It went roughly like this.

The designer said it was a real loss: two years of skilled collaborative work and the place where the guild actually spent their social lives, and seeing it erased caused real grief.
The lawyer said its only bits on a server. Nothing in the physical world changed, and if changed bits can count as a harm then every file you overwrite on your laptop is a harm too.
The designer asked how that's different from someone deleting the only copy of the novel I'm working on. That's also just bits.
The lawyer said the novel has consequences outside itself — my livelihood, a contract, a physical book at the far end — whereas the game city's value only exists if the players decide to care about it, so the loss is something they're doing to themselves.
The designer said all value works like that: a house is just matter plus people caring about shelter and home, and a landmark is just stone plus a community caring about it. The guild cared about the city the same way, so if demolishing the landmark is a real harm, erasing the city should be too.
The lawyer said there's also consent and exit. You log into a game knowing the rules allow exactly this, and you can log off whenever you want. Losing the city is closer to losing a bet than to being robbed, even if it stings.

Go through this move by move and say who is right about what, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each one. Then say what both of them are missing, and come down on whether the destruction of the city was a real harm in any sense that matters — not just who wins the argument.
