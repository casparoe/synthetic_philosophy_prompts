I've been re-reading Turing's 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence, especially the section on extrasensory perception. He does something I find stranger and more interesting than a plain skeptical dismissal: he says, in effect, that if telepathy were real it would threaten the imitation game, but then he redescribes the test so that telepathy cannot give either side an unfair advantage—the machine is housed in a room shielded from telepathic influence, and a telepathic human competitor is allowed to compete under the same conditions. He seems to treat ESP as something to be neutralized in the experimental design rather than adjudicated on the evidence. I've been calling this move epistemic quarantine. The core idea is that when a disputed capacity is neither impossible nor established, but would act as a confound if real, the rational response is to build a comparison that is invariant under the capacity's truth or falsity, without affirming or denying the capacity itself. This seems different from methodological naturalism, which excludes the supernatural by fiat, and from agnosticism, which suspends judgment while leaving the confound in place. Quarantine is a design principle, not a metaphysical stance. I want an honest assessment. Is this a recognizable position in philosophy of science or epistemology, perhaps under another name? Am I overreading Turing, who may have been making a rhetorical concession rather than recommending a general method? And does the principle genuinely generalize to cases like near-death reports, plant cognition, or prayer studies, or does it break down when the disputed capacity is characterized so vaguely that shielding cannot be specified, or when quarantining the confound changes the very phenomenon under study? In your answer, please separate what is settled about Turing's text and its standard interpretation from what is genuinely contested among scholars, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
