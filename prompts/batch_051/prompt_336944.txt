I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy seminar on the ethics of rescue, and I am trying to reconstruct the principle our instructor has been using. The seminar's organizing question is whether, and in what way, the numbers count when you can save only one of two groups. I have kept careful notes on her rulings, and I can't yet get them to sit under a single rule.

Here are the cases she has ruled on. In all of them the people are adult strangers, equally situated, and no one has any special relationship to the rescuer unless I say otherwise.

Ruling 1, the culvert case. Five hikers are trapped in one culvert and one hiker in another, and the rising water allows time to dig at only one site. She said the rescuer should dig out the five. When a student proposed flipping a fair coin, she rejected it, on the ground that a fair coin gives the single hiker too strong a claim. But when someone asked whether the single hiker should get any positive chance at all, for example one in six, she said the cases so far did not force an answer and left that question open.

Ruling 2, the burning-wing case. A firefighter can enter one wing of a burning building; three people are in one wing and one person is in the other, and there is no time for a second entry. She said the firefighter should enter the wing with three people. She again declined to discuss lotteries or chances.

Ruling 3, the storm case. You can steer a boat either toward one person who will certainly be saved if you do, or toward five people who, because of the weather, have only a one-in-five chance of being reached in time. She said either choice is permissible, because the expected number of lives saved is the same on both options.

The new case we have been given is this. A rescue coordinator faces the culvert case again: five people trapped in one culvert and one person in the other. The coordinator's standing protocol runs a weighted lottery before each rescue, with a five-sixths chance of selecting the group of five and a one-sixth chance of selecting the single person. In this instance the lottery selects the single person. The coordinator can either obey the selection and dig toward the one, or set the lottery aside and dig toward the five.

I would like to predict how our instructor will rule on this new case, and I want the principle as much as the prediction. Please work through the two or three principles consistent with her rulings so far—straight aggregation, on which you should save the greater number outright and maximize expected lives saved under uncertainty; a weighted-lottery view, on which each person's claim buys a proportional chance and the single person therefore gets one chance in six; a fair-chance view, on which each side gets an equal chance, though Ruling 1 seems to reject it; and any variant you think fits better. Say exactly what each of those principles requires in the new case, and explain why the earlier rulings do or do not force a choice among them. Then give your best prediction of her ruling and your reasons for preferring that reading of the record. If the record underdetermines her principle, say where the fork lies and identify the single additional case or question that would best settle it. Finally, tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail: which consideration about the new case, or which alternative reading of one of the earlier rulings, would most plausibly push you to the opposite prediction, and what would make you change your mind.
