I am not trained in philosophy, but I have spent years working through the grandfather paradox on my own, and I have come to think the usual telling of it misses the deeper point. The real trap is not that the gun jams or that the traveler loses his nerve; it is that he can be there, raising the gun, only because the killing has already failed. On the single-timeline reading, the past already contains whatever stopped the deed. On the branching-timeline reading, he can shoot a man in another branch, but that man is not the grandfather he is descended from, so the original contradiction gives way to a different one.

I would like a villanelle that does justice to that trap. Use the standard nineteen-line form: five tercets and a closing quatrain, built on two rhymes. The first line of the poem and the third line of the first tercet are the refrains. The first refrain should return as the last line of the second and fourth tercets and as the next-to-last line of the closing quatrain; the second refrain should return as the last line of the third and fifth tercets and as the final line of the poem. Make the first refrain the traveler's intention, the act he keeps insisting is possible, and make the second refrain the answer that blocks the act, understood as a structural or logical constraint rather than a mechanical failure. The refrains should not be empty echoes: let each return shift in meaning as the context changes, so that the same words begin as determination and end as something closer to the trap reasserting itself. The closing quatrain should leave the two claims locked in tension rather than pick a winner. The poem should show the situation rather than name it, so avoid leaning on the word "paradox" inside the lines.
