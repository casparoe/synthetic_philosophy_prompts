I keep noticing something in my own body that I don't have a good word for, and I'd be grateful for help figuring out whether philosophy already has an established term for it.

Here's the phenomenon. When I catch a set of keys tossed at me from across the room, my hand is already in the right place, oriented the right way, fingers opening at the right moment, before I've had anything like a thought about where the keys will land. Same when I walk through my apartment in the dark: my body just takes the corner into the hallway, including a slight duck under the low bit of the doorframe, and I only know any of that "afterwards" if I stop and notice it. It isn't effortful, and it isn't the kind of thing I could write down as a list of rules and then exectute the rules.

I know "muscle memory" is a phrase people use for this, and I know psychology talks about procedural memory, but both of those feel off to me. Muscle memory sounds like it's about the muscles themselves, which seems to miss that this is me, a whole agent, coping with a situation. Procedural memory sounds like a stored subroutine, whereas what strikes me is that the situation itself seems to pull the movement out of me in real time.

So my question is: is there an established philosophical name for this kind of knowing/acting — not just the fact that the movement is automatic, but the way the body is aimed at the world before and below explicit thought? And I don't just want a label; I want to know what the label picks out and what it doesn't. How is this different from ordinary intending (I intend to make coffee, so I go to the kitchen), from a reflex, and from a skill I can explain while doing it? Or is the whole idea that this is "aimed at" or "about" the world in some sense a mistake, and I should just call it a motor program and leave it at that? I'm after the exacty of the thing, not just a word.
