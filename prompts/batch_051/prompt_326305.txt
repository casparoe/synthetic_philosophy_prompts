I run an evening math circle for adults who passed school algebra without trouble but whose idea of a proof is a sequence of moves to be rehearsed. Next week I have ninety minutes to get at the difference between being able to reproduce a proof and understanding one, and I want to build the session around the proof that the square root of 2 is irrational. I do not want you to explain the proof to me; I want a plan for how I should teach it. What has to be in place before the first line makes sense, and in what order should the pieces be introduced so that the contradiction lands as something the argument forced rather than a trick? Should the square root of 2 stay as the carrying example, or would something like the proof that the sum of the first n odd numbers is n squared let me separate 'each step follows' from 'the proof rules something out' more cleanly? I expect this audience to arrive with misconceptions such as that 'rational' simply means 'has a repeating decimal,' that a proof is only a list of algebraically allowed moves, and that the contradiction shows the square root of 2 does not exist at all. At what point in the sequence should each of these be headed off? What should I leave unsaid at this level, and what single question can I ask at the end to tell whether they have understood the proof rather than merely followed it?
