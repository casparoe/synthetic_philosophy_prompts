I'm designing a handout for an upper-level undergraduate jurisprudence course on desuetude, and I'd like you to act as an expert professor of legal philosophy in producing it. The handout should be a sorting quiz called "What do you actually think about laws nobody enforces?" The central question is what we should make of laws that remain formally on the books but are systematically unenforced: dead-letter blue laws, ordinances revived after decades against a single disliked person, statutes whose social function has been entirely displaced by new institutions, and criminal provisions used only selectively against disfavored groups. Please think carefully and begin by brainstorming, before you commit to the final instrument: list the distinct positions someone could hold (I expect at least something like a dormant-but-valid positivism, a desuetude-as-repeal realism, a rule-of-law congruence view that treats the gap between declared rule and official action as the defect, and a republican anti-arbitrariness view that is more worried about selective or sudden revival than about blanket non-enforcement, but refine and add as you see fit), the diagnostic distinctions that actually separate them, and a pool of concrete cases.

Then produce the quiz itself: eight to ten questions, each one a specific, concrete scenario with two or three answer options about what the respondent would do or judge in that case. The sorting must work by what people say about cases, not by which label they like, so no question should simply ask "are unenforced laws still law?" and no answer option should read like a slogan from a theory. After the questions, give a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto the positions, and make the key robust to realistic mixed combinations rather than assuming everyone is a pure type. Finally, add a short instructor's note telling me which pairs of positions the instrument cannot cleanly separate, which combinations of answers are logically possible but unoccupied, and your one-sentence diagnosis of each unoccupied combination — is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored?
