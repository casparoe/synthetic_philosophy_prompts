I recently spent an evening arguing with a German friend and an American friend about rule-following, and I came away unable to locate myself. My German friend defended rules as worth heeding even when nobody is harmed by breaking them — he would wait at an empty crosswalk simply because the rule is there, and he thinks queues are a small piece of civilization. My American friend treated every rule as subject to individual moral review: what matters, on his view, is whether conscience approves, not whether order is preserved. Each of them assumed I was on his side, but when I went through cases in my head I found I was on both sides depending on the case. I would like to know what, if anything, my verdicts add up to.

Here are the cases, as honestly as I can report them. (1) An empty intersection at two in the morning, the pedestrian light red, no car in sight anywhere: I wait for the green, and crossing would feel like cheating something, though I cannot name what. (2) Someone cuts in front of me in a bakery queue; it costs me forty seconds, yet I feel a spike of real anger, and I regard the anger as fitting rather than as a mere flaw of temperament. (3) A friend parks in a loading zone with hazard lights on to run an EpiPen up to a neighbor's apartment; I do not merely excuse this — I would think worse of someone who would not do it. (4) A coworker has been taking printer paper, pens, and a stapler home for a year; I would not report him, and I would privately distrust someone who did, though I also think what he is doing is wrong. (5) A coworker is quietly falsifying safety-inspection records to cut costs; I would report that, and I would feel I had done right, even though reporting feels very close to the thing I said I distrusted in case (4). (6) A neighbor without a permit parks in a handicapped spot to grab a coffee; I want him ticketed, despite the fact that in that moment the cost to any actual disabled person is likewise small. (7) A colleague at work is required to open each class with a pointless motivational exercise, and another colleague quietly skips it and teaches real material instead; I side with the one who skips it.

The only candidate I can form is something like this: rules deserve to be obeyed when they sustain a shared, visible order that everyone leans on, such as lights, queues, and parking markings, but they deserve to be enforced against other people only when real harm is at stake. Yet that principle predicts I should be stern with the office-supplies coworker and indifferent to the queue-cutter, and I am neither. Please test this candidate against my verdicts and repair or replace it. I am after a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely, with each clause motivated by the cases that force it; then its verdicts on a case I keep going back and forth on, namely this one: I am asked to sign a form attesting that I witnessed a consent signature that I did not literally see, though I know the person genuinely wanted to sign it. Finally, use whatever survives to say where I stand relative to the German and American folk moralities I began with, whether one of those two outlooks has the better of it, and which of my verdicts I would have to overturn to count as consistently one or the other. Please answer in American English.
