I am a political theorist presenting a paper at a workshop next month on protest, automation, and the rule of law. The thesis I am defending is this: large-scale, anonymous, automated digital disobedience — for example, a bot swarm that non-destructively floods a government portal to protest a specific law, or a script that mass-submits valid-looking but harmless applications in order to jam an unjust administrative process — can be a legitimate mode of civil disobedience even though it is not public in the usual sense, and the standard requirement that a disobedient be willing to accept legal punishment is the wrong test for this class of action. I am not claiming there are no constraints at all; I am claiming that the canonical constraints were built for face-to-face disobedience and need to be re-derived for automated action.

The audience: mostly political philosophers working in the liberal and republican traditions on civil disobedience, political obligation, and the rule of law, plus two or three computer scientists who work on platform governance and abuse-resistant system design. The philosophers will be worried about publicity, nonviolence, fidelity to law, and whether anonymous automation can perform the communicative role of disobedience; the computer scientists will be worried about externalities, deniability, and how systems can be gamed once this is normalized. 

My argument sketch, so the objections have something to target: automation changes the economics of disobedience by lowering participation costs and letting small numbers of people express a widely shared grievance; the action can still communicate through its target, timing, and stated demands even without an identified author; and the old willingness-to-accept-punishment test was a proxy for sincerity and respect for law under conditions of visible, expensive disobedience, not a principle that survives when enforcement is asymmetric, anonymity is protective, and punishment can fall on the wrong people. I expect the hardest objections to come from the republicans on accountability and from the computer scientists on collateral damage.
