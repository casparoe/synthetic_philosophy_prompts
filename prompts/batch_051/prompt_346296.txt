Im giving the toast at my uncle's retirement from city council next month. He spent twenty-seven years there and they asked me to speak because I'm the only one in the family who ever sat and listened to him talk through his work. Here's the thing I keep coming back to. About twelve years ago he cast the deciding vote to let a trash incinerator go up next to a mostly Black neighborhood, knowing the pollution would land hardest on people who already had the worst asthma rates in the city, because the developer's money was the only way to keep all three branch libraries from closing. He said at the time that he thought it was wrong, and he did it anyway, and he has never once let anybody tell him it turned out fine. I've been chewing on that by myself, in a plain way rather than a scholarly one: can someone do a thing they believe is wrong for a reason they believe is right, and still be a person worth honoring, without pretending the wrong part just washes off? Half the room will be political people, including a couple of organizers from that neighborhood who fought him on the vote and still call him a sellout, and the other half will be our family, who mostly know him as the guy who missed Christmas Eve to sit through budget hearings. Please write me the toast, about five minutes when read aloud. I want it to do what he'd actually respect: don't make him a saint and don't forgive the decision on his behalf, but also don't turn his retirement dinner into a public apology or a debate. Let the speech sit inside that question I've been chewing on, so it sounds true to who he became after the vote rather than who he was before it. And please no headings or bullet points or bold or tables, just continuous prose like a letter someone would read out loud, in American English.
