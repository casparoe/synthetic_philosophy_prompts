As a novelist, I have a professional stake in a word that is now being stretched into claims I cannot easily assess: interpretation. I want a working definition of interpretation — not a dictionary restatement — that can be run against the following cases.

1. A careful reader explains why a character's betrayal, though it surprises on first reading, is prepared by details earlier in the novel; other careful readers can contest the explanation on textual grounds.
2. A translator renders a Russian poem into English, aiming less at word-for-word fidelity than at preserving the poem's pacing, irony, and implied attitude toward its subject.
3. A saliency method highlights the pixels of a chest X-ray that most increased a classifier's confidence in a malignant label; the output is a heatmap with no accompanying statement of reasons.
4. A large language model, given only the prompts and responses of a second AI system, writes a fluent account of what the second system was attempting to do, without access to that system's weights, activations, or training data.
5. A distillation routine replaces a large neural network with a smaller one that matches its outputs on a task, and produces no account of the larger network's behavior.
6. A music theorist annotates a score to show how the dissonant harmony of a finale is prepared by a motif stated in the first movement.

State your definition, apply it to each case, identify the verdict you find least secure, and either revise the definition or defend that verdict. Finally, say what your definition implies for the claim, increasingly made under the heading of recursive interpretability, that AI systems can now interpret other AI systems.
