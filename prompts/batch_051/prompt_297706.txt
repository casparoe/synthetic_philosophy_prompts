A federation's constitution has a clause: "The federal government shall have exclusive authority over commerce among the constituent states, and over the transmission of messages for hire across state lines. The states shall retain authoirty over public morals, public order, and the protection of persons within their territory." The clause was written when "transmission of messages for hire" meant telegraph and maybe telephone, and the drafters said it was meant to cover common carriers moving messages between states for money.

Now a social media platform is incorporated and has its only servers in State A. A user in State B posts a video that is legal in State A but criminal under State B's hate-speech law. State B orders the platform to remove it and fines the company when it doesn't. The platform says State B can't reach it because the constitution's commerce-and-messages clause gives exclusive federal authority over cross-border message transmission; State B says the clause wasn't about user content, it's just a public order/morals regulation within its territory. Which reading is stronger? Take it from the text, from the purpose of the clause, and from what the drafters would have said, and tell me which method should win. Also say how subsidiarity cuts here, if at all. Answer in American English.
