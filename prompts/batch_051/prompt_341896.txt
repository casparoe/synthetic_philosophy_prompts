I am a novelist by profession, at work on a book set during the French Revolution, and I have been reading Rousseau alongside my research. For several months I have kept circling back to Book I, chapter seven of The Social Contract, where Rousseau argues that anyone in the social compact who refuses to obey the general will may be constrained by the whole body, and that this constraint amounts to being forced to be free rather than to a violation of freedom. I want to make that claim more than a paradox I accept or reject in an armchair.

I run a monthly salon for fiction writers, about a dozen published novelists and advanced students, all interested in political philosophy but with no formal training in it. We meet for two hours. Design an activity, game, or structured simulation for that group that makes participants feel, before they can name it, the pull and the discomfort of Rousseau's claim about being forced to be free. They should not hear the phrase, or get any lecture, before the debrief.

The activity should be a real social-contract exercise with small but genuine stakes—perhaps the allocation of quiet writing hours, desirable workspaces, the right to schedule a reading, or something similarly legible to working writers. The rules should be arranged so that participants first form private preferences, then deliberate, then bind themselves to a collective decision, and finally encounter the moment when someone who voted against the rule, or refused to accept it, is nonetheless constrained by the group. The force of Rousseau's idea should emerge from what happens to them, not from a facilitator explaining it.

The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the distinction between private interest and the general will, the difference between a mere majority preference and a general will properly understood, and the recurring objection that being forced to be free is either a contradiction or a dangerous redefinition of freedom. Be explicit about what the game is likely to teach falsely—for instance, that the general will is just whatever the majority decides—and where the facilitator must supply something the game cannot reproduce: the substantive metaphysical claim that there is a common interest distinct from any individual's private interest, and that constraint in its service can count as freedom. Also say what predictably goes wrong when people run such an exercise, how to keep participants from reducing it to a purely tactical negotiation, and how to handle the person who genuinely refuses and walks out.
