I work in credit-risk modeling, so using reference classes is half my job: this borrower gets compared to thousands of borrowers with similar income, debt load, and payment history, and nobody calls that a category mistake. But last weekend I got into an argument with my brother about whether the United States might come apart in the next century, and when I tried to take the outside view—using the base rate of relevantly similar cases instead of the details of this one—I said that most of the large, complex states in the historical record have eventually collapsed, been conquered, or broken up, so the base rate for still being around in 2125 is not obviously close to one. My brother got genuinely annoyed and said you cannot throw Rome, the Ottomans, and the Soviet Union into one bucket with the United States; every one of them is its own thing, the circumstances are totally different. Fine, except he is the same person who, two weeks earlier, when our cousin announced she was marrying a man with two previous short marriages, immediately started citing divorce base rates as if they settled the question.

So there is an asymmetry: we comfortably feed a particular marriage, borrower, or patient into a reference class of similar cases, but feeding a polity or an empire into a reference class of similar polities feels like an error—people say this time is different. I want to know whether anything actually justifies that asymmetry, or whether it is mostly an intuition we have because states feel unique and the examples are vivid. Please try to steelman the strongest candidate distinctions you can find—sample size, how alike the units in the class really are, whether a state is a self-aware actor that can change policy once it sees a forecast, whether collapse is a different king of event than divorce or default—and then tell me honestly which of them, if any, survive pressure. Test them against nearby cases: if the United States is too unique for reference-class reasoning, what about a small, poor state that has many historical analogues? If a corporation can be put into a reference class of similar firms, what exactly disqualifies a state? And if the assymetry comes mainly from this-time-it-is-different intuitions, say whether we would be better off dropping it.
