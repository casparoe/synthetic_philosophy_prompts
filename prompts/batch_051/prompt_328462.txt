I've been trying to pin down what defamation law actually commits itself to about facts versus opinions, and the ratings-and-forecasts cases are where it gets wierd. The basic rule is that you can recover for a false statement of fact but not for an opinion. Yet courts treat a wildly wrong credit rating or an analyst's price target that misses as protected opinion, even though a forecast looks as checkable as anything: either it comes true or it doesn't. But a forecast becomes actionable when it smuggles in a false claim about the present, and the more hedged a report is, the safer it tends to be. What picture of the fact/opinion distinction does that practice presuppose? Is the law working with a metaphysical distinction (facts are about settled states of the world, and the future isn't settled), an epistemic one about how the claim is justified, or just a free-speech compromise wearing an epistemological costume? Can a claim be checkable enough to be called false but still not factual enough to be protected? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say where the practice is underdetermined rather than committed, and what would have to change if the presupposed view were swapped out.
