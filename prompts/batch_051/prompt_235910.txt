Write a short but substantive dialogue between two philosophers of mind who agree that representational content must be naturalized in broadly teleological terms, but who disagree about which teleological facts fix content. The first defends a historical, selected-effects account: what an internal state represents is determined by what the mechanism producing it was selected for doing in the organism's evolutionary past. The second defends a current, systemic-role account: content is determined by the role the state now plays in keeping the organism alive and reproducing — the feature of the environment it tracks and how downstream systems exploit that tracking.

They argue about a single invented case. In a species of katydid, a sensory interneuron and its downstream evasive jump originally evolved to detect and escape a parasitoid wasp that is now extinct. The environmental shift is recent, so natural selection has not yet re-shaped the mechanism for the new task. Today the interneuron fires mainly at the high carbon-dioxide concentrations found in pockets of dense leaf litter, and the jump-away behavior now prevents the katydid from asphyxiating in those pockets. The historical theorist claims the state represents the long-vanished wasp attack, so in the current environment it usually misrepresents carbon-dioxide pockets as danger from a wasp. The current-role theorist claims the state represents asphyxiation risk or oxygen-poor air, and that the mechanism's evolutionary provenance has become irrelevant to what it represents.

The conversation should involve real back-and-forth: each character should restate and answer the other's strongest objection rather than talk past it. Have the historical theorist press the current-role view to distinguish genuine representation from any stimulus-response connection that happens to aid survival, such as clotting, inflammation, or shivering. Have the current-role theorist press the historical view with a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of the katydid assembled without any evolutionary history, which would behave identically yet on the historical account would lack perceptual content. Both positions should be developed with equal force; neither should be a straw man; each should make at least one meaningful concession and then refine or modify their view. End the dialogue with each character naming the further case or empirical finding they think would most cleanly separate the two accounts, even if the dispute is not resolved.
