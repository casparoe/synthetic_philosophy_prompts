I'm trying to get my views on life extension and death into a defensible shape before a seminar, and I keep going round in circles. My background view is broadly pro-longevity, but I've got a pile of nagging deathist-sounding intuitions and I can't tell whether they're compatible with the rest.

Here's where I stand, roughly:

(a) Death is bad for the person who dies because it deprives them of the good life they would otherwise have had. So dying at 40 is a bigger tragedy than dying at 90, other things equal.

(b) Ageing is the biggest avoidable cause of that loss, so I think we should treat ageing as a medical problem and push hard on research into extending healthy lifespan, possibly by a lot.

(c) But I reject the full deathist package: I don't think death is what makes life meaningful, I don't think finitude is inherently valuable, and the "natural therefore good" move strikes me as plainly bad.

(d) That said, I cant shake the feeling that a completely open-ended life would flatten something. Long projects get their shape partly from the fact that time is finite; relationships have an urgency partly because the shared time is limited. I'm not sure this is just nostalgia.

(e) I also think a lot of the "oh I'd never want to live to 100" talk is adaptive preference or sour grapes. People learn to love an option they can't escape, and I don't think we should treat that as deep wisdom about whether longer lives are worth having.

(f) But if an actual 90-year-old wants to refuse a life-extension treatment, I'd defend their choice as fully autonomous. Not false consciousness, not a symptom — their call.

(g) I'd be uneasy about a society that pressured everyone to live as long as possible, but I'd probably accept gentle nudges toward longevity once the treatments are cheap and safe, the way we nudge people not to smoke.

(h) Part of my hesitation about radical life extension is just that it would change the character of suicide, grief, and risk in ways I can't predict. That's a practical worry, not a principled defense of death.

I honestly cant tell where this comes apart, if it does. I'd like an audit rather than a lecture: which commitments actually conflict, which only look like they conflict, and what the least costly revision is. Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than saying they're all equally bad.
