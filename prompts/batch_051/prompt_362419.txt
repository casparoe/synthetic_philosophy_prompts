I am a PhD student in economics, working mainly on time-inconsistency, commitment devices, and dynamic contracting, and I have no formal training in constitutional theory. In an interdisciplinary reading group, the political theorists and constitutional lawyers keep invoking "precommitment" and "Ulysses contracts" as though they were describing the same phenomenon I formalize, but their examples carry normative weight that my models do not seem to license. I would like a systematic translation. Restate the constitutional-precommitment position — roughly, that a constitution is a polity binding itself against its own foreseeable future preference reversals, on the model of Ulysses binding himself to the mast — in the vocabulary I work in: time-inconsistent preferences, commitment devices, incentives and constraints, and contracting with one's future selves. Then give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly? Which translate only under distortion — for example, assumptions about who the principal is, whether the later majority is the same agent as the earlier one, and whether the constraint is genuinely self-imposed rather than imposed by predecessors? And which claims cannot be stated in my framework at all, even with fudging? I am especially interested in whether the claim that an earlier generation may legitimately bind a later one that never consented is expressible in the language of mechanism design, or whether its absence from the translation signals that the constitutional claim was doing different work than the term "precommitment" advertises.
