I run a seminar for doctoral students in political theory who are fluent in negative liberty, rights-as-side-constraints, and toleration debates, but who have never worked with preference orderings or social choice functions. Next session I want them to come away genuinely understanding Sen's liberal paradox — the impossibility of combining a Pareto condition with a minimal-liberalism condition under which each of at least two individuals is decisive over some pair of social alternatives — and, more importantly, to see why the result is philosophically interesting rather than a quirk of an artificial formalism. Plan the session for me, not as background exposition but as an ordered teaching plan.

I need the sequence in which the prerequisite ideas must be introduced and what each one makes possible; the single worked example that should carry the whole demonstration, with an account of what it must have and lack and an explicit justification of whether to adapt the standard reading-a-scandalous-book case or replace it with something less dated; a map of the misconceptions the students will predictably bring or form — I especially expect them to read decisiveness as the right to choose for everyone and to assume the impossibility depends on meddlesome preferences — together with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off; and a final check-for-understanding exercise that would separate someone who merely followed the example from someone who has grasped the structure of the result. Also say what you would explicitly refuse to formalize or discuss at this level. Present your plan as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
