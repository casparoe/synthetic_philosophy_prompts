I am preparing a review chapter on what it is for agents to act together, and I keep noticing that debates in this area proceed by single-contrast examples: two walkers moving at the same speed, one by coincidence and the other by agreement; a factory in which each worker tightens one bolt without knowing what the others are doing, versus a crew building a single product together; a prisoner whose arm is physically moved by a guard, versus someone who cooperates under a threat. Each case is used to disqualify one account, but the standard against which the cases are supposed to count is never written out in full. I want that standard made explicit before I assess any particular theory.

Please write out the desiderata that an adequate theory of joint action — or shared or collective intentionality — must meet, where "adequate" means it can explain when several individuals constitute one acting group rather than merely acting in parallel, and can underwrite the distinctive shared responsibility, credit, and blame we assign in such cases. For each requirement, give the motivating case or consideration. For the list as a whole, mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of particular camps. In particular, address whether an adequate account must be reductive to individual attitudes; whether it must scale from dyads to large, anonymous, or loosely structured groups such as crowds, firms, or open-source collaborations; whether it must attribute the doing or the intending to the group itself rather than to the members; whether it must handle coercion, incompetence, and self-deception among participants; and whether it must remain neutral about the substrate of the agents involved (human beings, non-human animals, organizations, or artificial systems). Flag any pairs of these demands that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since I find those tensions more illuminating than any single view's scorecard. Then, in a brief closing paragraph, identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude the most familiar available theories, and justify that choice.
