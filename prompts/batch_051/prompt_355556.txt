I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've agreed to deliver the opening remarks at a one-day workshop on antimemes—ideas, images, or messages whose own content tends to suppress their retention and spread. The room will mix memory researchers and philosophers who already use the term carefully with science writers and funders who will be hearing it for the first time; the remarks need to land for both groups. Please write five minutes of opening remarks. They should do more than define an antimeme from a safe distance: the speech should make the audience experience the difficulty of holding one in mind, and the form of the remarks—what they repeat, what they decline to summarize, where they circle back—should be part of the argument. Avoid letting it collapse into a stunt or a cheap joke about the audience having already forgotten something. The talk should leave the newcomers able to follow the rest of the day while giving the specialists a substantive question of their own, such as whether the category picks out a real kind of idea or merely relabels things that are unmemorable for ordinary reasons.
