A friend who's been reading Han Feizi walked me through an argument and I can't shake it. It goes like this.

(1) The Legalist starting point is that you shouldn't design a state around people being virtuous, loyal, or public-spirited, because such people are rare and you can't verify who's faking. Everyone — ministers especially — acts on interest: they're honest when honesty pays and corrupt when corruption pays more.

(2) So the only stable system is one where the incentives do the work: clear laws, fixed standards, harsh punishment for missing your stated target, big reward for hitting it. You don't need officials to be good; you need them to calculate.

(3) But laws don't apply themselves. Someone has to draft them, apply them to particular cases, and punish violations. Those someones are officials, so by (1) they're calculators too.

(4) So an official will enforce the law against a colleague or subordinate only when that's in the official's own interest, and will take bribes or look the other way whenever that pays better. Since the whole point was to stop counting on people being good, we can't just say "most enforcers will be conscientious."

(5) The obvious fix is to police the police: set up inspectors and auditors with their own rewards and punishments. But by (1) the inspectors are calculators too, so by (4) they'll police only where it pays. To make them do it, we need another layer watching the watchers, and so on without end.

(6) So the Legalist program either collapses into an infinite regress of organs watching organs, or it quietly smuggles virtue back in at some layer — somebody doing the right thing even when the incentives don't quite cover it.

(7) So Han Feizi's own principles show that rule by law can't actually replace rule by virtue; the difference is just where you choose to plant your faith in un-incentivized decency.

I want to reject this, because it seems to prove too much: the same argument would show no organization — a firm, an army, a tax office — can run on incentives without a layer of genuine virtue somewhere. But when I try to find the step to deny, each one looks forced. If you had to reject one step, or point to exactly where the argument slips, which is it, and what does rejecting it cost the Legalist?
