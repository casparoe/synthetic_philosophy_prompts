I am taking an undergraduate module on the foundations of machine learning and formal epistemology, and we have just covered Solomonoff induction. As I understand it, the proposal is as follows: enumerate all computable hypotheses, assign each hypothesis h a prior of 2^{-K(h)}, where K(h) is the length of the shortest program that computes h, and then update on observed data by ordinary Bayesian conditionalisation. This is meant to formalise Occam's razor, because shorter hypotheses receive exponentially more prior weight, and there is a convergence theorem to the effect that if the data are generated by some computable distribution, the predictions merge with it in the limit. If that is right, I do not see why the proposal leaves any room for further dispute about simplicity in science. Why do statisticians still select models using apparently improvised complexity penalties such as the Akaike information criterion, and why do scientists still treat the choice between a simpler and a more complex curve as a matter of substantive judgement rather than something to be settled by measuring Kolmogorov complexity? Moreover, since every computable hypothesis receives a non-zero prior, in what sense does Solomonoff induction ever rule a hypothesis out, rather than merely making it very slightly less probable? My suspicion is that I am reading the result as licensing more than it actually licenses, but I cannot identify where the inference goes wrong.
