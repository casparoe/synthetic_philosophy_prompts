I'm on the advisory board for a small conservation trust, and we've got a decision to make next month. Here's our track record, as best I can summarize it:

- We approved releasing sterile genetically modified mosquitoes in a city to suppress the yellow fever mosquito and cut dengue. The modification is self-limiting: it doesn't persist in the wild, and any effect fades once we stop releasing.
- We approved, with monitoring conditions, a conventional reintroduction of a locally extirpated native butterfly into a restored coastal prairie. No genetic modification — just captive breeding — and in principle the population could be removed.
- We rejected a proposal to use a gene drive to push a fungal-resistance allele through an isolated population of a native frog threatened by chytrid. The allele would have spread on its own and we couldn't call it back; the applicants understated how much the frogs move between watersheds.
- We also rejected a de-extinction proposal that would have used genome editing and a surrogate species to recreate a recently extinct island songbird. The concern there was that the result would be a novel genome reproducing in the wild, not a genuine return of the species.

Now the proposal in front of us: release blight-resistant genetically engineered American chestnut trees into Appalachian forests, where they'd cross with surviving wild chestnuts and spread the resistance trait permanently. The trees carry a gene that lets them tolerate the chestnut blight fungus, which basically extirpated the species a century ago. Supporters pitch it as restoring a keystone native tree; opponents say its an irreversible, self-propagating genetic change to a wild population, and once pollen moves there's no meaningful recall.

I don't need the right answer in the abstract — I want the decision that best coheres with our own precedents. Tell me what we should decide, which of our past rulings is the closest analog on each side, and, if the precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle we'd be adopting with each choice. At the end, say how confident you are in the rule you're attributing to us and single out the point you're least sure of.
