I work as a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I recently went down a rabbit hole reading about decision theory. As I understand the coherence arguments — the Dutch book arguments and the representation theorems — they show that if a person's preferences cannot be represented as maximizing expected utility relative to some probability and utility assignment, then the person's choices can be exploited, and therefore rational preferences must take the form of expected utility maximization. Applied to me, that would mean I should have a complete utility function: for any two options or outcomes, there should be some rate at which I am willing to trade one against the other. But that is not how my own preferences feel from the inside. I can rank ordinary choices without trouble, but with major life choices I run into what seem to be incommensurable values. For instance, I would not do something I consider dishonest no matter how much career benefit it would bring, and when I try to compare that commitment against other things I care about — living near family, doing interesting work — I cannot locate any trade-off rate that feels like mine. So I am left with what looks like a dilemma: either the theorem is telling me that I am irrational, or it is telling me that I secretly have a determinate utility function that I simply cannot introspect. Which is it, and should I try to train myself to have explicit trade-offs for everything? Or have I misunderstood what the coherence arguments actually establish?
