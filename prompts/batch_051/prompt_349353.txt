I am a novelist, and I am currently revising a manuscript in which metaphor is not ornamental but structural: a small set of recurring images carries much of the book's thinking about grief, work, and weather. I keep finding passages where a metaphor seems to be doing real work and others where it seems to be coasting on an automatic verbal habit, and I want a principled way to tell the difference before deciding what to cut or keep. Please rank the following three accounts of metaphor by their usefulness to a practising novelist engaged in this kind of revision, assigning each to one of four tiers: indispensable, frequently useful, occasionally useful, or likely to mislead. The three accounts are: (1) the Davidsonian view, on which a metaphor has no special metaphorical meaning beyond its literal content; it works by prompting readers to notice similarities or connections, so the effect is caused rather than encoded in the words; (2) the conceptual-metaphor view, on which metaphor lives in thought before it lives in words, and surface expressions in sentences are realizations of deeper, systematic conceptual mappings in which one domain is structured in terms of another; and (3) the interaction view, on which metaphor works by bringing two systems of associated ideas into contact so that each organizes the other, producing something not exhaustively paraphrasable in literal terms. For each account, give its tier for this specific task, and justify the placement in a sentence or two. If the three accounts serve different purposes, do not simply average them; say how you would divide the labour among them.
