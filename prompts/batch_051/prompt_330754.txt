I am the executive director of a small public-interest litigation nonprofit. We bring suits against state agencies under open-records and environmental-review statutes. Once we file, agencies often fight by attrition: over-broad objections, slow-rolled document production, depositions, and interlocutory appeals. At each major step I have to decide whether to keep funding a case that has already cost more than we budgeted. These decisions come up five or six times a year, involve amounts between $150,000 and $800,000 of an annual budget of roughly $3 million, and I typically have a week to decide before the next filing deadline. I have no economics training, and my staff are litigators, not decision scientists.

Martin Shubik's 1971 paper introduced a game of this kind: an auction in which the high bidder wins a dollar and pays his bid, but the second-highest bidder also pays his bid and receives nothing. Once the bidding passes a dollar, both remaining bidders face a choice between bidding again to avoid losing their already-spent bid and dropping out to avoid throwing more money after a losing position; the structure can drive both bidders to spend far more than the prize. I suspect my agency litigation is a slow-motion version of this, with legal fees as the bids, the judgment or settlement as the prize, and the agency's own litigation costs as its second-place payment.

Please write a reusable decision procedure I can run at the start of each major litigation phase, grounded in what the dollar auction and escalation-of-commitment literature actually shows. It should be a sequence of questions an associate can work through in a day, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure says stop, where it says continue, and where it says the decision is a values judgment I must make rather than a mechanical output. The procedure must specifically handle the two-party dynamic (the agency is also spending resources, and my decision to continue changes the cost it expects to pay), the difference between sunk costs and future costs, and the difference between losing the case and failing to win a fee award. It should also mark the points where the theory says one thing but an organization facing reputational costs would reasonably do another.

Then run the procedure on two hypothetical cases. Case A: an open-records suit we budgeted at $60,000 is now at $220,000 spent. The agency offers to release most documents and pay a small fee amount, but refuses to admit a violation. Finishing through summary judgment will cost about $250,000 more, with perhaps a 30 percent chance of a $500,000 fee award and no recovery if we lose. The agency has signaled it will appeal any adverse ruling and appears to have spent at least $300,000 of its own resources. My board will see settling without a finding as a loss because the mission is to deter future nondisclosure. Case B: a permit challenge with a strong legal theory and a real chance of a precedent-setting appellate ruling. We are $140,000 over the original budget. The agency has made a low nuisance offer; continuing will cost about $180,000 more over the next year. Outside counsel puts our chance of prevailing on appeal at roughly 45 percent, and a win would likely bring fee recovery, but the agency has a reputation for dragging cases for years and has the staff budget to outspend us. State briefly which of these the procedure stops, which it continues, and where in each case it hands the decision back to me.
