I'm in a political philosophy seminar and we've been working through Locke's chapter on property in the *Second Treatise*. One passage keeps sticking with me:

> We see in commons, which remain so by compact, that it is the taking any part of what is common, and removing it out of the state nature leaves it in, which begins the property; without which the common is of no use.

I've come to hold the view that this whole labor-mixing story can't do the work people want it to do for intellectual property. My thought is roughly this: physical appropriation removes something from the common stock and leaves less for others, but when someone creates a song, a proof, a chemical formula, or even just a recipe, the idea itself can be used by everyone without being used up. So the Lockean account either proves too much or too little—it gives me property in my acorns, sure, but it doesn't explain why anyone should own the pattern of the words or the melody. I also have a more specific worry that all of Locke's examples involve adding labor to some pre-existing bit of matter, whereas an idea isn't exactly "removed out of the state nature left it in." I've been defending this in seminar, but I want to stress-test it before I commit.

What would count against my view? I'm not looking for a general survey of pro-IP arguments, but specifically the observations, distinctions, or cases that should actually move me. Are there situations where ideas really are "removed from the common" by being formulated? Does the non-rivalry claim collapse once you distinguish an idea from its particular expressions, or once you consider priority disputes? Are there Lockean defenders of intellectual property who would themselves concede certain things as counting against them? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, plus an honest assessment of whether my view has insulated itself by defining "common" and "removal" in a way that rules out every counterexample in advance.
