I'm teaching a course on Renaissance political thought, and we spent a lot of time on Machiavelli's discussion of fortune and virtù in The Prince, especially his claim that fortune governs roughly half of our actions and leaves the rest to us. The bit I can't shake is his image of fortune as a flood that can be contained in advance by building dykes and banks, combined with his insistence that a prince succeeds when his manner suits the times and fails when it doesn't, yet people can't really change their natures. I want to know whether Machiavelli's argument survives this development. Which premise is actually under pressure: the unpredictability of fortune, the claim about fixed natures, or something else? And is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still useful—for example, that residual uncertainty just moves to model risk, or that the relevant virtù is now something like institutional humility rather than boldness?
