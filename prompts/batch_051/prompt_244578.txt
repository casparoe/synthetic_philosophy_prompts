I spent most of my career as a research chemist, and in retirement I've finally had time to read philosophy and comparative religion properly, mostly through a small discussion group at the public library. Last week we were working through some material on whether "religion" is a coherent cross-cultural category—we'd read a bit about Buddhism, Confucianism, and the idea of secular or civil religion—and the conversation kept looping back to the same dead end. Every time someone tried to pin down a definition or taxonomical boundary, one of the old-timers in the group would say, "Buddhism isn't a religion, it's a way of life," as though that settled things. She used it to brush off the observation that plenty of practicing Buddhists describe themselves as religious, to dismiss the idea that "religion" might be doing useful comparative work, and to suggest the whole category is just a confused Western imposition.

I know the line is common, but I've never been sure what it's actually claiming. Could you help me seperate out the distinct precise theses hiding in it? I can dimly see several possibilities: that Buddhism has no creator god, that it is more about practice and discipline than assent to propositions, that it doesn't fit a Christian-shaped model of church membership and creedal belief, that the term "religion" carries parochial baggage from European intellectual history and shouldn't be exported, or simply that her own picture of Buddhism doesn't resemble the religions she grew up around. Are some of these trivially true and others genuinely contestable, maybe false? And which of these readings, if any, would actually support using the line to close down the question whether "religion" is a coherent category? I'd like to bring a menu of readings to the next meeting rather than talking past her again.
