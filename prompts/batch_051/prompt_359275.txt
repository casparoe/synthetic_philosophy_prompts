In my metaphysics course we've been picking apart the old idea that conceivability is a guide to possibility. The basic move, which shows up in Descartes and in a fancier form in the zombie argument, is that if you can clearly conceive of a scenario without uncovering a contradiction, that's at least good evidence the scenario is possible — Descartes even thinks clear and distinct conception is strong enough to prove mind and body are really distinct, because he can conceive mind without body and vice versa. The argument seems to lean on a background assumption that an impossible scenario will put up some kind of resistance when you try to conceive it: you'll notice the contradiction, or the picture will refuse to come together.

But I keep coming back to a worry from outside the readings. Nowadays you can hand an image generator a prompt like a square circle or a door that is entirely open and entirely shut at the same time, and it will produce something that look like a picture of the thing; a text model will happily give you a smooth little explanation of how the door is both open and shut. Nobody thinks those systems have thereby shown that the scenario is possible — they just produce plausible-looking outputs from a description, with no resistance at all. So I'm not sure what to do with the old argument. Does this development actually damage the conceivability-to-possibility step, and if so, which premise does it hit? Or does it just show that the argument never really depended on generating images or fluent descriptions in the first place, so a cleaned-up version survives? I'd like to know whether the argument still goes through, needs repair, or collapses into something weaker.
