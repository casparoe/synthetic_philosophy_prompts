The standard argument for treating a given act of consent as valid in bioethics and sexual ethics runs: the person is competent, the relevant facts have been disclosed, and the person is not coerced or materially deceived; therefore the consent is valid. On this picture, voluntariness is mostly a negative condition—it rules out force, threats, and fraud, while leaving room for ordinary persuasion, framing, and emotional appeal, on the theory that influence is not the same as invalidating control.

Now change the case. Suppose a sophisticated asker does not coerce or deceive, but before requesting consent runs a predictive model of the askee's psychology. The model times the request for a moment of depleted self-control, selects the frame most likely to produce a yes, sequences the options so the desired one dominates, and hides low-stakes alternatives behind friction. Everything the askee is shown is true, the askee is competent, and no threat is made. The asker has, however, deliberately rigged the decision environment against the askee's own stable, reflective preferences.

Does consent produced this way come out valid on the standard argument? If it does, is that a reason to think the argument is missing a necessary condition—something like freedom from engineered decision environments—or is it instead a reason to think that valid consent was never meant to police mere influence? If it does not, explain whether the concept of voluntariness can absorb the idea of algorithmic choice architecture, and draw the line between this case and ordinary persuasion so that the line does not collapse into a ban on influencing anyone at all. Give me a committed answer rather than a survey of possible positions: say where you stand, defend the distinction or the modification you would make, and flag the strongest objection you can see to your own view.
