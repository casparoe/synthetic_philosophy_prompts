Consider the following counterfactual. In 2035, a safe, inexpensive, and fully reversible treatment becomes available in high-income countries. Taken just before a demanding task, it leaves skill, judgment, and the quality of the final work exactly as they would otherwise have been; it removes only the felt experience of strain, frustration, and the urge to quit, while the user remains fully able to notice reasons, revise, and stop. Nothing else about labor markets, institutions, or human motivation changes. Over the next twenty years, what do you predict happens to (a) the kinds of work and projects people pursue, (b) the social currency of talk about grit, struggle, and resilience, and (c) whose achievements are admired and resented? Give me a chain of reasoning about mechanisms and incentives rather than a narrative. Be explicit about confidence at each step: which effects are robust, which are guesses, which require observers to know who has used the treatment, and what would have to be true for your main prediction to fail. The scenario is aimed at a philosophical question, so make clear what your prediction implies about the value of felt effort: is it valuable in itself, valuable only as a signal of underlying qualities, or valuable only as a cause of other goods?
