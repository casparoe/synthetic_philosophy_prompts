I am preparing a handout for a political philosophy seminar in which we spend two weeks on the international happiness rankings and what, if anything, a government should do with them. I would like you to build a decision tree that sorts a respondent into a position, starting from the assumption that the government has reasonably reliable data about the distribution of self-reported life satisfaction among its citizens. Every branch node should be a yes/no question that a third-year undergraduate could answer without already knowing the labels, and each question should isolate a claim on which the positions genuinely divide. The tree should include, in an order you justify, at least the following questions: whether self-reported life satisfaction is what wellbeing consists in or only one fallible indicator of it; whether the state may directly aim at making citizens feel satisfied with their lives or should instead aim at the freedoms, capabilities, and material conditions that make a good life possible; whether the policy target is the population average or the worst-off; and whether annual cross-national rankings create a Goodhart-like pressure to game the indicators. The leaves should include, as named positions where they exist, subjective welfarism about state purpose, a capability view, an objective list view, liberal neutrality, and a skeptical position that the surveys are not cross-culturally comparable; unoccupied combinations should be marked as undefended with a one-sentence diagnosis of why no one seems to occupy them. Please keep the tree to roughly ten to fourteen leaves, flag any pair of positions your questions cannot separate, and explicitly mark where the field is genuinely contested—for example, whether life-satisfaction reports have the cardinal and interpersonally comparable character that averaging requires—so the handout does not present a contested view as the received one.
