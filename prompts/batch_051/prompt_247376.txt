My city just replaced every flat bench at the bus stops on Main Street with a new bench that has a metal armrest in the middle, and they also put little steel studs along the wide concrete ledges outside the transit center. The transportation department says these are purely practical: the armrest helps older people push themselves up, and the studs keep pigeons off the ledge. Nobody will say anything about the fact that every spot they changed is a place where people used to sleep. I don't really care whether they are lying; I care what the practice presupposes. What view of the relation between design and politics does a city commit itself to when it builds public seating that stops a particular use and then insists the object is just a bench? Does the purely-functional story presuppose that an artifact is neutral as long as somebody can give a non-political reason for it? What would show that the armrest has a politics of its own, over and above what the designer intended? And is the city's stance consistent if it elsewhere tries to design public space to shape behavior, like putting lights in an alley to stop loitering? What does the practice strictly presuppose about whether artifacts have politics, and where is it underdetermined between rival views?
