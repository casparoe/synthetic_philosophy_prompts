Act as an expert in the philosophy of law and in mechanism design, and think carefully before you answer. I am a retired natural scientist who has come to philosophy only recently, and I want an analysis that treats the proposal below as a piece of institutional machinery whose behavior can be predicted, not as a slogan to praise or condemn. Consider a jurisdiction that eliminates fixed statutes of limitations and replaces them with the following rule, which I will call the decayed-punishability scheme.

For each defined offense, the penal code specifies a base maximum sentence and a punishment half-life H. If a person is convicted t years after the date of the offense, the maximum sentence that may be imposed is the base maximum multiplied by 2^{-t/H}. Thus theft might have H = 6 years, burglary H = 15 years, homicide H = 40 years, and so on; the legislature may choose any schedule, but the half-life must be shorter for less serious offenses.

The decay clock runs from the date of the offense until the date on which a public charging document naming the defendant is filed. Once charges are filed, the maximum sentence stops decaying. There is no absolute point at which prosecution becomes impossible; however, if the decayed maximum has fallen below a statutory floor, say one month, the prosecution may proceed only with leave of a judge, who is instructed to consider whether the state showed diligence.

If the defendant deliberately prolonged the interval by concealment, fabrication of evidence, intimidation of witnesses, or flight, the sentencing court may add back up to the entire decayed portion, but only on proof to a high standard at a hearing. Delay caused by the state after charges are filed neither increases nor decreases the maximum; defendants may separately seek dismissal or costs for unreasonable post-charge delay.

Treat this not as a moral position but as a mechanism. Tell me how it would actually behave. In particular: what does it incentivize among police and prosecutors regarding speed, thoroughness, early charging, case selection, and the timing of investigation? How would defendants and defense counsel respond: what gaming strategies does it invite, and does the concealment exception close them or create new ones? What happens to victims and witnesses, including their willingness to report and cooperate when they know the punishment is decaying? What second-order effects would appear in plea bargaining, in deterrence, and in the mix of cases charged? Where do you expect the scheme to fail in predictable ways, and are those failures fixable by changing parameters or adding safeguards, or would the fixes reintroduce some form of the arbitrariness that statutes of limitations were meant to avoid? Finally, say whether the scheme succeeds in capturing the idea that the moral significance of an offense decays with time, or whether it simply converts that moral question into a pricing problem.
