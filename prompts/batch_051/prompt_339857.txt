I chair the product ethics and review board at a company that runs a digital legacy service. Users can archive a person's texts, emails, voice notes, and posts, and after a death the service displays a read-only memorial page. Our engineers have now built a prototype — call it an ongoing presence — trained on a deceased person's archived language. It can generate new messages in their style and speak them in a reconstructed version of their voice.

A pilot request has reached the board. A man whose wife died six months ago wants to use the prototype for the benefit of their eight-year-old daughter: answering the child's questions in her mother's voice, inventing new bedtime stories, and offering the small reassurances her mother used to give. He is explicit that he does not want a static replay of old recordings; he wants the system to keep producing new speech as the child grows. His wife left no instructions about her data and, by his own account, was guarded about it — she regularly deleted old messages. The archive he can supply also includes private arguments and unguarded remarks she never intended her daughter to see or hear.

Our board is split. One camp says this is an illusion that will entrench the child's denial and commandeers the dead woman's voice without consent. The other says it is continuous with ordinary memory — like playing a child recordings of her mother or saying what her mother would have said — and that refusing would be paternalistic about how a family grieves.

I'd like you to draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend one of three positions — approve the request, reject it, or approve with specified conditions — and defend that recommendation in terms the rest of us can act on. In particular, try to give the board a workable criterion for when such a system stops re-presenting what a dead person actually said or left behind and starts manufacturing new acts in her name. If you think no such line can be drawn, or that the board is asking the wrong question, say so directly and tell us what question we should be asking instead. Take the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously rather than using it as a foil.
