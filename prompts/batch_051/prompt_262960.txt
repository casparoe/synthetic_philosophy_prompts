Write a "which view of language identity do you actually hold?" sorting quiz for an undergraduate philosophy of language course, built around the question of when Latin became Italian. The quiz should have nine questions, each presenting a concrete case or forced choice. Use these cases, or close structural analogs: a 9th-century scribe who believes he is writing Latin but whose text a modern Romance linguist would call early Old French; a 13th-century Florentine merchant who writes private accounts in the vernacular while calling it volgare; a 16th-century editor who standardizes Tuscan and calls the result Italian; two mutually intelligible speakers on opposite sides of a modern political border whose varieties are counted as different languages; and a 21st-century speaker of a non-standardized Romance variety with no state or literary standard behind it. The questions should probe four distinctions: whether language identity is fixed by internal linguistic structure or by external social facts; whether a language boundary must be sharp; whether continuity of transmission suffices for identity; and whether mutual intelligibility is necessary, sufficient, or neither. Questions must be answerable without knowing the names of the positions, and no question may simply ask respondents which theory they accept; options should be verdicts or actions in the case, not labels.

Provide a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto these positions, plus any further position the answer space requires: (1) sortal essentialism: Latin and Italian are distinct languages with a sharp, if epistemically uncertain, boundary; (2) intelligibility view: boundaries are constituted entirely by mutual intelligibility; (3) political/sociolinguistic view: a boundary exists exactly when standardizing institutions or an authoritative speech community recognizes one; (4) continuist/genealogical view: Italian is simply modern Latin under a different name; (5) speaker-identity view: the decisive fact is what speakers took themselves to be speaking; (6) eliminativist/nominalist view: named languages are fictions and only idiolects and dialect continua are real. Build the scoring key from the logical space of the answers rather than from this list: identify unoccupied answer combinations, and say in one sentence for each whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of positions the questions cannot separate without appealing to explicit self-identification. End with one sentence for each leaf stating its most awkward commitment.
