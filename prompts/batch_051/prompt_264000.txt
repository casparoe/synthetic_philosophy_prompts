Could you give me some comments on this short essay I've been fiddling with for my blog? I'm not an academic or anything, just someone who moved from Ohio to the Netherlands a few years ago and couldnt stop noticing how differently the word "liberal" gets used over here. I'm mostly worried about three things: whether I'm drawing the US/Europe split in a way that actually holds up, whether the "same instinct, different enemy" bit is overblown, and whether the ending lands or just trails off. Here's the draft:

A few years ago I moved from Ohio to the Netherlands, and the first time a Dutch colleague described himself as "liberal" I assumed we'd agree about most things. We did not. To him it meant wanting a smaller state, lower taxes, and being suspicious of big government schemes, which to me sounded almost Republican. But he was also pro-immigration, socially tolerant, and hated the far right, so he didnt map onto my categories either. I got curious and started paying attention to how the word works on each side.

In the US, "liberal" basically names a side in a two-sided fight. It means the person is left of center on the economy, race, climate, and most social issues. It's a coalition label more than a theory. Someone can be a liberal without spending five minutes thinking about, say, John Stuart Mill. It mostly answers the question "which team are you on?"

In Europe, "liberal" is much closer to a temperament or a set of commitments: free markets, free trade, civil liberties, and a deep skepticism of concentrated power, whether that power is the state, the church, or the majority. It cuts across the left-right spectrum in ways that confuse Americans. You can have liberal parties that are economically conservative but socially progressive, and the word still does real work.

The standard explanation is that American liberals kept the name but drifted toward using the state to fix social problems, while European liberals kept the older meaning. I think that's half right but it hides something. My hunch is that both uses come from the same underlying instinct, a fear of unchecked power, aimed at different enemies. In the US, the thing that felt like the real threat to ordinary people was concentrated private power and majoritarian cruelty, so the liberal instinct pointed toward using government as a shield. In Europe, the thing that felt like the real threat was the state itself, plus maybe the church, so the liberal instinct pointed toward limiting government and protecting individual choice. Same reflex, different villain.

If that's right, then a lot of the transatlantic "why can't they see they're not real liberals" stuff is misfiring. The word isn't broken in one place and working in the other; it just gets pointed at whatever power looks biggest from where you're standing. Which maybe means the interesting question isn't what the word really means, but whether that underlying instinct is still a good guide now that the biggest powers don't stay on one side of the Atlantic.

I'm still not sure the split is clean enough, because American liberals clearly also care about civil liberties, and some European liberal parties support at least some safety net. So maybe I've overdone the contrast. Anyway, I'd really value a critical read.
