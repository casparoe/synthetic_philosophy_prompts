Reading group on transitional justice, and the one lawyer in the group keeps deflating the South African TRC by calling it a plea bargain scaled up to a nation. His line is: either way the state trades a lighter or zero sentence for an admission, either way the accused gives up the right to silence, and either way we accept the exchange because a full trial is costly and uncertain. So, he concludes, amnesty-for-truth isn't some exotic compromise of justice, just a thoroughly ordinary piece of criminal procedure, and the people who call it a moral tragedy are romanticizing trials we could never have run anyway. I think this is too quick, but I can't articulate where the analogy gives out.

I'd like a careful audit. Lay out the correspondence table: prosecutor, accused, charges, the bargaining chip, the body that approves the deal, the sentence, the public record, and the victim's role. Then the places the mapping breaks or quietly imports features of a normal plea bargain that the TRC didn't have. Be explicit about which disanalogies matter for his conclusion and which are just cosmetic. Then say whether his conclusion — that amnesty-for-truth is ordinary criminal justice rather than a compromise of it — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if not, either propose a better analogy or say why the TRC might be sui generis. Keep it pitched at a non-lawyer with a philosophy background; I don't need case law.
