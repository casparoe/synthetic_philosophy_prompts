Ok so I've got this thing gnawing at me. I was reading a blog (by a programmer, I think, not a philosopher) and it gave this summary of Gödel:

"Kurt Gödel proved in 1931 that any logical system strong enough to contain ordinary arithmetic will have statements that are true but can't be proved inside the system. He constructed a sentence that basically says 'this statement cannot be proved.' If the system could prove that sentence, the system would be inconsistent; if it can't, then the sentence is true but unprovable. So no fixed set of rules can ever capture all of mathematical truth. That's why Gödel's theorem is normally taken as proof that a computer running on fixed rules could never match a human mathematician, and why any project that tries to make a machine prove theorems by following rules is doomed in principle."

I've seen basically this same paragraph used on forums to argue AI will never do real maths, and our maths teacher sort of shrugged and said 'yep, something like that.' But the leap from 'one sentence can't be proved inside one system' to 'humans beat machines forever' feels dodgy to me, and I don't know enough logic to check it. Can you go through that summary and tell me which bits a logician would say are accurate, which bits are simplified, and which bits are just wrong? And does the conclusion about AI actually follow from the accurate bits, or is it a seperate claim wearing Gödels theorem as a costume?
