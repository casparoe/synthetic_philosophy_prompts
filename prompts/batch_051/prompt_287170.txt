During a reading group I have joined since retiring from a career in the natural sciences, we have been working through the large-scale narratives of human history associated with writers such as Harari and Diamond and the work of their critics. At the most recent session, a recurring one-liner in that literature — that large-scale human cooperation rests on shared fictions — was being used to explain at one stroke the stability of money, law, the corporation, the nation, and organized religion. Half the room regarded the statement as a deep insight; the other half regarded it as verbal sleight-of-hand. I found I could not tell which it was, because I suspect the words "shared," "fiction," and "rests on" are each doing more than one job at once.

I would like a careful disambiguation of the slogan. Lay out the distinct precise theses it could be asserting, organized along lines such as these: whether "fiction" is being used to mean (i) a proposition that is false, (ii) a category whose existence is constituted by collective belief or acceptance, (iii) a rule whose binding force exists only because people treat it as binding, or (iv) merely a representation lacking a simple physical substrate; whether "rests on" is being used in a causal, functional, constitutive, or merely genealogical sense; and whether the claim is about how large-scale cooperation originally arose, how it is maintained, or how it is legitimated. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and say which readings would actually support the different argumentative uses the slogan was being put to — for example, explaining the growth of large states, or debunking the authority of laws and money.

Then, because I gather the slogan has attracted criticism, address whether the familiar objection that much large-scale cooperation rests on shared interests, coercion, or physical infrastructure rather than on any belief at all defeats the slogan or only some readings of it. I would also like to know whether the slogan's rhetorical force depends on sliding between an unobjectionable institutional-fact reading and a much stronger false-belief reading, and which reading is the best reconstruction of the claim that someone who repeats it should be held to. Please indicate how confident you are in the various verdicts and single out the point you are least sure of.
