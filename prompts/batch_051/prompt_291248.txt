I'm a journalist on the science and ideas beat, and lately I can't stop reading coverage of war-crimes and atrocity trials. I keep noticing a move in how those pieces are written. The defendant is described as unremarkable — not a sadist, not an ideologue, someone who liked order and got his reports in on time. The writer pauses to register that this very ordinariness is what makes the case so frightening. So far so familar. But then the same ordinariness starts doing a second, contrary job: he was a tiny man in a vast machine, so the prose goes passive, and his concrete choices quietly drop out of the story. The thing that first deepened the horror ends up dissolving responsibility. I need a sharper carve than the word "ordinary," since one word is doing two incompatible jobs. Does this drift have an established name? I don't mean the general observation that perpetrators are often unremarkable; I mean the specific rhetorical move from "he was disturbingly normal" to "the system did it." If there's no ready name, give me one, with a definition that keeps it distinct from the ordinary-perpetrator observation, from excuse-by-obedience, and from the claim that institutions bear moral responsibility while individual participants merely comply. And I don't just want a label — I want to know exactly where the edges are, and whether the two uses of "ordinary" are really contradictory or only look contradictory because the word is being used in two different senses.
