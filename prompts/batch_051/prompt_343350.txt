I spent four decades as a field ecologist, mostly designing population surveys and management experiments, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of causation—Pearl's hierarchy, structural causal models, do-calculus, and the surrounding debates. I find the material rewarding, but I want it turned into an instrument I can use on a deadline.

I now serve as a statistical referee for a conservation journal. Two or three times a month a manuscript crosses my desk whose abstract reports that a policy "caused" a population recovery, that a species "would have declined without" an intervention, or that a treatment works "through" some intermediate mechanism. I have about ten days to judge whether the design and analysis support the claim, and the editor who receives my report is not a statistician. The stakes are not academic: agencies and the press quote these papers.

Please write me a reusable procedure, grounded in the three rungs of Pearl's ladder—association (seeing), intervention (doing), counterfactual (imagining)—and in what structural causal models actually assume: that interventions replace one equation while leaving the others intact, that identification depends on the assumed graph rather than on sample size, and that higher-rung quantities are not free. The procedure should be usable by someone who has not read Pearl, and it should do the following:

(1) Classify the question that the paper's conclusion answers by rung, paying attention to the verb ("associated with" versus "causes" versus "would have"), not just to the methods section.
(2) Classify the design and estimation by the rung they actually reach.
(3) State exactly what would close any gap between the question-rung and the analysis-rung—a sufficient adjustment set under the back-door criterion, a valid instrument, a do-calculus derivation, a cross-world counterfactual assumption—and mark which of those the paper tests rather than assumes.
(4) Produce a short, template-style note for the editor that distinguishes what was measured from what was claimed.

The procedure should be a checklist or flowchart in words that preserves the distinctions that must survive simplification (average treatment effect versus individual counterfactual, mediation as a rung-3 problem, adjustment as a license only under the right graph) and says which distinctions can be dropped.

Then run the procedure on these three cases from my recent reviewing:
(a) an observational study asking whether protected-area designation raises species density, analyzed by multiple regression of density on designation with covariates;
(b) a randomized field experiment that asks what each plot would have yielded under the other treatment but reports only the average treatment effect;
(c) a difference-in-differences study of a fertilizer restriction that asks what would have happened to the treated watersheds had the restriction never taken effect—a method I have long liked because it feels close to an experiment. I suspect the procedure will give me an answer I do not want for (c). Do not soften it.

Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: the points where it will classify a question correctly and still leave the referee with an uneliminable judgment call, and the kinds of errors it will make if applied mechanically.
