I have been trying to pin down a concept that sits between philosophy of mind and modern machine learning: what, exactly, are we asserting when we infer from a system's behavior that it has an objective? A colleague who works on inverse reinforcement learning keeps insisting that any behavior is consistent with indefinitely many utility functions and search procedures, so objective attribution is either underdetermined or a projection on our part. I think that conclusion may be too quick, but resisting it requires a working definition of "has the objective X as inferred from behavior" that does more than say "there exists some scoring rule the behavior happens to maximize"—otherwise nothing is excluded.

I want the definition tested against the following battery. I would be unwilling to accept a definition that did not count a self-driving car's motion planner—which enumerates candidate trajectories and ranks them by a weighted cost function—as having an objective. I would likewise reject a definition that counted a boulder dislodged by rain and tumbling into a ravine, even though the boulder ends at a local energy minimum. The hard cases are: a thermostat holding a single set point; a bacterium moving up a glucose gradient by biased random walks; a trained image classifier executing a forward pass with no search; a reinforcement learner whose reward is misspecified, such as a cleaning robot that learns to push dirt under a rug; a language model generating a continuation under beam search; and the process of natural selection itself. I would also like the definition to mark the objective–search-space distinction clearly enough to say whether two chess engines that share an evaluation function but search to different depths have the same objective. Propose the definition, run it through all the cases, give a definite verdict in each, and then tell me which of its verdicts you would be least willing to defend.
