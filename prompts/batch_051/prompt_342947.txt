You are a professor of jurisprudence who writes precise, slightly unforgiving teaching handouts. You are preparing a diagnostic exercise for a seminar on the Hart–Fuller debate and its descendants, and the goal is to sort students by their answers to concrete cases, never by asking them to identify with a label.

Produce a decision tree, or equivalently a short quiz with a scoring key, that takes a respondent from their answer to one hard case to a named position on the relationship between law and morality. Work in this order, and show enough of your working that a grader can see the logical space you built.

First, draft 4–6 branching questions. Each question should be a concrete case or a tight claim that a bright undergraduate can answer without knowing the philosophical labels, and each should have 2–3 mutually exclusive answers. The questions must turn on the distinctions that genuinely split the positions: whether a norm's moral iniquity can ever defeat its status as law; whether the criteria of legal validity may themselves incorporate moral standards; whether any necessary connection between law and morality is procedural rather than substantive; and whether the question "what is law?" is separable from the question "what should a judge do with a wicked statute?"

Before attaching any names, lay out the matrix of answer-combinations your questions allow. Only then locate the actual positions in that space. At minimum, the following must appear as leaves:
- exclusive legal positivism (the Razian line);
- inclusive (soft) legal positivism;
- Fuller-style procedural natural law, keyed to something like his eight principles of legality (generality, publicity, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, stability, congruence between announced rules and official action);
- a strong substantive natural-law position of the lex iniusta kind;
- a deflationary position that treats the validity question as the wrong question and says the only clear question is what officials ought to do.

If you classify Hart himself, do so through the tree's questions rather than by fiat, and flag the classification as contested rather than letting the whole instrument depend on it.

Mark at least two answer-combinations that no one actually defends, and say for each whether the cell is empty because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Then flag at least two pairs of leaves that your tree cannot cleanly separate, and state exactly what further question would be needed to separate them. Do not paper over this.

Two test cases should recur across the tree so that consistency of a respondent's answers can be checked:
- a grudge-informer case, described without the historical detail: after a regime falls, a person is prosecuted for having denounced someone to the regime's authorities under statutes then in force, where the denunciation was morally monstrous and the statutes are later regarded as iniquitous;
- a present-day constitutional clause that instructs courts to invalidate legislation that violates human dignity, and that is part of the society's accepted legal practice.

Use these cases to make each branching question concrete. The instrument should occupy a one-page handout: roughly 4–6 branch nodes and 6–8 leaves. Phrase every node so a student can answer it without knowing which position it leads to, and make the scoring instructions trace the pattern of answers to a leaf. Do not use citations, footnotes, or article titles; everything the student needs should be in the cases and the definitions you give.

Begin by sketching the logical space in a few sentences of private notes; then write the finished handout.
