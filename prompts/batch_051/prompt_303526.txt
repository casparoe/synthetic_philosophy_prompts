A parallel I can't make my mind up about. Natural languages are full of homonyms: 'pitch' can be a throw, tar, a musical frequency, a sales talk, and we resolve which sense is meant from context and tone, mostly without noticing. In C++ or Java, the symbol '+' is similarly overloaded: it can be integer addition, float addition, string concatenation, and the compiler resolves which operation is meant by looking at the types of the surrounding expressions. Both systems seem to allow one surface form to carry several distinct meanings and disambiguate it locally at each use. Is there a real structural analogy here, or am I just pattern-matching on the word 'overloaded'? If it's real, does anything transfer? For instance, overload resolution in programming often picks the 'most specific' applicable overload, and when there isn't one the program just fails to compile. Should that predict how speakers pick the 'most natural' reading of a pitch-type homonym, or when ambiguity becomes irreperable and the conversation has to stop and ask 'wait, which pitch do you mean?' Or does the analogy break because a type system is fixed and formal while context and tone are open-ended and negotiated? Please answer in American English.
