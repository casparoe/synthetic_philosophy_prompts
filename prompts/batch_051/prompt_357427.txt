I am taking a graduate seminar on the ethics of consumption, and this week we are reading Henry David Thoreau's Walden alongside some recent work on voluntary simplicity. The seminar handout includes this paragraph summarizing Thoreau's position:

"Thoreau's central claim in Walden is that people waste their lives on work whose only real purpose is to pay for goods they do not genuinely need. The root problem is desire, not social structure: because wanting more than you need is what forces you to work, the remedy is to reduce your wants until they can be satisfied with very little labor. This is why Thoreau lived in a small cabin and practiced a kind of asceticism, deliberately forgoing comforts so that his cravings would weaken. His ideal is individual self-sufficiency: producing what one consumes, avoiding dependence on trade, and stepping back from the social institutions that generate artificial wants. If enough people followed his example, economic activity would contract, but that would be a gain rather than a loss, because real needs would still be met while the rest of our labor would be exposed as pointless."

I reread the chapter "Economy" and now suspect this summary flattens what Thoreau is doing. Here is the passage I keep returning to, from "Economy" in Walden:

"Most men, even in this comparatively free country, through mere ignorance and mistake, are so occupied with the factitious cares and superfluously coarse labors of life that its finer fruits cannot be plucked by them. Their fingers, from excessive toil, are too clumsy and tremble too much for that."

Please act as a philosopher who works on the ethics of consumption and the history of American Transcendentalism, and help me audit the handout. Start by reconstructing what Thoreau is actually claiming in the passage, then go through the summary sentence by sentence: what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what would a careful defender of Thoreau call misleading or overstated? I am especially interested in whether the summary conflates a diagnosis about the hidden cost of certain forms of labor with a psychological prescription about shrinking desire, and whether it turns what is at least partly a social critique into an apolitical personal technique. Use the passage as evidence throughout, and be explicit when the passage does not support something the summary attributes to Thoreau. If my own framing already presupposes a mistaken reading, say so plainly rather than just patching up the summary.
