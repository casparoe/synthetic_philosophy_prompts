In a long scientific career I learned to treat the word infinity as an abbreviation: a statement was acceptable to me only if it could be reformulated as a claim about finite quantities, bounds, or limiting processes, and the rule served me well as a filter against loose talk. Since retiring into philosophy of mathematics, I find myself applying the same policy to debates about actual infinities—Cantor's transfinite hierarchy, Hilbert's Hotel, the question whether the past could be infinite. Recently I dismissed an argument that an actually infinite past is impossible because it moved from the oddities of Hilbert's Hotel straight to a metaphysical conclusion; I notice I was treating completed infinity as having no clear content rather than engaging the argument. Is this policy defensible as a general epistemic rule, and how could I tell from the inside whether I am imposing a sensible discipline or merely protecting my finitist sympathies from challenge?
