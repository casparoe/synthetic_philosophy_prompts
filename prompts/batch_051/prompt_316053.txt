I keep noticing what looks like the same structural move in two arguments that are never discussed together, and I can't decide whether it is a genuine isomorphism or just two infinite regresses sharing the word "self."

The first is Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. As I understand it: you act from the way you are; to be truly responsible for the act, you would have to be truly responsible for the way you are, at least in the mental respects that matter; and to be responsible for the way you are, you would have had to choose to become that way — but that choice would itself have issued from a prior way of being, for which you would again need responsibility. The regress can stop only at an unchosen given self or at a self that somehow brings itself into existence, and neither stop supplies the ultimate responsibility the argument had set out to find. Hence no one is truly morally responsible.

The second is the compiler-trust attack Ken Thompson demonstrated. A compiler binary is altered so that it inserts a backdoor into a login program when compiling it, and also inserts the same self-propagating behavior into any compiler it is used to compile. Afterward the compiler's source can be completely clean; auditing source tells you nothing about what the binary will do, because the binary's behavior when compiling its own successor is exactly the thing under dispute, and every text you can inspect is downstream of the object you need to trust. The only stopping points are arbitrary: trust an unverified bootstrap binary, or rebuild everything from first principles.

The mapping I think I see is: act-to-character-back-to-character-formation corresponds to compile-to-compiler-back-to-bootstrap; the evidence available inside the system is always certified by the very level that needs certifying; and the demand for a non-arbitrary terminus cannot be met from inside. Strawson says no internal self-examination can vouch for the self; Thompson says no internal source audit can vouch for the compiler. Both end in a leap.

But the disanalogies might decide the matter. A compiler is an artifact with a correctness spec, and an outside party could in principle build an honest one from scratch; there is a fact about which binaries are clean. A person has no spec and no external constructor, and "responsible for the way you are" is a normative relation rather than a merely epistemic one. So either Strawson's argument is Thompson's with the escape hatch removed — and that is exactly what makes true responsibility impossible — or Strawson's regress is generated by an illicit demand (that you be responsible for your own constitution) that has no analogue in Thompson's scenario, where nobody concludes that building software is impossible just because no one can be "responsible for the compiler."

What I want to know is whether this parallel is deep or superficial. If Thompson's regress can only be stopped by importing trust from outside, does that strengthen the Basic Argument, since the self has no outside? Or does the Thompson case show what a regress looks like when no normative condition is in play, and thereby expose the premise that responsibility for an act requires responsibility for the way you are as doing the work illicitly? Where does the mapping match, where does it come apart, and does anything transfer from a solution or non-solution on one side to the other?
