Exam question:

Explain the difference between the Great Man theory of history and structural explanation. Then use that distinction to assess the claim that, without Hitler, there would have been no Nazi Germany. Treat separately (a) the Nazi seizure of power in 1933, (b) the general character of the regime after 1933, and (c) the Holocaust, and say whether your verdict is the same for each.

Student answer:

The Great Man theory says that the main driver of historical events is the character and decisions of exceptional individuals, while structural explanation says that the deeper causes are impersonal — institutions, economic conditions, the distribution of power — and that individual leaders are largely interchangeable carriers of possibilities the structures have already created. The Nazi case is a useful test because the two kinds of explanation seem to divide it between them.

For the seizure of power, the structural side looks much stronger. Weimar was already fragile before Hitler: the Versailles settlement, hyperinflation, then the Depression, the absence of a solid democratic consensus, and the willingness of the conservative elites and Hindenburg's circle to hand power to a populist right-winger all made some form of authoritarian nationalist outcome highly likely. A pure Great Man account of the takeover, as though Hitler conjured the crisis out of nothing, is implausible. What Hitler's individual contribution supplied in this part of the story was tactical: exploiting opportunities that the structures had left open.

Once we ask about the specific character of the regime and its crimes, however, the individual becomes harder to replace. Without Hitler, Germany in the 1930s would probably still have fallen under some right-wing dictatorship, but not one with this specific ideological program: no racial hierarchy as the organizing principle of the state, no drive for living space in the east, and, most importantly, no Holocaust understood as a systematic, industrial, continent-wide extermination of the Jews. The structural preconditions explain why a far-right regime was available; they do not by themselves explain why the regime took this particular path rather than another. This is why the intentionalist school is right to keep Hitler's ideological obsessions at the center of the explanation. What the functionalists describe as the cumulative radicalization of competing bureaucracies should therefore be treated not as a rival explanation but as the machinery through which Hitler's program was realized: the direction came from the top, while the bureaucracy supplied the improvisation and the rivalries.

One has to be careful not to overcorrect. The fact that the Depression and the miscalculations of the conservative elites were necessary for the Nazi takeover does not show that individuals drop out of the explanation; necessary conditions and sufficient causes are different, and an overdetermined outcome is not the same as an inevitable one. By the same token, the fact that Hitler was necessary for the particular outcome does not, all by itself, vindicate the Great Man theory. But it does show that the structuralist program has a gap: it cannot say why one of its available paths was taken.

On balance, structural explanation accounts for the general shape of the outcome, while attention to Hitler accounts for its specific content. The answer to the counterfactual question is therefore split: the seizure of a far-right dictatorship in Germany without Hitler is extremely likely, but a Nazi Germany in the full sense — and certainly the Holocaust — is not. So the Great Man theory is not right about everything, but it is rehabilitated in a restricted form: at decisive junctures, which individual occupies the crucial position determines which structural possibility becomes actual.
