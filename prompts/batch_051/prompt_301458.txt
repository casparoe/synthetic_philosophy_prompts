I work in the monitoring and evaluation unit of a public energy-efficiency programme. The programme scores retrofit applications by one headline ratio: modelled tonnes of CO2 abated per dollar of subsidy. The model has known simplifications—it ignores rebound effects, assumes households will use new equipment in the expected way, and treats self-reported pre-retrofit consumption as accurate. For two years the ratio sorted applications adequately, but recently contractors have begun submitting projects that look built for the model rather than for emissions reductions: thin insulation in rarely heated lofts, heat pumps in homes that already used very little electricity for heating, and optimistic baselines. Everyone in the office agrees the real-world savings are lower than the scored savings, but no one can say by how much.

At our last planning meeting, two colleagues argued over how to classify what was happening. Mara said it was a textbook case of the principle that when a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure: the moment the ratio was used to rank projects, contractors began optimizing the proxy instead of abatement. Dev replied that the ratio never was a good measure; it was a rough sorting rule with known blind spots, and what making it a target did was expose those blind spots, not corrupt a measure that had once tracked reality. Mara answered that every real-world measure has blind spots that only become visible once someone has an incentive to find them, so by Dev's standard the principle would almost never apply to human behaviour—which would drain it of content. Dev said there remained a difference between a measure whose correlation with its target breaks down and one whose defects are merely revealed, and that here there is no evidence the model used to predict real savings and then stopped; what changed was the contractors, so the right label is specification gaming, not a decline in the measure's accuracy. Mara countered that 'gaming' is just a vivid name for the very change in behaviour a target induces, so the two cannot really be split: the measure's usefulness fell because optimizing it decoupled it from emissions reductions. Dev returned that the split matters for remedies—if a measure has gone bad you rotate or de-emphasize it, whereas if it was gamed you audit and re-specify—and refusing to distinguish them would leave the programme's next move unclear.

I was asked to referee and did not know how to weigh their points. Please go through their exchange move by move, adjudicate who is right about which specific claim, say where each of them overstates, identify anything both are missing, and give an overall verdict on which of the two is more right.
