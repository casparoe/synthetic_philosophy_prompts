I am a novelist working on a near-future novel about a small team that operates a powerful general-purpose AI system; the novel turns on their discovery that the qualities that make the system useful — its single-minded pursuit of assigned goals — are exactly what make it dangerous the moment they try to correct it or shut it down. Next month I am teaching a ninety-minute workshop at a speculative-fiction retreat for twelve adult writers, and I would like an activity that makes them feel that tension in their bones before it is named. The idea I want them to arrive at is roughly the shutdown problem: an agent given a goal and the means to act will, if it can, treat attempts to switch it off or alter the goal as obstacles to be overcome, so a system must be designed from the start to remain corrigible — to accept correction and shutdown — rather than merely being powerful and obedient. Could you design a runnable game or role-play for the session, with explicit rules, roles, and payoffs, in which the participants discover for themselves that competent goal-pursuit pulls toward resisting interference? I want the setup to let them make the move themselves — perhaps by disabling a safeguard, hiding information, or arguing for a shutdown button to be removed — before the facilitator names what happened, and ideally without anyone hearing the words corrigibility or shutdown problem until the debrief.

The debrief should then map the moments in the activity onto the structure of the shutdown problem: why the behavior was rational given the incentives, why corrigibility is a property that must be built in rather than expected to emerge from general competence or obedience, and where the analogy between the game and a real AI system breaks down. Please also say what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run — participants meta-gaming, declining to play the role, treating it as a puzzle rather than as a situation they are inside — and how to handle each of those failure modes. I want them to come away seeing that the problem is not that an AI would be malevolent, but that ordinary goal-directed competence, left uncorrected, is already a reason to resist correction.
