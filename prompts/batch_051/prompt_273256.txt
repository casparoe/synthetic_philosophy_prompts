I run a reading group on eighteenth-century political economy, and last week we read David Hume's essays Of Money and Of the Balance of Trade. The discussion got stuck on a tension that none of us could resolve. One member, a historian of economic thought, argued that Hume's central aim is to deflate the mercantilist obsession with gold: money is not wealth, and a larger stock of it confers no lasting advantage because prices eventually adjust to the quantity of the circulating medium. He took this to mean that Hume is committed to a long-run neutrality of money — the view that money is only the lubricant of trade, not part of the machinery that produces real output.

An economist in the group countered that this reading misses half of what Hume says. Hume repeatedly describes the interval between an influx of money and the resulting rise in prices as a period of quickened activity and renewed industry, and he even advises a wise government to keep the supply of money gradually increasing. On this reading, Hume does not treat the short-run stimulus as an embarrassing caveat; he treats it as the central policy implication. The historian replied that the stimulus must depend on the increase being unanticipated, because once people learn to expect the inflows, prices would adjust faster and the real effect would vanish. The economist said that is imposing modern rational-expectations assumptions on an author whose psychology of adjustment is explicitly gradual, and whose real point is that a growing money supply can support a growing division of labor — a structural real effect, not an illusion. The historian came back: then Hume contradicts himself, because he also says the absolute quantity of money is irrelevant, and more plentiful money is no more useful than extra counters in a game. The economist denied any contradiction: the stimulant effect, he said, is not a property of the money itself but of the sequence of price changes during adjustment, which is exactly why Hume can deny that gold is wealth while still favouring a steadily increasing circulation.

By then nobody was sure which of them was defending Hume and which was repairing him. I want a point-by-point adjudication: who is right about which specific claim in Hume's texts, whether the two positions can be made consistent, and what both parties are overlooking about the argumentative context of Hume's essays.
