I am a novelist. In the novel I am writing, a central scene has an evolutionary biologist explain to a room of laypeople how a heritable variant can become more common while making the individuals who carry it worse off and weakening the population they belong to. I need a thought experiment I can put in her mouth: a concrete case, realistic enough that a careful reader cannot dismiss it as science fiction, whose logic she can build aloud step by step so the audience feels the gene-level account doing real explanatory work rather than merely relabeling the same facts.

Construct that case to the following specifications. It should involve ordinary sexual reproduction in a plausible terrestrial species, with no invented genetics and no speculative biology. The gene-level account should predict that the variant spreads, while an organism-level account and a group-selection account should each predict that selection eliminates it. The gene-level advantage must arise within a single generation, in the mechanics of gamete formation, not in the carriers' lifetime survival or fecundity; otherwise the case would not separate the levels of selection and the scene would lose its point. The harm to the organism and to the group must be concrete and narratable within a single spoken paragraph rather than a list of parameter values.

After the scenario, walk through what each of the three views says about it and why, identify the structural feature that is doing the real work, say what the case collapses into if that feature is removed, and explain how a defender of the organism-level view would attack the gene-level verdict as question-begging. Reply as a numbered list of exactly seven items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
