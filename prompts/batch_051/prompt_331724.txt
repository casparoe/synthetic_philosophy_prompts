I host a small bioethics discussion group, and last week someone brought along a bill her state legislature is floating; we couldn't stop argueing about it. Right now the state uses the usual two-pronged death standard: a person is dead when either their circulation and respiration have irreversibly stopped, or all functions of the entire brain have irreversibly stopped. The bill adds an individual opt-out. Any competent adult can file a notarized 'circulatory-only' declaration saying that, for them, only the loss of circulation and breathing counts as death. If someone with such a declaration on file is later diagnosed brain dead, the hospital can't declare them dead or withdraw the ventilator untill their heart stops on its own. The one exception: the family can consent to withdrawing support if they also consent, in the same conversation, to donation after circulatory determination of death under the hospital's DCD protocol. Then the kicker: filing the declaration moves you down one category on every transplant waiting list for life. The drafters justify it as reciprocity—a person who has declared that their organs could, at best, be recovered after circulatory death has declared themself a worse potential donor, so they receive lower priority.

I'm supposed to write something on how this would actually play out if it passed. Walk me through it. Who signs and who doesn't, and how does the existence of the form change bedside negotiations between ICU staff and the families of brain-dead patients? What do transplant centres, ICU administrators, and insurers start doing within a year, and which group's behaviour changes first? Where does the scheme get gamed first? Finally, try to patch the worst failure without simply forcing everyone back onto the neurological standard.
