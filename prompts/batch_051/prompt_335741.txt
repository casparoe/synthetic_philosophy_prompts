Having spent my career in the physical sciences, I am used to concepts that earn their keep by providing something close to a decision procedure, and I find the term "corruption" in debates about money in politics frustratingly resistant to one. I can recognize clear cases and clear non-cases, but I cannot state a working definition of political corruption that survives contact with the intermediate ones. Please propose such a definition—or two, if you think the word is doing two jobs at once—and test it against these cases: a senator accepting cash on the condition that she vote a particular way on a bill before her committee; a retired citizen sending thirty dollars to a candidate whose views she shares; a billionaire funding a super PAC supporting a candidate he has never contacted, whose proposed tax change would save him far more than he gave; a trade association's lobbyist drafting legislative text the association favors while its political committee gives the legal maximum to the committee chair; a mayor's office telling a permit applicant that the permit will be processed once the applicant contributes to a charity the mayor controls; a regulator who voted to approve a merger joining the board of the merged firm shortly after leaving office; and a family foundation endowing a university chair named for a sitting appropriations committee chair who oversees the university's federal research funding. I am confident the first case is corrupt and the second is not; my views on the rest shift from week to week. I also suspect the word blurs together an idea about explicit exchange and an idea about whose interests get access to officials. Tell me which of your verdicts is least secure, and whether any case pushes you to revise the definition rather than defend the verdict.
