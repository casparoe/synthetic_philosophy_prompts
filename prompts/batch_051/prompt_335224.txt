I write about science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and I'm working on an essay about why the phrase "state of nature" gets thrown around so much — every time the power goes out, or a platform's moderation collapses, someone says we're seeing Hobbes for real. I want to move past that cliché by doing one careful reading: Lord of the Flies, the novel, as a test case for Hobbes's actual contract story, not the bumper-sticker version.

Here's the thing I can't stop thinking about. Hobbes says covenants without a power to enforce them are just words, but he also says the way out of the state of nature is to covenant to set up a sovereign. So the cure has to be administered by the disease, and I suspect the novel is precisely about that bootstraping problem. Ralph's camp creates rules, assemblies, a conch, and a signal fire — all covenant and no sword. Jack's band has the spear, the chant, and the hunters — all sword and no covenant. Neither, on Hobbes's own terms, looks like a commonwealth. And it gets better: Hobbes distinguishes a commonwealth by institution, made by covenant, from a commonwealth by acquisition, submitted to out of fear — and the novel might be arguing, against Hobbes's preference, that its actually only the acquisition kind that's stable under pressure.

So the piece I'm drafting asks: does Lord of the Flies show that the social contract has an unavoidable chicken-and-egg problem, or does it only show that the boys get the contract wrong in ways Hobbes already had a diagnosis for? I want a reading that (a) stays close to the actual novel — Piggy, the conch, the beast, the death of Simon, the ending with the naval officer and the adult war — and doesn't reduce it to a diagram; (b) gets the Hobbes side right, including the point that the sovereign is authorized by the subjects but isn't a party to their covenant; (c) is willing to say where the novel pushes back on the theory, rather than just illustrating it; and (d) ends by telling me what this adds to my essay that quoting Hobbes directly wouldn't.

Keep it under 900 words, and write it as if it's going to run as a magazine essay, not a term paper. And no spoiler warnings.
