A friend and I were discussing Aquinas's treatment of tyrannicide in De Regno, where he considers whether it is lawful to kill a tyrant. Aquinas argues there that if a community has the right to choose its own king, it may rightly depose him or restrict his power when he abuses it, because he has failed in his office; but he also says it is dangerous and contrary to apostolic teaching for a private person to kill a tyrant on his own authority, and that if there is no human recourse, the people should turn to God for deliverance. My friend and I then argued about what exactly this view commits him to.

She said the distinction doing the work is between public and private judgment. A community acting through its established authority can depose a tyrant because the decision is made by an organ whose function is to represent the whole, so even if some citizens disagree about whether the ruler is a tyrant, the act has a coordinating legitimacy that a private assassination lacks. The problem with private tyrannicide isn't that individuals might be wrong about the facts, but that it would let anyone act on their own say-so, dissolving the very rule of law they claim to defend.

He replied that this just pushes the question back: someone in the community still has to judge that the ruler has become a tyrant, and if the tyrant controls the institutions through which the community would act, the public/private distinction is useless exactly when it's needed. He added that Aquinas's own fallback to divine intervention seems to concede this, because it admits there can be situations where the rightful human remedy is unavailable—yet if we accept that, why is a private act never the right response to a truly unbearable tyranny? It seems like either the community's authority is always recoverable through some institutional channel, in which case the divine fallback is unnecessary, or it isn't, in which case the ban on private action needs a further justification.

She came back by saying the fallback to divine intervention isn't a political theory but a limit on political theory: Aquinas isn't claiming God will reliably intervene; he's saying that where no legitimate human authority can act, there may be no just political remedy, only the hope of one. That doesn't undermine the public/private distinction; it just means the distinction sometimes leaves people without a just option, which is tragic but not incoherent.

He said that's an unstable position: if tyranny can reach a point where the right response is to endure and hope, then the theory has no account of why the wrongness of private tyrannicide remains absolute, since it's now being weighed against nothing but a hope. And if the wrongness is meant to be a matter of the common good—avoiding civil war—then it should be sensitive to the expected consequences, which might in rare cases favor a private act.

I honestly couldn't adjudicate this. Please sort out who is right about which points, and say who is more right overall.
