I spent my working life in atomic spectroscopy, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy with the habit I used to bring to a difficult spectrum: I want criteria precise enough to fail. A question that used to sit at the back of the lab now sits at the front of my reading: in what sense are two electrons in a helium atom really two things, rather than one system with a doubled description?

I have arrived at a position I call the Exclusion Criterion of Particleness. Its central claim is that a thing is a particle of a kind — a genuine individual of that kind — only to the extent that its presence excludes other things of the same kind from some state they would otherwise be able to occupy. Individuality, on this view, is not carried by a label or by a set of identifying properties; it is carried by what a particle forbids. Two electrons in a helium atom, one assigned to the 1s orbital and one to the 2s, are two individuals because the antisymmetrized wavefunction would vanish if both were assigned exactly the same single-particle state; the exclusion principle, not the label, is doing the individuating. That the wavefunction changes sign when the labels are swapped while the physics does not no longer bothers me, because the label was never the individuator. By contrast, two photons in the same cavity mode are not two individuals by this criterion; the statement that there are two of them reports an occupation number, not a count of separate things. A Bose-Einstein condensate is, on my view, one mode with a very large occupation number rather than a swarm of N individual atoms.

The attraction, to my eye, is that this dissolves the problem of indiscernibility for fermions: two fermions can be exactly alike in every property and yet distinct, because their distinctness is a matter of exclusion from states rather than of discernibility. Bosons get exactly the reduced status that the formalism seems to want them to have — countable excitations of a field rather than objects.

I have read enough to know that this is disputed terrain, and that some philosophers of physics defend a position called weak discernibility as a way of keeping particles as individuals; I am not sure whether my criterion collapses into that position, or into the field-excitation reading of bosons, or whether it stands apart.

I would like an honest assessment, not encouragement. Specifically: Is the criterion coherent, and does it actually deliver the fermion-boson asymmetry I claim, or does it beg the question somewhere? Where does it break — what states or kinds of particles does it count wrongly? What is the nearest existing position, and where exactly does mine differ from it? Give me the two or three strongest objections and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. Finally, since I am new to the philosophical literature, please separate what is settled physics in what I have said from what is interpretive extrapolation, and do not present a contested reading among philosophers of physics as though it were the consensus.
