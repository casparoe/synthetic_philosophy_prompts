I recently encountered an argument that moves from the finiteness of the mind to the conclusion that an immortal life must eventually become unbearable, and I cannot tell whether the argument is sound or, if it fails, exactly where it fails.

The argument runs as follows. The mind is a finite system, and any finite system has only finitely many complete internal states. So the number of distinct total experiences available to me is finite. Consequently, if I lived forever, after some finite stretch I would have had every experience it is possible for me to have; everything after that would be a rerun of experiences I have already undergone. And a life that is, from some point on, nothing but reruns would be tedious or meaningless. Therefore immortality would not merely be difficult; it would be necessarily intolerable. The only open question would be how long the process takes.

I am suspicious of the move from the mind's being finite to experience's being exhaustible, but I cannot state the fallacy precisely, and I am also unsure how much of the underlying worry about immortality survives once the fallacy is removed. Is the argument sound? If not, state the mistake as precisely as you can, and then say what remains of the thought that an endless life must collapse into repetition. Does an infinite life need infinitely many novel experiences to be worth living, or can a finite palette of experiences, combined and re-encountered in different contexts and proportions, sustain it? And is the real difficulty perhaps not the exhaustibility of experience at all, but something about how desire and identity work over an open-ended life?
