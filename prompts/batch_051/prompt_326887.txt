I'm in a children's rights seminar and we read Feinberg on the child's right to an open future, and I keep getting stuck on the same point. As I understand the idea, kids have an interest in growing up into adults who get to author their own lives, so adults shouldn't foreclose the future the kid might want. But if you actually take that seriously, an enormous amount of perfectly ordinary parenting starts to look like a rights violation. Raising a kid inside a strict religious community, sure—that shuts off a lot of possible adult selves. But so does putting a six-year-old in elite gymnastics. Or the tiger mom whose kid practices violin three hours a day. Even raising a kid monolingual in a context where a second language would have opened doors seems to close some options. So why wouldn't the right to an open future require something like maximally neutral, option-preserving child-rearing—expose the kid to lots of belief systems and activities, no intensive specialization, no foreclosing any future the kid might choose—backed by some kind of parental licensing to enforce it? Everyone who defends the right seems to want to stop way short of that conclusion. I honestly can't see how you draw the line between protecting the kid's open future and just leaving the choice up to the parents with out making the principle not do any work. Is the right to an open future basically a right to have the maximum number of options left open, and are its defenders just unwilling to follow the argument where it goes? Or what am I misunderstanding?
