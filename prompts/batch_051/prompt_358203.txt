I am a PhD student in philosophy of religion working on late antique accounts of divine knowledge and their implications for temporal agency, and I have been unable to decide whether a parallel I keep noticing is deep or is just my own pattern-matching. I would be grateful for a careful assessment.

The parallel is between Boethius’s solution to the foreknowledge problem in Book V of the Consolation of Philosophy and the eternalist, or B-theoretic, picture of time in contemporary metaphysics. Boethius’s move, as I read it, is to deny that God’s cognitive access to what I call the future is temporally located at all. God does not literally foreknow, on this view, because a being whose life is possessed whole and at once has no future. From that atemporal standpoint the events of my tomorrow are not yet-to-come; they are already included in a completed totality. What God sees is thereby fixed, but Boethius insists the fixity is compatible with my acting freely, because the only necessity involved is conditional — given that God sees that I shall do such-and-such, it must be the case that I shall do it — and not a simple necessity inhering in the act itself.

For quite different reasons, the eternalist about time also denies that the future is not yet there. All times are equally real; the distinction among past, present, and future is a matter of perspective within the block, not a distinction in what exists. The block does not, by itself, say that future events are causally determined; it says only that they are already part of the world. Yet a familiar worry follows: if tomorrow’s action is already written into the block, then in what sense can I do otherwise?

So the structural parallel I keep returning to is this. Each position dissolves a threat of fatalism or modal collapse about the future by relocating the threatening already from the temporal order into something atemporal — in the one case, the standpoint of an eternal knower; in the other, the tenseless ontology itself. And each position then owes an account of why the fixity of the future, so relocated, does not amount to the necessity of the future.

I would like to know whether this is a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion. Does Boethius’s distinction between God’s atemporal cognition and our temporal agency have a live counterpart in the eternalist’s distinction between the tenseless block and the ability of agents in it to deliberate? Or does the analogy break because Boethius is making a claim about one exceptional being’s mode of existence, while eternalism makes a claim about everything, including the agents themselves? Is there anything that would transfer from one debate to the other, such as an objection to one being a limiting case of an objection to the other? And is there a point where the parallel predicts something false?

1. The mapping made precise — what plays the role of what on each side, stated as exactly as you can (one substantial paragraph).
2. Where the structures genuinely match (one or two paragraphs).
3. Where they come apart, including at least one thing the parallel would predict that looks false (one or two paragraphs).
4. Verdict — deep, superficial, or somewhere between, and why (one paragraph).
5. Confidence — how confident you are in the points above, and the single claim you are least sure of (a short paragraph).
