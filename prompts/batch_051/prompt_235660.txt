For Thursday's seminar, here's the puzzle, and I want you to actually work through the readings rather than give me your gut reaction. Suppose a jurisdiction has the following sacrilege provision:

"Any person who, in a public place and with the intention of insulting the religious feelings of a group, burns, damages, defiles, or publicly desecrates any object which that group holds sacred commits an offence."

Now the case. A provocateur art collective installs an augmented-reality piece in the plaza outside the main synagogue. Point your phone at the plaza and a photorealistic image of a Torah scroll appears floating above the steps; with a swipe you can set it alight, tear it apart, or throw it into a puddle of mud. None of that happens physically. The actual scroll is untouched in the ark inside the building, and the app is plainly labelled as a simulation. The collective's public statements leave no real doubt that they mean to insult the congregation's religious feelings. The drafting history shows the legislature was worried about vandals physically damaging sacred objects in churches and mosques; nobody was thinking about simulated objects or headsets.

Does the provision reach the collective? Does it reach the visitor who sets the virtual scroll alight? Work through four interpretive approaches seperately: the plain words, the purpose of the provision, what the drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable member of the congregation would take the words to cover. Say where they come apart and why. Then be honest about wich feature of the case is doing the real work — I'd say it's the non-physicality of the image — and say what happens if we change just that feature: the phone vibrates a little when the virtual flame lands, or the collective projects the image onto the synagogue wall, or a user physically scrawls an insult over a printed photograph of the scroll. End with your best argument for and against the idea that a coherent reading of the words reaches a purely virtual desecration.
