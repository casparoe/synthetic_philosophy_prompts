I have no academic training in economics or political theory, but I have spent a good deal of my spare time reading about prediction markets and about a proposal I have seen called futarchy, which I will describe rather than cite. As I understand it, the proposal runs roughly as follows: a society agrees on a measure, or a small set of measures, of national welfare; for any proposed policy, traders bet on how the measure will behave under that policy; and the policy that the market expects to produce the best measured outcome is adopted. The appeal is that it turns political choice into a forecasting problem, and it gives people with private information a financial incentive to say what they actually expect rather than what sounds persuasive.

I want to test this idea against a specific real case: the United States' decision to invade Iraq in 2003. The salient facts, as I understand them, are these. In the year or so before the invasion, the administration's public case for war rested on strong claims that Iraq had active weapons of mass destruction programs and might make such weapons available to terrorists; the claims were presented as much more certain than the underlying intelligence supported. United Nations inspectors had returned to Iraq and had not found the alleged programs in the months before the invasion. Congress authorized the use of force; the invasion began in March 2003; no active WMD programs were found afterward; and the war was followed by a long insurgency, large civilian and military casualties, and costs far above the official forecasts. I am not asking whether the invasion was justified. I am asking what the decision looks like when reconstructed through the futarchy framework.

To do this, I want a disciplined mapping, not a general reflection. Specify the policy options futarchy would have had to choose among, and be explicit about the simplification this requires. Choose a welfare metric that a futarchy would plausibly have used, and then show how much the answer depends on that choice: whose welfare counts, over what time horizon, and how Iraqi and American interests get weighed. Identify the conditional contracts traders would have been pricing, and say what kinds of private information, inside the intelligence agencies, the inspections process, the military, or neighboring governments, could plausibly have moved prices away from the public consensus that actually formed. Then say what the futarchic procedure would most likely have selected, and where it would have corrected the actual decision's errors, left them untouched, or introduced errors of its own. Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: say what this episode reveals about futarchy itself, especially about the difference between forecasting an observable state of the world and choosing a policy whose consequences depend on a counterfactual that will never be observed. Be honest about where the framework is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
