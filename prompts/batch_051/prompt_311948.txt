So a friend and I argued for like an hour last night about the void-for-vagueness doctrine, and I still cant shake the feeling that neither of us had it right. The practice is this: courts throw out criminal statutes as unconstitutional when they're too vague, on the grounds that people need fair notice of what's prohibited and that cops and prosecutors shouldn't be free to enforce the law however they like. A law against annoying passersby gets struck down, but laws that hinge on whether force was "reasonable" or a statement was "dishonest" or activity was in "the public interest" get upheld all the time, even though those words look just as open-ended to me. My friend says that's straightforward hypocrisy: the system talks as if statutes can have clear edges and then turns around and admits they can't. I think that's too quick, but I couldn't say what the practice actually presupposes about what a law is, or about meaning, or about how rules apply to cases at all. Can you reconstruct the implicit view of language or rule-following that makes it coherent to strike down a vague statute while upholding a standard built on "reasonable"? Does the doctrine strictly presuppose that general terms have determinate boundaries that ordinary people can locate, or is it compatible with the view that every term is open-textured at the edges? Suppose the open-texture view is right: does the fair-notice rationale just collapse, or does the doctrine get refocused on preventing arbitrary enforcement instead? And what would a court that actually believed rules never apply themselves have to change in how it does vagueness review? I'd also like a sense of where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, not just what it strictly requires.
