I keep going back and forth on when a long-run prediction gets to count as "right but early" rather than just false or unfalsifiable, and I want to know whether there's one principle behind my gut calls. Here are the verdicts I actually hold.

I don't think Malthus was simply wrong. The core mechanism — population can outrun subsistence and get checked — seems importantly true, even though the two centuries after him saw population explode and food per person rise rather than the permanent misery he expected. I'd say the mechanism was real but he badly underestimated how much the ceiling could move.

I don't give Ehrlich the same pass. The specific claim that hundreds of millions would starve in the 1970s and 80s turned out false, and moving the date to 2030 feels like an ad hoc rescue, not like Malthus being early.

I also think the claim that on a finite planet exponential growth of any physical quantity can't go on forever, so current rates must hit limits within a few centuries, is true but somehow not a prediction at all: no mechanism, no date, so it can't be early, late, or on time. But I can't fully articulate why.

And here's the one that bothers me most: a peak-oil person says global production peaks by 2005. It doesn't. Then they say fracking just delayed the geological limit, which is still there. I lean toward calling that unfalsifiable, but I can't see the difference between that and my defending Malthus in the first case.

Is there one principle that captures all these verdicts, and which of them does it force me to drop? State it precisely, with the case that does most of the work for each clause, and apply it to a case I haven't decided: someone predicts today, from a demographic model, that world population peaks around 2080 and then declines without famine. Finally, tell me what would change your mind — which of my verdicts is doing the least work, or where the principle you're offering is most likely to break.
