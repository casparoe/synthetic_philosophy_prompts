A friend who teaches a metaphysics seminar has spent the term drilling her students on disposition ascriptions—what a thing is disposed to do rather than what it happens to be doing or would happen to do. She has committed herself in class to all of the following verdicts:

- A glass packed in thick foam is still fragile; the foam merely masks the fragility—it leaves the glass fragile while blocking the breaking.
- A "sorcerer's glass"—a glass that a waiting sorcerer would instantaneously turn to steel just before it was struck, so that it would not break—is still fragile; the sorcerer is a fink, something that would remove the disposition just when its trigger arrives, rather than an absence of fragility.
- A match sealed inside a vacuum chamber is still flammable.
- A sugar cube dropped into water already saturated with sugar is still water-soluble.
- A key bent so badly that it no longer turns its lock is not disposed to open that lock.

These rulings show she rejects the simple conditional analysis—the view that a thing is disposed to M when S just in case it would M if S. But at least two more sophisticated rules fit everything she has said:

(i) A disposition is an intrinsic feature of the thing itself, specifiable without essential reference to any particular external object, which would produce the manifestation if the thing were suitably stimulated in the absence of finks and masks.

(ii) A disposition is what the thing would do in its normal or proper circumstances, with interference set aside, even if the thing's intrinsic makeup alone does not guarantee that outcome.

Here are the new cases I would like you to predict, on the strength of her track record:

(a) An ordinary, unbent key that exactly fits one particular lock. Will she say the key itself is disposed to open that lock, or will she say that only the key-plus-lock system has the disposition?

(b) A mutated allele that causes a metabolic disease only when the bearer ingests a rare plant protein that almost nobody eats. Is the allele itself disposed to cause the disease?

I care less about the bare verdicts than about the rule behind them. Please identify the candidate rules that fit her earlier verdicts; say where they diverge on (a) and (b); give your best guess about what she would say and why you prefer that reading; and name one further case, not on the list, that would most cleanly separate the remaining candidate rules. Finally, say what would change your mind about the best-guess reading—which argument or consideration would lead you to a different overall verdict, or where your reconstruction of her rule is most likely to fail.
