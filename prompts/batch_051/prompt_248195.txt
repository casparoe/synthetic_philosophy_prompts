The trainer was adamant that an technically true statement can still get us in trouble with securities regulators: if we leave out a material fact that would make what we did say misleading, or if we phrase things so a reasonable investor predictably takes away a false impression, the literal truth of the words won't save us. Fair enough. But then I went down a rabbit hole and learned that US perjury law seems organized around the opposite idea. A witness who gives a literally true answer to a question can't be convicted of perjury, even if the answer was crafted deliberately to mislead the questioner — courts have a name for this, the literal truth defense. So the law punishes true statements that mislead in one setting, and gives them a free pass in another. That can't both be right... can it?
