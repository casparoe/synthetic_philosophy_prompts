An alphabetic orthography like English's is an existing practice with a theory of language latent in its design: a small closed set of letters is treated as a notation for the phonemes (the contrastive sounds) of the spoken language, so 'cat' and 'bat' are written as differing by a single sound. Yet the same orthography omits a great deal of the speech signal—intonation, most regional variation, and details of pronunciation such as the puff of air on the p in 'pin' but not in 'spin'—and English in particular preserves material that is not pronounced in the word itself, as in sign/signature, or spells the plural ending as s in both cats and dogs even though the ending is pronounced differently in the two. The result is a system that is phonemic in its organizing principle but morphophonemic (tracking a word's abstract form across related words) and partly etymological in much of its actual practice. What theory of language, if any, does a system of this kind presuppose—about the units speech is made of, about whether a word is fundamentally a pronunciation or a persisting abstract form, and about the relation between writing and speech? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, separating what the script strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Mark where the practice is underdetermined, so that several theories of language would license the same design, and say whether the system's elements presuppose consistent things. Then say what in the orthography would have to change if the underlying view were replaced by the view that writing should track surface pronunciation only, and what would have to change under the alternative view that writing should represent semantic or etymological units and treat sound as secondary.
