I have been turning over why some identity statements can teach a fully competent speaker something, while a person being identical with themselves cannot. Please build me a case to test a view I keep running into informally: that all a proper name contributes to what is said is the individual it names, nothing more. Construct a short, mundane scenario in which a speaker who is fully competent with two ordinary proper names in active use is nonetheless genuinely informed upon learning that they name the same person, and in which the news cannot be explained away by descriptive content packed into either name, by indexicals, by translation from another language, or by a well-worn example such as Hesperus and Phosphorus. Then run the check: say what the name-means-the-bearer view must claim the identity sentence expresses, why it cannot locate the new information in what was said rather than in how the news was delivered, how a Fregean would answer by assigning the two names distinct senses, and the strongest reply available to a defender of the bearer-only view that does not smuggle senses back in under a new label. Finish by identifying the single feature of the scenario that does the real work, describing a version of the case without that feature, and saying whether removing it makes the puzzle dissolve. Please write the answer in British English.
