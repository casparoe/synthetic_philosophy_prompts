I keep seeing people move straight from a single case — someone refusing a drug that would blunt their grief, or someone insisting that grief after a bereavement is just depression by another name — to sweeping conclusions about whether a life without sadness or grief would be better or worse. I want the full specification before the verdict. Write me the list of requirements that an adequate theory of the value of negative emotions must meet, where "adequate" means good enough to guide a practical choice: when a person or a society can preserve, blunt, or cultivate sadness and grief, what should it do and why.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of only one camp. The list should at least include the following:

- The boundary problem. Any theory has to place clinical depression somewhere. If a theory's defense of grief also ends up licensing depression, that is a cost; if it draws the line only by stipulation, that is also a cost. State exactly what work the line has to do.
- The episode–disposition–capacity split. A theory might hold that an episode of grief is intrinsically bad, that the disposition to grieve is instrumentally useful, and that the capacity for grief is a constitutive part of loving. Are these three evaluations allowed to diverge, or does the theory owe us a single story? Which of the three is doing the real work when someone says "a life without grief would be worse"?
- The art puzzle. People deliberately seek out sad music, tragedy, and mournful fiction. Is it a requirement that the theory explain this seeking, or may it dismiss it as a harmless byproduct? If the theory must explain it, what would count as explaining it rather than merely redescribing it?
- The erasure test. Distinguish removing the capacity for grief before any loss, blunting a particular episode after a loss, and never having had the capacity in the first place. A theory should not be allowed to give the same answer to all three without argument. What constraint does this place on any proposal to engineer grief away?
- The misfire cases. Grief over a fictional character, anger at a slight that never occurred, jealousy in a secure relationship. An adequate theory has to say something principled about whether an emotion's value requires it to be fitting, and what to say when it is not.
- The replaceability constraint. If sadness only earns its keep as an information signal, then any substitute signal would do. A theory must either accept that consequence or say what makes the emotion itself non-fungible; it may not quietly evade the consequence.

Then give me a compressed scorecard of the main theoretical shapes — hedonist, Stoic error-theory, adaptationist-functional, and authenticity or self-constitution views — against the two or three requirements that do the most exclusionary work, and tell me which single requirement eliminates the most contenders. Keep the scorecard brief; the requirements are the point.

Finally, mark which of these requirements are genuinely settled and which remain contested among people who work on this topic, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus. If some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — that is, no consistent theory can meet all the non-negotiable ones at once — say so, because that is the part I most want to see.
