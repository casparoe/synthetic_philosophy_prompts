This one has bugged me for ages and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on who's right. The Death in Damascus case, as I understand it: Death has, with near-perfect accuracy, already predicted whether you'll stay in Damascus or flee to Aleppo tomorrow, and he'll be waiting in whichever city he predicted. Staying is therefore strong news that Death is in Damascus; fleeing is strong news that Death is in Aleppo. Assume you'd much rather die in Damascus than in Aleppo if you're going to die either way. Evidential decision theory says choose the act such that conditioning on it yields the better news, so staying looks better. Causal decision theory says your act doesn't move Death—his location was settled before you choose—so what your act reveals about a settled fact is a discovery, not a lever, and the correlation isn't something you can reach through your choice. The two camps just re-assert there founding intuition every time: correlation is not causation, versus a rational agent should use all the information in reach, including information about themselves. Nobody moves. My hunch is that this is actually two different projects (intervening on the world versus making accurate discoveries about it) running under the single label expected value, and that the deeper issue is whether deliberation is itself an intervention or a discovery—which neither side can state without begging the question. What kind of disagreement is this, exactly? Is it substantive, verbal, over standards of evidence, over starting intuitions, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress, or is progress the wrong thing to expect here?
