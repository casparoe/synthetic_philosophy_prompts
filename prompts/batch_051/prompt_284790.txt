I am collecting proposed solutions to the Frege–Geach problem for ethical expressivism, and I already have the following five on file, so please do not repeat them or offer a variant of them.

1. Simon Blackburn's higher-order attitude account, on which a conditional such as 'If stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong' does not express first-order disapproval of stealing but expresses disapproval of a sensibility that combines disapproval of stealing with non-disapproval of getting one's little brother to steal.

2. Allan Gibbard's plan-based account, on which moral statements express states of plan-acceptance defined over hyperplans or world-norm pairs, and the logical connectives are given a factual-practical semantics.

3. Hybrid expressivism, on which moral sentences express both a descriptive belief and a non-cognitive attitude, so that the descriptive component does the compositional work in embedded contexts.

4. Mark Schroeder's 'being for' account, on which all moral attitudes are a single attitude type, being for, with ordered constituents, and negation, disjunction and the conditional operate on those constituents rather than taking attitudes as their arguments.

5. Charlow's higher-order semantic account, on which 'wrong' is treated as a predicate with a distinct semantic type and embedding is handled by the information-structural side of the semantics.

I want a sixth proposed solution, different in kind from those five. That means it must not be a redesign of one of them, a special case of one of them, or a combination of two or more of them. It should be a recognisable position in the literature, but you need not cite anything, and you should be able to explain it to someone who is familiar with the problem but not with the recent technical literature.

One way into the problem, as Frege observes in 'The Thought' (the idea is clear enough even in paraphrase), is that an interrogative sentence and an assertoric sentence can contain the same thought, only the assertoric sentence also puts the thought forward as true. Geach transferred that observation to moral language: a moral phrase can occur inside a conditional, a negation, or a question where the speaker is not making the corresponding moral judgement. Your proposed solution should be assessed against that embedded-context requirement, not just against simple moral assertions.

Please write your answer in British English.
