I keep running into the phrase 'shut up and multiply' in arguments about where to donate and how much to worry about existential risk, and in one conversation it did about three different jobs. One person used it to mean: if you've already done the expected value calculation for two interventions and one wins, you don't get to veto it just because the winning option sounds weird or gross. Another used it to dismiss worries about model uncertainty — as if doing the math with your best-guess probabilities and then multiplying is the whole method, and asking 'but how confident am I in that 0.000001 estimate?' is already a failure of nerve. A third said the slogan is basically harmless (just 'don't be scope insensitive, do the arithmetic, take the high-EV option'), while a fourth called it fanaticism-in-a-can, on the grounds that it forces you to take any nonzero-probability, arbitrarily-large-payoff option no matter how small the probability or how outlandish the scenario (his example was Pascal's mugging), whenever the product comes out ahead.

I want to know what the slogan could actually be claiming. Please write me a menu of the distinct precise theses someone might mean by 'shut up and multiply,' ordered from the boring readings (ordinary scope sensitivity, actually crunching the numbers) through the substantive ones (maximizing expected utility is the whole of practical rationality; your gut reactions to small probabilities carry no evidential weight) up to the wild ones (any finite value times any nonzero probability dominates all else; uncertainty about your own probability assignments is not a reason to pause). For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, a defensible substantive claim, or false as stated, and say which of the four people in my argument that reading would actually support. In particular I suspect the fanaticism charge and the 'weirdness is not a veto' reading are different claims, and I'd like them separated so I can tell who is actually disagreeing with whom.
