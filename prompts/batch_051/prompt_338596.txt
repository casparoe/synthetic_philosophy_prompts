Ordinary evaluative practice seems to contain a striking asymmetry about the commensurability of pleasure and pain. People routinely accept that mild pains can be outweighed by moderate pleasures: they undergo uncomfortable medical tests, break in stiff shoes for a social occasion, or accept a mild headache after a pleasant evening. Many of the same people, however, refuse to accept that severe pains can be outweighed by any ordinary quantity of mild pleasure; they treat severe pain as belonging to a different category, not merely as occupying a farther point on a single scale. Yet they do not usually treat severe pleasure as belonging to a different category in the same way. In fact, someone who declined to endure a mild pain in exchange for an extraordinary pleasure might be regarded as irrational or unable to appreciate the pleasure, not as someone whose values are merely non-standard.

Is there a principled reason for treating only the negative end of the scale as lexically dominant? One candidate is a threshold view of negative well-being: below the threshold, pleasures and pains trade off in the usual way, but once suffering crosses the threshold, no amount of positive well-being can compensate for it. But that view would itself be a radical asymmetry unless it is given a special rationale. Give the strongest rationale you can for such an asymmetric threshold, then test it against a symmetrical proposal: if there is a pain threshold beyond which no amount of pleasure can compensate, should there also be a pleasure threshold beyond which no amount of mild pain can outweigh the pleasure? If you accept the first but reject the second, what feature of pain could justify this difference? If you reject both, how do you account for the ordinary judgment that some severe pains simply cannot be traded away?
