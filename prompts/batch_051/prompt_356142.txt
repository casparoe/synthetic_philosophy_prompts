Consider the following claim, drawn from Hobbes’s account of geometry in the early chapters of De Corpore and later polemical writings: geometry yields demonstrative knowledge because its objects—lines, circles, and the figures built from them—are not given to us as natural bodies are. We ourselves define and construct them, so the causes of their properties are fully in view. Natural philosophy, by contrast, cannot achieve the same certainty, because the causes it seeks are nature’s own work rather than ours.

This claim is usually treated as a point about the logic of demonstration or the metaphysics of artifacts. But it may also rest on a testable picture of what a finite knower must do to possess a cause, and of what makes a proof surveyable.

I want you to design the best test you can of the psychological or process reading of the claim—namely, that constructive access to the objects of a formal discipline makes its propositions more securely knowable, more reliably derived, or more readily extended than inferential access to natural regularities. The test should not rely on citations or a bibliography; work from the conceptual shape of the problem and describe what a feasible experiment, observation, or intervention would look like.

In particular, specify what the test would take as input, what would count as a result supporting the constructive-access reading, what would count against it, and what part of Hobbes’s underlying claim would remain untouched no matter how the results came out.
