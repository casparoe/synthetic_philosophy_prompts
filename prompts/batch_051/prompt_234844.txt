As a retired natural scientist who has only recently turned to serious philosophy, I find myself drawn to questions that sit on the border between the conceptual and the empirical, and I want to probe one that seems to me to sit there uncomfortably: does religious participation make people more moral, and what bearing does any answer to that question have on whether a secular society can sustain trust and cooperation on its own? I would like the investigation to center on a specific and less familiar version of the claim: that it is communal religious practice, not private theistic belief, that does whatever moral work gets done; that this work consists in forming people who are more honest, more cooperative, and more trustworthy toward strangers; and that secular societies manage as well as they do only because they are drawing down an inherited stock of such moral capital, a stock it is unclear they can replenish. Scandinavian societies with high social trust and low religious participation then become the hard test case: are they a counterexample to the claim, or evidence for a time lag between the decay of belief and the exhaustion of the capital it once produced?

Please take this as a claim that is partly conceptual and partly empirical, and tell me how one could bring evidence to bear on it. Specifically:

1. Say what "more moral" would have to mean if the claim is to be testable at all. Be explicit about which parts of the moral concept your operationalization captures, which parts it distorts or must leave out, and whether "more moral" can be treated as a single dimension or is really a family of properties — honesty, cooperation, trustworthiness, kindness to strangers — that may not move together.
2. Design the strongest test you can of the core causal claim — that communal religious practice does the work, not the beliefs themselves, not mere self-selection of already-cooperative people into congregations, and not just any dense social group but religious ones in particular. Give the design concretely enough that I can see what would count as a positive result and what would count as a negative one, and say which alternative readings the design can and cannot separate.
3. Address the secular-society question directly: what evidence would tell us whether high-trust secular societies are generating their own trust or consuming an inheritance, and why that question is harder than it first appears.
4. Finally, state honestly what even a clean result cannot settle — the parts of the claim that are definitional or evaluative rather than empirical, and the respects in which the choice of test already presupposes an answer to the question being asked.

Please begin your response with its bottom line in the very first sentence — your overall assessment of how testable the claim is and what testing can and cannot show — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating of the question.
