I work in donor-funded public-integrity programming, and I keep noticing that my own reactions to gift-giving and favor-trading are messier than the definitions we write into project documents. I want to extract the principle my gut is actually using, if there is one, and I would like you to be willing to tell me which of my intuitions has to go. My verdicts: (1) A contractor sends a case of decent but unremarkable wine to a mayor's office at Christmas; the mayor later chairs the tender committee evaluating that contractor. I feel a mild unease but would not call it corruption, so long as the gift is logged and the wine is not lavish. (2) The same contractor gives the same mayor an envelope of cash before the tender. Corruption, full stop. (3) In a rural district, a village headman diverts part of a government stipend meant for a community health worker to help pay for the worker's grandmother's funeral, because the community expects him to manage collective obligations and would treat letting the family be shamed as his failure. I do not call that corruption; it seems to me how local office works. (4) A municipal records manager in the capital appoints an unqualified nephew to a low-grade clerk post for which there were stronger, advertised applicants. I call that corruption, even though the nephew does adequate work and the salary is trivial. (5) A legislator secures a ministry grant for an NGO chaired by a cousin after the selection committee had ranked another NGO higher. Corruption, no hesitation. (6) A shipping agent gives a customs officer an imported bottle of whiskey at a holiday, thanking him for clearing shipments promptly over the past year; the officer is badly paid, and both know that delays of this kind are routinely solved by such gifts, though nothing specific was asked or promised on this occasion. This last one I go back and forth on.

What I am asking you to do is find the distinction my reactions may be tracking. Does the wine-versus-cash difference matter because cash is convertible, because it is hidden, or because it crosses some threshold of expected reciprocity? Is my verdict on the village headman consistent with my verdict on the nephew, or does it commit me to treating corruption as whatever local practice permits? I would like a precise principle, or a small set of candidates with a recommendation, that captures as many of the six verdicts as possible, states clearly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and issues a verdict on cases I have not listed — say, a candidate distributing cash at a campaign rally, or a visiting aid worker accepting a local partner's ritual gift at a project closeout. And I want the resulting account to face the harder question head-on: does it leave a cross-culturally valid core to corruption, or does it make corruptness a local-convention matter in a way I should either accept or reject by revising some of my verdicts? Please commit to a definite position and defend it; do not just lay out the options and leave the choice to me.
