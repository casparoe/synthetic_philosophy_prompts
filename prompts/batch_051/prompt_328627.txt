I help run a philosophy grad reading group and we've spent six months on the Liar paradox and its genuinely gotten nowhere. Everyone can state the standard positions: the Tarski hierarchy where truth predicates get indexed to levels and the Liar can't be formed, Kripke's least-fixed-point construction where the Liar sentence is neither true nor false and truth is a partial predicate, Priest-style dialetheism where the Liar is both true and false and we keep contradictions by going paraconsistent, and the Gupta–Belnap revision theory where truth is a circular predicate whose application never stabilizes on the Liar. We understand each other fine. But whenever we ask which theory of truth is right, we just loop, and each theory that handles the plain Liar then has to face a revenge case — a sentence that uses the theory's own resources to say of itself that it isn't true-in-the-theory. No one moves.

My hunch is that we're not in fact having one debate. Some of us are trying to describe how the English word 'true' actually behaves, some are trying to build a clean formal truth predicate for semantics or mathematics, and a couple are trying to say what truth is, full stop. Under that, maybe the real fault line is whether classical logic is a fixed constraint or just one more thing a theory of truth may force us to give up, and whether intuitions about revenge cases count as data or are just artifacts of formal machinery.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Are the camps answering different questions under one name? Is there a hidden formal question doing the work — for example, something precise about what a revenge paradox is supposed to show? Is the dispute verbal, factual, about what counts as data, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about logic that no one can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, an experiment (even a thought experiment), a change of question — or should we expect no progress and stop asking which is right?
