Thomas Hobbes argues in Leviathan that no one is fit to judge his own case, because self-interest corrupts judgment and turns each party into a judge in his own behalf. I have been trying to work out whether that principle applies to modern mandatory arbitration, where the person deciding the case is not oneself but is an arbitrator chosen by, paid by, and repeatedly returned to by one side. In consumer and employment contracts of adhesion, the drafting party selects the provider, the individual almost never negotiates or reads the clause, and the provider has a repeat-player incentive to favor the repeat customer over the one-shot claimant. The usual defense of private arbitration is that the parties consented to it. But the consent being defended is consent to a boilerplate clause embedded in a take-it-or-leave-it contract. Does Hobbes’s argument against self-interested adjudication survive contact with this arrangement, or does it need repair? I want to know whether the relevant bias is the arbitrator’s financial interest, the drafting party’s structural advantage, or something else, and whether the consent argument carries the same force it had when arbitration was a genuinely negotiated alternative between merchants with comparable power. Answer in no more than 350 words.
