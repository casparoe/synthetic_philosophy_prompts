I have been reading Macbeth alongside Machiavelli's The Prince, and I keep returning to the pair of ideas he uses to explain political success: fortune, meaning what comes to us from outside and from chance, and virtù, meaning the inner boldness, skill, and adaptability with which a person responds. The witches' prophecies seem to sit exactly on this boundary: they announce what will happen, yet Macbeth still chooses to act, and much of the play is about whether his choices count as the audacious seizing of opportunity Machiavelli admires or as something less masterful, such as imagination, fear, and a tyranny that no longer controls events. Could you offer a reading of Macbeth through Machiavelli's ideas of fortune and virtù? I would like to know where the play fits Machiavelli's picture of the prince who masters fortune, where it complicates or resists that picture, and what it suggests about the difference between genuine virtù and mere recklessness, especially in Macbeth's response to the witches, his wife's urging, and his growing dependence on prophecy.
