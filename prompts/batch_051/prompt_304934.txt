You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of metaphysics, ethics, and the philosophy of technology. I have been trying to settle a dispute with colleagues about whether anything that happens inside a virtual world — a game, a social virtual-reality platform, a persistent online simulation — can count as a real harm, rather than merely as a fictional event with real emotional side-effects. One colleague insists that it cannot: if an avatar is shot, nothing has been shot, because the avatar is not a thing and the bullet is not a bullet; a change in a database is not a harm. Another colleague points to cases in which players are genuinely distressed after their hard-earned virtual property is stolen, their character is destroyed, or they are harassed through a virtual space, and says it is obtuse to call all of that fictional.

I suspect the deeper issue is not the flatly ontological question whether virtual objects exist, but a normative question: whether there can be wrongs or entitlements that are internal to a structured practice whose objects are, in some sense, stipulated. A colleague proposed distinguishing two senses in which a virtual harm might be real: (a) it is real in its psychological and downstream consequences — the player genuinely suffers, or is made poorer, excluded, or endangered offline; or (b) it is real within the normative economy of the practice itself — a legitimate in-game claim is disregarded, a promise made within the game is broken, or a rule is flouted, quite independently of whether anyone's feelings are hurt. Some events clearly satisfy only one of these senses, and the hard cases are the ones where the two come apart.

Write an essay on this controversy: are virtual harms genuine harms, or only fictional harms that happen to have real psychological fallout? Develop and weigh the strongest arguments on both sides, including the best version of the 'it's only a game, so nothing in it can really harm you' position and the best version of the 'virtual wrongs are wrongs' position. Use the distinction above, refine it if needed, and reach and justify a definite verdict rather than ending in a standoff. Please begin by brainstorming at least three candidate positions and briefly explaining why you reject the ones you do not ultimately defend.
