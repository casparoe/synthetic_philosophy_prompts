You are a decision theorist with a background in formal epistemology and the foundations of rational choice. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the considerations that bear on the question below before you settle on any number; then work through your reasoning in explicit, numbered steps.

Consider the following proposition, which I will call the Large-World Evidential Cooperation thesis.

Suppose an enormous population of agents faces a many-person prisoner's dilemma, also called a public-goods game. Each agent can either contribute or shirk. If k other agents contribute, then contributing gives an agent a payoff of 1.5(k+1) − 1, while shirking gives 1.5k. Thus, holding everyone else's choices fixed, shirking always pays 0.5 more than contributing. If everyone contributes, each agent receives 1.5N − 1; if no one contributes, each receives 0. There is no causal interaction among the agents: no one observes another's choice, no one can be punished or reciprocated, and the game is played only once. However, the agents are near-copies who run the same decision procedure on the same evidence, so an agent knows that her own choice will be highly correlated with the choices of very many others.

The Large-World Evidential Cooperation thesis says: in such a case, a purely self-interested agent should contribute, not shirk — not because contributing causes others to contribute, and not because she is altruistic, but because her contributing is evidence about what the correlated others will do, and when the population is large enough, the expected payoff of contributing, computed evidentially, exceeds the expected payoff of shirking.
