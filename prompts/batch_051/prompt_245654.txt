I sit on the research committee of a climate-focused philanthropic foundation, and next month I am presenting to our full board a proposal to move the foundation from opposing all outdoor solar geoengineering research to publicly supporting a deliberately narrow, transparent, publicly funded program of stratospheric aerosol injection field studies — small, short-duration releases to study aerosol microphysics and to validate models, with negligible expected climate effect. The board is mostly experienced climate campaigners and environmental-law policy staff, joined by two atmospheric chemists and a development economist, and several members worked on the Paris Agreement process and care as much about procedural legitimacy and the Global South as about climate outcomes. My argument, in one line: a research moratorium is a poor safeguard, because if deployment ever happens without consent, by a state or a wealthy private actor, we would lack even the observational baselines to detect and attribute it, whereas small governed experiments let us build exactly the monitoring and transparency infrastructure that makes covert or reckless deployment harder to carry off. I will say plainly that I am not arguing for deployment, and that nothing in the proposal commits the foundation to ever supporting it.

Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet. I know the headline objections will be moral hazard, termination shock, and the who-decides problem, but I need more than the slogans. How would this particular board, in their own vocabulary, press each of those three lines of attack, and which less obvious objections would they raise that I have not listed? Order the objections by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, phrase each the way a board member would actually put it, and give me, for each, a short reply plus an honest note on where the reply is softest. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one aimed at the weakest load-bearing premise of my position.
