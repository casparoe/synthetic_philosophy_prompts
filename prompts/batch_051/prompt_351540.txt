I have ended up defending a strong version of expected value fanaticism in decision theory, and I want to know how exposed the position really is. The view, as I hold it, is that expected value maximization is the correct theory of practical rationality, and that there is therefore no positive probability small enough, and no finite payoff large enough, for the pair to be excluded from deliberation on principle. More precisely: if an option offers a real-valued probability p > 0 of a finite value V, and p·V is greater than the expected value of every alternative, then a rational agent is required to take that option. This commits me to accepting, in principle, that a one-in-a-billion chance of preventing an astronomically large number of severe harms can outweigh the certainty of saving a single identified person's life right now. I recognize that this sounds monstrous; what I want to know is whether it is also false, and what kind of false it would be.

Play this straight rather than merely appealing to how repugnant the verdict is. What would count against the view? I want the defeaters ranked by how decisive they would be, and I want the assessment to be honest about the difference between evidence that is fatal, evidence that is merely awkward, and objections that a committed fanatic would dismiss as changing the subject. In particular, weigh: stochastic-dominance arguments; the claim that sufficiently small probabilities are epistemically unusable or manufactured, so that they cannot bear the weight the theory places on them; arguments that utility must be bounded or that risk attitudes must be folded into the evaluation of prospects in a risk-weighted or rank-dependent way; the St. Petersburg and Pascal's-mugging arguments; the complications raised by infinities and by aggregating expected value across possible worlds; and the charge that the view is protected from every counterexample only by redescribing utilities or by insisting that real agents never face such choices. Which of these would a committed expected-value fanatic concede as counting against them, and which would they reject? Finally, I want your verdict on whether the view, as I have stated it, risks anything at all, or whether it has been quietly insulated into unfalsifiability — and whether that insulation, if it exists, is itself a decisive problem or only a defect in how I have formulated the position.
