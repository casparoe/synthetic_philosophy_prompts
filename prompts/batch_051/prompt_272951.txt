My friend has been reading Han Feizi and keeps defending this line: you cant govern by virtue, so you have to govern by incentives. I asked him to lay it out step by step, and he gave me this:

(1) Rule by virtue works only if a ruler can, as a normal matter, count on officials and subjects to do their duty from character, without a personal reward or punishment attached to the particular act.
(2) But most people are self-interested: they will reliably do their duty only when duty has been made to serve their interest.
(3) So a government that counts on people doing their duty from character, without such attached incentives, is counting on something that will not reliably happen.
(4) Therefore, rule by virtue is an unreliable basis for government.
(5) Therefore, a ruler should govern exclusively through explicit incentives — rewards for compliance and punishments for noncompliance.

I half buy it, but the step from (4) to (5) feels too quick, and I also suspect the argument proves too much: if (2) is true of everyone, the officials who run the reward-and-punishment system are self-interested too, so why would the incentive system itself be dependable? I cant put my finger on which step, if any, actually fails. Say exactly which step fails and why, or whether the argument goes through; if some weaker conclusion can be salvaged, say what it is. Start with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
