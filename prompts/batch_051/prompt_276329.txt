Im a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've been going back and forth about cultural loss. The theory I find compelling is that value has to be value for someone: a language, a craft, an art form matters only because it matters to people—it helps them flourish, gives their lives shape. On that view, if the last speaker of a language dies content with her choice to let it end, and no one left feels the absence, then nothing of value has been lost. The world is just down one unused storage system.

But I can't make that feel true. As somebody who works with language, I have a firm intuition that a language is a storehouse of a way of seeing—an instrument for noticing certain things—and when it goes the world is literally thinner, even if no one notices. I'd say the same, a little less strongly, about a dying craft tradition or a musical form. And I feel roughly nothing for the loss of, say, an old table manner or a once-popular fashion. That gradient itself feels like a clue, but I don't know what it points to. I also don't want to just be sentimentalizing my own medium.

Then tell me what it would cost to debunk my intuition (is it sacralization, aesthetic snobbery, scope confusion?) and what it would cost to amend the theory (does it have to posit unexperienced value? does it get arbitrary?). Pay special attention to the gradient: does either side explain why languages should matter more than hairstyles better than the other? End with your own negotiated verdict rather than just splitting the difference. Keep the answer under 500 words, in American English.
