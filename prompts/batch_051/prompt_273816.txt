A familiar argument in debates about the choice of calendar epoch holds that the epoch is a pure convention: no year is intrinsically year one, and whether one inserts a year zero is likewise a matter of stipulation, so the only defensible criterion is which convention best serves present human purposes. On that basis, the argument continues, a secular and culturally neutral epoch—such as the Holocene calendar, which adds 10,000 years to the Common Era count—is rationally preferable to an epoch tied to a contested religious reckoning. But the epoch is no longer, if it ever was, a purely notational choice: it has become load-bearing in date standards such as ISO 8601, in software that sorts, formats, and subtracts dates, in legal deadlines, and in historical databases, so that changing it now carries functional costs and switching risks. Does the argument survive this development? If it survives, say exactly what it still establishes about the current epoch even when replacing it is costly; if it needs repair, identify which premise the development undercuts and whether the argument can be restated in a surviving form; if it is dead, say what remains of the claim that the epoch is a convention at all.
