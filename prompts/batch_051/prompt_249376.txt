I'm in a graduate seminar on the semantic paradoxes, and after we worked through the Liar, a classmate showed me an argument that he says makes all the standard moves beside the point. I can't locate the flaw, and I'd like a careful diagnosis.

His setup: the Liar sentence L — a sentence that says of itself that it is false — is usually taken to show that we must give up bivalence, or at least treat "false" with special care. My classmate says none of that matters, because he can reproduce the paradox's destructive effect without ever using negation or the predicate "false," and without assuming bivalence. Let T be our language's truth predicate, and write T(C) for the sentence "C is true." Let P be any sentence you like — say, "the moon is made of green cheese." Now consider the sentence C that says: "If C is true, then P." In other words, C is the sentence T(C) → P. Then argue as follows:

(1) By the T-scheme, C is true if and only if (if C is true, then P): T(C) ↔ (T(C) → P).
(2) Suppose C is true.
(3) From (1) and (2), we get: if C is true, then P.
(4) From (2) and (3), by modus ponens, P.
(5) Since P followed from the supposition that C is true, conditional proof gives us: if C is true, then P.
(6) From (1) and (5), C is true.
(7) From (5) and (6), by modus ponens, P.

So P follows outright, and P was arbitrary. The argument appears to use only the T-scheme, modus ponens, and conditional proof — no bivalence, no negation, no appeal to "false." That would mean any language with its own truth predicate and ordinary conditional logic proves every sentence, which is absurd. But if the argument is sound, then every proposed solution to the Liar that keeps those three ingredients is dead on arrival.

I'd most like to keep the T-scheme, since it just seems to say what "true" means, and I'd also like to keep modus ponens and conditional proof, since giving those up feels like abandoning logic rather than solving a paradox. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the right move is to restrict or abandon one of these, or something else the argument silently takes for granted, I want to know exactly how the resulting theory of truth handles the original Liar and what it can no longer say or prove.
