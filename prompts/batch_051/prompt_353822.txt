I've been going back and forth with a friend about something from my philosophy of law class, and I want to pin down where the disagreement actually lives. Take the blue bus case: a pedestrian is hit by a bus and can only remember that it was blue; it's uncontested that one company operates 80% of the buses on that route and the rest are grey. No witnesses, no debris, nothing else. In the civil version, most people think a jury can find for the plaintiff. Now make it a criminal hit-and-run — proof beyond a reasonable doubt — and add in the gatecrasher version: we know exactly how many people entered the rodeo and exactly how many paid, and we pick one attendee at random and try to fine him purely because he's 80% likely to have skipped. Intuitively the criminal conviction and the gatecrasher fine feel wrong, but I can't tell whether that's an epistemic point or just a policy. My friend puts the probability that a rational juror should vote to convict in the airtight criminal case at around 0.05; I'm somewhere near 0.6 when the base rate is truly airtight.

So my actual question: give me your credence that a naked statistical fact — a correctly specified, airtight base rate of 0.8 that this defendant did it, with zero individualizing evidence — should be enough for a rational juror to convict in a criminal case. Don't tell me the law bans it; I want the number you'd defend, plus the decomposition. Break it at least into: (a) whether 'beyond a reasonable doubt' is really a threshold on subjective probability, and roughly what threshold you set it at; (b) whether the unease about naked statistics is about the kind of evidence rather than its probative value (statistical versus individualizing); (c) whether the real issue is that reference classes are usually under-specified, and whether that worry disappears once the class is airtight and the defendant falls into it by logic rather than demographic shortcut; and (d) whether some moral or political principle — treating defendants as autonomous agents, the state having to address this particular person, that sort of thing — is doing the actual work. Assign a rough probability to each sub-claim as you frame it and show how they combine into the final credence. Then tell me whic term you're least confident in, and what argument or piece of evidence would move it most.
