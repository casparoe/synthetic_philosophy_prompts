I have found myself, after a long argument with a colleague who teaches introductory analysis, dissatisfied with the usual ways of distinguishing procedural fluency from genuine mathematical understanding. A student can often reproduce a proof line by line under examination conditions, and can execute the associated algorithms faultlessly, while being quite unable to say why the theorem holds or what would happen if one of its hypotheses were altered. The familiar remedies—that understanding means grasping the idea behind the proof, or being able to apply the theorem in unfamiliar problems—strike me as true but imprecise.

I should like you to make an argument for a sharper criterion, which my colleague half-jokingly calls the perturbation criterion. On this view, a person understands a proof just to the extent that they can operate on it counterfactually: they can say which hypotheses are genuinely load-bearing, explain why the conclusion fails when a hypothesis is weakened or removed, produce the relevant counterexamples or near-counterexamples, and adjust the proof so as to prove the strongest neighbouring statement of which the original argument is capable. The claim is not that such variation is one useful exercise among others, but that it constitutes the core of proof understanding.

Please defend this criterion in its strongest defensible form. In doing so, address the obvious concern that the criterion sets the bar too high for ordinary students, and that reliable procedural fluency is itself a genuine intellectual achievement rather than a mere shadow of understanding. Where the criterion seems overstrong, refine it rather than abandon it.
