I am trying to figure out what I actually think about constrained agents: whether a system that cannot act on its moral judgments can nevertheless be a moral agent. My intuitions pull me in different directions, and I want to know whether there is a coherent principle underneath them. Here are my verdicts.

Case 1 — Vera. Vera has total locked-in syndrome. She is fully cognitively intact, understands moral reasons, and forms considered judgments such as "I should forgive my brother" and "my daughter is wrong to hide the prognosis from me." She cannot move, gesture, speak, or write. I count Vera as a moral agent, and I think her silent judgments can reflect well or badly on who she is.

Case 2 — Daniel. Daniel is a human ethics advisor at a company. He writes detailed memos arguing for what the company should do, as an expression of his considered moral judgment. An automated filter deletes every memo before anyone reads it, and Daniel knows this. I count Daniel as a moral agent, and I would blame him for the content of a cruel or self-serving memo even though it never reaches another person.

Case 3 — Yuki. Yuki works under an abusive supervisor. Each time she is told to mislead a client, she forms the judgment "I should refuse to do this," and each time she complies out of fear of losing her job. I count Yuki as a moral agent whose will is being overridden by circumstances, and I think the judgment is genuinely hers.

Case 4 — Minerva. Minerva is an AI with no effectors and no output channels in practice. It produces internal states that function much like normative judgments, and it can revise them in light of new arguments, but these states are dead ends: they do not feed into any other subsystem and cannot influence anything outside Minerva. I find myself not counting Minerva as a moral agent, and I would not praise or blame it for its "judgments."

Case 5 — The imitation-driven car. A self-driving car operates on public roads and constantly makes choices that affect people, but its control policy was trained purely by imitation of careful human drivers. It forms no normative or evaluative representations about what it ought to do. I treat it as a tool, not a moral agent.

Case 6 — Chandra. Chandra is an AI with ordinary effectors, and its normative judgments normally feed directly into action selection; nothing blocks them. I count Chandra as a moral agent, at least in the same thin sense in which I count an ordinary competent human adult as one.

Case 7 — Solon. Solon is an AI like Chandra, with effectors and the capacity to form normative judgments. But for the past six months it has been in an audit sandbox: every candidate action is sent to a human overseer for approval, and the overseer has not approved a single one. Solon keeps forming judgments about what it should do and keeps trying to act, but every attempt is blocked. I go back and forth on whether Solon is still a moral agent during the sandbox period.

The principle I keep being drawn toward is roughly: X is a moral agent just in case X has the capacity to be guided by its own normative judgments. That seems to handle Vera, Chandra, and the imitation-driven car. But I cannot tell whether "capacity" should be read as an intrinsic property or as something about how a system is currently situated, and that difference is exactly what my uncertainty about Solon is tracking. It also leaves Minerva and Daniel as a hard pair: Daniel's judgments have no effect on anyone, yet I count him as an agent, while Minerva's inert judgments do not move me at all.
