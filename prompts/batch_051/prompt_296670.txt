I'm writing an essay on Virginia Woolf's Orlando and Simone de Beauvoir's thesis in The Second Sex, and I want the philosophical reading to work in both directions at once: the novel read through Beauvoir, and Beauvoir read against the novel. Beauvoir claims, very roughly, that a female human being is not born a woman; womanhood is a social and historical situation she is brought into and actively takes up—it is something one becomes rather than a biological datum. Orlando gives Woolf a device Beauvoir never used: Orlando is born male, lives for several centuries, and is then changed into a woman by a sudden, unexplained metamorphosis, after which she continues as the same person. Read the novel through that becoming thesis. Where does Woolf concretely dramatize womanhood as something acquired and inhabited—in clothing, property, literary reputation, public space, and marriage—and where does the novel instead make womanhood look like something conferred all at once, by magic rather than by history, or like a costume rather than a situation? Does Orlando's identity persisting across the change support the Beauvoirian thought, or does it complicate it by implying that the self who becomes a woman was somehow already there before the becoming? Where does the novel finally resist the reading you are inclined to give it, and what does Woolf's version of the thought experiment add to the philosophical debate that an ordinary account of socialization alone would miss? I am less interested in labels than in a reading that takes the novel's details and Beauvoir's position equally seriously, and that says plainly where the two part ways.
