Since retiring from a long career in the natural sciences, I have lately been reading the classical pragmatists, and their accounts of truth have left me both drawn to the theory and unable to silence a contrary intuition. Peirce’s proposal — that truth is the opinion which an indefinitely extended community of competent inquirers would converge upon if inquiry were pushed as far as it could be pushed — strikes me as genuinely clarifying: it ties truth to the methods that actually stabilise belief, dispenses with the picture of truth as a mysterious correspondence with a reality forever hidden behind our backs, and explains why questions that no possible evidence could settle are idle. James’s more practical rendering, according to which a true belief is one that proves itself in experience over the long haul, likewise seems to capture something real about how beliefs are actually tested. But I cannot shake the intuition that truth outruns inquiry. It seems to me, in a way continuous with my scientific training, that there was a definite number of insects in a specific patch of woodland an hour before an unobserved downpour, that a single unrecorded lightning stroke occurred at some exact moment and place, and that the corresponding claims are determinately true or false now, even if every trace has vanished and no community of inquirers could ever be brought to converge on the answer. It is one thing to say that inquiry is our only route to knowledge; it seems quite another to say that what inquiry would eventually agree upon is what truth consists in. The Jamesian version, moreover, leaves me with the familiar worry that a belief could be useful without being true, or true but practically worthless to a given inquirer.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want an account of what would have to be true — about inquiry, about evidence, about the concept of truth — for the intuition that there are inquiry-transcendent facts to be the thing that gives way, and what sort of argument would actually debunk it rather than merely overrule it. I likewise want to know what it would take for the Peircean or Jamesian theory to be amended or abandoned: for instance, whether the pragmatist account should be demoted from a definition of truth to a criterion of warranted assertibility, with a thin correspondence notion left standing behind it, or whether its handling of unverifiable facts is a fatal defect. And I want a considered judgement as to whether there is a reading on which both sides survive — a sense of ‘truth’ in which the pragmatist is right and a sense in which the realist intuition is right — without settling for an equivocation that does no work.
