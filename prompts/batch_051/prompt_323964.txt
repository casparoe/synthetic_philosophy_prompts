I've been arguing with people in my politics seminar about whether small states can actually stay in a confederation without either being dragged around by the big members or quietly turning the thing into a bare free-trade zone, and somebody proposed a mechanism I can't stop thinking about. A twelve-state confederation with common tariffs, shared labor standards, and a common development fund adopts a "derogation" rule: any member can unilaterally suspend any shared obligation for one year, but it has to (i) pay into the common fund an amount equal to an audited estimate of the compliance costs it avoids by suspending, and (ii) surrender its vote in the council for the duration. After a year it can renew once, with the payment doubled and the vote still suspended; after that it either resumes compliance or can apply to "exit" the obligation entirely, which requires a two-thirds vote of the other members. If they refuse, the state must comply or leave the confederation altogether; if they approve, the state pays a permanent annual solidarity fee set by the council, and the others may impose equivalent border adjustments on its goods.

I want to think through this as a mechanism, not argue about whether opt-outs are defensible in principle. Walk me through what it does to which obligations get suspended and by whom, where the payment/auditing step gets gamed first, what the renewal option actually gets used for, and where the first real crisis comes from. What does the system converge on — a two-tier union, a slow unraveling, something else? And is there a fix for the worst failure mode that doesn't just turn the rule into a dead letter?
