Below is a Frankfurt-style case of a kind much discussed in debates about moral responsibility. In the standard version, an agent, Jones, is deliberating about whether to perform some act that will harm a colleague. Unknown to Jones, a neuroscientist, Black, has installed a device that works as follows: if Jones were about to decide not to perform the act, an involuntary precursor would appear — a specific pattern of neural activity over which Jones has no direct control — and Black's device would then directly cause Jones to perform the act anyway. In the actual course of events the precursor never appears: Jones decides on her own to perform the act, and Black does nothing. The standard case is normally taken to show that Jones is morally responsible for an act she could not have done otherwise, and therefore that the principle of alternative possibilities — the thesis that one is morally responsible for what one has done only if one could have done otherwise — is false.

Now vary one feature. Suppose Black's device is not a guarantee. If the precursor appeared, the device would be activated; but its intervention would succeed only with probability 0.9. With probability 0.1 it would misfire and leave Jones free to act on whatever intention she had formed. In the variant, the precursor never appears, so the fallibility is never tested and what actually happens is exactly what happens in the standard case.

I want a careful analysis of what this single change does to the argument. In particular:

(1) On the most defensible analysis of 'could have done otherwise', did Jones, in the actual sequence, have the ability to do otherwise? Be explicit about what you are holding fixed when you answer — for example, whether you treat the device's 0.1 chance of misfiring as a genuine possibility in the relevant possible worlds, or only as a fact about other, more distant worlds.

(2) Does the variant still support the claim that moral responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise, or does it now support a weaker claim, such as that responsibility does not require a guaranteed alternative?

(3) Does the original case now look like a special limiting case, reached by setting the probability of failure to zero? If so, should we be suspicious that the original intuition depended on treating a very reliable intervention as though it were an infallible one?

If you think the question as I have posed it rests on a contested or misleading assumption — for example, that 'could have done otherwise' admits a single analysis independent of what is held fixed, or that a thought experiment of this sort can settle rather than discipline a metaphysical debate — then say so plainly and give me the best corrected version of the task, rather than politely complying with the framing.
