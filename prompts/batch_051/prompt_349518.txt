I've got a distinction I've been using and want taken apart. I call it "testimonial export." Suppose someone tells you a moral verdict — "that was unforgivable" or "it was fine, it was consensual." If you act on it in the moment, that's ordinary reliance on moral testimony, and I want to say it's usually fine. But if you file it away and six months later it's shaping who you distrust, who you blame, who you hire, in situations the source never saw, that's testimonial export: a borrowed verdict that now circulates as if it were your own, underwriting inferences nobody ever testified to. My claim is that pessimists about moral testimony have the target half-wrong. The initial borrowing isn't the problem; the export is, because the testifier only vouched for their case, but now you're the author of a general policy with no first-personal grip on the underlying reasons. So exported testimony always leaves a justificatory deficit, no matter how reliable the source. Is this a real distinction or just "moral understanding" with a logistics metaphor?
