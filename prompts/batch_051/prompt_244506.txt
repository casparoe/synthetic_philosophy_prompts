Two colleagues of mine — Mara, who works on formal verification, and Dev, who runs our red-teaming — spent an afternoon arguing about how to interpret the numbers from our pre-deployment safety evaluation of an aligned language model. They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not untangle it. Here is the exchange as I remember it.

Mara: We're reporting average success rates on a benchmark of typical user prompts. For safety, that's nearly worthless. Users who want the model to do something harmful will search until they find an input that breaks it. Safety is a universal property over adversarial inputs — or at least over a defined set of them — so we need a worst-case guarantee, not a frequency-weighted average.

Dev: A worst-case guarantee over all possible prompts is impossible for open-ended language, and any guarantee we can actually verify is relative to a restricted input set. That just moves the guess from "which distribution of users" to "which set of inputs." The real question is expected harm over the distribution of use we will actually face, and that is exactly an average-case question.

Mara: But the actual distribution of use includes adversarial users. An average over typical prompts is dominated by benign queries and systematically underestimates harm, because it gives tiny weight to exactly the inputs an adversary is hunting for. The distribution that matters for safety is the one an adversary would draw from when trying to break the model.

Dev: Then use a red-teaming distribution. That is still an average-case estimate, just over a more relevant distribution. And as soon as you say "the worst an adversary can find within a realistic budget," you are making an assumption about what adversaries will do. You have hidden an average over adversaries inside a sentence that sounds like a guarantee.

Mara: The difference is that a universal claim over a well-chosen input set does not depend on how often any input occurs. If deployment patterns shift, a claim over all paraphrases, or all prompts below a fixed length, remains informative as long as the real inputs stay inside the set. An average-case estimate is invalidated the moment the deployment distribution drifts from the benchmark.

Dev: A universal claim is invalidated the moment the real inputs leave the set. Set misspecification and distribution shift are the same vulnerability; you have just put the fragile assumption in the boundary of the set instead of the weights on the set.

Mara: Not the same. If the set is finite and checkable, a universal claim can be verified exhaustively, or at least searched adversarially, and then you have a certificate for the whole set. An average-case claim can only be sampled, and you are left hoping the sample was representative.

Dev: For any set large enough to matter, exhaustive checking is impossible, and adversarial red-teaming is literally sampling from a cleverly chosen distribution. So the "certificate" is a sample-based estimate wearing the word "guarantee." What you are actually defending is not a guarantee but a policy of being extremely conservative in defining the input set.

Mara: Being extremely conservative is the point. Safety is about the severe tail of outcomes, and rare catastrophic failures are precisely what averages estimate badly. A worst-case methodology at least errs in the direction of caution.

Dev: That is a decision-theoretic preference for minimax over expected value, not a fact about what can be verified. If we are maximally conservative about the set, we will never deploy, because for any wide set there is some prompt that makes the model say something bad. At some point the set has to be weighted by importance, and you are back to averaging.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each turn, say which claims are sound, which are overstated, and which are mistaken, and briefly justify each assessment. Then say who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
