At our little reading group last week two friends got into an argument I couldn't pull apart. We were discussing Leibniz's principle that two distinct things must differ in some property—they can't differ in number alone—and someone brought up Black's two-sphere universe: a world containing nothing but two iron spheres in an otherwise empty space, exactly alike in size, composition, temperature, and every other intrinsic feature, hanging motionless two miles apart.

The exchange went roughly like this, as best I remember it. Dan said the two-sphere world is obviously possible, so Leibniz's principle is simply false. Ruth said no—each sphere occupies a different place, so they do differ in a property after all: one is here and the other is there. Dan replied that in a completely empty space 'here' and 'there' aren't real properties, because theres nothing to define them against; if you swapped the two spheres the world would be identical in every respect, which is exactly what it means for them to be indiscernible. Ruth said that was too quick: the distance between them is real, so the two ends of that distance are distinct places whether or not anyone can label them from outside. Then Dan brought in physics, since he used to work in it: identical particles in quantum mechanics cannot be individually labeled like little billiard balls—the mathematics gives one joint state and doesn't assign a separate trajectory to either one—yet the claim that there are two of them is still perfectly meaningful. Ruth answered that maybe the right lesson from quantum mechanics is to stop talking about particles as individual little things, not that Black's world is settled. And that is where they stalled.
