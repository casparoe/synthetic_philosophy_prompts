Two people in my game theory class got into it over the absent-minded driver problem, and I want a point-by-point verdict because they ended up talking past each other. Quick setup: a driver reaches two identical-looking intersections, can't tell them apart, and can't remember whether he's already passed the first. Exit at the first yields payoff 1; exit at the second yields payoff 4; going straight through both yields 0. He chooses a plan in advance, knows he'll have no memory, and, because the intersections look identical, the plan can only be: exit with probability p at whatever intersection he finds himself at.

Here's the back-and-forth.

A: The rational plan is the one that maximizes expected payoff before the trip. If he exits with probability p, expected payoff is p(1) + (1 - p)p(4), which is 5p - 4p². Differentiating gives 5 - 8p, so optimal p is 5/8. Expected payoff is 25/16. That's the answer. The fact that he'll forget doesn't change the reward structure; he should just commit to randomizing 5/8 at each intersection.

B: No, he can't just "commit." Imagine he actually wakes up at an intersection, p=5/8 in his pocket. Conditional on being at an intersection, the probability it's the first is 1/(2-p), not 1. So with p=5/8, the chance it's the first is 8/11 and the chance it's the second is 3/11. If he continues, his expected payoff is (8/11)(4)(5/8) = 20/11, which is about 1.82; if he exits, it's 1. So he would deviate to continue. A rational plan has to be self-consistent at the moment of choice: at an intersection, continuing must not be strictly better than exiting. That means 4p/(2-p) = 1, so p = 2/5. It has a lower ex ante payoff, but at least the driver would actually carry it out.

A: But that is letting the later self bully the planning self with probabilities that are caused by the later self's own planned behavior. The conditioning is circular. Before the trip, the driver knows he will be at an intersection with probability 1, so "I am at an intersection" tells him nothing he didn't already use when he picked p. Under your rule, he knowingly picks a plan that gives 1.36 instead of one that gives 1.5625, just to make some self-locating calculation come out symmetric. If he could bind himself, he'd bind himself to 5/8. The question is what plan is best, not what plan survives being second-guessed by a forgetful self.

B: But he can't bind himself, and the later self is the same agent with the same preferences and no new payoff-relevant information. Your "best plan" is one the agent would reject every time he actually has to act: at p=5/8, continuing has a higher expected payoff than exiting at the moment of choice, so he would always continue, and his actual policy would be p=0 — which is much worse. A theory of rational action that recommends an action the agent himself would not perform given his own beliefs is either changing the decision problem or smuggling in a commitment device that isn't there. Sequential rationality for imperfect recall has to require that the strategy is a fixed point of the beliefs it generates.

They didn't move past this. I'm not sure whether one of them is right, or whether the paradox is that there's a genuine conflict between the ex ante and the moment-of-choice perspectives. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and say what both are missing. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
