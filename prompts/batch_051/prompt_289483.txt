I work in the financial-crime unit of a mid-sized bank, reviewing alerts from the transaction-monitoring system and deciding which cases to escalate for a full investigation or a suspicious-activity report. I do this maybe fifteen times a week, usually with under half an hour per case, and my supervisor signs off on every escalation. The evidence is never the clean kind: the monitoring system flags a pattern (but it has a false-positive rate nobody has written down), a teller remembers that a customer seemed nervous or was vauge about where the money came from, the customer gives a story that may be true or rehearsed, a collegue passes on a tip from someone who may have a grudge. I'm aware of the usual Bayesian picture, but I remember from a course that standard conditionalization assumes you learn some piece of evidence for certain, and that Jeffrey conditionalization is meant for the case where an observation just shifts your probability in something without making it 1 — so you don't update all the way to P(H|E), you average P(H|E) and P(H|not-E) weighted by your new, imperfect confidence in E. What I don't have is a way to run that in practice, on a Tuesday afternoon, with numbers I mostly have to make up.

That's what I'd like you to write: a decision procedure for my role, a checklist I can actually use to combine uncertain pieces of evidence — an alert, a teller's impression, a customer's account, a tip from a coworker of uncertain reliability — into a decision to escalate or not. The procedure should say what questions I ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure says no, and, crucially, where it has to stop and hand the decision to a person because a number is a judgment call rather than a fact. It should be honest about which steps are real Jeffrey-style updating and which are guesswork dressed up as arithmetic. Then run it on at least two cases: one where a suspicious alert plus a vague teller memory plus a plausible customer story might or might not escalate, and one where the procedure gives an answer I probably won't like (say, it tells me to escalate on thin evidence, or it blocks me from escalating on a case my gut says is dirty). Finally tell me what this procedure can't do for me, and whether I'm actually entitled to trust it more than gut feel given that my prior probability for any given customer being a criminal is very low.
