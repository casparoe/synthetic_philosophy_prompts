So I just got back from a long argument with my friend about reasons, and my gut is all over the place, but I suspect there's a pattern in there somewhere. I want you to help me find the principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that best captures my verdicts, state it precisely enough to run on new cases, say which of my cases is doing the work for each part of it, and tell me which of my verdicts, if any, I have to give up.

Here is what I actually found myself saying.

Jones wants a gin and tonic after work. He sees a bottle labeled gin in the cabinet, pours it, and is about to drink it, but the bottle is actually full of petrol. I have no problem saying Jones has no reason to drink it, whatever he currently thinks.

A woman is planning to see a movie at the 7:00 showing, but the listing she read was wrong and it actually screens at 5:00. If she leaves now she makes it; if she waits until she thinks she needs to leave, she misses the whole thing. I'd say she has a reason to leave now, even though she has no idea the time is wrong.

A man is cold and nasty to his wife, and he genuinely has no motivation to treat her better. I still want to say he has a reason to treat her better. My friend kept saying I was just smuggling in an external reason, and I couldn't quite defend it.

A severely depressed woman has stopped caring wether she lives or dies and sees no point in eating or taking her medication. I say she has a reason to eat and take the pills anyway. The depression itself seems to be doing the work, not her current desires.

A man's only real desire is to count blades of grass. I'm perfectly fine saying he has a reason to count blades of grass, but I can't bring myself to say he has a reason to learn to paint or write a novel just because those are worthwhile activities.

A selfish, spiteful man has no interest in helping the stranger on his doorstep. Later he has a religious conversion and becomes devoted to helping others. I don't think he had a reason to help the stranger before the conversion, and I do think he has one after. But I can't see how that fits with the nasty husband, who also lacked the motivation yet I said had a reason.

Is there a coherent account of reasons that gets all of these, or close? If not, wich of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And what does that account say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a smoker who has no desire to quit and never formed one, but who would very much want to quit if he vividly imagined himself with emphysema in ten years. Does he have a reason to quit now?
