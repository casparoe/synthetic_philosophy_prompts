I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called Situations of Commitment, mostly philosophy majors, sixteen students, and we meet for seventy-five minutes. The unit I am revising is on escalation of commitment, and I want to stage the classic dollar auction rather than lecture about it: bidders compete for a modest real prize I supply, the highest bidder wins and pays their bid, but the second-highest bidder also pays their bid and receives nothing. I want the students to discover for themselves how a sequence of individually reasonable decisions can trap someone in a contest that, at the start, no one would have chosen to enter.

Please design the runnable activity for me, on the assumption that I will not lecture up front and will name the concepts only after the exercise. Give me the rules in a form I can read aloud, including how many students bid while the rest observe, whether the auctioneer may warn participants once bidding passes the prize value, and a timeline for the seventy-five minutes that leaves real room for discussion. The debrief should map specific moments in the auction onto the structure of escalation: the point where a bidder raises solely to avoid becoming the loser, the point where the prize value is passed, and the way sunk costs get recast as something a person is still defending rather than something already lost. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it — students refusing to bid, collusion, principled early withdrawal, or escalation not getting far enough to feel dangerous — and how I should handle each failure without deflating the point. Finally, be frank about which feature of escalation of commitment the game cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply myself in the debrief so students do not mistake the auction for a complete account of the phenomenon.

Keep the entire plan to no more than 1,000 words, with the most space given to the rules and timeline, the next most to the debrief, and the least to the diagnosis of likely failures.
