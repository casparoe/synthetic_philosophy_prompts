In Clark and Chalmers's paper The Extended Mind they introduce a parity principle near the beginning: if an external resource is doing a job that, were it done inside the skull, we would have no hesitation counting as cognitive, then that external resource should count as part of the cognitive process. Later, when they try to handle the Otto/Inga case, they seem to add extra conditions. The notebook only counts, they suggest, because it is reliably there when Otto needs it, its contents are automatically endorsed, and he trusts it in roughly the way he would trust his own memory. Those look like different standards. The first says same job, same credit, pretty much regardless of reliability or past history. The second says Otto's notebook gets in only because it is unusually stable and transparent, not just because it does the same job as Inga's memory. Is there a genuine tension here? Is the later appeal to reliability a quiet retreat from the parity principle, or can the two moves be read as stages of a single view? What is the most honest reconciliation, and what would count as special pleading?
