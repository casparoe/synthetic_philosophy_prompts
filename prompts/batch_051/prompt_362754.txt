Last weekend I had a few people over, and two friends—Mara, who does modal logic, and Jon, who works on the foundations of physics—got into a long argument about the grandfather paradox. I didn't take notes, so the back-and-forth below is my reconstruction: the moves are theirs, but the phrasing is mine. I kept out of it, mostly refilling drinks, so I don't think I tipped my hand either way.

M: The paradox gets its power from an ambiguity in "can." Tim is standing in 1940 with a gun, at close range, facing his grandfather. Can he kill him? There's a sense in which yes: he has the ability, the weapon, the target. And there's a sense in which no: if we hold fixed all the facts, including the fact that Tim exists only because his grandfather survived, then the killing isn't possible. "Can" shifts with the set of facts we hold fixed. So there's no flat contradiction; the two senses just need to be kept apart.

J: But notice what the consistent story has to look like. Tim pulls the trigger and somehow fails—the gun jams, the shot grazes him, Tim trips. Each individual failure can have a mundane cause. What can't have a mundane cause is the pattern: the world has to serve up a failure every time, for every would-be paradox, exactly when consistency demands it, with no local mechanism linking the demand to the event. You've traded a contradiction for a run of coincidences.

M: Logic only requires consistency, not explanation. A world that keeps not contradicting itself is weird yet stable. You're asking me to produce a cause for the failure, and maybe there is one in the local sequence—a speck of rust, a change of heart. But even if there isn't, an unexplained fact is not an impossible fact. The story proves impossibility only if it forces both "Tim kills grandfather" and "Tim exists." Those two can't both be true; one has to give. Which one gives is settled by the world's whole shape, not by a deduction.

J: I think you're smuggling in exactly what needs to be shown: that for every would-be contradiction, something gives, as a kind of law of nature. That's a self-consistency principle, and it isn't derived from logic or physics; it's a hand-imposed rule saying no closed causal loops erase themselves. But if time travel is what generates such loops, then adding the rule is just admitting the original concept was broken. A constraint with no local implementation doesn't explain why Tim fails; it just bookkeeps the fact that he must.

M: I can live with that bookkeeping. My claim was never that time travel is physically cheap—only that the classic paradox doesn't show it's logically impossible. The story as told tries to have it both ways: it asserts the traveler exists and that he succeeds in erasing his own cause. The contradiction belongs to the storyteller, not to time travel.

J: Fine, but then the paradox does its real work one level down. If Tim's success is impossible in the strongest sense—incompatible with the whole history—then in what sense is he "able" to kill at the local moment? At most we've preserved a word. The time traveler becomes a component of a pre-arranged history, not an agent who could have done otherwise. So the paradox shows that time travel, combined with ordinary local physics and genuine alternate possibilities, forces the inconsistency. You can save time travel by sacrificing agency, or save agency by rejecting time travel; you can't have both.

M: Not obviously. In some consistent histories Tim fails because of his own traits—he hesitates, he recognizes the old man, he can't bring himself to do it. There the failure has a local, psychological cause, and the agent isn't a puppet; he acts from who he is.

J: But then you've changed the case. The original setup was that Tim is the kind of person who would and could kill. If you now write him as someone who would flinch, the paradox hasn't been solved; it's been dodged. The hard version is the one where every local factor pushes toward success and failure still arrives, with no local step that could have made it happen. In that version the "agency" is decorative, and the paradox has done exactly the destructive work it was meant to do.

I walked away thinking they had each scored points, but I couldn't sort out who was right about which specific step, and I suspect they were partly talking past eachother about what the paradox is even supposed to show. Can you go through the exchange almost line by line and adjudicate it? For each move, say who's right and why, where one of them overstates or understates, and what neither of them considered. I'm less interested in a final winner than in seeing exactly where the argument turns.
