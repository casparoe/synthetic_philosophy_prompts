Suppose a novelist who runs a serialized fiction project with a small writers' room adopts the following rule. No story decision may be made directly. Every substantive question — whether a viewpoint character would defect, whether a promised subplot should be resolved off-page, which of two arcs to pursue — must be assigned to a resolver, who has one ninety-minute session to either answer from their own judgment or split the question into at most three sub-questions and delegate each to another resolver who sees only the scenes and character notes relevant to that sub-question; delegates may split again, to no more than three levels, and only the recomposed top-level answer, with a log of the splits, reaches the novelist. Walk through how this institution would actually behave across a season: which kinds of story questions survive being factored and which are destroyed by it, what it does to continuity of voice, theme, and foreshadowing, whom the room begins to hire and how participants game the splitting and delegation rules, and whether what comes out the top meaningfully resembles a single deliberating intelligence. Where does it fail first, and can those failures be repaired by tightening particular rules without abandoning the mechanism?
