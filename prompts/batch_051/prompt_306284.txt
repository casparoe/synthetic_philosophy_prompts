Last night a friend and I spent about three hours going in circles over whether objects persist by being wholly present at every moment they exist, or by having different temporal parts at different times, the way a road has different spatial parts along the stretch it covers. He kept insisting that endurantism is the only view that respects real change, and that perdurantism turns a person into something spread out in time with no single thing there at any moment. I kept bringing up the problem that one and the same person can be straight now and bent later, and his fix — just relativize properties to times — kept sounding to me like it redefined change away rather than explaining it. We both left annoyed.

Now I'm less interested in picking a side than in figuring out what I should be demanding from a adequate story about how ordinary objects last through time. Give me a full list of desiderata, where each item comes with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement. The cases: change and temporary intrinsics, coinciding objects like the statue and the clay, fission and fusion, gradual replacement of parts, and how we actually count and reidentify ordinary things in speech. The distinctions it can't collapse: strict identity over time versus persistence conditions versus what a thing is at a single moment, and a thing's history versus the thing itself. Then add any hard constraints from logic — transitivity of identity, the indiscernibility of identicals, whatever you think truly binds — and say which items on the whole list are non-negotiable and which are just endurantist or perdurantist prejudices. Tell me whether any subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable, because that's where the real action is. End with a brief scorecard for endurantism, perdurantism, and the stage view against the criteria, but keep it short; the criteria are the thing I actually need. Please write in American English.
