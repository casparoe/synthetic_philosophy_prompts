Please describe the most important implications of von Neumann's views on the brain for how I should think about the current wave of claims that large language models or neuromorphic chips work "like the brain."

I cover science and ideas for a general audience, so I keep hitting this. In The Computer and the Brain, von Neumann works through the comparison between the nervous system and digital computers. His starting point is that the brain's individual components are, by engineering standards, terrible: neurons fire slowly, they're noisy, they can fail, they need chemical resets. But instead of concluding the brain is a bad computer, he argues it gets its reliability and power from massive parallelism, redundancy, and a different kind of organizing principle—maybe partly analog, maybe more statistical than arithmetical, definitely much lower precision per operation but with a huge number of operations happening at once. So reliability out of unreliable parts.

If I take that seriously, what should change in how I report when a startup says its chip is "brain-like," or when a researcher says a language model "thinks" the way I do, or when someone says whole-brain emulation is mostly a matter of neuron count? I'd like the three or four implications you'd put at the top, with reasons, and a clear statement of which one I should most resist and which one I should most insist on in print.

And I want a committed answer—not a careful survey of ways one could read him. Take a definite position and defend it, even if it means saying some very smart people have been misleading.
