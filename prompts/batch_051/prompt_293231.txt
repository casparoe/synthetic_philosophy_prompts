My friend and I spent way too much of last weekend arguing about abandonware. He'd found a site full of old DOS-era games and utilities, mostly made by companies that dissolved twenty or thirty years ago; technically the copyrights didn't vanish with the companies, but he downloads them without a second thought and, honestly, so would I. Then I asked whether he would take a bike that's been chained to a rack for a year, with the chain rusted through and no sign the owner is ever coming back. He said no, that would be stealing. But he also said copying a current, expensive piece of software is wrong. So here's the pattern I can't get to cohere: the owner's absence seems to dissolve the wrong in the software case but not in the physical object case, and copying itself seems to be treated as wrong when the company is alive but fine when it's gone — even though the acts are identical apart from whether the owner is still around to care.

Is there a principle that gets all three of those judgments right, or is one of them just confused? Give me the strongest candidate justification you can build (rivalrousness? incentives? no living owner is harmed? abandonment?), then test it honestly against nearby cases: photocopying an orphaned novel versus stealing that novel from a library, a program whose author is alive but unreachable versus one whose author is dead, and a bike whose owner is dead versus one whose owner is just disorganized. Then tell me which of the original judgments to keep, reverse, or drop. Please answer in American English.
