I sit on my university's research-conduct commitee, and a case has come up that's splitting us down the middle. The code of practice includes a clause that predates most of our current methods: "A researcher who has put forward a hypothesis must cease to advocate it, and must not seek or hold funding for it, once it has been falsified by observation or experiment." A doctoral student in my department published a model predicting that a particular measurable pattern would appear in a long-term monitoring dataset; a collaborating group tested it, the pattern didn't appear, and she nevertheless kept working on the model and submitted two grant applications to develop it further. Her defence is that the failed test only refutes her model if you also accept an auxiliary assumption about how the monitoring archive has been calibrated — an assumption, she says, that belongs to the older framework and that the newer competing model simply doesn't need, because it defines the measured quantity differently. Her head of department has filed a complaint quoting the clause back at us and insists it is a disciplinary matter, not a methodological one. The clause was written in the 1980s, when the drafters clearly imagined falsification as a clean yes/no event; they did not forsee a field in which what counts as an observation against a theory is itself live in dispute.

What I need is an interpretive analysis of what the clause actually requires of her. Take it first from the plain words; then from the purpose of the clause; then from what its drafters would have said if they had been asked about her case; then from what a reasonable researcher reading the code today would take it to mean; and finally say which approach should govern and whether she is in breach. I also don't think you can duck the bigger question: if we read it strictly the code seems to have taken Popper's side in advance, and she is making a Kuhnian move, so is there a coherent way to apply it without first settling whether science during a revolution is rational? I don't want a bibliography or the names of legal theories; I want you to reason it through from the clause and the case.

In your answer, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question. British English, please.
