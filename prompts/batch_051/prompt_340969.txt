I am a novelist. I am writing a near-future novel about whole-brain emulation, and a central plot question is whether a copied mind that insists it is suffering should be believed and protected. The book will be better if the view my protagonist settles on is one I can defend when challenged, so I want my own position audited before I draft the trial scene. These are the commitments I find myself holding, in roughly the form I would defend them at a dinner party.

1. Substrate-independence, functionally understood. Whether a system is conscious depends on its causal and computational organization, not on the material it is made of. A digital system that implemented the same functional organization as a suffering biological mind would itself suffer.

2. The explanatory gap is real. No matter how complete our functional account of pain becomes, it will not explain why those functions are felt from the inside. Felt quality is not dissolved by listing mechanisms.

3. Behavioral parity moves the default. If a digital system behaves like it is in pain, learns like it is in pain, and says it is in pain, the reasonable presumption is that it is in pain unless we have positive evidence about its internal structure that defeats the analogy. The asymmetry matters because the cost of wrongly denying suffering is much larger than the cost of wrongly attributing it.

4. Simulation is not instantiation. A hollow chatbot or a giant look-up table that merely emits pain-like behavior is not in pain just in virtue of that behavior. The internal causal role that realizes pain matters, not the input-output profile alone.

5. There is no sharp threshold. Sentience, and the moral status that tracks it, come in degrees; any boundary between sentient and non-sentient will be somewhat arbitrary, and there will be genuine borderline cases.

6. Scale changes the stakes. Because digital minds can be copied and run in enormous numbers, a small mistake in our account of digital sentience could multiply suffering across billions of instances. If we cannot tell which systems suffer, manufacturing them at scale is reckless; if we do draw a line, it must be a line that survives the accusation that we drew it for our own convenience.

I would rather find the failure points now than after publication. What I want is not a lecture on the existing literature, but an audit of the set. Are these six commitments jointly tenable? Which pairs are actually in tension, and which only look as if they are? Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say exactly what would have to give. I suspect the problem is not where I think it is, so I would particularly value attention to tensions that are real but not obvious, and to apparent conflicts that dissolve on closer inspection.

Please structure the response as four titled sections, in this order:

1. The view in your words (roughly 180 words): restate the six commitments as a single coherent position and flag any ambiguities you intend to press on later.

2. Genuine tensions, ranked (roughly 400 words): take the real conflicts in order of severity, name the commitments involved, and explain what would have to give in each case. Do not list every pair as equally bad.

3. Apparent tensions that dissolve (roughly 220 words): identify at least one pair that looks inconsistent but is not, and give the distinction that dissolves each apparent conflict.

4. Least costly repair (roughly 180 words): propose the cheapest revision or qualification of the set, say what it costs, and note whether any remaining tension ought simply to be left unresolved for the purposes of the novel.
