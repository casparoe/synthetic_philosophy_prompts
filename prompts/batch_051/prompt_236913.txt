I've been turning over the view that you can't get aesthetic judgments secondhand, and I keep thinking it's right. The principle has a clean shape: an aesthetic judgment is about how a work strikes you, so if you haven't experienced the work, it hasn't struck you, and someone else's verdict can't hand you that judgment. Being told a poem is 'uncanny' seems like being told a pepper is hot—a report of someone else's encounter, not knowledge of the poem. And yet I absolutely rely on aesthetic testimony in practice: if three people whose taste I trust tell me a film is tedious and manipulative, I'll believe them, I'll act on it, and I'd call that aesthetic knowledge, not mere gossip. Which should give way, and what would it take for one of them to go? Give me the strongest version of each side, then say what it costs to drop the intuition that secondhand aesthetic knowledge is real, what it costs to give up or weaken the principle, and whether there's a reading of 'aesthetic judgment' (versus 'aesthetic knowledge' or 'taste') on which both survive. American English, please.
