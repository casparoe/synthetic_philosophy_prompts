I am a high school student taking a philosophy of science elective, and I have been trying to understand why work on verisimilitude seems stalled. Popper's idea was that science aims at truthlikeness: since our best theories are probably false, progress consists in getting closer to the truth rather than hitting it. But the formal program looks stuck. Popper's own comparative account was shown to imply that no false theory could be closer to the truth than another. The alternatives that followed split into similarity-based approaches, which place possible worlds in a likeness space and measure how far a theory's worlds are from the true world, and content-based approaches, which compare theories by their correct and incorrect consequences, often after restricting to relevant consequences. These accounts disagree about basic cases and even about what the formalism is supposed to capture. My working hypothesis is that the field has quietly split over which of three jobs verisimilitude is meant to do: serve as a semantic measure of distance from the truth, guide theory choice under uncertainty, or characterize scientific progress. People pursuing one of these jobs often regard the others' formal work as beside the point. I am not asking you to pick a winner among the formal approaches; I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is that a fair account of the stalemate, or is there a genuine formal or philosophical disagreement underneath it, and what could actually count as progress — a theorem, a conceptual distinction, or a change of question — or should none be expected? Please give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
