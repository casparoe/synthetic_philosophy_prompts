Okay, here's one I use in my decision-theory seminar, and I want you to actually work through it instead of just waving at the word fallacy. You're a decision theorist with a good grip on expected utility and time-inconsistent choice. Take a breath, think carefully, and start by brainstorming a couple of readings before you settle on one.

The textbook sunk-cost case: I pay $100 for a nonrefundable concert ticket. On the night I'm exhausted and going would have negative net utility all things considered. The standard analysis says the $100 is sunk; from here on only future costs and benefits count, so staying home is rational. Familiar enough.

Now vary it. The agent is a sophisticated present-biased person, beta = .5, delta = 1, who knows her bias. She buys a $400 nonrefundable pass to a ten-session exercise class. Her only reliable self-control device is an unbroken perfectly-attended streak: if she misses even one session, the identity that keeps her exercising dies, and she will predictably skip most of the remaining sessions, losing the fitness benefits she bought the pass to secure. Tonight is session 4. Taken on its own, session 4 has negative expected utility: shes sore, sleep-deprived, and has a deadline. But attending preserves the identity, which keeps sessions 5 through 10 alive; skipping destroys it.

Her felt reason for going is partly that she already paid for the whole thing and skipping would feel like wasting that money. Notice: the thought that moves her mentions the sunk cost.

Your task: (1) restate the standard sunk-cost argument precisely enough that we can see exactly what it forbids; (2) run it on this case; (3) decide whether this is a genuine case where honoring a sunk cost is rational, or whether it only looks that way because a forward-looking consideration, the fragile identity, is doing the real work. In particular: if we strip out the payment but give her the identical identity mechanism for free, does her decision change? And what does that show about whether sunk costs, as such, ever belong in a correct ex post calculation? I'm less interested in the one-word verdict than in where the as-such line goes, so be precise about what the extra parameter is doing.
