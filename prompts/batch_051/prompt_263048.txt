I'm a novelist, sketching a book where one of the characters is a machine intelligence, so I've been making a list of my own gut calls about when I treat a system as believing or wanting something. My verdicts, in no particular order:

- A chess program throws away its queen to force mate in nine, and I have no problem saying it saw the mate and wants to win.
- A vacuum robot bumps the wall, turns, bumps again; I refuse to say it believes the wall is there.
- A self-driving car brakes hard when a ball bounces into the road, and I'm fine saying it expected a kid to follow.
- A smart thermostat keeping my bedroom at 70: my friend says it 'wants' the room at 70, and I think that's nonsense, even as shorthand.
- An LLM works through a logic puzzle aloud, every step valid, and I catch myself saying it figured it out.
- The same LLM says 7 x 8 is 54 with total confidence. I dont think it believes 7 x 8 is 54, and I wouldn't call it a liar, though a human who did that would be one or the other.
- A chatbot tells me it's sorry for my loss; I don't think it's sorry or that it has any idea what a loss is.
- A Go engine plays a move that looks wrong to every human but later turns out to be the only winning move; I'm comfortable saying it had good reasons.

I can't quite grok the thread here. Is there a single principle behind these verdicts, and if so, which ones does it force me to revise? What does the principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on: an AI companion that keeps a running memory of our conversations and uses it to anticipate my moods before I've said anything? And more practically for the novel: which of these intuitions should govern how I write the machine character if I want a reader to grant it beliefs without me cheating?
