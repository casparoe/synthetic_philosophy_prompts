Okay so I keep noticing a thing in myself and my students and I don't have a word for it. I teach a probability course and I spend the first week telling people to treat any claim they're unsure about as a proposition, put a number on it, update by Bayes, the whole routine. But mathematical claims seem to break the recipe in a way I can't quite articulate. Last week a student had 50% on Goldbach's conjecture and 80% on 'every even number bigger than 2 is the sum of two primes,' which is literally the same statement said differently — and when I pointed that out they immediately agreed the two are equivalent, but before I pointed it out they seemed to hold both numbers at once. That's not ordinary sloppiness, because they'd have endorsed the equivalence if asked; the mismatch came from the equivalence not being computed right then. Standard probability theory would just call this incoherence, sure, but ordinary incoherence is supposed to be fixable by sitting down and being careful, and this kind can survive any finite amount of care — there are infinitely many logical consequences and equivalences, and nobody can enumerate them. I get the same feeling when I assign probabilities to open problems: my number seems to track how likely I think the community is to settle it soon, not some hidden chancy process about whether it's true. So here's my question: is there an established name for this — uncertainty whose object is uncomputed logical structure, as opposed to uncertainty about a future coin flip or even about an already-flipped coin you haven't seen? If there is a name, what are its nearest cousins and how is this narrower? If there isn't, what would you call it, and where exactly is the boundary between 'just hasn't run the computation yet' and 'genuinely irrational'?
