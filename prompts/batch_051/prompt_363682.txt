Been having a running argument with my little brother about long-term AI predictions—he says nobody should trust doomy forecasts because Malthus made a long-run prediction and was wrong, so I finally went back and read the Essay. This bit from the first chapter stuck with me (Malthus, An Essay on the Principle of Population, 1798, ch. 1): "It is an acknowledged truth in philosophy that a just theory will always be confirmed by experiment. Yet so much friction, and so many minute circumstances occur in practice, which it is next to impossible for the most enlarged and penetrating mind to foresee, that on few subjects can any theory be pronounced just, till all the arguments against it have been maturely weighed and clearly and consistently refuted." im trying to figure out what i actually think about all this, and whether the following hings hang together. (1) Malthus's core claim—that population presses against subsistence and pushes living standards back toward it—was basically right for the preindustrial world, so saying he was just "wrong" is too quick. (2) I also accept the standard empirical verdict: in the century after he wrote, at least in England, population grew quickly and real wages rose, so the prediction he made failed when it was tested. (3) I think you can't in general rescue a failed prediction by saying it will come true later—if the core prediction fails in the domain it was actually about, that's disconfirmation. (4) But I also think "right but early" can be a legitimate status when a theory identified a real mechanism but the background conditions later changed for reasons outside the theory (fossil fuels, the demographic transition, that kind of thing). The Malthus passage is partly what's messing with me, because you can read him as saying a just theory won't always get confirmed by experiment right away, which sounds like permission for (4). So my question is whether this is a consistent package or whether I'm quietly cheating somewhere. Give me an audit rather than a lecture: which commitments genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest repair, and what does it cost?
