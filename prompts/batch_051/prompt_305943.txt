I'm a novelist, and the vocabulary I actually think in is craft vocabulary: drafts, revision, first person versus close third, interiority, voice, free indirect style, the difference between a note I write for myself and a sentence that has to earn its place for a stranger. I've internalized one rule from that world more than any argument I've read: a sentence isn't finished when it matches what I felt; it's finished when it can do its work for someone who isnt me. Lately I keep circling Wittgenstein's private language argument, and it sounds like it's arguing for exactly that rule, but in a discipline I don't fully speak.

So here's what I want. Restate the private language argument, the whole shape of it — its claims about sensations, about the move where you point inward at a feeling and name it and what could make that naming right or wrong, about why a language no one else could in principle understand might not count as a language at all — in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Not an analogy, not a simplified version: a translation, the way you'd translate a passage into a register you know. Then tell me what didn't survive the translation. Is there anything in the argument that has no counterpart in the rule that a sentence is only finished when someone else could read it? And is that leftover a deficiency in the craft vocabulary, something it's constitutionally bad at saying, or a sign that the argument itself is looser than it looks once it's outside philosophy-speak? I care about the parts that don't map more than the parts that map cleanly.
