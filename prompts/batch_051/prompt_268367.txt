I have a standing policy for judging whether a large historical transition was, on the whole, good for the people who lived through it: I look at whether the people who still had the older way of life available kept choosing the new one, and I treat a transition that ran almost entirely in one direction—where virtually no one went back—as decisive evidence that the change was an improvement rather than a mistake.

I applied this policy last month in an argument about the agricultural revolution. A friend argued that the adoption of farming was a net catastrophe for human welfare, citing the usual evidence: early farmers were shorter and show more skeletal markers of nutritional stress and disease than hunter-gatherers; many foragers met their needs with far less labor than early farmers; and farming brought crowding, famine, epidemic disease, and durable inequality of a kind that mobile forager bands largely avoided. I replied that the transition ran only one way. Societies that adopted farming did not, in any substantial numbers, abandon it to return to hunting and gathering, even where foraging remained a live option nearby for centuries; farming and pastoralism ultimately spread across nearly all habitable land where they were viable. I concluded that this one-way direction is decisive evidence that successive generations had good reason to choose farming, and that the view of agriculture as a mistake must be missing something.

I now doubt that the test is as decisive as I made it sound. A practice can spread while making most of its individual bearers worse off, since a farming population can outgrow and displace foragers numerically even if each farmer is worse off; foraging skills are lost within a few generations; and once land fills up, reversion may be impossible even for a group that would prefer it. So I want to know whether the policy itself can be defended. Is a one-way-transition test a reliable way to evaluate historical changes? If it is not, is there a repaired version—one that keeps whatever is right about the thought that people with real alternatives kept choosing or retaining farming—without licensing me to dismiss the case against the agricultural revolution? Or was I using the test as post hoc support for a prior I did not want to give up?
