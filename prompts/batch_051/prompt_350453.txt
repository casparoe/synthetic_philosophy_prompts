The dispute about freedom in political philosophy seems to me to have been stationary for a long time. Isaiah Berlin set the terms by separating negative liberty—roughly, freedom from interference by other agents—from positive liberty—roughly, self-mastery or rational self-direction. The republican alternative, developed in its contemporary form by Philip Pettit, then claimed that both miss what matters most: freedom as non-domination, the secure standing of a person who need not live subject to another's arbitrary will. Yet the same stock of cases keeps circulating, and each side reads the same case as confirming its own theory: the contented slave whose master never interferes; the prisoner; the addict; the worker who must accept harsh terms because there is only one employer; the person shaped into wanting only what she is permitted to do; the subject of a mild autocrat who rules well.

Almost everyone now accepts, in some version, MacCallum's point that freedom claims have a common three-place form—an agent is free from some constraint to do or become something—so the dispute is not about logical form. But that point has not produced convergence; it has mainly exposed that the disagreement sits inside the specification of the three places.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey. My suspicion is that the positive/negative/republican debate is really three disputes running under one name. The first is conceptual: which items may occupy the constraint position—only other agents' actions, or also internal obstacles such as compulsion, ignorance, and adaptive desires, or also facts about the option set itself, such as poverty and lack of capacity? The second is normative: given that every social arrangement trades some freedoms against others, which unfreedoms are politically salient or unjust, and why? The third concerns the subject of freedom: is the agent whose freedom is at issue the empirical person with her present preferences, or a self that can be in error about its own interests, so that freedom may require self-mastery rather than the satisfaction of current desires?

Tell me, as precisely as you can, what kind of disagreement this is: verbal, substantive, normative, or several distinct disagreements under one heading. Where do the three positions make incompatible claims about the same thing, and where do they merely define terms differently or answer different questions? And given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a linguistic or experimental test, a formal reconstruction of the concept, a political argument about which constraints matter, a change of question—or is some part of the impasse one we should expect to be permanent, and why?
