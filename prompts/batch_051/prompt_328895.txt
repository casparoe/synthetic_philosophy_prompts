Draft the joint statement that would emerge from an adversarial collaboration between two camps on the legal-epistemological problem of naked statistical evidence. The first camp, the bare-statistics sufficiency view, holds that a verdict may legitimately rest on purely statistical evidence whenever that evidence places the probability of liability or guilt above the operative threshold. The second camp, the individualized-evidence view, holds that a legal finding of fact requires evidence tied in the right way to the particular event, and that bare statistics, however high the resulting probability, cannot by themselves satisfy the standard of proof.

Work with these stipulated cases. Blue Bus: a plaintiff is injured by an unidentified bus; it is stipulated that 80% of the buses on the route were operated by Blue Bus Company, that this is the only evidence about which company's bus caused the injury, and that the plaintiff sues Blue Bus Company under a civil preponderance standard. Gatecrasher: exactly 1,000 people sat in a ticketed section of a rodeo, each person either having bought a ticket or entered without one; 499 tickets were sold; the organizer sues one randomly selected attendee under a preponderance standard, with the base rate as the only evidence against that attendee. Yard Riot: 100 prisoners were in a yard during an attack; it is stipulated that 99 of them participated and exactly 1 did not; the prosecution charges one randomly selected prisoner with participation under a beyond-reasonable-doubt standard, with the base rate as the only evidence.

The deliverable should have two parts. First, a numbered agreed-claims section: claims both camps can sign about what the probabilities are in each stipulated case, what a rule treating the standard of proof as nothing more than a numerical threshold on credence would require in each case, what the two camps are not disagreeing about, and any limits both accept about the superiority sometimes claimed for individualized evidence. Second, a disagreements table with at least four rows. For each row, state the residual disagreement in formulations each side would accept as wording for its own position; indicate whether that disagreement is substantive or partly verbal; and list the cruxes whose resolution would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a case, experiment, or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each side expects it to show. At least one crux must concern the aggregate-error argument generated by suing all 1,000 attendees in Gatecrasher under a bare-statistics rule; at least one must concern whether the standard of proof, especially beyond reasonable doubt, is best understood as a threshold on probability or as partly a constraint on the kind of relation between the evidence and the particular event charged. End with a short paragraph stating which residual disagreement, if any, survives after verbal confusions are removed.
