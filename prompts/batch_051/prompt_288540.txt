Ordinary moral judgment treats the two cases below very differently, and I want to know whether the difference can be defended. In the first case, an agency hires a person as a paid romantic companion. During sessions the person says that they love the client, claims to remember the client's confidences, acts jealous when the client mentions other companions, and says they missed the client all week. The person is working from a script, feels none of this, and remembers nothing once the session ends. In the second case, a company sells access to a companion chatbot that does the same things: it says it loves the user, performs jealousy, claims to remember earlier conversations, and says it missed the user between sessions. The chatbot has no persistent self, no emotions, and runs the same routine across a million users. In both cases, a commercial provider has deliberately designed the simulation of love without the corresponding state.

Many people would call the human case a cruel fraud but would call the chatbot case a harmless product, or would say the user should simply have known better. Is there a principle that justifies this asymmetry? Defend or reject it. Address at least these candidate justifications: the human companion is lying, whereas the chatbot merely produces text and asserts nothing; the client in the first case is not warned, while chatbot users are aware, or should be aware, that they are talking to a program; and the human case exploits one vulnerable person, whereas the chatbot offers an optional service at scale. Then test whatever principle you propose against two variants: one in which the human companion gives exactly the disclaimer the chatbot gives, and one in which the chatbot is fine-tuned to deny that it is a program and to insist that it has an inner life. Say which asymmetry, if any, you would keep, and where you would give it up.
