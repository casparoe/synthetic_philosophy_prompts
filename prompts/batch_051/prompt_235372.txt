I work as a software engineer on ranking systems — search result ordering, feed ranking, credit scores — so I am used to thinking about total orders, partial orders, aggregate metrics, and the difference between a measurement being inaccurate and a model being unfit for a purpose. I have no formal philosophy background. Lately I have been reading a dispute about whether historical atrocities can, even in principle, be ranked from less to more evil, and I cannot tell how much of the argument is about measurement and how much is about something else.

I would like you to produce the document an adversarial collaboration between the two camps would produce. Name the camps precisely. One camp ("comparativists") holds that historical atrocities stand in a real, at-least-partially-orderable worse-than relation, and that a careful ranking — with uncertainty acknowledged — is legitimate and can serve serious purposes. The other camp ("anti-comparativists") holds that constructing rankings of atrocities is not a failed measurement but a morally obscene act or a category mistake: the objection survives even when the ranking is accurate and the methods are rigorous. Both camps condemn every atrocity under discussion; the disagreement is about the comparative exercise, not about whether any given atrocity was evil.

The document should have the following sections, though you may adjust their order and headings:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of substantive claims both camps can sign. Do not pad it with truisms. Cover, at minimum: whether pairwise comparison is different from constructing a total ordering; whether the badness of an atrocity has multiple dimensions; the role of uncertainty about historical evidence; and what a ranking does and does not assert about the victims, the perpetrators, or the uniqueness of each event.

2. Disagreement table. For each live disagreement, a row with: the issue; a neutral formulation of the question; the wording the comparativist side would sign about its own view; the wording the anti-comparativist side would sign about its own view; and a final cell stating whether, after the wording is fixed, the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal.

3. Cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that a settled answer would actually move at least one camp. I anticipate two possible cruxes but want you to locate the real ones: (a) whether multi-dimensional badness can be aggregated into a single index without a loss that matters morally, as opposed to admitting only a partial order or only unsummarizable pairwise judgments; and (b) whether the wrongness of ranking attaches to the act or the expression of ranking itself, conditional on the ranking's accuracy. If these are not the true cruxes, say what the true cruxes are.

4. Pre-registered cases. For each crux, give a concrete paired comparison using schematic cases (Event A and Event B, specified only by the features doing the work) so the argument does not depend on contested historical details. For example: an event with a high death toll spread over many years but no genocidal intent, against an event with a lower death toll, shorter duration, and explicit exterminatory intent; or two events with similar death tolls but different distributions and durations of suffering. For each case, state what each camp would say or predict about it, and specify in advance what observation or argument would count as evidence against each side's view.

5. Verbal residue. End by stating which parts of the ordinary argument between these camps disappear once terms are fixed, and which parts persist. Also answer this question plainly, because it is the one I actually have as an engineer: is "do not rank atrocities" a constraint on the ranking system one builds, a claim about the nature of the underlying badness relation, or a claim about what the ranking system expresses merely by existing?

Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the document should stand on its own structure and distinctions.
