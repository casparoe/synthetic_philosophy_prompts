I was present for an argument between two colleagues who both work in decision theory, and I was not able to referee it. I would like a point-by-point adjudication.

The setup they shared is the absent-minded driver problem. A driver is on a highway with two identical exits; he cannot tell the exits apart and cannot remember whether he has already passed one. If he exits at the first exit, his payoff is 0; if he exits at the second, his payoff is 4; if he continues past both exits, his payoff is 1. Because the exits are indistinguishable, any policy he can actually carry out assigns the same probability p of exiting, whichever exit he finds himself at. He knows all of this before setting out and wants to maximize expected payoff.

Before the argument, they agreed on two calculations. First, evaluated before the trip, policy p gives

(1 − p)(4p + 1 − p) = (1 − p)(1 + 3p),

which, over 0 ≤ p ≤ 1, is maximized at p = 1/3, with expected payoff 4/3.

Second, suppose the driver is at an exit and p governs both the current choice and any future choice. Conditional on being at an exit, the probability that he is at the first exit is 1/(2 − p), and the probability that he is at the second is (1 − p)/(2 − p). So the expected payoff of the policy p from that standpoint is

[1/(2 − p)]·(1 − p)(1 + 3p) + [(1 − p)/(2 − p)]·(1 + 3p) = 2(1 − p)(1 + 3p)/(2 − p),

which, over 0 ≤ p ≤ 1, is maximized at p = 2 − (√21)/3, approximately 0.472.

Then the argument went like this.

Marta: The relevant decision is made before the driver sets out, because that is when he can choose a policy and evaluate it as a whole. Among the policies he can actually implement, p = 1/3 uniquely maximizes the trip's expected value, so that is the rational policy. If he would begin to prefer something else once he is at an exit, that just has the familiar shape of a dynamically inconsistent preference; the standard response is to treat the earlier, plan-level evaluation as authoritative rather than to let a temporally situated self overturn it.

Jonah: That assumes the pre-trip standpoint is privileged, but it is not. When the driver is actually at an exit, he has evidence he did not have before, namely that he is at one of the two exits, and a rational agent maximizes expected utility given all of his evidence. Given that evidence, the policy that maximizes expected payoff is p ≈ 0.472, not p = 1/3. The ex ante calculation deliberately ignores the very information the agent now has, so it is not an answer to the choice he now faces.

Marta: That is not what conditionalizing alone gives you. Suppose the driver at the exit chooses only his current action, with his belief about which exit he is at and his expectation about his future behavior both based on some strategy p. Then exiting has expected value 4(1 − p)/(2 − p), while continuing has expected value 2(1 + p)/(2 − p). So exiting is strictly better only when p < 1/3, continuing is strictly better only when p > 1/3, and the unique self-consistent point is again p = 1/3. To get p ≈ 0.472 you have to treat the current and future choices as one single act — the whole policy p — rather than as separate choices. That is a substantive, contestable assumption about how choices at indistinguishable information sets are related, and packaging it as mere conditionalization does not make it neutral.

Jonah: The connection is not an added assumption; it is in the setup. The two exits are indistinguishable and the driver's decision procedure cannot tell them apart, so it has to produce the same probability at both, which means the only coherent object of choice is the common p. Moreover, even on the sequential reading you just described, at p = 1/3 the driver is exactly indifferent between exiting and continuing, each action being worth 8/5, whereas treating the policy as a single act yields a strict gain in conditional expected payoff from 8/5 to roughly 1.669. So the ex ante plan is at best weakly self-enforcing, while the situated agent has a strict reason to choose the on-the-road optimum.
