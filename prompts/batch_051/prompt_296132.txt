So I spent last night arguing with a friend about how math departments teach the history of calculus. The standard story is that Newton and Leibniz basically had it right but were sloppy about rigor, and then Cauchy and Weierstrass came along and cleaned it up. Meanwhile an undergrad who reasons with infinitesimals in a real analysis class gets marked wrong, but Leibniz's dy/dx notation is treated as totally fine and everyone says he sort of discovered the derivative. That whole practice, the textbook narrative, the way old results get translated into modern notation, the grading norms, seems to quietly presuppose that mathematical ideas are stable things that get discovered in pieces and later made rigorous, and that what Euler meant by a function is really our concept of a function, just fuzzier. What does that practice actually commit you to? Could you run a math department perfectly well while denying that the old authors were talking about the same objects we talk about, or does teaching and using their results force some amount of presentism? And if you did take the line that the old authors were working in a different conceptual world, what concretely would have to change in how classes are taught and graded? I care less about the history than about the tacit philosophy of mathematics baked into how it is taught.
