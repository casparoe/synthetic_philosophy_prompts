I am giving the after-dinner speech at a retirement dinner for a macroeconomist who spent four decades in the Treasury and then a central bank, and who has quietly organised his life around an idea from Keynes's 1930 essay Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren. He takes seriously the thought that the economic problem, the struggle for subsistence, is not the permanent problem of the human race; once genuine needs are met, the harder question is how to use one's hours well. He draws a sharp line between needs we have simply as human beings and needs we have only because their satisfaction shows we are ahead of someone else, and he regards a career spent serving the second sort as a poor bargain. He has largely acted on this: he turned down most of the badges of professional importance, stepped back the moment his finances allowed, and has no intention of becoming an emeritus adviser, part-time consultant, or fixture on the conference circuit.

Write the speech for me. The room will be mixed: professional economists who will catch every Keynesian inflection, and family and friends who will not. The central difficulty is that the ordinary grammar of a retirement speech is quietly at odds with the view of the good life he actually holds. The conventional speech says he worked hard, produced a great deal, and has now earned some rest; that treats leisure as a brief reward and the person as the sum of their outputs. His Keynesianism reverses this: sufficient means are supposed to release a person for the real business of living, and the use of that freedom is not a consolation prize attached to a career. Please write something warm and lightly funny, roughly six or seven minutes when spoken, which honours him without praising the hours he logged, without promising that he will keep advising, writing, and attending conferences, and without implying that his post-retirement life is a lesser thing than being useful. It should not become a lecture either; the Keynesian structure should govern what the speech can and cannot say, rather than being explained in the abstract.
