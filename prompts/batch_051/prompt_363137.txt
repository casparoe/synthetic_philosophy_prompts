I'm a neuroscience PhD student, and a few months ago a friend in the philosophy department sat me down with the causal exclusion argument. The rough version: if the physical activity in the brain is already a complete cause of my movements, then the mental description—the intention, the expectation, the decision—seems to have no causal work left to do. Its probably too crude put that way, but it got under my skin.

Since then I've adopted a rule for how I talk and write about behavior in the lab: I don't let mental states take causal credit. Not "the rat pressed the lever because it expected the pellet," not "the participant's intention caused the button press." In papers and lab meetings I rewrite these as: the expectation is realized in such-and-such neural process, and that process does the causing; the mental gloss is just a stand-in or a coarse summary with no extra causal information. I still talk normally at home ("I reached for my keys because I wanted to leave"), and I dont actually believe my wanting is causally inert. But my advisor says my official writing has quietly become epiphenomenalist, and I've noticed I now discount papers whose interpretation leans on mental causes, and I correct undergrads who say "she decided to stop." So the rule is doing more than tidying prose. Is it defensible as an explanatory policy? Is there a version that keeps the rigor without sliding into a metaphysics I never actually argued for, and how would I know if I've already slid?
