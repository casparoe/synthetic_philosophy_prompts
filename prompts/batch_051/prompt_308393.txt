I am a student in a philosophy of criminal law course, and we have spent several weeks on strict liability without ever stating precisely what is missing in the cases that strike us as punishment without fault. I would like you to propose a working definition of criminal fault: the element whose presence makes condemnation seem warranted and whose absence makes liability look like mere punishment without fault. Then test the definition on the following six cases, saying for each whether fault in your sense is present, absent, or only ambiguously present: (1) a pharmacist who fills a prescription exactly as written, where the physician wrote a lethal dose and the patient dies; (2) a cashier who sells alcohol to a teenager presenting a convincing false ID; (3) a driver who glances down to adjust the radio and strikes a pedestrian; (4) a landlord fined for lead paint that a previous owner applied before the landlord bought the building; (5) a defendant who knew the physical facts of the act but believed, as a symptom of psychosis, that God commanded it; and (6) an expired-meter parking violation. To anchor the exercise: I am fairly confident that (3) involves fault in some extended sense and that (6) does not require fault at all, while I keep changing my mind about (2) and (4). After running the definition through all six, tell me which classification is least secure, and whether that instability reflects a defect in the definition or a genuine ambiguity in the concept itself.
