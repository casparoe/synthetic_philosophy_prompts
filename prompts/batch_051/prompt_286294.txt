Having retired from a long career in the natural sciences, I have recently turned to philosophy, and I find myself troubled by a form of argument that seems to make a well-known statistical phenomenon into something that pure logic forbids. I hope you can help me locate my error precisely.

The argument concerns a treatment T and a control C tested on a population that is exhaustively divided into two subgroups, G1 and G2 (one may think of two sexes or of two clinical sites). It runs as follows:

(1) In G1, the recovery rate among those who received T is higher than the recovery rate among those who received C.
(2) In G2, the recovery rate among those who received T is higher than the recovery rate among those who received C.
(3) Every patient in the population belongs either to G1 or to G2.
(4) Therefore, for any given patient, whichever subgroup they belong to, receiving T would give that patient a higher probability of recovery than receiving C.
(5) If one intervention gives every individual a higher probability of recovery than another, then the first intervention must have a higher recovery rate in the population as a whole.
(6) Therefore, T must have a higher recovery rate than C in the whole population; accordingly, the familiar Simpson reversal—in which each subgroup favors one treatment while the aggregate favors the other—would be impossible in any dataset whose proportions are stated truthfully.

The argument has always struck me as a statistical analogue of what decision theorists call the sure-thing principle, and each step appears, taken alone, quite natural. Yet I have computed such tables myself and know that the reversal described in the final step can occur. So something must be wrong. Is the argument valid? If it is not, identify precisely the step at which it fails, characterize the fallacy, and state the corrected premise or additional conditions that would make the inference sound.
