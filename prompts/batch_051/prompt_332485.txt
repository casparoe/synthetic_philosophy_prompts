I’ve ended up on what I take to be the boring mainstream view about naked statistical evidence: a pure base rate shouldn’t be enough to support proof beyond reasonable doubt, no matter how high it gets. The usual cases make it vivid — if a plaintiff can show their bus was probably from the Blue Bus Company because that company runs 80% of the buses on the road, most people are fine awarding civil damages, but almost nobody thinks you could send the company to prison for homicide on that alone. Ditto the gatecrasher: 950 out of 1,000 people at the stadium paid nothing, I was at the stadium, so it’s overwhelmingly likely I jumped the fence; still doesn’t feel like proof beyond reasonable doubt. I think the reason isn’t just ceremonial or risk-averse. Something about the way the evidence is disconnected from the particular individual or act makes it the wrong kind of proof.

But I want you to try to break this view. What would count against it? Is the distinction between statistical and individualized evidence coherent when pushed, or does it collapse once you realize eyewitness testimony is just another probabilistic signal? Are there formal results or hypotheticals where naked statistics seem intuitively sufficient — and if so, what feature makes them sufficient? What would the world have to be like, or what case would have to come along, for my position to be wrong, not just in a lawyerly sense but in the sense that the principle itself is false? Give me a ranked map of exposure: fatal, serious, awkward. And tell me which considerations would actually move you if you held my view, and where your reasoning would be most likely to give out first.
