Here's a problem I keep coming back to, and I've realized I don't have a settled answer myself. We talk as if there's an obvious line between therapy (treating a disease or deficiency) and enhancement (going beyond normal functioning), but I've never found a definition of that line that handles the cases I actually care about. I'd like you to take a real run at it, not just survey the options.

Give me a working definition of "enhancement" — or of the therapy/enhancement distinction, whichever you think is the better target — that gets the following cases right:

1. Glasses that correct a child's myopia to 20/20.
2. Glasses or surgery that give the same child 20/10 acuity, better than typical.
3. A routine childhood vaccine.
4. Ritalin for a child with an ADHD diagnosis.
5. Ritalin for a healthy grad student studying for qualifying exams.
6. Ritalin for an adult whose attention problems are real but fall just short of diagnostic threshold.
7. Growth hormone for a child with growth hormone deficiency.
8. Growth hormone for an otherwise healthy child at the 1st percentile for height, whose parents are also short.
9. A beta-blocker for a violinist with performance anxiety.
10. Modafinil (a wakefulness drug) for a shift worker who is chronically sleep-deprived.
11. A carbon-fiber running blade for a double amputee (the sort Paralympic sprinters use).
12. An SSRI (antidepressant) for someone with mild, subclinical low mood who wants to feel better than they ever have, not just back to baseline.
13. Breast reconstruction after a mastectomy.
14. Breast augmentation for someone with no disease who wants a different appearance.

My own quick verdicts, for what they're worth: 1, 4, 7, and 13 are clearly therapy; 5 and 14 are clearly enhancement, and I guess I'd put 2 in the enhancement column though I'm not happy about it, since 20/10 is within the range some people are just born with. The vaccine case (3) bothers me most, because its neither treating anything nor intuitively an enhancement, but it sure leaves the immune system better than it was. And I genuinely don't know what to do with 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, or 12.

So: propose the definition, run it through all fourteen (or tell me if one of the cases is ill-formed), say where it gives a verdict you'd want to revise or defend, and tell me whether you think the distinction is doing real work or is just a moral/political line dressed up in medical language. And since I'd actually use this in a seminar: tell me how confident you are in the definition, and which single classification you're least sure of.
