Analyze the International Astronomical Union's 2006 redefinition of "planet" — the vote that demoted Pluto — as a case study for the principle of bivalence, the thesis that every meaningful declarative statement is either true or false. I don't need the astronomy rehearsed; I need the semantics. As background, the adopted definition requires that a planet orbit the Sun, be massive enough for its gravity to make it roughly spherical, and have cleared the neighborhood around its orbit; Pluto satisfies the first two but was judged to fail the third. Now take the sentence "Pluto is a planet." First, fix it before the vote, in 2005. Was it true, false, or neither at that point? Work through what a defender of bivalence is committed to saying, on what grounds she could say it, and what someone who allows truth-value gaps should say instead. Second, if "planet" was a vague predicate with borderline cases, what exactly did the vote do? Was it a precisification — a sharpening of a vague term that closes a truth-value gap — or a stipulative change that altered the meaning of "planet" so that the 2005 sentence and the 2007 sentence differ in content, or a discovery of a fact that had already settled the question? Lay out what each reading commits us to about whether the gap was real. Third, run the analysis in the other direction: what does the Pluto episode show about bivalence and truth-value gaps? Does it suggest that many apparent gaps are just unsharpened predicates waiting on stipulation or discovery, or that at least some gaps are genuine and vote-proof? Where does bivalence handle the case well, where does it misdescribe it, and where is it simply silent? Be disciplined about the mapping, and don't force the episode to speak for either side.
