I'm putting together an undergrad philosophy unit on intellectual property and the ownership of ideas, and I've hit the usual problem: every essay I read argues from one pet case (a two-second sample, a drug patent, a fanfic) and then quietly generalizes to all of copying. I want to flip the order. Before I show students any named theories, I want a spec: what does an adequate theory of when a person may copy, use, or build on someone else's idea or expression actually have to get right? I don't need citations or a reading list, just the desiderata themselves, each with the case or consideration behind it, and a note on whether it's non-negotiable or just the preference of one camp.

For my course the theory has to speak to both expressive stuff (novels, songs, code, characters) and functional stuff (drugs, seeds, industrial processes), so I dont want a theory that secretly only fits copyright or only fits patent. The list should at least let us decide these cases sensibly: buying a used textbook and reselling it versus sharing the PDF of it; a remix built around a two-second sample; a company patenting a gene sequence or a molecule isolated from a plant that a local community has used medicinally for generations; a company tweaking a drug formulation just as its patent lapses; an orphaned work whose rightsholder can't be found; noncommercial fan fiction; reverse engineering a competitor's software; and someone giving a conference talk that is reworked by an audience member before the speaker publishes. For each requirement say wether it's non-negotiable or a camp preference, and tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories. If two of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied (strong incentive rewards versus cheap access, for example), flag that plainly. Then, in one short paragraph each, say how a Lockean labor-desert view, a Hegelian personality view, and a utilitarian incentive view fare against the list—but keep that part brief, I mostly want the criteria.
