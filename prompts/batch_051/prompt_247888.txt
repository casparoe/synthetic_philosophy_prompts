I'm a retired physical chemist who's been working through philosophy of mathematics lately, and the Church-Turing thesis has been bugging me. As someone who spent a career around instruments and experiments, I'm used to asking of any claim: what would have to happen for me to change my mind about it? I cant get that kind of purchase with the Church-Turing thesis.

Here's how I understand it. There's an informal notion — what a human mathematician, a machine, or anything else can compute by following a finite mechanical procedure without insight or luck. The thesis says that informal notion coincides exactly with the formal notion of a Turing-computable function, equivalently with lambda-calculus definable functions, general recursive functions, and the rest of the usual formalisms. Because one side of that equation is informal, the thesis can't be proved like an ordinary mathematical theorem. The usual line is that the evidence consists of the convergence of all those independent formalisms plus the fact that nobody has ever found a counterexample. As a scientist that leaves me unsatisfied: nobody has found a counterexample looks like a null result, and the convergence of formalisms might only show that the same intuitive notion got formalized several times.

So my question is whether there is any actual test of the Church-Turing thesis — an observation, experiment, or designed search that could count for or against it, even in principle. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of mathematics and logic, think carefully, and start by brainstorming a few candidate tests: the convergence of formalisms, the failure of every proposed hypercomputer, systematic surveys of what working mathematicians judge to be computable, the behavior of physical systems, whatever seems strongest. Then pick the strongest candidate and design it concretely. What exactly would we look for? What result would favor the thesis and what result would count against it? How would we distinguish genuine evidence against the thesis from the possibility that we simply havent thought of the right computation yet? Finally, be honest about what even a positive result would leave untouched: which part of the thesis is genuinely epistemic and which part is really a definition or a stipulation dressed up as an empirical claim.

I should say I dont want a literature review or citations here, just your own reasoning. If you bring in terms like hypercomputation, define them briefly. And please don't retreat to "it's philosophy so it can't be tested." I want to know exactly how far the testing approach can be pushed before it gives out.
