Take the Abu Ghraib prison abuse scandal — the one that broke in 2004, with U.S. soldiers humiliating and abusing Iraqi detainees and photographing it — and analyze it through the situationist critique of virtue ethics. You know the rough story: the abuses came out, and the official line was roughly that a few bad apples were to blame. I want you to use the situationist position — the view that appeals to robust character traits don't do much explanatory work, and that features of the situation and the institutional environment drive far more of what ordinary people actually do — to work through the episode. Say what this view tells us the soldiers, the officers above them, and the investigators were facing and should have done, and what it explains about who ended up doing what: the soldiers who abused, the ones who went along, the ones who didn't participate, and the person who turned the photos over to investigators. Somewhere in there, separate the modest claim that situations matter a great deal from the stronger claim that what we call character is mostly retrospective storytelling, because Abu Ghraib might support the first without supporting the second.
