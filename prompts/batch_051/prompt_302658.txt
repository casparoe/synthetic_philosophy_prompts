Two friends in my reading group got into it last weekend about a hiking trail behind campus. The trail got re-routed after a landslide, and the volunteers rebuilt the washed-out section with gravel and bricks, so now it curves around the far side of the ridge instead of cutting straight up. On the way back, one of them said "same trail, new look," and that somehow turned into a full metaphysics fight. I mostly just listened, and I cant figure out who actually had the better of it.

Dana said the trail is plainly the same trail: things persist through change, and an object is wholly present at each moment it exists. She said that if you think the trail is made of time-slices — an earlier dirt-and-straight stage and a later gravel-and-curved stage, strung together like still frames — then nothing ever really changes, because the earlier bit is just one way and the later bit is just another; change becomes a difference between parts rather than something one thing does. She also said our ordinary identity claims (the ranger's map, the sign at the trailhead, "I walked this trail last summer") pick out one continuing thing, not a four-dimensional worm. Ravi said the whole-at-each-time view runs straight into a problem with change: in March the trail was dirt and straight, in May it's gravel and curved, so if the very same whole trail is present at both times, one and the same thing has incompatible properties — unless you relativize the properties to times. He said the clean fix is temporal parts: the trail is a four-dimensional thing with an earlier dirt-stage and a later gravel-stage, and calling it "the same trail" just means those parts are connected in the right way. Dana came back that she relativizes openly, not secretly: the trail is dirt-at-March and gravel-at-May, which is one thing having different properties at different times, no contradiction. She added that Ravi's worm sounds more like an event or a process than an object, and that if persons are like that too, then a promise made last year and kept today would be made by one temporal part and kept by another, which sits uneasily with ordinary responsibility talk. Ravi answered that time-indexed properties are suspicious, because "dirt-at-March" and "gravel-at-May" aren't properties the trail just has; they're relations between the trail and a time, and once you hide the time in the predicate you've quietly built temporal parts into the language. He also said the worm isn't an event — it's an object spread through time the way a trail is spread through space.

I think that's a fair reconstruction of what they said. Could you referee it? I want a point-by-point verdict: who's right about the problem of change, who's right about whether temporal parts make change unreal, who's right about time-indexed properties, and whether either of them is assuming something about how trails (as opposed to chunks of matter) persist. Then say who is more right overall, if either.
