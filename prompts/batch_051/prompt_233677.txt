I am a PhD student in formal epistemology, and I have been asked to draft the final essay question for an advanced undergraduate/master's seminar on bounded rationality. The unit I am responsible for is logical uncertainty and logical induction. Please write the exam question, a model answer of roughly 900 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks.

The course has presented logical induction as follows, and you should treat this as shared background rather than something the question tests as recall. A logically uncertain reasoner is modeled as a market in which probabilities are prices: on each day t the reasoner quotes a price p_t(φ) for each sentence φ of a formal language. A trader may buy or sell shares that pay 1 unit if φ is true and 0 otherwise, subject to a finite risk tolerance and a polynomial-time computability restriction. A logical inductor is a computable pricing process on which no such trader can earn unbounded profit over time. The results taken as established in the course include: for efficiently generated sequences of sentences, prices of provable sentences are driven toward 1 and prices of refutable sentences toward 0; and the reasoner's prices become approximately coherent in the limit on any fixed finite set of sentences. These guarantees are asymptotic and relativized to computationally bounded traders; they do not require coherence at any finite stage.

Design a three-part essay question built around one crux: why ordinary probabilistic coherence already presupposes an impossible logical omniscience for a finite reasoner, and why the logical induction criterion is best understood as replacing that synchronic requirement with a different kind of norm rather than as a mere weakening of coherence. Part (c) should confront students with this proposal from a classmate: because prices of theorems converge to 1, one can decide arithmetic by waiting until a sentence's price crosses 0.9 and then announcing 'theorem.' The question should force students to diagnose exactly why the proposal fails, distinguishing eventual correctness from certifiable correctness and saying precisely what the no-exploitation criterion does and does not guarantee.

The exam is a 90-minute essay. In the model answer, please keep two things strictly separate: (i) what is established within the framework, namely the convergence and no-exploitation results and their formal consequences, and (ii) what is genuinely contested among people working on logical uncertainty, for example whether the framework succeeds as a normative theory of rational belief under logical uncertainty and whether an asymptotic criterion is the right idealization. Do not present a contested reading as if it were consensus. The marking scheme should allocate the 20 marks across the three parts, name the specific moves that distinguish an A answer from a B answer, and note the characteristic error a B-level answer makes in each part. The question should be written so that a student who has only memorized labels ('logical omniscience,' 'logical inductor,' 'trader') cannot pass.
