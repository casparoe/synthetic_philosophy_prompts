I'm a law student working on a paper about mandatory arbitration, and I keep coming back to an old gym membership agreement I signed in college because its a nice artifact of how these clauses age. The arbitration clause reads: "Any dispute, claim, or controversy arising out of or relating to this Agreement or your use of the Services shall be resolved exclusively by final and binding arbitration before a single arbitrator, rather than in court. All arbitrations shall proceed on an individual basis; no claim may be brought on a class, collective, consolidated, or representative basis, and the arbitrator may not consolidate the claims of more than one Member." It was drafted in 2008, when the "Services" were a treadmill and a locker room. The company has since become a wellness platform: my membership auto-renews into an app that tracks sleep and heart-rate data, and the company sells that data to insurers.

Here's the case that's tripping me up. A consumer group stood up a website that auto-generates individualized arbitration demands against the company for alleged state privacy-law violations, and roughly 60,000 people filed in the first week — people call it "mass arbitration." The company responds with two arguments: (i) these privacy claims don't "arise out of or relate to" the Agreement or the Services as the drafters understood them, because the claims concern a data business bolted on long after the clause was written; and (ii) a bot-assisted wave of identical demands is in substance a class or collective action, so the clause's ban on class, collective, and representative proceedings blocks the whole wave, or at least lets the company shut it down without arbitrating each claim individually. I dont care much about the practical legal answer; I want the interpretive anatomy. Walk me through what the plain text, the purpose of the clause, what the 2008 drafters would have said if asked, and the reasonable member reading of the words each yield on those two arguments, then say which approach should win and why — or whether the clause is just genuinely indeterminate on these facts. And if mass filing is the only way individual claims become worth bringing, does the case tell us something about what mandatory arbitration actually privatized?
