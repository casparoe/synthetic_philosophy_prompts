So I've been rereading the Origin the other week, and I noticed something in chapter seven that I'd previously skimmed over because the eye and the geological record get all the attention. In the section on neuter or sterile insects, Darwin treats the sterile workers as a special difficulty, one that looked like it might be fatal to the whole theory, because the workers have these elaborate instincts and bodily differences but can't pass any of it on. His move is to say that natural selection can operate at the level of the family, not the individual: the fertile parents are still being selected for their ability to produce sterile offspring with those useful traits.

I want to think about whether that argument still survives a proper gene's-eye view. If we accept something like inclusive fitness or the modern gene-centred account, then the family-level language looks like it's doing the same work but with a shakier unit. But I'm not sure that's quite right, because Darwin's actual target is the intuition that the individual organism is the exclusive unit of selection. The sterile worker problem forced him to expand the unit before anyone had a clean notion of kin selection. So my question is: does Darwin's family-selection argument for neuter insects survive the modern units-of-selection debate, or is it a historical stopgap that we should now discard because the gene-level explanation is strictly better? And if it does survive, what exactly is the surviving part? Is it just the negative point that individual-level selection can't be the whole story, or does the family-level move still have a positive role?

I guess I'm asking whether the later theoretical development repairs Darwin's argument by precisifying it, or whether it shows the argument was aimed at the wrong level all along. Can you answer in British English? Cheers.
