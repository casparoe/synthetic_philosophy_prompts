I have been trying to work out whether my gut moral reactions actually hang together as an ethics of care, or whether they are just a familiar jumble of partiality, sentiment, and professional scruple. Here are my verdicts on a series of cases, recorded as honestly as I can. I want you to tell me what principle, if any, unifies them; what that principle implies about some further cases I have not settled; and which of my verdicts it might force me to revise.

1. A parent cancels a long-scheduled work presentation to stay home with a child who has a fever. I think this is not merely excusable but required.

2. A nurse breaks ward protocol and stays an hour past her shift to sit with a frightened patient who has no family. I think this is fine, and I would praise it even though it burdens the next shift a little.

3. An emergency physician at a multi-casualty incident quietly arranges for his daughter, who has a moderate fracture, to be treated ahead of strangers whose injuries are more severe. I do not think this is fine.

4. A woman whose father was abusive has not spoken to him in twenty years. When he becomes frail, she refuses to care for him or to pay for his care. I do not think she owes him that care.

5. A son arranges excellent, kindly professional care for his mother but visits only twice a year and leaves all the emotional work to the staff. I think he has not fully met the obligation, even though her material needs are met.

6. A man visits a disabled former coworker every Sunday because he promised to do so years ago, but he no longer feels anything and counts the minutes. I think he is doing something good, but I would not call it care, and I suspect that at some point the performance becomes dishonest.

7. A teacher gives extra attention to the pupils she finds endearing and leaves an equally struggling but difficult pupil to the worksheets. I do not think this is fine; care should not simply track how much one likes someone.

8. A man donates a kidney to a stranger abroad rather than to his brother, who then waits years and suffers badly. I cannot call the donation wrong, but I also cannot shake the sense that his relationship with his brother somehow failed, or that something in his priority was mistaken.

9. A person gives to famine relief every month but never forms any picture of the recipients. I think this is good, but I do not think it deserves the name care, and I am unsure care ethics is the best account of why it is good.

There is at least one pair of verdicts I cannot yet reconcile: (1) and (2) seem to license partiality, while (3) forbids it in what is otherwise a close relationship. Please say precisely what, in a care-ethical framework, explains the difference — or whether no single care-ethical principle can deliver (1), (2), and (3) together without becoming ad hoc.

Also please apply your principle, or candidate principles, to these unsettled cases: (a) a nurse who lies to a dying patient that her estranged son called, because it calms her; (b) a parent who forces a child to practice an instrument out of solicitude, but in a way the child experiences as control; (c) the attentive care a zookeeper gives to an aged, non-ambulatory chimpanzee; (d) a hospital that introduces empathy-training workshops while failing to hire enough nurses — do the workshops count as care in the relevant sense?

Finally, state the principle, or small set of candidates, precisely, tying each clause to the case or cases that motivate it; say which of my listed verdicts, if any, it cannot accommodate; and tell me whether that is a reason to revise the verdict or to modify the principle.
