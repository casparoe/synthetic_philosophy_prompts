I am taking a seminar this semester on moral disagreement and social choice, and one idea we keep circling back to is "moral trade": the thought that people with deeply conflicting moral commitments can still make exchanges that each side regards as a net moral improvement by its own lights. A vegetarian might agree to eat meat at a family wedding in exchange for an omnivore relative making a substantial donation to an animal welfare charity; a strict deontologist might make a donation they regard only as supererogatory in exchange for a utilitarian friend agreeing to spend a month avoiding every minor deception, which the utilitarian regards as trivial and the deontologist regards as a matter of principle. Each party does something they would not otherwise do, at some personal or moral cost, in order to get the other to do something they themselves regard as morally important, and neither changes their underlying view.

For our final paper we have been asked to think through an institutional version of this idea, and I have been sketching a campus "moral exchange" with the following design. Any member may post a commitment of the form "I will do X, and I ask in return that someone do Y," where X and Y must be specified in advance and must be verifiable through receipts, logged hours, or time-stamped photographs. Each member must list the moral framework from which they are trading, and the system will not register a trade between two members who both list the same broad framework, since the whole point is to trade across disagreement rather than to coordinate among like-minded people. A committee verifies completion and maintains a public reputation score for each member, which rises when their commitments are completed and falls when they default. Members may walk away from an uncompleted commitment by declaring the trade void, but this is recorded on their public profile. The same act may not be counted toward two different commitments, and arranging trades with close friends who share your framework is treated as a rule violation.

I am not asking whether this is a good or bad idea in the abstract. I am trying to work out how it would actually behave: what it would incentivize participants to do, where it would get gamed first, and what second-order effects it would produce among members, among the verification committee, and in the way people speak about their own moral commitments. Does the design most obviously fail because cheap, easily verified actions drive out morally weighty but unverifiable ones, or because people exaggerate how strongly they hold a framework to improve their bargaining position, or because reputation becomes a currency in its own right, or because the whole arrangement functions as a form of moral licensing? Are these failure modes fixable by adjusting the rules, or do they reveal something about moral trade itself that resists institutionalization?
