I'm working on objective Bayesianism and I keep going back to this passage in Laplace's *A Philosophical Essay on Probabilities*. He says: "Strictly speaking it may even be said that nearly all our knowledge is problematical; and in the small number of things which we are able to know with certainty, even in the mathematical sciences themselves, the principal means for ascertaining truth—induction and analogy—are based on probabilities; so that the entire system of human knowledge is connected with the theory set forth in this essay."

Take him seriously for a minute: if even the stuff we usually treat as certain is connected to probabilities, then the question of whether there are rational priors isn't just a technical aside. I want the spec for an objective rule for assigning priors. What would a defensible rule have to get right? Give me the desiderata, with the case or argument behind each. Include at least: what it has to do with symmetries and ignorance, why it should or shouldn't be invariant under reparameterization, what it owes to updating and conditioning, and whether it needs to reproduce common-sense limits in games of chance or statistical practice. Flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which are more like philosophical taste (e.g., several people say Jaynes's stuff on transformation groups is doing important work and several say it's parochial). Then score Laplace's principle of indifference, maximum entropy, Jeffreys's priors, and reference priors against the list, and tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidates. Finally, say how confident you are in any claim that some pair of desiderata can't be jointly satisfied, and single out the point you're least sure of.
