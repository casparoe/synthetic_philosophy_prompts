Over the last couple of years I've gone down the wild animal suffering rabbit-hole, and now I cant get my old environmentalist instincts to line up with my animal-welfare instincts. I used to treat habitat destruction as basically axiomatic bad, but once you count the suffering of the individual animals rather than species or ecosystem health, the verdicts get weird. I want help figuring out whether there's a single principle that captures my gut calls on the cases below, or whether some of the calls have to go.

Here are my verdicts:

1. Draining a brackish marsh that is almost entirely mosquitoes and midges. Huge broods, most of them eaten or dead within days. My gut says this is fine, maybe even a net improvement, because you're mostly preventing the creation of enormous numbers of very short, mostly miserable-looking lives. I dont feel bad about the individual mosquitoes.

2. Draining a healthy freshwater marsh with frogs, turtles, herons, the works. Long-lived animals that seem to have decent lives. Gut says bad, full stop, even though that marsh also contains a ton of insect suffering you'd be preventing.

3. Clearing a patch of old-growth forest and replacing it with a soybean monoculture. Deer, bears, songbirds, the usual. My gut says bad, but when I try to defend that I realize the soybean field will support far fewer wild vertebrates, so the long-run wild animal density plummets. Maybe the transition is bad (displaced animals starve) but the steady state is less suffering? I still want to say bad, but I can't say why.

4. Paving over a weedy vacant lot with a parking lot. Mostly insects, rats, maybe a groundhog. I feel basically neutral, maybe slightly good. The place is boom-and-bust invertebrate life; nothing in it strikes me as having a robustly good life.

5. Replacing forest with pasture for cows. Here I'm genuinely torn. If the cows have net-positive lives, the pasture might be an improvement over the wild residents it displaced, even though those cows get castrated, dehorned, transported, and slaughtered. But it doesn't feel like an improvement; it feels like swapping one kind of suffering for another.

6. Flooding a valley for a reservoir. The drowning and displacement of thousands of small mammals and reptiles on the day the dam closes feels horrific, even if the resulting lake eventually has fewer, longer-lived animals. I want to call it bad, but I suspect that's just my squeamishness about the visible deaths.

I keep reaching for a principle like: destroying habitat is good when it prevents lives that are on average not worth living, and bad when it destroys or prevents lives that are worth living. But that seems to require me to know whether a mosquito's life is worth living, and it makes case 1 and case 2 look identical until I've made a judgment call about the residents. It also gives me nothing for case 5.

So: is there a coherent principle that gets most of these, and which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? What does it say about cases I haven't settled, like rewilding farmland, or culling an overabundant deer herd so the survivors end up healthier? I'd rather have an ugly principle that fits the verdicts than a clean one that ignores three of them, but be honest about which of my verdicts are the ones that need to bend.
