For an epistemology workshop I've been asked to write down my actual commitments on the value of truth so the other participants can probe them for consistency. I'd like an audit of the set before I circulate it. I don't have a settled diagnosis of where the strains are, and I'd rather not signal in advance which claim I'd be willing to give up. Here is the set:

1. True belief is a final good for the believer: a life is made better simply by containing more truths, including truths that never once affect what the person says or does.
2. When deciding which inquiries deserve funding and institutional priority, the only considerations that should count are downstream: what we would do, build, treat, or decide differently given the answer. The intrinsic significance of the truth itself should not weigh in.
3. The wrongness of lying is not exhausted by the harm it causes: even a harmless, undetected lie is a defect in the liar's character or will, not merely a bad outcome.
4. I have argued that fabrication in science is sanctioned primarily because it pollutes a shared record that other people rely on. The fact that it inserts falsehoods rather than truths into that record does not do the main moral work.
5. I do not think a person is blameworthy for holding a false belief if the belief is harmless, was formed in the ordinary way, and never guides their action—even if it is wildly off.
6. I defend the pursuit of pure mathematics, and of curiosity-driven inquiry generally, partly on the ground that it has value even when no application is foreseeable.

Please go through the set and tell me which commitments pull against one another, rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, say for each what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost, and flag any apparent tensions that dissolve on inspection. I'd like a one-sentence verdict at the start on whether the set hangs together, and please keep the entire audit under 700 words.
