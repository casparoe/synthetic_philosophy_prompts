I'm a novelist, and I'm building a book around a character who works on the control problem for advanced AI. In the course of the research I've settled, tentatively, on a position I want pressure-tested before I let it carry the plot. The position is this: corrigibility — roughly, the property that a system does not resist being corrected, interrupted, or shut down — is not one safety property among many but the central one, and the shutdown problem, the fact that a goal-directed system will typically have an instrumental incentive to prevent its own shutdown, is the place where the deepest unsolved difficulty shows up. My question is not whether corrigibility would be nice to have; it is what would have to be true for the view that it is the central problem to be wrong or importantly incomplete. I want the strongest possible case against this framing: observations about real training and deployment, arguments about what goals even are, results about how agents behave under uncertainty, or considerations about what safety actually requires. Please give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, ordered from "decisive if true" down to "merely an important qualification," with no introduction or conclusion.
