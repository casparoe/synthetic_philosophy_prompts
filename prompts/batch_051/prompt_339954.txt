Suppose we adopt the following working account of phenomenal consciousness: an organism counts as conscious just in case (i) its sensory states are integrated into a single point of view; (ii) those states are globally available for flexible, goal-directed action, rather than merely triggering fixed reflexes; (iii) at least some of its states feel good or bad to it; and (iv) it adjusts its behavior through experience in ways that go beyond mere habituation and sensitization. Now take the bay scallop, an animal I have been handling all season at the shellfish farm where I work. It has no centralized brain; its nervous system is organized around three pairs of ganglia. Along the edge of its mantle sit dozens of tiny eyes, each with a lens and a mirror-like reflecting layer. When a shadow passes over it, the scallop claps its shells together, jets water from inside, and darts a short distance away; it also closes up in response to direct touch. The limited experimental work available suggests it can habituate its defensive responses, but it shows no clear evidence of flexible, multi-step problem-solving, and whether anything feels good or bad to it is precisely what is contested. Does the scallop qualify as conscious under the four criteria? Work through the criteria one by one. Where the evidence or the criteria leave the verdict genuinely open, say so rather than forcing an answer, and explain whether the scallop is best classified as a borderline case, as a nonmember, or as a case that shows one or more of the criteria themselves need revisiting.
