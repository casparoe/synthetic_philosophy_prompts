I am preparing an essay for a sixth-form philosophy competition on decision-making under moral uncertainty, and I have drafted some notes. I would be grateful for comments on the argument before I write it up. The notes are below.

Working title: 'Why moral uncertainty cannot be handled like ordinary uncertainty.' The intended target is the view that someone unsure which moral theory is correct should list one's credences in each live theory, score each available act under each theory, multiply, sum, and choose the act with the highest expected choiceworthiness. My claim is that this procedure only looks like ordinary expected-utility reasoning, and that the analogy breaks down exactly where it needs to hold.

Notes. (1) In ordinary uncertainty about the world, the outcomes of a decision are all measured in a single currency, and expected-utility rankings are unchanged by positive re-scalings of that currency: doubling all payoffs does not change which gamble is best. (2) Under moral uncertainty, the theories need not share a currency. Utilitarianism measures acts in expected well-being lost; a rights-based deontology says some acts are forbidden outright and supplies no quantity of how much worse one act is than another in the relevant sense; a virtue theory may have no numerical ranking of acts at all. Calculating expected choiceworthiness requires assigning a number to each act under each theory on a common scale. (3) The scale problem: because the theories themselves supply no common unit, each theory's scores may be re-scaled separately, and different permissible re-scalings can reverse the overall ranking. The proponent must therefore justify a privileged scaling, for example that one death averted under utilitarianism equals one promise broken under deontology. But that equivalence is itself a contentious first-order moral judgement, so the supposedly neutral method has smuggled the original disagreement into its units. (4) A standard reply is to normalise impartially, for example by setting each theory's least preferred option in the current choice set to 0 and its most preferred to 1. I suspect this fails because the normalisation depends on the choice set: adding a further option can stretch or compress one theory's range and reverse the ranking of the options already present. (5) My modest conclusion is that moral uncertainty is better described as a constraint than as a sum to be maximised. Each theory in which one has non-trivial credence may veto acts it regards as egregiously wrong; once several acts survive, the final choice must be made under some first-order commitment rather than a neutral meta-rule.

I would particularly value comments on three points: whether (3) is fair to the strongest version of the expected-choiceworthiness view; whether the normalisation objection in (4) succeeds, or whether some other normalisation evades it; and whether conclusion (5) is too deflationary, or whether that is itself the point. I would also be glad of suggestions for making the argument accessible in roughly 1,200 words for a general sixth-form audience.
