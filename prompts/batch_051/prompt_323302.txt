I've been collecting paradoxes about legal self-reference — the kind of trouble you get into once a set of rules is allowed to make and unmake rules, like in Nomic or in amendment clauses. Here's what I have so far:

(1) The self-repealing rule: a provision that says of itself that it's repealed or void. If it succeeds, it removes itself, so there's nothing left to have done the removing; if it fails, it's a valid rule that somehow can't do its only job.

(2) The self-validating rule, the bootstrapping one: a provision whose whole content is that it was validly adopted or is in force. A rule can't lift itself by its own procedural bootstraps — but if it can't, what is it even saying?

(3) The self-entrenching rule: an ordinary amendment adopts a provision saying it can never be amended or repealed. The amendment power created it, and now that same power is permanently bound by its own creature.

(4) The amendment-procedure suicide clause: you use the amendment procedure to delete the amendment procedure itself. The deletion looks valid if the old procedure was still in force when you used it, but invalid the moment it succeeds, since there's no procedure anymore.

What Im after is a fifth item: another structural pattern of self-referential rule-trouble that isn't a rewording, special case, or combination of these four. Maybe it's not about a single rule pointing at itself — I'm not sure. Whatever you come up with, say how it relates to the four I already have, and whether it's really a paradox or just an ordinary conflict of rules. And please write the answer in American English.
