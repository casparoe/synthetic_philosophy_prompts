I am a retired natural scientist — chemistry, mainly — who has only recently begun reading philosophy of science seriously, and I have found myself puzzling over an impasse in an old public debate rather than over anything in my former field. The debate is about whether Malthus was fundamentally right, and it has the curious property that the two sides seem to accept much the same historical record yet do not move one another. I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a verdict on which side is correct.

The first position, roughly, is that the Essay on Population identified a real and still-operating tendency: population can grow geometrically while the means of subsistence grow at best arithmetically, so that pressure against resource limits is recurrent. On this view the past two centuries do not refute Malthus; they show the pressure being repeatedly relieved by new inputs — fossil energy, synthetic nitrogen, improved crop varieties, the extension of cultivation into new land — and by the demographic transition. The core claim is, as they put it, right but early: it concerns long-run carrying capacity, and a theory with that horizon should not be expected to be overturned by a single century or two. The second position holds that this is precisely what is wrong with the theory. It survives, they say, only by being immunized against evidence: every failed forecast is explained away by a fresh auxiliary assumption, and the eventual limits are always receding. This side often points to the 1980 wager between Paul Ehrlich and Julian Simon over whether a basket of commodity prices would rise or fall over the following decade, and observes that the Malthusian side lost. The first side replies that a commodity-price index over one decade is not a test of a claim about long-run carrying capacity at all.

Coming from a discipline in which one tried to expose a hypothesis to a decisive test, I find myself unsure what kind of disagreement this is. My working hypothesis is that it is not fundamentally a dispute about the demographic and economic record, but about what could count as a falsifier of a long-run prediction: what one side sees as decisive disconfirmation, the other sees as an observation window too short to matter. But I can also see competing diagnoses. It might be a hidden empirical disagreement about how much substitutability and innovation is actually left. It might be a disagreement about scientific method — whether calling a theory “right but early” is a legitimate way to handle a long-run claim or merely the ad-hoc rescue of a refuted one. Or it might be a difference in bedrock intuitions about whether nature imposes hard limits that ingenuity can defer but never remove. Which of these, if any, best captures the structure of the dispute? And on your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a kind of evidence, a formal criterion, or perhaps a better-formulated question — if anything would?
