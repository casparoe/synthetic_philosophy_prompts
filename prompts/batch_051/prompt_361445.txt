In Turing's 1950 Mind paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence, in the section on the Argument from Consciousness, he considers the objection that the only way to be sure a machine thinks is to be the machine and feel oneself thinking. He paraphrases the objection and then basically says this demand leads to solipsism: if you required that, you could never be sure anyone else thinks either. So he offers the imitation game as a replacement for the question Can machines think? rather than as a direct test of thought itself.

Here's what I keep going back and forth on. Suppose someone builds a chess engine that plays at grandmaster level, and a critic says: that system has competence without comprehension. It doesn't understand chess, it just computes good moves. The claim sounds empirical—it makes a distinction between two possible internal states of the system—but I can't see how to get evidence for or against it as opposed to just saying we don't know whether it comprehends. Design the best concrete test you can, in chess or a simpler domain, that would push you toward either interpretation. Say exactly what result would count for each side, and then be honest about what part of the claim no test could reach even in principle. Is the difference between competence with comprehension and competence without comprehension a real difference, or is it the kind of thing we project onto a system after we've settled all the behavioral and mechanistic facts?
