I’ve been reading Schopenhauer’s *On the Basis of Morality* and Nietzsche’s *The Antichrist* back to back, and i keep getting pulled between them on the question of pity. Schopenhauer makes compassion the entire foundation of ethics: he says that the only morally genuine actions are those in which one person participates in another’s suffering as such, not because it benefits them or because they want to feel good about themselves. There’s a passage that’s stuck in my head where he writes that compassion is

> the immediate participation, independent of all ulterior considerations, primarily in the suffering of another, and hence in the prevention or elimination of it; for all satisfaction and all well-being and happiness consist in this.

He means that when I see someone in pain, the barrier between that person and me is, for a moment, actually lowered—I don’t merely *imagine* their pain, I suffer *with* them, and that is the only thing that makes actions morally good. And he’s pretty explicit that this is not a duty or a rational principle, it’s something that happens in experience, and the range of it is just the range of how much of the world’s suffering can get through your defences.

But then Nietzsche sees something rotten in exactly that. In *The Antichrist* he calls pity the practice of nihilism—he thinks it multiplies misery by making suffering contagious, that it undermines the very conditions for human flourishing because struggle, hardness, and even a kind of cruelty toward oneself are needed for anything great to grow. Pity, he says, is a hostile, depressive force that weakens both the pitier and the pitied. He’s not just being edgy: he’s saying that what Schopenhauer identifies as the sole moral phenomenon is actually a disguised will to comfort, a way of dragging everyone down to the level of the most miserable. So the two of them are looking at the same feeling and one sees the whole of morality in it while the other sees an enemy of life.

Here’s my problem. I find Nietzsche’s critique genuinely compelling—i can think of cases where someone’s compassion becomes a kind of invasive, smothering thing, or where it keeps someone in the identity of a victim, and i can feel the force of the claim that a life organised around avoiding suffering can’t produce much. But I also can’t shake Schopenhauer’s intuition: when someone really sees another person suffering and is moved to act, *just because* the other is suffering, there does seem to be something different about that act—it’s not egoism, it’s not self-congratulation, and it feels like the closest thing we get to moral reality. I don’t want to be told which side is right. I want to know what it would take for the Schopenhauerian intuition to be the thing that gets debunked, what it would take for the Nietzschean critique to be the thing that’s overstated, and whether there’s a reading on which both survive—e.g., a distinction between healthy and pathological forms of pity, or a way of separating the psychological origin of compassion from what it’s like and what it does in particular cases. And i want you to be clear about which parts of this are settled (if any) and which parts are genuinely contested among people who know these texts well. Please write in British English.
