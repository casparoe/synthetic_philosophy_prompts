You're a philosopher of language who works on speech act theory; think carefully and start by jotting down what you take to be the core commitments of each side before you write anything.

I want you to draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between an Austinian conventionalist and a Gricean intentionalist about performative utterances — utterances that do what they say rather than just describing something, like making a promise, placing a bet, ordering somebody around, or pronouncing a couple married. Treat both positions as serious, not strawmen.

The two agree that some utterances are actions, and that conveying information isn't the whole job of language. But the conventionalist holds that an utterance has the force of, say, a promise only because it satisfies a public, socially recognized procedure — the right person, the right words, the right situation — while the intentionalist holds that the force is fundamentally a matter of the speaker's meaning intention being recognized by the hearer, with shared conventions being just one fairly reliable way to get that recognition to happen.

Draft the document they would both sign. Give me: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims, non-vacuous ones about particular examples, about where the two explanations overlap, and about where they come apart; (2) a disagreements table with each remaining disagreement, the conventionalist's wording of their own view, the intentionalist's wording of theirs, and a judgment on whether that row is substantive or merely verbal; and (3) two or three cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — with, for each crux, a concrete case or test both sides accept as bearing on it (things like a promise made in an improvised private code that no public convention covers, or a performative produced entirely by an automated system with no human speaker present) plus each side's prediction in advance and what each side says would change its mind.

Really work at the wording untill it's neither vacuous nor slanted.
