Consider the paradox of voting in rational-choice theory. In a large national election, the probability that one vote is decisive (turning a defeat into a tie or a tie into a win) is astronomically small. Voting also has real costs: registering, informing oneself, traveling, waiting in line, and forgoing work or leisure. A purely instrumental expected-utility calculation therefore seems to imply that a self-interested rational voter should stay home, since the expected outcome difference of one vote, weighted by the probability of being pivotal, is worth far less than the costs. Yet turnout in many large national elections is substantial. The question is how to close the gap between the instrumental calculation and observed behavior.

Here are nine proposed explanations of voter turnout, stated plainly:

A. Process benefits: Voting is directly pleasant or meaningful to the voter—a civic ritual, a chance to be seen, an outing—so the time and effort are not experienced as a cost.

B. Expressive voting: The payoff is in the expression itself, as with cheering at a match, flying a flag, or posting a slogan. Voters get utility from expressing loyalty or moral outrage whether or not the vote changes any outcome.

C. Civic duty and warm glow: People have internalized a norm of voting; casting a ballot yields private satisfaction and abstaining produces guilt or shame, independently of any effect on the result.

D. Altruistic consequentialism: Voters care about the welfare of everyone affected by the outcome. Even if the chance of being decisive is tiny, it is multiplied by the enormous total stakes of an election, so the expected value of voting can still exceed the costs for an altruistic voter.

E. Pivotality overestimation: Voters are roughly rational but hold biased beliefs, systematically overestimating how likely they are to be decisive or how bad it would be if their side lost by one vote.

F. Mobilization and selective incentives: Parties, unions, churches, and campaigns lower the costs of voting with registration drives, mail ballots, and rides, and attach social or material rewards to turnout. Voter participation is produced by organizations, not by isolated calculation.

G. Social pressure and observability: When whether one votes is visible to family, neighbors, or canvassers, disapproval or the prospect of being asked about one's voting behavior raises the cost of staying home.

H. Rule-based or Kantian reasoning: Voters follow a rule such as "what if everyone stayed home?" or "do what would be best if universalized." They treat their action as part of a collective practice rather than as a lever that must pay for itself causally.

I. Habit and identity: Voting is a learned habit, often acquired early and reinforced by partisan identity, and then repeated without fresh cost-benefit deliberation each election.
