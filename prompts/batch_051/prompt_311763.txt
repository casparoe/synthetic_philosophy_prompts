I've ended up running a small fan wiki for the Sherlock Holmes stories—nothing serious, just a handful of us who argue about what counts as canon (and don't get me started on Watson's shifting war wound). Lately the debate has spiralled into something more abstract: if there was never any such person as Holmes, what does it even mean to say "Holmes is a detective" rather than "Holmes is a doctor"? A friend lent me an introductory philosophy of language book, and I copied out the passage on empty names; the book calls the standard view Millianism, though I couldn't say much about it beyond what's in the paragraph:

"On the standard view, a proper name's whole meaning is the object it refers to, so a name like 'Sherlock Holmes' is empty: there is no bearer for it to pick out. Sentences such as 'Sherlock Holmes is a detective' and 'Sherlock Holmes is a doctor' therefore fail to express complete thoughts and are neither true nor false. It follows that there is no fact of the matter about which of them is right, since there is no Holmes for either sentence to be about. When fans say it is true in the stories that Holmes is a detective, they are not genuinely asserting anything; they are simply joining in a shared game of make-believe whose rules the stories supply. Strictly speaking, nothing is true of Holmes at all."

Something about this passage leaves me uneasy, but the author writes with enough confidence that I can't tell whether I've actually spotted a mistake or am just being precious about an old obsession. I'd be grateful for a careful audit: which claims would a philosopher working on empty names or truth in fiction accept as fair, which are overstatements or run together things that ought to be kept apart, and does the final line—that nothing is true of Holmes—really follow from the earlier point that the name lacks a referent?
