i run the small-grants program at a mid-sized animal welfare foundation, and every quarter my committee has to compare interventions aimed at wildly different animals: a project to enrich housing for farmed pigs against one to cut sea lice infestations in farmed salmon, or a campaign to protect a wild elephant herd against a project that would improve the lives of billions of farmed crickets. we keep saying brain size or neuron count should matter for how much an animal's suffering counts, but every time we try to make it concrete someone produces a different number and the room splits. i don't want a one-off ruling on a particular pair of animals; i want a reusable decision procedure my committee can actually run.

the principle we're trying to operationalize is roughly: an animal's moral weight should track its capacity for welfare, and brain features — absolute size, neuron count, encephalization quotient, neuron density, whatever — are fallible proxies for that capacity. i want the procedure to keep what that principle actually says, but be runnable by four generalist program officers who last did neuroscience as undergrads, in under two hours per decision, and produce something we can defend in a three-page funding memo to a board that includes a former neurobiologist and a donor who thinks elephants matter more than insects, full stop.

spell out the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than fact, and where you expect the whole thing to misfire. then run it on at least three cases: pigs vs salmon, octopus vs chickens, and wild elephants vs farmed crickets — and for that third one, just let it land where it lands even if the procedure says something my board won't like. and please commit: give me your actual recommended thresholds and weightings, not a menu of options, then flag the one place you'd be most nervous defending the procedure in front of that board.
