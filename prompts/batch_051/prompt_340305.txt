I'm a graduate student in a formal epistemology seminar, and this week we discussed van Fraassen's Reflection principle. A friend in the statistics department, who hadn't done the reading but is a fluent Bayesian, later summarized the principle for me as follows: "Reflection is the rule that your current credence in a proposition should equal the expected value of the credences you'll have later, so a rational person is required to adopt whatever opinion she expects to hold in the future. It's really just conditionalization stated over time, so any Bayesian already accepts it. The obvious problem is that you can know now that you'll later be exhausted, drunk, or upset and will believe things you'd now reject; a rational person can foresee her own future unreliability, so the principle is plainly false as a description of actual thinkers, and restricting it to ideal agents would just beg the question. The only surviving takeaway is that a rational person should treat her future self as an authority and should never plan to change her mind."

My professor, by contrast, described Reflection as a diachronic coherence condition on the credences you now assign conditional on your possible future opinions, not a piece of life advice, and hinted that the anticipated-exhaustion counterexamples misfire in an instructive way. I suspect my friend's version is largely right but subtly unfair, and I can't yet say exactly where the unfairness lies.
