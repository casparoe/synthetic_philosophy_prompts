Heres a rule I follow when I interpret things, and I want to know if its defensible. Whenever theres a dispute about what a text means—a novel, an email, a song, even a vaguely worded policy at work—I treat the authors own later statement of what they meant as basically decisive, as long as it doesnt flatly contradict the actual words on the page. For example, last week a friend and I were arguing about whether a song was sarcastic or sincere. I found an interview where the songwriter said it was sincere, and for me that ended it. My friend says this is just the intentional fallacy, and that a text means what a competent reader gets from the text itself; the authors later gloss is just one more interpretation from someone with an obvious stake in looking coherent. I get that, but I also know my own readings are biased and underinformed, so I lean on the author. Is my policy defensible? If it isnt, when should post-hoc authorial statements actually settle meaning, and whats a better version of the rule that keeps my deference from tipping into gullibility?
