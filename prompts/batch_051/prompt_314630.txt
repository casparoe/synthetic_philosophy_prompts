At a dinner party a few weeks ago, two friends of mine spent most of the evening arguing about a mutual acquaintance, Naomi, a woman in her late sixties. Naomi had been offered a place in a trial for a treatment that, on the best available evidence, would add roughly thirty years of healthy life, and she declined. Her stated reason was not fear of side effects or of the trial itself; she said that she wants her life to close while it is still whole, that a human life is a narrative that needs an ending rather than an indefinitely postponed one, and that she had held a version of this view long before the treatment was announced. I found the case harder than I expected, and my two friends took sharply opposed positions.

Here is the exchange as I remember it.

(1) Marcus said Naomi's decision is plainly rational. She has a coherent conception of a good life — a completed story with a shape, not a sheer maximization of years — and she applies it without factual error. He said we rightly respect a person's considered values when they are internally consistent and stably held, even when we would choose differently for ourselves.

(2) Elena replied that this is exactly what a rationalization would look like. She called it a case of deathism — not a wish to die, but the habit of converting an unavoidable limit into a positive ideal. Her main point was sour grapes of the oldest kind: when a good is perceived as unattainable, people lower their estimate of it and then sincerely experience that lowered estimate as wisdom. Death has been the one genuinely non-negotiable fact for all of human history, she said, so it is precisely where a culture would generate beautiful stories about finitude and the dignity of a natural close. Naomi's view may be sincere and stable, but adaptive preferences are usually sincere and stable; the relevant question is whether the value was formed under the shadow of the thing's unavailability.

(3) Marcus answered that Elena's test is impossible to apply. If we ask whether Naomi would value a natural death had she grown up in a world with life extension already available, the answer is probably no — but the same is true of nearly every deep value a person has. Raised elsewhere, she would value different things. By that standard every value is adaptive, and the phrase stops being a criticism of a particular preference and becomes a description of the human condition.

(4) Elena shot back that not all constraints are alike. Some are accidents of biography; death was a universal necessity that shaped every human value at once. A value formed under a universal necessity is exactly the one to interrogate when the necessity is finally removed, because the removal lets us see for the first time how much of the value was consolation. She added that the natural experiment is about to run: if a real therapy arrives, the hypothesis that the "narrative closure" view is mostly consolation predicts that it will be voiced disproportionately by people who expect to gain less from the therapy, while a hypothesis that treats it as a fully autonomous value predicts something closer to diffusion across the population. The data will matter.

(5) Marcus replied that Elena's whole procedure stacks the deck. Wanting more life is itself a default drive, shaped by evolution and by social reinforcement, and nobody is asking the extension enthusiast to prove that his preference is not a bias toward the familiar or an inability to accept limits. If a value's history makes it suspicious, the suspicion has to run in both directions. At the very least, he said, Elena had not shown Naomi to be irrational; she had shown only that Naomi's value has a history — and all values have histories.
