In much of contemporary physics, elementary particles of the same kind—two electrons, for example—are treated as numerically distinct individuals even though the formalism attributes exactly the same intrinsic properties to each: same mass, same charge, same spin, same everything. And yet the counting is not merely verbal: when a physicist says a helium atom has two electrons, she does not take herself to be saying the same electron twice. What theory of individuality and identity does this practice presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose the falsity of the principle of the identity of indiscernibles, or can it be reconciled with that principle by appealing to relations, weak discernibility, or some distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic properties? Press the question by considering Max Black's thought experiment of two exactly similar spheres in an otherwise empty universe: if that scenario is genuinely coherent, what does it show about whether the physics practice is a real counterexample to the principle or only a descriptive convenience? Reconstruct the implicit metaphysics of individuation that the practice commits one to, distinguish what the formalism strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.
