For the past several months I have been reading, on my own, about people who have had the main bundle of fibers connecting the brain's two hemispheres cut as a last-resort treatment for severe epilepsy, and I follow a discussion group in which two members have been arguing about what these cases show about the unity of consciousness. One of them defends what he calls the single-stream view: after the surgery the patient is still one person with one stream of consciousness, and the strange experimental results reveal broken communication inside a single mind rather than a mind that has actually divided. The other defends what she calls the dual-stream view: after the surgery there are two separately conscious streams sharing one body, and only one of them can normally speak.

The two of them agree on the basic facts, which is part of why the argument has gone in circles. In the standard setup, an image shown only to the speaking hemisphere can be named aloud, but an image shown only to the silent hemisphere cannot be named, even though the left hand can point to or draw the correct match. In the famous chicken-claw-and-shovel setup, the speaking hemisphere is shown a chicken claw while the silent hemisphere is shown a snow scene; the patient's right hand picks a chicken, the left hand picks a shovel, and when asked why the left hand picked the shovel, the speaking hemisphere invents an explanation about cleaning out the chicken coop rather than saying anything about a snow scene it never saw. They also agree that the two hands sometimes seem to act at cross purposes, as though the hemispheres had different intentions, while in everyday life the patients often appear roughly normal. What they dispute is what these facts imply about how many streams of consciousness there are, and what could even settle such a question.

The two of them have now decided to try a good-faith adversarial collaboration and produce a joint statement, and I want that document. It should begin with a numbered list of claims both sides can sign: not just the uncontroversial anatomy and experimental results, but the strongest shared interpretation they can honestly defend, including remarks about what each side would count as evidence. It should then lay out the remaining disagreement precisely, first in neutral terms and then as each side would word its own position, so that neither feels caricatured. Next it should identify the cruxes: the sub-questions on which the dispute turns, the ones such that a settled answer would move at least one side. For each crux it should describe a concrete case or experiment both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If any part of their dispute is really about words, for example about what counts as a single stream or a single subject, say which part and why. Format the result as an agreed-claims section followed by a table of disagreements.
