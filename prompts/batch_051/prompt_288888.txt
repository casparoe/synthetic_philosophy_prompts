I have recently been volunteering with a land trust, and at a meeting someone described an overgrown parcel as something we were morally obliged to preserve because it is habitat. I asked whether anyone had considered the aggregate welfare of the animals that parcel would produce over the next fifty years, and whether a parking lot might actually be better for animal welfare even if it is worse for biodiversity and beauty. The question landed badly, but I kept thinking about it, and it led me to a view I have been calling the Recolonization Discount.

Start from a premise I am only granting for the sake of argument, not asserting: that most wild animals, especially small, fast-breeding vertebrates and insects, live lives in which suffering outweighs enjoyment, so that a healthy, densely populated habitat is, considered as a system, a producer of net suffering. It seems to follow that destroying such a habitat spares a great deal of future suffering. I suspect the naive form of that argument double-counts the benefit. Cleared land rarely stays cleared; it is recolonized, often within a season or a few years, by species that specialize in disturbed ground, such as rodents, generalist birds, and pioneer insects, and these are exactly the fast-breeding, high-mortality species whose lives the premise says are among the most negative. So the welfare gain of destroying a patch is not the elimination of the old community's future suffering. It is the difference between the old community's net welfare and the replacement community's net welfare, minus the suffering of the transition itself. I call that the Recolonization Discount: the reduction, for welfare purposes, in the expected benefit of destroying habitat once you account for the community that will replace the one you destroyed.

I want an honest assessment of this as a concept rather than as a policy conclusion. Is the discount a real feature of the welfare calculation, or does it rest on a false picture of how recolonization actually works? Does it reduce to something already defended under a less clumsy name, such as a replacement argument applied to predation or disturbance ecology? And am I wrong to treat it as a discount rather than something that can exceed one and flip the sign of the calculation, so that some habitat destruction turns out to be welfare-negative because the replacement community is worse? Tell me the strongest objection to treating it as a discount, and say where your verdict is most fragile: which empirical finding about recolonized land, or which change in the underlying welfare premise, would most change your assessment.
