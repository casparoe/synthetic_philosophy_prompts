Take the Gibbs paradox in statistical mechanics and analyse it as a case study in the identity and indiscernibility of quantum particles. I'll set the episode up so you don't need to know the details ahead of time. Two identical volumes of the same ideal gas at the same temperature and pressure are seperated by a partition. Pull the partition out. Thermodynamically nothing happens: same gas, same temperature, same density afterwards, so the entropy shouldn't change, and it doesn't. But if you count microstates in the straightforward classical way, treating each molecule as a labelled particle, the state count balloons once the partition is gone (each of the 2N molecules can now be anywhere in the whole box, a factor of 2 to the 2N), and you'd predict an entropy increase of 2Nk ln 2, with N molecules on each side, for an operation that is perfectly reversible. That's the paradox for identical gases. For genuinely different gases, by contrast, there is a real entropy of mixing, and it has exactly that same form. Gibbs's fix was to divide the state count by N! to treat permutations of identical molecules as the same state, and quantum mechanics later gave the move teeth: for truly identical particles, swapping two of them doesn't produce a new state at all, and symmetry or antisymmetry is built into the counting.

What I want you to do is run the analysis both ways. First, use the metaphysics to explain the physics: does the quantum resolution show that identical particles are not individual objects, or only that we can't keep track of which is which and need to count our ignorance differently? What would Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles say about the two gases, and does the paradox confirm it, refute it, or leave it beside the point? Second, use the physics to constrain the metaphysics: if you hold that quantum particles are individuals with a primitive thisness — a label not grounded in any property — can you still get the right thermodynamics, or does the entropy-of-mixing problem rule that sort of view out? Where does the mapping break down? For instance, two different isotopes are almost the same stuff and still give the full entropy of mixing, but make the gases exactly identical and the entropy of mixing drops to zero; the amount of mixing entropy doesn't scale with how different the gases are. What does that discontinuity tell us about the line between 'same' and 'different' particles? I'm genuinely not interested in the equations themselves; I want the conceptual question of whether particle identity is real or bookkeeping.
