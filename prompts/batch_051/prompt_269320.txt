I spent thirty years in a lab before retiring, and I've lately been reading philosophy of science and social science; one thing I can't turn off is the urge to chase an argument until it either breaks or takes me somewhere I can't live. Here's one that's been doing exactly that, about the gender-equality paradox.

(1) Cross-national gender-equality indexes are a reasonable proxy for how strongly a society pushes men and women into different roles.

(2) If sex differences in career interest (say, STEM vs people-oriented fields) were produced mainly by that social pushing, then societies that push less should show smaller sex differences, not larger ones.

(3) But the much-discussed finding is that more gender-equal societies show larger sex differences in STEM-related choices (I'll grant the finding is real enough for the sake of argument, even if the data has its critics).

(4) So these career-interest differences are not mainly produced by gendered social pressure.

(5) The main driver must therefore be biological—evolved differences in interests or aptitudes.

(6) And if that's right, trying to get more equal sex ratios in fields is fighting a natural cause, so it's either futile or coercive.

Every step sounded fine when I said it to myself, but (6) is a much stronger claim than I think the evidence can carry. I'd prefer not to reject (3) outright, tough I suppose I could pay that price if I have to. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then walk me through the reasoning; no preamble and don't restate the argument back to me.
