I am giving a departmental colloquium next month in a cognitive and social psychology program, and I would like a Q&A prep sheet. The talk defends the following thesis: the replication crisis is not best understood as a problem with frequentist inference as such, and the proposed Bayesian remedies — replacing p-values with Bayes factors or posterior probabilities, or switching from hypothesis tests to Bayesian estimation — would be gamed in the same way as p-values unless the field dismantles the underlying binary publication filter, the practice of treating a single study as a verdict about whether a finding exists and can be published. My argument has three moves: (1) p-hacking, optional stopping, and file-drawer effects are individually rational responses to the filter rather than mere sloppiness; (2) any threshold statistic — p < .05, BF > 10, posterior probability > .95 — creates the same incentive once careers depend on crossing it; (3) therefore reform should target publication and career incentives, such as result-blind publication, cumulative evaluation of evidence, and decoupling hiring and promotion from discovery counts, rather than the choice of inferential framework.

The audience: mostly experimental social and cognitive psychologists, including a reformist minority who already report Bayesian analyses and believe Bayes factors solve optional stopping; two senior quantitative psychologists who spent their careers teaching null-hypothesis significance testing and regard the campaign to abandon p-values as a moral panic; and one consulting statistician who regards Bayesian inference as the obviously correct framework and is sympathetic to the view that frequentist tools caused the crisis.

Please prepare me for the question period. I want the objections in roughly the order this audience will actually raise them — phrased the way each camp would phrase them, not as a statistician would phrase them — with, for each: (a) how likely it is and how damaging it is; (b) what I should concede up front; (c) a two-minute reply; and (d) a note on where that reply is weakest. I also want the question I should be hoping nobody asks, and what I should do if somebody asks it anyway. Make the objections concrete enough that the prep sheet could be used by someone who has read my slides: include the specific methodological counterexamples, such as optional stopping under a Bayes factor, calibrated posterior probabilities under assured vs. optional stopping, or preregistration as a filter-independent fix, rather than generic worries about subjectivity. Around 900–1,200 words.
