After a long argument with a close friend last night, I am no longer sure whether a policy I have followed for years is rationally defensible. The policy concerns how I choose among competing explanations. Whenever two or more accounts of some event or pattern fit everything I currently know, I try to estimate the length of the code each account would need: roughly, how many words or instructions it would take to state the account's assumptions and then derive the observations from them. I treat the account with the shorter code as not just slightly better but strongly favored, and I usually act on it and dismiss the others. A few weeks ago I applied this to a family dispute. My sister offered a detailed story about why a relative had behaved strangely, with several specific motives and a chain of coincidences, while I worked with a much barer account—two plausible features of the relative's situation—that seemed to me to cover the same facts in about a fifth of the words. I sided with the bare account, and I still think I was right in that case, but the argument with my friend has shaken my confidence in the general rule.

My friend pressed three objections. First, he said that the length of a description depends on the language or set of concepts one is allowed to use, so my rule merely relocates my prior commitments instead of disciplining them. Second, he said a short description can cheat by hiding all the real work inside the meanings of its terms; his example was that "he's just like that" is almost the shortest account imaginable, yet it does no explanatory work unless it quietly contains a whole theory of the person. Third, he asked how brevity is supposed to combine with probability: if both accounts fit the data, he said, then features such as independent testimony, novel predictions, or known base rates should matter at least as much as the number of words, and a bare account that ignores them is not more rational, just more compressed.

I would like to know whether my rule is defensible as stated. If it is, what exactly licenses treating shorter descriptions as strongly favored, and how should I answer the language-dependence and hidden-complexity objections? If it is not defensible as stated, what is the closest defensible rule, and what does it leave out?
