I'm working on a feature about why some moral practices just evaporate, and a historian I interviewed—he writes on the death of dueling—gave me an argument over coffee that I can't shake. The problem is that it proves to much: if the argument works, it seems to take down all of moral progress, not just the cases it's aimed at. I've tried to find the bad step and keep ending up unable to give anything up.

His argument went like this. (1) If a practice ends because the incentives of interest, status, and shame shift, rather than because the better arguments against it actually persuaded people, then its ending gives us no reason to think the new norm is any more correct than the old one; the change is just a relocation of honor and advantage. (2) Dueling died in Britain in the 1830s and 40s not because someone finally found the decisive refutation—anti-dueling sermons, essays, and satires had been around for ages—but because the honor code got repointed. The man who issued a challenge started looking hot-headed and a bit shabby, while the man who declined one stopped looking cowardly. Status and shame did the work, not argument. (3) He said the same structure shows up in footbinding and, on the less flattering readings, in British abolition. Arguments against the slave trade were old hat by the time it ended; what changed was the economic picture (the West Indies mattered less, free trade mattered more) and a national self-image that made the trade look like a stain on Britain's honor. I'd read Williams's old economic-decline story about abolition and Appiah's honor story about dueling before, but he welded them into something bigger. The arguments were, he said, the soundtrack rather than the mechanism. (4) But then our current conviction that these practices are wrong was itself produced by the same non-rational machinery—interest, status, shame—that kept the practices going. So that conviction isn't something we earned by reasoning; it's just where the machinery happened to deposit us. (5) And that can't be right, because abolition is the one case everybody wants to call unambigous moral progress. If we can't say our post-abolition norms are better than the ones they replaced, we can't say anything is better.

So: which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd really like to keep (5) and the claim that our rejection of slavery is justified, but maybe that's the step that has to go.
