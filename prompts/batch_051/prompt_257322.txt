I am a high school student drafting a position paper for my school's philosophy journal, and I keep running into a tension in ordinary thinking about sadness and grief that I would like help working through.

The tension, stated plainly: we seem to believe both that sadness is a defect to be removed and that sadness is a part of a good human life. On one side, we medicate prolonged sadness, praise people for recovering from a loss quickly, steer conversation away from a grieving friend in order to distract or fix them, and would not choose for a child a safe, contained bereavement even if we believed it would make the child deeper or more compassionate. On the other side, we say that someone who has never grieved has missed something important, that loss taught us what matters, and we treat tragic art — novels, songs, films that end badly — as among the most profound things we make, not in spite of the sadness but partly because of it.

These two stances do not look like they can both be right, and I do not want the easy resolution that sadness is valued only in art while real-life sadness is to be eliminated. That line seems unprincipled to me, because art about grief borrows its power from real grief, and we praise real people, not only fictional characters, for having been deepened by loss.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order, and keep to the approximate lengths:

1. The asymmetry, sharpened (about 150 words). State the tension in its strongest form, and separate the modest claim that sadness is sometimes instrumentally useful from the stronger claim that a life containing grief can be better overall than a life without it.

2. The case for inconsistency (about 250 words). Give the strongest argument that these two stances cannot both be kept, and that our practices of eliminating sadness are the ones that are correct while our praise of sadness is sentimentality.

3. The best attempt at justification (about 300 words). Construct the strongest defense of the asymmetry you can. For example, you might distinguish prospective choice from retrospective evaluation, or distinguish sadness as a passing felt state from grief as a relation to what one has lost. Show how your defense handles the cases of medication and of consoling a grieving friend.

4. One hard test case (about 200 words). Apply your defense to a safe pill that would permanently remove the capacity for grief, not merely numb a current sadness, offered to a healthy adult who has not yet suffered any major loss. Say whether your view tells that adult to take it, and whether you can accept that answer.

5. My verdict (about 150 words). State which stance survives, whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or rejected, and what the hardest remaining problem is.
