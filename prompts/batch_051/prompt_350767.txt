I have been trying to reconcile two different pictures of optimization, and I want to know whether a familiar argument from evolutionary biology survives a specific development in machine learning. The familiar argument is that natural selection is a poor optimizer. It can only climb, one small step at a time, toward whatever improves fitness in the moment; it has no view of the rest of the fitness landscape, cannot tolerate a temporary loss to cross a valley, and cannot jump to a distant region of genotype space. Selection is therefore usually contrasted with an intelligent designer: it settles onto whichever local peak it happens to be near, and its products should be expected to be workable rather than optimal. I know this landscape picture has never been the only view, but it is still the standard way the limitation gets formulated.

I used to find that argument decisive. I now suspect the deep-learning literature has quietly weakened it. Stochastic gradient descent is also a local, shortsighted hill-climber on a non-convex error surface, yet it reliably trains deep networks that perform impressively well. The emerging picture I keep encountering is that in sufficiently high dimensions the old fear of poor local optima largely does not materialize: bad local minima are rare, or barely worse than good ones; saddle points are the real obstacle; noise helps the optimizer escape shallow traps; and overparameterization makes the landscape more benign. I do not know how much of that emerging picture is actually settled, and I would like that question kept distinct from what follows.

The substantive question is whether this development should change our assessment of natural selection. Does it show that the usual argument was overstated, because a local gradient-follower in a high-dimensional space can be a far better optimizer than the rugged-landscape picture implied? Or does the analogy fail exactly where it matters — because evolution has no backpropagation, no large batched gradient signal, no overparameterization in the relevant sense, and an environment that keeps moving while selection climbs it? I want to know whether the old argument survives this development, needs repair, or should be replaced, and what gradient descent can and cannot genuinely teach us about the power of selection. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on these topics, and flag any place where you are presenting one contested position as if it were the consensus.
