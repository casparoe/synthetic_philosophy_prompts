A friend and I recently spent an evening arguing about a populist mayor who pushed through an unpopular development plan while insisting it was what the people really wanted. My friend said the phrase was empty because a clear majority of current residents opposed the plan. I said their current preferences should not be taken at face value, because they had been formed without any obligation to justify themselves to the people the plan would affect. A third idea kept surfacing that neither of us could state cleanly: that talk of the people might not describe a pre-existing majority at all, but might instead help bring a different public into being.

Afterward I realized we had been running three accounts of the will of the people. The aggregative account holds that the people's will is what a majority of actual, sincere individual preferences supports. The deliberative account holds that it is what the same people would converge on after full information and mutual justification, even if that departs from their current preferences. The constructivist account holds that the will is not discovered but produced: a people becomes a collective subject only through political representation, so the will is whatever that constituted subject comes to recognize as its own, and there is no fixed demos to query independently of the act that constitutes it.

I would like you to build a single, realistic scenario in which these three accounts come apart: the aggregative account designates one of two options as the will of the people, the deliberative account designates the other, and the constructivist account says the question is ill-posed because the decision itself changes who the people are. Constraints: the setting should be a mid-sized municipality or a membership organization, not a nation-state; no science fiction; no lying, no strategic voting, and no manipulation of the agenda; and no empirical information hidden from voters that an ordinary canvassing effort could not surface, so that any deliberative departure must trace to the demand for mutual justification rather than to concealed facts. The divergence must come from the structure of the preferences and of the decision itself, not from procedural tricks.

After you state the scenario, do the following checks. For each of the three accounts, give its verdict on the two options and show the reasoning. Then identify the single structural feature that makes the three accounts peel apart. Then say what happens if that feature is removed: does the case collapse into an ordinary majority-rule problem, and if so, which account absorbs the others? Finally, say how a defender of the aggregative account would attack the case, and what they would claim is doing the illicit work in the deliberative and constructivist verdicts.
