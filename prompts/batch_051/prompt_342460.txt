I am a PhD student in philosophy of law working on what is sometimes called repugnant markets: exchanges that are technically feasible and that willing parties would enter, but that a society nevertheless refuses to treat as ordinary trade. I keep returning to a knot in the way the United States draws the boundary between what may be priced and what may not, and I would like you to help me untie it. Consider the pattern. Federal law makes it a crime to sell a kidney, and almost no one defends such sales. Yet the same legal system allows private plasma centers to pay donors for blood plasma; allows sperm donors to be paid and egg donors to be compensated at rates that reach into the tens of thousands of dollars; and, in several states, allows commercial surrogacy, while other states and many foreign jurisdictions forbid it. The official framing in the gamete and surrogacy cases is striking: the payment is described as compensation for the donor's time, discomfort, and risk, never as a price for the gametes or the child, even though the amounts track exactly what would shape a market price, such as scarcity, reported traits, and a donor's previous success. Outside the body, the same legal culture makes it a crime to buy a vote, while spending large sums to influence an election through campaign contributions and independent expenditure committees is an ordinary feature of democratic politics. And most states make it illegal to raise prices sharply for necessities during a declared emergency, while demand-responsive pricing of a very similar kind is unremarkable in ride hailing, air travel, and hotels outside emergencies.

I want to know what theory of wrongful commodification this patchwork presupposes, if any single theory does. My hunch is that it cannot be rendered principled under one principle, and that its parts rest on different ideas. The bans on organ sales and vote buying seem most naturally to presuppose that certain goods are corrupted or degraded by being given a price, as if the transfer itself carries a wrongness independent of the parties' circumstances. The ban on price gouging, by contrast, seems to presuppose only that coercive circumstances undermine genuine consent, which says nothing against high prices in ordinary conditions; while the vote-buying ban might instead rest on harm to third parties who do not sell their votes. I am unsure whether these differences are real, and I am unsure what to do with plasma and gametes under any of the readings. If corruption by pricing is the operative idea, the permissive treatment of paid plasma looks inconsistent, since plasma is a bodily good like a kidney. If exploitation is the operative idea, it is hard to say why a tightly regulated kidney sale is more exploitative than paid egg extraction. And if third-party harm is the operative idea, an ordinary voluntary kidney sale between consenting adults is not obviously the best target for a ban.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory or theories of commodification that would make this pattern as principled as possible, clearly separating what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Then tell me where the pattern is philosophically underdetermined, in the precise sense that two or more rival theories would license the same line. If the best reconstruction still leaves contradictions, say which existing rule or framing would have to change, and what the boundary would look like after the revision. Finally, tell me whether a single legal order can coherently run on a corruption theory in one part of the market boundary, an exploitation theory in another, and a third-party-harm theory in a third.
