Since retiring from a career in the natural sciences, I have been working my way through contemporary debates in distributive ethics, and a seminar speaker recently left me with an argument I cannot dissolve. The background is this. The leveling-down objection is widely taken to be a decisive problem for egalitarianism: if equality itself had value, then moving from a distribution in which everyone is well off to one in which the better off are made worse off and no one is made better off would count as an improvement in at least one respect, simply because the second distribution is more equal. Most people find that conclusion unacceptable. Prioritarianism is usually introduced as the way to keep a special concern for the badly off without admitting that equality has independent value; it holds that the moral importance of a benefit increases with how badly off its recipient is.

The argument that troubles me runs as follows. (1) The leveling-down objection shows that no moral weight can attach to the shape of a distribution over and above the well-being levels of the individuals in it. (2) Prioritarianism differs from utilitarianism precisely because it gives greater weight to a given benefit when the recipient is worse off. (3) Being worse off is a matter of how a person's well-being compares with the well-being of others in the distribution; it is a relational fact about the recipient's place in the distribution. (4) Therefore the prioritarian's extra weight attaches to a relational feature of the distribution, not to anything intrinsic to the recipient's own state. (5) But by (1), no relational feature of a distribution can carry moral weight. (6) So prioritarianism is committed to the very thing the leveling-down objection rules out, and it is not the stable third position it claims to be. (7) Since we plainly have special reason to direct resources to the badly off, this conclusion proves too much and must be resisted.

I would like to keep both the insight behind (1) and the prioritarian concern for the badly off, but I cannot see how to hold them together without rejecting some step in this chain. Which premise or inference should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, please say which premise is least costly to give up and describe the view that results.
