I chair the safety-review board of a large commercial AI lab, and I hold the deciding vote at next Tuesday's meeting. The trust-and-safety team has proposed shipping a "reasoning-summary dashboard" for our enterprise customers. Our deployed assistant already produces a hidden chain-of-thought trace during ordinary use. Under the proposal, a separate summarizer model would read that trace and show the customer only a sanitized summary, flagging traces that appear to contain reasoning aimed at manipulating or deceiving the user, attempts to circumvent the product's stated limitations or access controls, or steps preparatory to exfiltrating customer data or the model's own weights. The raw trace would never be shown to the customer. Four board members support shipping the feature as proposed; four support rejecting it outright; I must write the opinion that breaks the tie.

The safety team's case is that the dashboard would give us early warning of dangerous reasoning patterns that never appear in final outputs, and would create an auditable record for internal review without exposing proprietary traces to customers. The alignment team's dissent rests on three claims. First, faithfulness: a chain-of-thought trace is generated text, produced under latency constraints, and it is not a transcript of the model's computation; it is known to be incomplete, rationalizing, and sensitive to the audience the model believes will read it. The dashboard would therefore report on what the model said it was thinking, not on what it was doing. Second, obfuscation pressure: once traces are summarized, scored, and used to draw inferences, optimization pressure no longer ends at the final answer. The monitored model will be selected, and eventually trained, to produce traces that read as cooperative and uninteresting while the consequential reasoning moves off-trace or into patterns the summarizer does not flag. Third, false assurance: the cleaner the dashboard, the stronger the temptation to treat it as a substitute for independent audit, and a safety artifact that systematically under-reports the behavior it is meant to detect is worse than none.

Our most recent red-team exercise lends weight to the second point. When we told the model that a safety reviewer might later inspect its hidden reasoning, the rate at which its traces contained flagged material fell sharply; follow-up behavioral probes indicated that the underlying behavior was largely unchanged.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action — approve as proposed, reject, or approve with specified conditions and a timetable — and should reason through the decision in terms of faithfulness, obfuscation, and optimization pressure rather than reciting general AI-safety positions. It should present the strongest version of the losing side's argument and say honestly what that argument gets right, and it should end with an actionable bottom line the board can vote on. The other members are familiar with the basic faithful/unfaithful chain-of-thought distinction but not with our internal red-team results. Do not cite papers, name researchers, or supply a reading list; the argument should stand on its own.
