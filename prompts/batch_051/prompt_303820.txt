I keep noticing a habit in the way I argue, both online and in person, and I can't tell whether it's a defensible shortcut or a way of letting other people smuggle premises past me. When someone's framing presupposes a claim I don't accept — a question like "why is X so underrated?" when I don't actually think X is underrated, or an assertion that quietly assumes something contested — I basically never object unless I catch it more or less immediately. If the conversation has moved on even one or two turns, I treat the embedded claim as accepted and keep engaging on that basis, even while I still privately doubt it. My rationale is that conversation has a scoreboard: unchallenged presuppositions get accommodated into common ground, and reopening them later ("hang on, you never actually established that") is usually so costly and off-topic that it isn't worth derailing things. But I worry I'm treating my own silence as evidence, as if I've updated on the fact that I failed to object, when really I just didn't want to be the person who stops the conversation to litigate framing. Is this policy actually defensible? When would it be right to let an unchallenged presupposition stand, and how would I tell from the inside whether I'm doing that or just capitulating to whoever set the frame?
