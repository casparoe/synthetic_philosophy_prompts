I'm drafting a position paper on how a community should respond when its collective memory of an event conflicts with the documentary record, and I'd like a consistency check on the view I've assembled before I build an argument on it. My commitments are:

1. When the question is what in fact happened, the record has authority over living memory: it may correct the memory, because belief about the past is answerable to evidence and the record is the stronger evidence.
2. Collective memory is not merely defective history. Its work — sustaining identity, mourning, political continuity — is legitimate and can be served even by partly inaccurate narratives.
3. The two can, and should, be separated institutionally. In truth-governed settings (school curricula, museums, courts, official inquiries), the record should prevail over the memory wherever they conflict; in commemorative settings (memorial days, monuments, rites), the community's memory may prevail. A demonstrably partial monument may therefore stand while a textbook carrying the same narrative should be revised.
4. There is an asymmetrical restraint to this: when the community is subordinated and the demand for correction comes from authorities who have historically controlled or spoken for it, outsiders and experts owe deference to the community's own account and should not publicly press the correction against its self-understanding.
5. The bare falsity of a collective memory does not settle what should be done with it. Whether to correct a memory is partly an ethical-political judgment about what the correction would do now — whose standing it would alter, what it would reopen — not a deduction from the record.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only appear to? Please rank the tensions by severity, say for each what would have to give, and don't treat every pairing as equally problematic.

Structure the response in four sections: "Preliminary audit" (one short paragraph, stating whether the set is broadly coherent and which commitment is carrying the most weight); "Ranked tensions" (the body, roughly three short paragraphs, each naming a tension, why it is real, and what would have to give); "Apparent tensions that dissolve" (one short paragraph, covering a pair that looks incompatible but need not be, and why); and "Least costly revision" (one paragraph: the single change that would clean up the most, and what it gives up).
