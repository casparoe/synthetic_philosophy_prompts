I'm a novelist, and my current project follows a French labor inspector and a Japanese salaryman who end up sharing a taxi in Tokyo—haven't figured out the mechanics of that scene yet, but that's a plotting problem for later. Researching them has surfaced a tangle I can't get untangled. My French character takes it as obvious that the 35-hour workweek is a civilizational achievement: leisure as a right, a legal fence against work eating the rest of life, even if some people would voluntarily work more. My Japanese character stays at the office until eleven most nights, never takes all his vacation, and is quietly proud of it, and people around him treat that as dedication—the old Protestant work ethic, basically—rather than a pathology. Yet when a colleague of his works himself into a stroke, karoshi, everyone, including him, starts talking about it as exploitation, as a system that consumed a person, not as a choice the man made. So the exact same 70-hour week appears to be virtuous industriousness when it's a founder or a devoted salaryman, a thing the law should prevent when it violates a 35-hour cap, and an injustice when it kills a Japanese worker. I can't find the principle that sorts those three cases, and my characters keep talking past each other about it. Is there one, or is this just an inconsistency we live with? If you can find the best justification for the asymmetry, tell me which of the three judgments it would force me to give up, keep, or revise. And please answer in American English.
