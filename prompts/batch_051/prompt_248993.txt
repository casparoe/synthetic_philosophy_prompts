I keep seeing Good's theorem cited as proof that refusing free information is always irrational. The version I was taught goes like this:

(1) Before you see the evidence, write down a plan: for every possible result, choose the act you would perform after seeing that result.
(2) Any act you could choose while ignorant can be matched by a plan that does that same act no matter what result arrives. So the fixed acts are a subset of the plans.
(3) The expected value of the best plan equals the expected value of, after seeing the evidence, always picking the act that maximizes expected utility conditional on what you saw.
(4) Since the best plan is chosen from a bigger set, it can't be worse than the best fixed act.
(5) So the expected value of free information is never negative, and turning it down is irrational.

I half-suspect step (3) is where the sleight of hand is, but I can't make the failure precise. Is the argument valid? If it is, what's the hidden assumption that lets it go through, and does the 'never negative' conclusion survive if seeing the evidence changes what you want, or if the evidence reveals a possibility you hadn't included in your original list of states? If it isn't valid, which step exactly fails?
