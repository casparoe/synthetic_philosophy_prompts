A state education department proposes replacing open elective registration for the final two years of secondary school with an "aspiration default" system. Each term an algorithm assigns each student a default bundle of electives; it is trained on the elective histories of former students whose later surveys report high life satisfaction and earnings, and it matches current students to those former students on prior grades, stated interests, and household characteristics. Students and parents may change any elective at any time, but to do so they must attend one meeting with a "choice advisor" — a counsellor trained in motivational interviewing whose performance bonus is tied to the share of default assignments she retains. Students who do nothing keep the algorithm's bundle.

The department defends the scheme as choice-preserving: no one is forced into anything, and the system merely uses inertia for the student's own good. But I keep thinking of a passage in John Stuart Mill's On Liberty, chapter 3:

"Where, not the person’s own character, but the traditions or customs of other people are the rule of conduct, there is wanting one of the principal ingredients of human happiness, and quite the chief ingredient of individual and social progress."

If there is something right in Mill's remark — that a life run by other people's choices, however benevolent or data-driven, loses something important — I want an account of how that loss would actually show up in this scheme's operation, not in the abstract. Who opts out and who doesn't? How would students, parents, advisors, and schools game the system? What second-order effects appear in the algorithm's training data, in the counselling sessions, and in the spread of different "experiments of living"? Which of these failure modes could be fixed without gutting the default, and which are baked in?
