Two friends of mine spent a whole train ride arguing about whether you can dismiss belief in an afterlife the way A. J. Ayer did, by calling it unverifiable, and neither would give ground. (1) One said Ayer's actual point was that "surviving death" isn't a real hypothesis: no possible experience could settle it, because after the body dies there'd be no way to establish that the post-mortem thing is the same person, so the claim lacks verifiable content. (2) The other said that's way too quick: a soul carrying your memories and personality and watching its own funeral would just be the truth conditions for having survived death, so the claim is verifiable in principle—Ayer only wins by quietly assuming identity requires a body. (3) The first replied that this evidence cheats, because it builds exactly the contested criterion (same-memory continuity with no public checks) into the example; you can stipulate any amount of continuity and then declare the claim confirmed, which shows it never became a genuine hypothesis. (4) The second ended by saying ordinary identity claims survive dreamless sleep and amnesia only on similar grounds, and we don't call those meaningless, so either Ayer's test proves too much or it wasn't being applied evenly.

They asked me afterward who was right and I honestly couldn't say.
