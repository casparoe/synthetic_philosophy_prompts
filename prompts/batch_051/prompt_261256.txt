I run a philosophy workshop for advanced undergraduates, typically around fifteen students, meeting for two hours. I have been rereading Gilbert Ryle's The Concept of Mind, and I want to build a session around a passage near the start of the first chapter. Ryle describes a child taken to watch a military parade. The child has had the battalions, batteries, and squadrons pointed out as they march past, and then asks when the division is going to appear. The mistake, as Ryle presents it, is not that the child has a false belief about the division's schedule but that he has slipped into treating the division as if it were another unit of the same kind as the battalions and batteries, something that could take its turn in the parade. I want my students to feel that sort of mistake from the inside: to commit it or nearly commit it, to feel its pull, and only afterwards to see what they were doing.

Please design a runnable activity—a game, a role-play, a structured task with rules and payoffs—that would produce this. It should take about seventy-five minutes, leaving the rest of the session for debrief. The activity should make the mistake arise from what participants do, not from a facilitator explaining it. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto Ryle's notion of a category mistake, say what the activity cannot reproduce about Ryle's larger attack on the ghost in the machine and what the facilitator will have to supply, and anticipate where the activity is likely to go wrong when run and how to handle it. No lecture up front, and please do not use the terms category mistake or ghost in the machine until the debrief.
