Last month I got into an argument with a friend about a travel article that described a people with phrases like “the English are reserved,” and I found myself defending a line of thought that now seems to unravel far more than I wanted it to. I’d like help locating the mistake.

Here is the reasoning as I later wrote it out.

(1) A statement such as “the English are reserved” can be true only if something — the English — actually has the trait of being reserved.
(2) But a nation is not a single thing with a single character. It is a huge collection of individuals whose membership is fixed by historical accidents: wars, marriages of rulers, colonial lines. Had those accidents gone differently, a different collection of people would be called “the English.”
(3) Traits like reserved, warm, and industrious belong to individual people. A collection of people can be said to have such a trait only insofar as its members have it in some appropriate proportion; the trait is not something over and above the individual facts.
(4) But whether the members have a trait in the right proportion depends on where the boundary is drawn. The same underlying facts about individuals will produce one “national” average under one border and another under a different one, so the attribution is partly an artifact of the line someone happened to draw.
(5) So national-character statements do not describe a real feature of a nation; they merely summarize an arbitrary boundary.
(6) If that reasoning is sound for nations, it applies just as forcefully to any group whose boundaries are set by historical or institutional accidents.
(7) Therefore, if national-character statements are rejected on these grounds, then statements like “the jury was skeptical,” “the board is cautious,” and “the market panicked this morning” must also be rejected.

That is what stops me. I don’t think ordinary people, courts, or newspapers treat those group descriptions as meaningless or false, and I don’t want to give them up. But I also don’t want to hand back the only ground I seem to have against broad generalizations about peoples.

Where does this reasoning go wrong? If it doesn’t go wrong at any single step, then I have to choose which premise to surrender, and I want to know what that surrender costs. I’d most like to keep my hostility to national-character talk while holding on to ordinary group descriptions, but I suspect that combination may be expensive. Once the repair is made, is “national character” a coherent notion, or is it coherent only in a sense too weak to matter? And does the answer to the coherence question settle whether it is ever acceptable to generalize about peoples, or is that a separate question?
