Last night I had a long argument with a friend who works in philosophy. My side of it, stripped to its essentials, was that Wittgenstein's family-resemblance discussion is a precursor of, or at least reducible to, an ordinary machine learning problem: for concepts like game there is no set of necessary and sufficient features, only overlapping similarities among examples, so any boundary one draws is a modeling choice rather than the discovery of an essence. My friend refused to accept the reduction. His objections, as I reconstructed them, were roughly these: (1) family resemblance is not a claim about any particular distance metric or feature representation, because Wittgenstein does not treat similarity as a single, well-defined relation that could in principle be computed; (2) the examples falling under a family-resemblance concept are held together by their use within a language game, and a language game is a practice with rules, purposes, and normative pressures, not a distribution from which one samples; (3) a trained classifier that sorts games from non-games would not answer the question Wittgenstein was asking, since the question concerns what it is for a word to have meaning at all, and not which decision boundary generalizes best. I found I could not state his position in my own vocabulary well enough to judge how much of this was a real objection and how much was cross-disciplinary noise.

I would like you to do a systematic translation and then a residue report. I am fluent in the vocabulary of machine learning: embeddings, feature spaces, clusters, prototypes and exemplars, nearest-neighbor methods, distance and similarity metrics, decision boundaries, manifold structure, unsupervised learning, and training distributions. Restate the family-resemblance view, together with the connected claim that a word's meaning is its use in a language game, in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Do not help yourself to anything in the machine learning picture that has no counterpart in Wittgenstein's view; where you must fudge, for instance by mapping a language game onto a distribution over contexts or a similarity relation onto a metric, flag the fudge explicitly and say which assumptions it imports. Then tell me, point by point, what did not survive the translation. Which of my friend's three objections map cleanly onto things a machine learning engineer can state, such as the absence of ground truth, metric selection, underfitting, or the gap between training data and the test distribution? Which get distorted if I insist on an embedding plus a metric? Which have no expression in my vocabulary at all? Finally, I want your verdict on the residue: is it a defect in my current vocabulary, something a richer formalism could in principle capture, or is it evidence that my original reduction was less clear and less complete than I thought? If the latter, tell me what I would need to add to my vocabulary in order to state the remaining claim at all.
