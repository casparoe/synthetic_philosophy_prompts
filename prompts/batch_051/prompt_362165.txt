A colleague in the law faculty makes the following claim: the legal system's well-known refusal to ground a verdict in naked statistical evidence is not a psychological quirk, a preference for vivid testimony, or a covert moral objection to treating persons as mere members of a class. It tracks a genuinely epistemic norm. Proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and even the civil preponderance standard, requires particularized support—some evidential thread linking this defendant to this event—rather than a bare probability derived from group membership. The colleague points to the gatecrasher hypothetical (a spectator chosen at random from a crowd in which most people have not paid) and the blue bus hypothetical (a plaintiff who can show only that the defendant operates a large majority of the buses on the route, with no evidence about the particular bus) as evidence that the law systematically discounts otherwise sufficient probabilities.

I am skeptical. I suspect the effect is an artifact of how such cases are presented: once statistical and individualized evidence are matched for prior probability, concreteness, and risk, the gap will shrink or disappear, and what remains is better explained by caution or by how the information is formatted than by a distinct epistemic standard.

Then say what a positive result would genuinely establish and what it would leave untouched—for example, whether a robust behavioral difference would show that the anti-statistics norm is epistemically sound, or only that it is widely endorsed and enforced.
