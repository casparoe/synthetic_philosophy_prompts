In my emerging tech ethics seminar we spent a week on collective decision problems, and I got weirdly into the unilateralist's curse. The basic idea, as I understand it: when several people can each singlehandedly take some action that affects everyone, and each of them has their own private estimate of whether that action is a good idea, the person who actualy ends up acting will tend to be the one with the most optimistic outlier estimate. Even if everyone is well-intentioned and reasonable, the group's behavior gets selected for the highest private estimate, because that's the person who pulls the trigger—and the more people who could act, the more likely someone's estimate sits above the threshold. The usual proposed fixes are things like: before acting, ask why you're the only one who thinks this is good, or force yourself to justify it publicly first.

I want to run the He Jiankui CRISPR-babies case through this lens. The background as I know it: in 2018 he used CRISPR to edit the CCR5 gene in two embryos, trying to make the resulting twins resistant to HIV, announced it at a summit, the scientific community pretty much all judged it premature and unethical, and he was later convicted and sent to prison in China. And germline editing wasn't some impossible trick only he possessed—lots of labs had the relevant skills, and the broad consensus in the field was not to do this yet. But he went ahead anyway.

So: analyze the case as an instance of the unilateralist's curse. Do the disciplined mapping—who are the players, what are the private estimates of value, what exactly is the unilateral action, and does the theory predict that someone like him would act? Does it explain why it was him rather than someone else? Then flip it and tell me where the model misdescribes what happened. It seems to me the curse assumes a bunch of more or less independent noisy estimates clustered around the true value, but in science the estimates are probably correlated through competition, prestige, and career incentives, and the pool of people who can actually act is small and asymmetric, not a big symmetric group. Does that break the model, or just complicate it? And finally, say what the episode does to the theory: does He Jiankui make the unilateralist's curse look like a useful tool for understanding risky emerging tech, or like a stylized story that doesn't survive contact with a real case? Do the standard fixes—public justification, asking why I'm the only one who thinks this is okay—actually help here, or would they have been beside the point?
