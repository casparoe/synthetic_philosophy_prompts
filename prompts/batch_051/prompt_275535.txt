I have been following the two-envelope debate for some time, and I am struck by a strange feature of it: the formal moving parts seem to have been settled for decades in mutually compatible ways, and yet the disagreement does not wind down. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself rather than another proposed solution.

To fix the setup: you are shown two envelopes and told only that one contains twice as much money as the other. You pick one and may switch after seeing how much it contains. The argument for switching says that if your envelope contains x, the other contains either 2x or x/2, each with probability one half, so the expected value of switching is 1.25x, which is greater than x; since nothing in this depended on x, you should switch no matter what you see. But by symmetry, before opening either envelope you have no reason to prefer one, and the pair-by-pair calculation gives an expected gain from switching of zero. That is the paradox.

As I read the situation, one side treats the puzzle as closed. On this view the switch argument commits an identifiable error: it assumes that the two possibilities are equiprobable conditional on every observed amount, which amounts to assuming a probability distribution over the possible amounts that cannot exist. Once a genuine prior over the smaller amount is supplied, the conditional chance that you hold the smaller envelope is generally not one half; for some observed amounts switching is favorable, for others it is not, and before seeing anything the expected gain from switching is zero. The paradox is a fallacy, and the only remaining task is pedagogical.

The other side concedes all of that as mathematics and still insists the puzzle has not been dissolved. For them the interesting residue is conceptual: the original scenario supplies no prior over the possible amounts, so before a prior is added there is no well-defined expected value of switching at all, and the intuitive pull of the switch argument and of the symmetry argument both outrun the formal correction. On this view the paradox shows that expected-value reasoning, and more broadly principles that instruct you to switch on the basis of an observed amount or to be indifferent on the basis of symmetry, are only well-defined against a background probability model that many ordinary decision problems do not supply; and that when the amounts are unbounded, or the relevant distribution is improper, rational choice can remain underdetermined even though a choice is forced. They treat the two envelopes as a real crack in the standard apparatus rather than a one-off fallacy.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a substantive mathematical disagreement, since both sides accept the same formal results. It looks to me like a disagreement about what counts as a satisfying dissolution of a paradox: one side wants the invalid step localized and removed, while the other wants an account of why two seemingly authoritative reasoning patterns conflict, and what rational choice requires in the gap before a prior is imposed. If that is right, the debate has stalled because the two sides are answering different questions under one heading, and neither has said what would settle the other side's question.

Diagnose the disagreement. Is my hypothesis right, or is there a hidden formal or empirical question still in play, or a genuine dispute about the norms of rational choice? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress: a new formal distinction, a principle that ranks the two standards of resolution, an experiment or theorem, a reframing of the question, or something else. Be as explicit as you can about whether the impasse marks an ill-posed question or simply a hard one.
