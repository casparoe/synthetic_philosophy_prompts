I've been following a personal rule lately and I can't tell if its good epistemology or just paranoia. Here it is: whenever someone offers me a bet or a trade I didn't ask for, I treat the fact that they offered as evidence against my own view before I even hear their argument. So if a coworker who knows I own shares in a company offers to buy them at the current price, or a friend offers me three-to-one odds that a policy I support is going to fail, my default is to decline and quietly lower my confidence. My reasoning is something like: if the deal were actually good for me, and this person is about as informed and rational as I am, they wouldn't offer it. The offer itself leaks information, like Groucho Marx not wanting to join a club that would have him as a member: the very existence of the offer is bad news about my position. But my brother says I'm overgeneralizing. People also make offers because they need cash now, or because they're more risk-tolerant than me, or because they enjoy gambling, and in those cases the offer reveals nothing about the underlying facts. He also says if everyone followed my rule there would be no markets. I think he has a point but I also know I can't evaluate every offer on the merits without getting taken advantage of. So: is my policy defensible? Under exactly what assumptions about the other person's knowledge, priors, and motives does an unsolicited offer count as real evidence against me? And is there a retreat version of the rule that keeps the protection without throwing away genuinely good trades — say, one that treats bets and trades differently, or strangers and friends differently, or repeated and one-off deals differently?
