I've been taking a philosophy of law class and something keeps bugging me: it feels like law and ordinary moral judgment have the same shape of gatekeeping rule, but I can't tell whether the parallel is deep or just a pun. In American law, to bring a lawsuit you need standing — roughly, you have to show you yourself suffered a concrete injury, not just that someone did something wrong and you're outraged about it. In ordinary life there's also a kind of "standing," except it goes the other direction. The main case is the hypocrite: when someone who cheats on their partner tells a friend not to cheat, the criticism feels like it doesn't count, even if it's correct. There you're filtered out not because you weren't hurt by the wrong, but because you're implicated in the same kind of wrong. Law even seems to have a little version of that with the equitable doctrine of clean hands, where a wrongdoer can be denied relief.

So the parallel I keep seeing is this: legal standing excludes people without the right kind of stake (bystanders, people who are merely bothered as a matter of principle), while moral standing excludes people who are themselves dirty (hypocrites, fellow participants). One filter cares about whether the wrong happened to you, the other about whether you did it yourself. Are these really the same underlying idea — something like, only the party with the right relation to the wrong gets to press the complaint — or do they only share a word? I'm especially interested in where the mapping breaks. For instance, if the analogy held, you'd expect a disinterested bystander to lack standing to blame a thief, since the bystander wasn't robbed; but that feels wrong to me — we generally think a clean bystander can blame even from a distance. And in the other direction, you'd expect American courts to throw out lawsuits filed by wrongdoers simply because they're wrongdoers, but (clean hands aside) a crook can certainly sue someone who cheated him. So is there a deeper structural identity underneath those differences, or is the whole thing a loose analogy pushed too far?
