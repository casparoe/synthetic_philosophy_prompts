Build me a case where Kant's Formula of Universal Law permits an act but a Singer-style generalization argument condemns it. I'm putting together a teaching handout on why "what if everyone did that?" isn't just Kant's test with consequences bolted on, so the direction of the divergence matters and I don't want the two tests to just agree on another stock example. Constraints: ordinary-life scenario; no tax evasion, false promises, or not voting; and the maxim has to be a realistic general policy, not gerrymandered so that universalizing it is trivially harmless. Then show your work. State the maxim and run Kant's two tests explicitly — contradiction in conception (can the maxim even be thought as universal law?) and contradiction in will (can you rationally will the world where it is universal law?) — and say why neither fires. Then say what everyone doing it would foreseeably bring about, and give the generalization-argument verdict, where I mean roughly: an act is wrong if the consequences of everyone doing that kind of thing would be bad, absent a special reason. Then say whether a normal, fully-informed agent could prescribe the act under Hare-style universal prescriptivism, or whether Hare lands with one of the other two. Finish with which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens if you remove it, and the strongest Kantian reply someone would use to block the case — and whether you'd buy it. American English, please.
