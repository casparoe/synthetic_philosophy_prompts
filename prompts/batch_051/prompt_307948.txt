I keep noticing the same slide when people talk about reinforcement learning, and I don't have a word for it. Someone starts out correctly: the reward signal is just a scalar that gets fed into the update rule, and negative reward makes the actions that preceded it less likely. Then, with nothing argued in between, the same person starts saying the agent was punished, or that it avoids states it learned to avoid because it finds them aversive, or, in the strong version, that a sufficiently complex system trained this way could literally feel distress in those states. The same thing happens in the positive direction, when a high reward gets reported as the agent "liking" a state. The entire move happens between the scalar dropping or going negative and there being something it is like to be the agent.

I'm not trying to resolve machine consciousness here. A thermostat's error signal almost certainly isn't felt, but I also don't think we can rule out in advance that some training setups, especially in systems that already have other sentience-like capacities, could produce felt pleasure or distress. So the slide can't just be dismissed as anthropomorphism, and it can't just be accepted either. What I want to know is whether this specific assumption — that a reward signal is, or straightforwardly indicates, an experienced like or dislike — has an established name. If it does, where are its boundaries: when, if ever, is the move from negative reward to felt distress legitimate, and when is it a category mistake? If it doesn't, coin a term for it and mark it off against the things it gets blurred with, like ordinary anthropomorphism, taking the intentional stance toward a system, gaming the reward function, and the problem of other minds. A bare label won't help me; the boundaries are the whole point, since I'm trying to think about when AI welfare talk is doing real work and when it's just leaky language. Please keep it under 450 words and in American English.
