In a discussion about the French Revolution I was defending the view that the purges of 1793–94 — the destruction of the Girondins and then the fall of Robespierre and his allies at Thermidor — are best explained by short-term factional and military contingencies rather than by any deep logic of revolution. Another participant cut off the discussion with the slogan that revolutions devour their children, as though that were enough to settle the question. I want you to take that slogan seriously and then rule on it. First, treat it as a piece of language and disambiguate it: what distinct precise claims could it be expressing? Consider at least a descriptive regularity about revolutionary leaders dying by their own instruments; a mechanism claim about a ratchet or selection effect by which a revolutionary coalition purges its moderate wing and then its radicals, each faction becoming the next old regime; a claim about an inner contradiction in revolutionary ideology; and a merely dramatic or rhetorical use that asserts no testable claim at all. For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. Second, take a definite position: which of these readings, if any, gives the best interpretation of the French Revolution from 1789 through Thermidor, and which should be rejected, and why? Do not end with a menu and leave the choice to me; give your committed verdict and defend it. Your answer must be continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, written as if in an email or letter, and with no citations or bibliography.
