So I just got out of a long argument with a friend about discrimination law and I cant stop turning it over. The legal system picks out a list of protected characteristics — race, sex, religion, disability, age, sexual orientation, that kind of thing — and treats discrimination on those grounds as specially suspect, with heightened scrutiny and real remedies. But there are alot of other traits that, in most places, never get that treatment: height, physical attractiveness, being shy, having an annoying voice, being bad at small talk. My friend kept insisting that the principled line is immutability: protected traits are the ones you can't change, which is why race and sex count and hair color doesn't. But I pushed back that religion is on the list even though it's in some sense chosen or changeable, while plenty of unchosen traits (being very short, having a stutter, being deeply introverted) get no protection at all. We went around in circles.

I'd like you to act as a philosopher of law who works on discrimination and equality. Please think through this carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming three or four candidate principles that could rationalize the protected-characteristics list — immutability, centrality to identity, a history of group subordination, the action-versus-trait distinction, whatever comes to mind — and test each one against the actual pattern of what gets protected and what doesn't. Then reconstruct the implicit theory of wrongful discrimination that the practice really presupposes, as opposed to what it merely fits well with. Is the list coherent, or does it quietly presuppose several inconsistent theories at once? And what would discrimination law have to look like if we swapped that implicit theory for a clean only-unchosen-traits principle: which grounds would get added, which would get dropped, and what would stop making sense?
