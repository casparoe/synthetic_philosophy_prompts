I am a novelist, and the book I am working on follows a woman in her thirties who grew up in a conservative Christian church, left it, and is now trying to determine which of the demands she inherited were truly part of the faith and which were simply the cultural air of a particular time and place. The novel keeps circling a question I do not want to get wrong: how does anyone draw an honest line between culturally conditioned commands in scripture and transcultural ones? I keep coming back to three hard cases — head coverings, slavery, and dietary law — but I want to anchor the relevant scene in one tight comparison.

Take two passages from the Pauline letters, which I will paraphrase rather than quote. The first is 1 Corinthians 11:2–16, where Paul commends the Corinthians for maintaining the traditions he handed on, instructs women to cover their heads when praying or prophesying, grounds the instruction partly in the order of creation and in what he says about the angels, and yet also insists that in the Lord woman is not independent of man, nor man of woman. The second is Galatians 3:27–28, where Paul says that those who have been baptized into Christ have clothed themselves with Christ, and that in Christ there is neither male nor female. These seem to pull in different directions about whether the distinction between male and female remains a load-bearing category in the community, and about which passage, if either, may be set aside as belonging to Paul's cultural moment.

Are the two passages in genuine tension, or only apparently so? I would like the strongest candidate reconciliations — for example, different senses of the phrase in Christ, different audiences or occasions, a distinction between soteriological standing and ecclesial order, a distinction between a culturally specific practice and the principle it served, or a genuine change of mind — and I would like a verdict on which reading is honest and which is special pleading. In particular, test each proposed reconciliation against the other two cases: the New Testament household codes that tell slaves to obey their masters, and the dietary rules of Leviticus set against the later texts that treat those rules as lifted. Please separate what is settled, in philology or in what the immediate contexts require, from what is genuinely contested among serious interpreters, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Structure the answer under these headings, in this order, with approximate lengths:

1. The two passages and their contexts — 200–250 words
2. The sharpest statement of the conflict — 100–150 words
3. Candidate reconciliations, in their strongest forms — 350–450 words
4. Costs, circularity, and special pleading — 150–250 words
5. Testing the reconciliations against slavery and dietary law — 150–200 words
6. Settled versus contested — 150–200 words
7. Your verdict, with a note on your confidence — 100–150 words
