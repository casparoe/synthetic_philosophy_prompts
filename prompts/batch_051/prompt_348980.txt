I'm in a moral philosophy seminar and I have to hand in a one-page views profile on moral worth next week, so I want a consistency check before I commit to anything. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Moral worth requires acting from duty: the agent has to do the right thing because it's right, not merely in accordance with duty. That's why Kant's shopkeeper, who gives correct change only to protect his reputation, does the right thing but his act has no moral worth.

(2) But I don't think the agent has to be consciously thinking this is my duty at the moment of action. Someone whose character is settled can just see what the situation calls for and act, and that still counts as acting from duty. I also think an act done both from duty and from love can have moral worth — the duty motive doens't taint the act.

(3) I think Huck Finn's decision to help Jim escape has moral worth, even though Huck sincerely believes he's stealing and doing wrong; he's moved by Jim's humanity, not by the thought that he's doing what's right.

(4) I also find the one-thought-too-many case compelling: a husband who, on seeing his wife in danger, first deliberates about whether he's morally required to save her seems deficient in a way that one who just pulls her out is not. The duty thought looks like the problem, not a plus.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how severe, which only look like they are, and what the cheapest single revision would be. Could you write it as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion, in American English?
