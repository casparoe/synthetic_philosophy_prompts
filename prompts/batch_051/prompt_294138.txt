Two colleagues, Claire and Marco, recently argued about a passage in Pascal's Pensées concerning the relation between justice and force. In the passage, Pascal reasons roughly as follows: people should follow what is just, and will in any case follow what is strongest; a justice with no force behind it can be defied by the wicked with impunity, while a force with no justice in it is condemned as tyranny; so the two must be joined. Yet, since human beings cannot put force behind justice, they have instead fitted the name of justice onto what is strong. Claire took this to be a reductive thesis: in civil life, she said, the word 'justice' tracks power, and regimes call just whatever the strong can make credible. Marco objected that this cannot be right, because Pascal distinguishes two different failures — justice minus force is impotent, force minus justice is tyrannical — which presupposes an independent standard of justice; otherwise the accusation of tyranny would be empty. On Marco's reading, the remark about making the strong just is a diagnosis of the second-best arrangement people fall into, not an admission that justice is nothing but force.

Claire replied that the independent standard does no practical work. Once it is granted that justice cannot make itself obeyed and that what actually happens is that the strong get redefined as just, no test inside the political order can tell apart a case where force was made to serve justice from a case where justice was redefined to flatter force. Every regime will claim to have corrected its force with justice, so the distinction is inert.

Marco answered that Pascal does supply at least a partial test: pure force is condemned and invites resistance, whereas a stable order must win the kind of assent that only the appearance of justice can produce. That, he said, is why domination spends so much effort dressing itself in justice. If justice were merely a name for what the strong do, the dressing-up would be pointless; the need to appear just shows the distinction is real even within Pascal's own account.

Claire countered that this shows at most that people have a sentiment of justice, not that the sentiment tracks anything. Force needs the look of legitimacy the way counterfeit money needs the look of currency: demand for the appearance does not prove the real thing exists, only that people are moved by the appearance. Pascal's theology may supply a true justice, but inside his political analysis all we ever observe is force plus the management of opinion.

Marco replied that this is too quick. Even if true justice cannot be perfectly identified from inside the political order, the difference between orders that are condemned and orders that win settled assent does some discriminating work; otherwise one could not even call the bad case tyrannical. And Pascal's point, on Marco's reading, is a constraint claim rather than a reduction: no regime can realize justice without acquiring enough force to protect it, and no regime that ignores justice can survive its own coerciveness. That leaves room for genuine differences between an order that moves toward joining justice and force and one that merely forces.

Both of them then asked me to referee, and I could not. Please sort out who is right about which specific points, say what each side concedes or misses, and give an overall verdict about who is more right.
