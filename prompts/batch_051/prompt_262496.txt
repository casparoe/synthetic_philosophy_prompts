I've been reconstructing Tolstoy's theory of art from What Is Art? and want it stress-tested against a case rather than merely summarized. On the account I have in mind, art is the deliberate transmission of a feeling: the artist has a feeling, fixes it in words, sounds, images, or gestures, and the audience catches the same feeling the way one catches an infection, rather than merely recognizing the feeling or enjoying the form. On this account, genuine art is distinguished from counterfeit art by three conditions: the feeling transmitted is individual rather than borrowed from previous art; the expression is clear enough that the infection actually gets through; and the artist is sincere, actually experiencing the feeling in question. Tolstoy regards sincerity as the most important of the three. Separately, he evaluates art morally by the kind of feeling transmitted, holding that good art transmits feelings which draw people together, while art that flatters a nation or a class is bad art however infectious it is.

Now consider a professional mourner who keens at funerals in a society that expects stylized lamentation. She did not know the deceased and arrives without prior grief, but her formulaic wailing reliably brings the family to tears. Sometimes, while performing, a diffuse but genuine grief rises in her as well; often it does not, and the family weeps regardless. Take this case through the three conditions for genuine art, and then through the moral criterion. Is she making art in Tolstoy's sense? If the verdict turns on sincerity, say whether that condition is doing real work here or whether it should be restated as a claim about reliable transmission of affect rather than about the artist's inner state. Please give the response in American English.
