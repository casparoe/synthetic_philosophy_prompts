Convene an adversarial collaboration between a presentist and a B-theoretic eternalist about time. The presentist holds that only the present exists; the eternalist holds that all times and their contents exist equally, and that no moment is objectively present. Both have agreed to take this passage from McTaggart's "The Unreality of Time" (Mind 17, 1908, pp. 456–473) as their shared starting point:

"Positions in time, as time appears to us prima facie, are distinguished in two ways. Each position is Earlier than some, and Later than some, of the other positions. And each position is either Past, Present, or Future. The distinctions of the former class are permanent, while those of the latter are not. If M is ever earlier than N, it is always earlier. But an event, which is now present, was future and will be past.

Since distinctions of the first class are permanent, they might be held to be more objective, and to be more essential to the nature of time. I believe, however, that this would be a mistake, and that the distinction of past, present and future is as essential to time as the distinction of earlier and later, while in a certain sense, as we shall see, it may be regarded as more fundamental than the distinction of earlier and later. And it is because the distinctions of past, present and future seem to me to be essential for time, that I regard time as unreal."

Write the joint statement they could both sign after a good-faith effort. Include (1) a numbered section of claims both can accept, covering at least what the passage says and presupposes, which phenomena any adequate account of time must respect — the appearance of passage, the truth of ordinary tensed statements, the permanence of earlier–later relations — and what both sides reject, including McTaggart's conclusion that time is unreal; (2) a disagreements table in which the two positions are stated precisely, each in wording its own side would accept, with no strawmanning; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, the concrete cases, arguments, or bodies of evidence both sides agree bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I suspect the main cruxes will be whether the specialness of the present is an objective feature of reality or merely perspective-relative, and whether tensed truths require tensed truthmakers; include these if you find them genuine, but replace or supplement them if the real cruxes lie elsewhere, and say why. Where a disagreement is merely verbal — for example, over how to use "exist" — identify that part and say which substantive disagreements survive it. The statement should read as a negotiated document two philosophers could actually put their names to, not as a survey.
