In a departmental seminar on pedagogical philosophy, a colleague circulated a handout that summarized V. I. Arnold’s essay On Teaching Mathematics as follows: “Arnold argues that the modern axiomatic style is a pedagogical catastrophe, since it presents mathematics as a game of formal deduction from arbitrary definitions; his central claim is that mathematics is just a part of physics where experiments are cheap, and therefore the only defensible way to teach it is through motivating examples and real applications, with any emphasis on proof, abstraction, or rigor before intuition treated as a symptom of the so-called Bourbaki disease.” The handout then drew the conclusion that a course genuinely built on Arnold’s principles would postpone rigorous proof indefinitely, and that a teacher who insists on formal justification is thereby misunderstanding what mathematics is. I know the essay well enough to suspect that this is a caricature, but I want to be precise about where the distortion enters before I say so in the seminar.

The passage of Arnold’s that the handout seems to be leaning on says, as I remember it, that mathematics is a part of physics—an experimental science in which experiments happen to cost much less than they do in physics—and that the attempt to present mathematics as a purely deductive science has done real damage to the teaching of it. Please give me a careful accuracy audit: which parts of the handout’s summary are faithful to Arnold, which parts subtly overstate or conflate claims he keeps separate, and whether the pedagogical conclusion about postponing proof actually follows from the passage as you understand it. I am especially interested in whether Arnold’s remarks about the experimental character of mathematics and his hostility to a certain style of rigorous exposition commit him to the anti-rigor pedagogy the handout attributes to him, or whether one can accept his diagnosis while still requiring formal proof from students.
