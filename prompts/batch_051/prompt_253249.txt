I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and last week I watched two friends nearly fall out over a health story that was doing the rounds. The story: a woman in her late twenties had spent months reporting fatigue, joint pain and brain fog; a new GP told her it was probably stress, suggested mindfulness, and didn't order any blood tests. Turns out she had an autoimmune condition that a rheumatologist caught a year later. My friends' arguement about it went like this.

Maya said it was a clear case of epistemic injustice — the GP assigned her a credibility deficit because she was a young woman with vague symptoms, which is a stereotype, not an evidence-based judgement.

Sam pushed back: you weren't in the room. GPs see dozens of patients a week with exactly those symptoms, and most of the time it is stress or anxiety. Downgrading a patient's self-report isn't automatically prejudice; it can be rational triage.

Maya: but he didn't run tests or book a follow-up. If you genuinely can't tell, the cautious move is to investigate, not to dismiss. And the pattern of women's pain being taken less seriously is well documented, so treating this as a one-off mistake is too generous.

Sam: now you're doing the thing you're accusing him of — using a group statistic to judge an individual without any evidence about him. Maybe he'd have ordered bloods for a man with the same story. You can't infer from the outcome that her identity did the work.

Maya: I don't need to read his mind. If someone's word is discounted because of a stereotype, even an unconscious one, that's epistemic injustice — being treated as less believable than the evidence actually supports, whatever his intentions were.

Sam: but 'whatever his intentions were' is doing a lot of work. If the discounting was a reasonable response to ambiguous evidence, it's just a wrong guess, not an injustice. And if it was unreasonable, call it a diagnostic error. What does adding 'injustice' buy you?

Maya: it buys you the fact that the error isn't random — it tracks social identity. That's what makes it an injustice rather than a mistake, and it tells you where to look for the fix: not just better tests, but correcting who gets believed in the first place.

Sam: fine, but then the concept is doing political work, not epistemological work. You've defined it so the same clinical decision is an injustice when it happens to a member of one group and a mere error when it happens to anyone else. That's a grievance, not a theory of knowledge.
