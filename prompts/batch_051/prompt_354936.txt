I volunteer one evening a week at a long-term pain clinic, mostly just sitting with patients. My mother is one of them: she has a constant low ache in her back and hip that she rates three out of ten, and she insists that what breaks her down is not the intensity but the fact that it never stops. Trying to make sense of her situation has pushed me into reading about negative utilitarianism, and I keep arriving at the same wall. The view I have been working through, sometimes called lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, says that there is a threshold of suffering such that no amount of pleasure or other goods can compensate for even one episode above the threshold, while harms below the threshold remain commensurable with pleasures in the ordinary way. The entire view seems to hang on being able to say what "suffering" is, and none of the familiar definitions I try can handle the cases that actually show up on the ward.

So here is what I would like. You are a professional philosopher who works on the ethics of pain and suffering and on utilitarian theories of value. Without citing anything, since this is for my own thinking rather than a paper, propose a working definition of suffering that could serve as the threshold concept in a lexical-threshold negative utilitarian view. Think carefully, and begin with a short brainstorm about the different jobs the word "suffering" might be doing here: a descriptive job about how a state feels, and a normative job about which states we refuse to let other goods outweigh. Then run your proposed definition through each of the nine cases below, tell me where it gives a clear answer, where it gives an answer you think someone might reasonably reject, and where it goes silent, and then revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts.

The cases:

1. A patient in a burn unit having a dressing changed without analgesia. I take this to be suffering, and I would distrust any definition that demoted it.
2. Stubbing a toe and feeling two seconds of sharp pain that is gone before one has finished cursing. I take this not to be suffering in the relevant sense, though it is certainly pain.
3. My mother's unending low-grade back and hip ache: bearable at any instant, but never absent, and slowly draining her sleep, patience, and mood. I am torn.
4. Severe clinical depression without bodily pain: the person hurts nowhere, but reports that everything has gone grey and says each morning that they wish they had not woken. I take this to be suffering, and I need the definition to explain why, since there is no pain sensation involved.
5. A person who reports that a needle in their hand feels sharp and is located in the hand, but who is entirely untroubled by it and does not bother to pull the hand away. I lean toward saying this is not suffering, but I do not know how to say that if suffering is defined by the presence of pain.
6. An otherwise comfortable person left alone in an empty room for ninety minutes with nothing to do. I take this not to be suffering, though it is unambiguously unpleasant.
7. Someone held for hours in anticipation of being tortured, before anything has been done to them: no injury, only dread. I am torn about whether the anticipation itself is suffering, and if it is not, when the suffering begins.
8. A lobster dropped alive into boiling water; assume for the sake of the case that it feels something pain-like before it dies seconds later. I am torn, and I also want to know whether the definition requires a self or a story that extends through time.
9. A bereaved spouse, a year after the death, still seized daily by grief. I lean yes, but grief sits awkwardly with the idea that suffering is something one has, the way one has a pain.

I want the definition to mark a threshold, because the theory needs there to be a fact about whether a given state is or is not suffering, even if our evidence about the state is uncertain and even if the underlying terrain is continuous. I would also like a short account of why the concept feels scalar even though the theory needs a boundary.

One last thing. If I am using "suffering" in a confused way, or if no clean definition can do the work a lexical-threshold view asks of it, or if I am trying to make one word do two jobs that should be separated, I would rather you say so plainly than give me a definition that quietly changes the subject. And at the end, tell me how confident you are in the definition you propose, and which of the nine cases you would insist on reclassifying if someone accepted the definition but rejected your verdict on that case.
