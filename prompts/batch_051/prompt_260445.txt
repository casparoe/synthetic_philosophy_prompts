I've been reading David Hume's essay Of Tragedy, collected in Four Dissertations, 1757, in connection with the paradox of fiction, especially the puzzle about why we value works that arouse negative emotions. Two claims in the essay seem to sit uncomfortably together, and I want to know whether they can be reconciled.

Near the beginning, Hume says that well-written tragedy gives spectators a pleasure from sorrow, terror, anxiety, and other passions that are in themselves disagreeable, and that the more the spectators are affected, the more they are delighted. His explanation is that the beauty of the representation and the pleasure we take in imitation are so strong that they become the dominant feeling and transform the subordinate painful passions into something agreeable — not merely overriding them, but changing their quality.

Later in the same essay, however, he confronts an objection to this account: if the pleasure of imitation is really so powerful, then it should make any disagreeable passion agreeable, no matter how base or violent. Hume does not grant that conclusion. He allows that some disagreeable passions are too violent, or too nearly connected with real misery, to be converted in this way, and that in those cases the painful feeling remains painful and the pleasure of imitation is not enough to redeem it.

The two passages seem to pull in different directions. The first presents the conversion of pain into pleasure as a general mechanism; the second admits limits on that mechanism, while leaving it unclear exactly where the limits fall and why. I can see several possible reconciliations: perhaps Hume means only that the dominant pleasure changes the overall tone of the experience without literally changing the character of the subordinate pain; or perhaps the second passage is a retreat that leaves his central explanation largely empty; or perhaps he has a principled distinction, never fully stated, between passions that can be aesthetically distanced and those that cannot. But I want to know which reading does the least violence to the text, and whether Hume can draw the line between the two classes of passions without making his explanation ad hoc. Are the two claims consistent, and if so, at what cost to the force of the first?
