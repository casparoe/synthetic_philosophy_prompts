A colleague claims that recognition is owed as an attitude, not merely as a pattern of behavior: doing the respectful thing is not enough if it is knowingly backed by contempt or indifference. I doubt the specifically attitudinal part earns its keep. Suppose two people behave identically — same deference, same uptake, same invitations to speak — but one privately regards the other as a full participant in reasoning while the other is only performing the role. Is there any observation, experiment, or measurement that could bear on which is which? Set out the strongest feasible test for the presence of the attitude of recognition, and be precise about what a positive result would and would not settle. In particular, say whether the test can separate the attitude itself from settled habits of action, from self-report and impression management, and from the agent's own belief that she has the attitude; then say whether any such result could move the normative claim that the attitude, and not only the action, is owed.
