I'm a philosophy professor, mostly in applied ethics, and I've ended up on the bioethics advisory panel of a private foundation that funds high-risk biomedical research. Lately the proposals we get are increasingly framed in terms of the hedonistic imperative — roughly, the view that suffering is a biological defect rather than an unavoidable part of a meaningful life, and that we should use biotechnology (gene edits, neural implants, designer drugs, animal modifications) to phase out felt badness from the world. I'm sympathetic to the view, which is why I'm on the panel, but right now we are making ad hoc calls on each proposal and the inconsistancy is starting to show.

Your task is to turn the hedonistic imperative into a reusable decision procedure my panel can actually run at the letter-of-intent stage. The panel meets quarterly, sees roughly a dozen proposals a cycle, and gives each proposal about 20 minutes before we vote whether it moves to full review. The foundation's board has final sign-off but usually follows our recommendation. The stakes are high: we are talking about changes to nervous systems that are often irreversible or semi-irreversible, sometimes in subjects who cannot consent, sometimes in non-human animals, sometimes at population scale. The panel is not made of philosophers — two clinicians, a neuroscientist, a lawyer, a disability-rights advocate, and me — so the procedure has to be usable by someone who has never read the theory, and it has to say explicitly which steps answer a factual question (will this actually reduce suffering?) and which steps answer a values question (is this change acceptable even if it works?), and where a proposal should stop with a no rather than drag on with a maybe.

Please give me the procedure as a table with these columns: step number; the question to ask; what counts as a sufficient answer; whether a failure stops the proposal or only flags it; and the judgment call that step cannot eliminate. Then, as prose, run the procedure on three hypothetical proposals: (a) a gene therapy that eliminates the capacity for chronic neuropathic pain in a patient population that currently has no effective treatment; (b) a broad-spectrum suffering-suppressant drug for livestock, intended to remove the felt badness of factory-farm conditions without changing those conditions; and (c) an optional neural implant for consenting adults that blocks ordinary frustrations and steadily raises hedonic tone, pitched as a step toward a post-suffering society. Make sure at least one case is one where the procedure gives an answer I will not like. Finish with a JSON summary using these fields: "procedure_name", "num_steps", "stops_with_no", "judgment_points", "cases", and "what_it_cannot_do".

Think of yourself as a professional philosopher who works on the ethics of biotechnology and on how to translate a principle into something non-experts can follow without losing what matters. Before you write the procedure, think carefully about the main ways the hedonistic imperative goes wrong when people try to implement it — papering over suffering rather than eliminating it, wireheading, trading away autonomy or meaning, irreversibility, consent problems, species differences, the slide into making people cheerful and compliant — and then say which of these you built into steps and which you left out and why. Be honest about where the procedure stops being a procedure and hands the decision to a person.
