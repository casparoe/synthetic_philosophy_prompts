I spent my career doing cloud microphysics and atmospheric chemistry, and I've only recently started reading decision theory and Bayesian epistemology, so forgive me if I've mangled this. I came across Good's theorem on the value of information, and I tried to reconstruct the argument for myself. It went like this:

(1) You face a choice among a fixed set of actions, and before you choose you're offered a costless signal that might be relevant to the state of the world.
(2) If you decline, a rational Bayesian picks the action that maximizes expected utility given their prior: max_a E[u(a, s)].
(3) If you accept, then for each realized signal x you pick the action that maximizes expected utility conditional on that signal, so your value from accepting is E_x[max_a E[u(a, s) | x]].
(4) For each x, max_a E[u(a, s) | x] is at least E[u(a*, s) | x] where a* is the prior-best action; averaging over x, the signal can't make you worse off in expectation.
(5) So refusing costless information can never be justified by expected utility; a rational person should never prefer not to know.

But people I'd hesitate to call irrational pay good money to stay ignorant, and I once threw away a unopened genetic-test kit because I didn't want to know my Alzheimer's risk. Either they're making an error, or one of my steps smuggles in an assumption I haven't named. I'd bet its step 4, but I can't find the flaw. Which step, if any, fails? Or is the argument valid and the ignorance straightforwardly irrational?
