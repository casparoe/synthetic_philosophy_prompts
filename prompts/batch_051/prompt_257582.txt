For a seminar on the paradox of fiction, I want to give my students two passages from David Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature that look, on their face, as if Hume is committed to both sides of the puzzle at once.

In Book I, Part III, Section X, "Of the influence of belief," Hume writes:

"As belief is almost absolutely requisite to the exciting our passions, so the passions in their turn are very favourable to belief; and not only such facts as convey agreeable emotions, but very often such as give pain, do upon that account become more readily the objects of faith and opinion."

In Book II, Part II, Section VII, "Of compassion," he writes:

"A spectator of a tragedy passes thro' a long train of grief, terror, indignation, and other affections, which the poet represents in the persons he introduces. As many tragedies end happily, and no excellent one can be compos'd without some reverses of fortune, the spectator must sympathize with all these changes, and receive the fictitious joy as well as every other passion."

A little later in that same section he adds:

"There remains only to take notice of a pretty remarkable phænomenon of this passion; which is, that the communicated passion of sympathy sometimes acquires strength from the weakness of its original, and even arises by a transition from affections, which have no existence."

Do these passages conflict, or can they be reconciled and at what cost? Set out the strongest candidate reconciliations — for example, different senses of belief, degrees of liveliness that fall short of full belief, a distinction between passions that move the will and passions that arise by sympathy, or a distinction Hume never draws explicitly — and then give a verdict on which reading is honest to the texts and which is special pleading. In particular, decide whether the second passage shows that Hume already has resources inside his own system to handle the paradox of fiction, or whether it shows him quietly abandoning in Book II the claim he makes in Book I.
