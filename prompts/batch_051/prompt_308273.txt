I am currently taking a university course on paradoxes, and we have spent several weeks on Zeno's paradoxes of motion, especially the Dichotomy — the argument that a runner cannot complete a course because they must first cover half the distance, then half of the remainder, and so on without end, which seems to leave neither a first move with which to begin nor a last move with which to finish. I have been trying to build my own checklist for what should count as a genuine solution to the Dichotomy rather than a way of changing the subject, and I want to test it against a case from seminar. Please act as a professional philosopher specialising in Zeno's paradoxes and the metaphysics of motion. Before giving your judgement, take a few moments to brainstorm the strongest possible reading of each criterion and of the proposed solution, and then work through the case carefully.

Here is my working checklist. A proposed response to the Dichotomy counts as a solution only if it meets all four of the following:

1. It identifies the specific premise or inference in Zeno's reasoning that it regards as false or invalid, and offers a reason for rejecting that step rather than simply asserting that motion occurs.
2. It does not beg the question by assuming from the outset that motion is possible.
3. It explains, rather than merely denies, the appearance that an infinite sequence of tasks cannot be completed in a finite time, while still vindicating the commonsense claim that the runner reaches the end of the course.
4. It stays faithful to the mathematics of infinite series and limits, without treating the convergence of an infinite series as a complete answer on its own.

Now consider this proposed resolution, which a fellow student defended in seminar. There is nothing contradictory, the student argued, in performing infinitely many distinct acts in a finite interval, provided each act is suitably brief. In the Dichotomy the required acts shrink without bound, so the total time they take is finite. Zeno's mistake was to assume that an infinite sequence of tasks can never be exhausted. The runner completes the entire infinite sequence of partial runs and, having reached the end, stops.

Is this a solution to the Dichotomy, given my four criteria? Please check it against each criterion in turn, and say honestly where the criteria leave the verdict underdetermined. Keep clearly separate the mathematical question whether the relevant infinite series has a finite sum from the metaphysical question whether a concrete agent can complete an infinite sequence of acts. Then discuss whether this borderline case shows that the checklist itself needs changing — for example, whether it needs to say more about how the run could start, or about whether the sequence of tasks has a first or last member.
