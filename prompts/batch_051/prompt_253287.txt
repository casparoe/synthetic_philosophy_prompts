Write the joint statement that a committed modern textualist and a committed modern purposivist in statutory interpretation could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should be the kind such a collaboration would actually produce: a record of what got settled, where the disagreement was sharpened, which parts turned out to be verbal, and what evidence would move each side.

Accept the following self-descriptions for the two positions.

Textualism: the legal effect of a statute is its ordinary public meaning at the time of enactment, read in the context of the whole statutory text and the standard canons of construction; the interpreter's task is to recover that public meaning, not to supply a purpose the court finds attractive or to improve the statute's outcomes.

Purposivism: the legal effect of a statute is what a reasonable interpreter would understand the enactment to be for, given the text in its public context; when the text underdetermines the answer, the interpreter may legitimately complete it by attributing to the statute the purpose that makes it the most coherent instrument of the kind the legislature appears to have produced, provided the reading remains within what the words can bear.

Both camps reject strict constructionism, the wooden reading that ignores context, and intentionalism, the attempt to recover the actual subjective mental states of individual legislators. Both agree that legislative history, if consulted at all, is evidence rather than law.

The statement must have four parts.

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of six to ten substantive claims about statutory interpretation that both camps can sign. Avoid truisms. The claims should cover at least: the authority of enacted text; the difference between semantic content and pragmatic enrichment; the role and limits of the canons; the distinction between interpreting and rewriting; and what each side would count as a clear interpretive failure if the other side's approach were applied badly.

2. Disagreements. A two-column table of three to five exact residual disagreements. Each column must state the claim so that the camp whose column it is would accept the wording of its own side, while neither side would reject the other side's wording as a caricature. After the table, add a short note on which parts of the disagreement, if any, the collaboration concluded were verbal or partly verbal—for example, disputes that turn on different uses of "meaning," "purpose," or "ambiguous."

3. Cruxes and tests. For each of the three cruxes below, state the crux precisely, identify which of the supplied statutes bears on it, record in advance the textualist's judgment and the purposivist's judgment about that statute, and specify at least one finding or argument that would push each side toward revising its position.

4. Conclusions. Close with a short joint statement, signable by both camps, describing how far the collaboration moved the dispute and where it left it.

The three cruxes are:

(a) Whether a purpose recovered from the text may authorize application of a statute to a case that the ordinary public meaning of the words does not cover, or whether such a case is a gap that only the legislature may fill.

(b) When a statute written before a new technology must be applied to that technology, whether the operative question is what the relevant word meant at enactment or what a reasonable lawmaker pursuing the enacted purpose would have specified for the new technology.

(c) When the text is genuinely ambiguous and ordinary interpretive resources do not settle the matter, whether the court may consult legislative history as evidence of the purpose the enacted words were chosen to serve, and whether doing so is a distinctively purposivist move or is compatible with textualism.

Use these three supplied statutes to illustrate the cruxes:

Statute A. "Upon admission of a will to probate, the court shall direct that the estate be distributed to the persons named in the will in the shares stated there." A woman, the sole beneficiary under her aunt's valid and unrevoked will, intentionally kills her aunt in order to receive the inheritance sooner. The jurisdiction has no other statute and no common-law rule dealing with a beneficiary who kills the decedent; the only question is how the distribution statute applies.

Statute B. A city ordinance enacted in 1985 provides: "A tax of eight percent is imposed on every sale, rental, or lending of a musical recording within the city, to be collected from the customer by the person making the sale, rental, or lending." In 2025, a streaming service charges city residents a monthly subscription to listen on demand to recordings streamed over the internet; no copy is transferred to or retained by the subscriber.

Statute C. A state statute imposes an excise tax on "the sale of any luxury vessel" but leaves "vessel" undefined. The text, read with the canons, does not settle whether a high-speed personal watercraft marketed for recreation is a "luxury vessel." A contemporaneous conference report on the bill states that the tax was intended to cover "motorized watercraft over twenty feet in length, or any watercraft with sleeping quarters." The report was never enacted.
