Assume the role of a philosopher of action and culture, working loosely in the tradition of Huizinga and Pieper, who treated play and leisure as activities that carry their own justification rather than serving as instruments to some further end. Do not simply equate leisure with free time, rest, or recreation.

Produce a working definition of leisure that correctly classifies the following cases:

Clearly leisure:
- A retired person tending an allotment garden for no audience and with no intention of selling anything.
- Friends playing cards on a Saturday evening for no stakes.
- A child building and rebuilding a sandcastle long after it has stopped being shown to anyone.

Clearly not leisure:
- An office worker completing a mandatory compliance training module.
- A gig driver taking fares to make rent.
- A student cramming the night before an exam.

Borderline or contested:
- A professional violinist practicing scales ahead of a paid solo concert.
- A burned-out employee spending Sunday in bed scrolling through short videos, not really wanting to do anything but unable to stop either.
- A competitive amateur cyclist in a local road race with a small cash prize and no sponsors.
- A cook preparing an elaborate dinner for close friends, anxious about how it will turn out.
- A worshipper attending a long, ornate liturgy, wholly absorbed, with no practical aim in view.
- A person playing a mobile game engineered to produce compulsive engagement and microtransactions.

Instructions for the answer:

1. Before committing, briefly brainstorm the candidate criteria you are considering (for example: autotelicity, freedom from necessity, effort, absorption, the activity's internal structure, its relation to festival or worship, its relation to work) and identify the two or three that seem most promising.
2. Then state a working definition of leisure.
3. Run the definition through every case above, including the clear ones, and say where it yields the wrong answer, no answer, or a strongly counterintuitive answer.
4. Revise the definition, or defend the surprising verdicts, as needed.
5. Say which single classification you find least secure and explain why.
6. Finally, indicate where your definition draws the line between leisure and each of its near neighbours: idleness, recreation, and play.
