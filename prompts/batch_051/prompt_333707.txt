I got my friend's paper and I honestly can't tell whether it's a 9/10 or a 5/10. The question was based on this passage from Galileo's Dialogues Concerning Two New Sciences (First Day):

"even if the imperfections did not exist and matter were absolutely perfect, unalterable and free from all accidental variations, still the mere fact that it is matter makes the larger machine, built of the same material and in the same proportion as the smaller, correspond with exactness to the smaller in every respect except that it will not be so strong or so resistant against violent treatment; the larger the machine, the greater its weakness."

The teacher's question was: "Explain what Salviati is trying to establish with this reasoning, and why the fact that he proceeds by imagining perfect matter matters for the kind of knowledge a thought experiment can give."

Here's what my friend wrote:

"Salviati is proving that the larger a machine is, the weaker it gets, even when it's built of the same material and with the same proportions. He starts by rejecting the idea that material imperfections are what cause large machines to fail: even if matter were perfect and had no accidental variations, the mere fact that it is matter would still make the larger machine weaker. Because matter is assumed to be unchangeable and always the same, he says we can treat this property as if it belonged to pure mathematics, so the result is a geometric proof that the large machine is not proportionately stronger. He then draws the conclusion that for every machine and structure, artificial or natural, there is a necessary limit beyond which neither art nor nature can pass. The thought-experiment part matters because he doesn't actually build the machines; he asks what would happen if matter were perfect, and this lets him isolate the essential cause of the weakness. So thought experiments like this give us necessary knowledge, because they remove everything accidental and show what follows from the nature of matter alone."
