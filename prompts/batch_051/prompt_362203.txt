Last night I had a long argument with a friend of mine, a law student who thinks a lot about whether single individuals are obligated to take small steps against very large harms. I wrote down four of her verdicts, and now I want to predict a fifth before I ask her about it directly. (1) She says it is wrong to buy a package of factory-farmed chicken for a single dinner, even though she grants that the purchase almost certainly will not change how many chickens are raised. (2) She says it is not wrong to skip voting in a House race her preferred candidate is certain to lose by an enormous margin; she said standing in line would just be civic theater. (3) She says it is wrong to buy a cheap T-shirt she has strong reason to believe came from a sweatshop, even though she cannot show that her refusal would improve the conditions of any particular worker. (4) She says it is not wrong to fly economy to a close friend's wedding across the Atlantic, despite the emissions; the harm, she thinks, is spread across so many distant and not-yet-born people that no identifiable person is wronged by her one seat.

Next month she is attending a catered conference dinner. The organizers have already ordered, and partly paid for, a fixed amount of factory-farmed chicken, and whatever is left uneaten will be thrown away; she cannot donate the leftovers or reduce the order. She is trying to decide whether to eat the chicken rather than see it wasted, or instead make her meal from the rice, vegetables, and bread. Her four recorded verdicts seem to me to be consistent with more than one underlying principle, and the conference dinner is exactly the kind of case where those principles might come apart. Before I raise it with her, please predict her verdict on the conference dinner, identify the most plausible principles that fit her record, say what each implies for the leftover chicken, give your best guess about which one she is actually running and why, and describe a further case that would cleanly settle the matter.
