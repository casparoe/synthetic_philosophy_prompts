Some of us in a philosophy of mathematics reading group have been going in circles for weeks on the old Benacerraf-style multiple-reductions problem, and we've decided to do a proper adversarial collaboration instead of another three-hour argument over coffee. The two camps are these. Alice is a die-hard reductionist about arithmetic: she thinks the natural numbers just are the finite von Neumann ordinals — 0 is ∅, 1 is {∅}, 2 is {∅,{∅}}, and so on — so the sentence '2 ∈ 4' is straightforwardly true, and it's a fact about the numbers themselves. Nima is a structuralist: he says numbers are only positions in the ω-sequence, and a question like whether 2 is an element of 4 is either relative to a chosen reduction or not the kind of question that has a determinate answer at all. His favorite move is to point out that under the Zermelo reduction, where 0 is ∅ and each successor is the singleton of its predecessor, '2 ∈ 4' comes out false — so the von Neumann 'yes' can't be a fact about what the numbers really are, just an artifact of one way of modeling them.

Write the joint statement they could both sign. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. The agreed-claims section should cover what both sides can sign about arithmetic itself, about what a set-theoretic reduction accomplishes, about why both reductions work equally well, and about what exactly the identity problem is. The table should give the genuine remaining disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording, with no strawmanning. Then identify the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side. For each crux, give at least one argument or case both would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. I suspect one crux is whether the reductionist can justify privileging the von Neumann reduction without already presupposing an identification; another is whether the structuralist can say that '2 ∈ 4' is true relative to the von Neumann reduction without thereby conceding that numbers have a set-theoretic nature. If you think part of the dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why, but don't let the document collapse into the mushy conclusion that numbers are whatever we feel like; keep the residual disagreement sharp.
