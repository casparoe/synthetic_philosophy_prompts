Wolfram argued that many systems built from simple rules are computationally irreducible: no general shortcut can determine their behavior ahead of time, so the only way to learn what the system does is to run it for as many steps as the question concerns. A further conclusion often drawn from this is that computational irreducibility sets a principled limit on scientific explanation. If the shortest accurate representation of a system's behavior is the system's own evolution, then no theory or law substantially simpler than the phenomenon can predict it, and in such domains science would have to settle for simulation in place of compressed understanding.

Now consider the later development of machine-learned emulators: models trained on many runs of a computationally irreducible system, such as a turbulent flow, a climate model, or a many-body simulation, that can then issue approximate predictions far faster than the full simulation, at the cost of small errors, uncertain behavior outside the training distribution, and few or no exactness guarantees. They are shortcuts in time and computational cost, though not shortcuts to exact answers.

Does the argument from computational irreducibility to the limits of explanation survive this development? Say which specific premise the emulator bears on and whether the argument can be repaired: does it bear on the premise that no shortcut exists, on the premise that explanation requires exact prediction, or on the premise that approximate and learned statistical regularities cannot constitute understanding? Or does computational irreducibility narrow to a thesis solely about exact, finite, closed-form solutions, leaving approximate and learned shortcuts untouched? I want to know what happens to the philosophical argument itself, not an assessment of how well any particular emulator performs.
