I have been thinking about the claim that meaning is use, and specifically about how a single word can bear a family of related senses that overlap without any single core. Suppose I distinguish three notions that are usually run together under the word "meaning." Call the first the semantic range: the full set of standing senses a competent speaker can recognize, including obsolete, technical, ironic, and marginal ones. Call the second the operative sense: whichever sense is actually in play on a particular occasion of use, fixed by context and speaker intention. Call the third the effective paraphrase: what a hearer would produce if asked, immediately afterward, what the word meant there, which is often a rough gloss rather than a faithful rendering of the operative sense. On this picture, words do not have meanings in anything like the way envelopes have addresses; they have something more like a spectrum with a shifting foreground, and most of our ordinary talk about "what a word means" is really an unstable blend of the semantic range and the effective paraphrase.

I find this useful for explaining a lot of everyday phenomena: why competent speakers can disagree about whether a sentence is ambiguous, why dictionary definitions feel both right and wrong, why people can successfully use a word while failing an explicit definitional task, and why linguistic change usually looks, in retrospect, like a drift of already-permissible marginal senses rather than a leap. But I am aware the picture might smuggle in a kind of semantic skepticism: if there is no single thing that is "the meaning," it becomes harder to say what a speaker literally said, or what a text commits its author to, or when a use is simply a mistake.

Please give me the most important implications of this three-way distinction for how I should think about two things: first, whether people ever genuinely misunderstand each other while using the same word, and second, whether we can still make sense of a word's being used incorrectly. Does the distinction dissolve those questions, sharpen them, or leave them untouched?
