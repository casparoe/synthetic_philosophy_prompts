I've been trying to figure out whether James's escape hatch actually works if you vary Clifford's shipowner case in one simple way. Clifford says in "The Ethics of Belief": "The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him."

Now suppose the shipowner doesn't just talk himself out of his doubts. He actually hires a surveyor, one he's used before and who has been reliable, and the surveyor gives the ship a clean bill. The shipowner knows, in a general way, that surveyors sometimes get bribed or lazy, but he has no specific evidence against this one. He then sincerely believes the ship is sound and sends it out. Did he have a "right to believe on such evidence as was before him" in Clifford's sense? I want a committed answer, not a survey of options: is he still guilty on Clifford's principle, and does this variant make James's point stronger or weaker?
