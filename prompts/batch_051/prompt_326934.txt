Write a sorting quiz for an advanced undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of psychology. The quiz is meant to classify respondents by their answers to concrete categorisation cases, not by the label they volunteer for themselves. The scoring key must distinguish prototype theory, exemplar theory, and the theory theory, with a prototype–exemplar hybrid available; the classical definitional view should appear as a leaf only if a respondent genuinely accepts necessary and sufficient conditions. The angle is that stock examples such as birds and fruit flatter prototype theory, so build most of the eight questions around categories with weak or absent perceptual prototypes: formal and mathematical categories, legal and institutional categories, and highly heterogeneous artefact categories. Each question should present a concrete case with three answer options phrased in ordinary terms — a typical member, a particular remembered instance, the underlying point or cause, or necessary conditions — so that no one needs to know the concepts literature to answer. The cases must include: an atypical member of a familiar natural kind; a forged or counterfeit artefact; a case in which one vivid remembered instance pulls judgement away from the category's central tendency; two items that share superficial features but differ in underlying causal structure; a context-sensitive category; a mathematical category with no perceptible prototype; and whether chess is a sport. State which answer patterns map to which leaf, and say plainly which pairs of positions the eight items cannot reliably separate, with one sentence on why for each pair. Format the instrument as exactly eight numbered questions, each no more than two sentences, followed by the scoring key and the leaf descriptions; give no introduction and no conclusion.
