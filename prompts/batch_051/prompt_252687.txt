David Hume held that although judgments of taste are in some sense subjective, a standard of taste nevertheless emerges from the convergent verdicts of competent judges — judges distinguished by fineness of perceptual discrimination, broad comparative experience, and freedom from prejudice — and that their joint verdict gives ordinary listeners reason to defer. Suppose Hume were shown a modern music streaming service whose recommendation model assigns quality scores to tracks by learning from hundreds of millions of users' plays, skips, and completions. Would he regard the model's scores as the kind of aesthetic testimony to which a listener could defer without having heard the music — an algorithmic analogue of the joint verdict of competent judges — or as a mechanical aggregation of the very unreflective opinion his criteria were meant to correct? Would it matter to him that the model's verdict is not the verdict of any listener at all but a statistical artifact of aggregated behavior? And what would he predict about the effect of habitual deference to such scores on the listening public's own powers of discrimination?
