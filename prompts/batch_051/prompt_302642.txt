So I recently went through a rough breakup, and I keep catching myself saying "I'm completely over him" in this wierdly rehearsed way, like I'm trying to talk myself into it. That got me stuck on whether self-deception even makes sense. My friend (philosophy grad student, naturally) sketched this argument that it doesn't:

1. If I deceive someone about p, then while I'm doing the deceiving, I believe p is false, and I intentionally get my target to believe p.
2. In self-deception, the deceiver and the target are the same person.
3. So if I deceive myself about p, then while I'm doing the deceiving, I believe p is false, and I intentionally get myself to believe p.
4. If I intentionally get myself to believe p while believing p is false, then either (a) I end up believing both p and not-p at the same time, or (b) I stop believing not-p at some point.
5. Option (a) is impossible: nobody can hold contradictory beliefs at the same time about the same thing. Option (b) isn't deception: in (b), I simply changed my mind, and nobody was lied to or tricked in the sense premise 1 requires.
6. Therefore self-deception is impossible.

I cant shake the feeling that something goes wrong around step 4 or step 5, but I can't find exactly where. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails and why? Or does the conclusion actually follow? Please answer in American English, since I'm in the U.S. and I don't want any shifts in wording to do the work for me.
