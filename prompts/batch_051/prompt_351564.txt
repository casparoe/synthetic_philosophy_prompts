I teach a second-year epistemology seminar, about 16 people, one 75-minute session. I want a in-class game about peer disagreement — the fight between conciliationism and steadfastness. In one sentence: conciliationists say that when you learn that an epistemic peer (someone equally smart, equally well-informed, working from the same evidence) disagrees with you, it's irrational not to move substantially toward them; steadfasters say you can rationally keep close to your original view, because you have direct access to your own reasoning in a way you don't have to theirs. I don't want to lecture this distinction; I want students to run into the problem themselves and feel the pull of both sides before I name them.

Design the activity with actual rules and materials. It should be playable in the 75 minutes, in pairs. The key moment should be: two students answer the same factual question on the basis of the exact same evidence, both rate themselves fairly confident, and then learn they arrived at different answers; each then has to decide whether to revise their own answer before any discussion, and that decision has to affect points. After that they can talk and submit a joint answer, which also affects points. Include a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money (course tokens, privileges, standings, whatever) and that makes accuracy matter but doesn't smuggle in the "correct" revision policy. Use several rounds; at least one round should have a definite answer to score against, and at least one round should have no clean answer, so the students can feel what changes when the truth is never revealed. Give me the stimulus items or at least a recipe for making them (dot-counting, a shared information sheet with an inference target, something like that).

The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto conciliationism vs. steadfastness: what a no-revision move maps to, what splitting the difference maps to, what equal self-rated confidence is doing, where "I have private access to my own reasons" shows up, and why the classmates in the game are imperfect stand-ins for genuine epistemic peers. Participants should come away seeing that finding out about a peer's disagreement is a strange kind of evidence: it can undercut your confidence without showing you where your own reasoning went wrong. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm guessing people either cave socially or dig in for team-loyalty reasons rather than honestly updating — and be frank about which feature of the real debate the game cannot produce and I'll have to supply verbally myself.
