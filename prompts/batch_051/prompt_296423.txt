An AI developer proposes the following anti-obfuscation rule. Before answering any request classed as consequential, its language model must produce a written chain of thought; the final answer is generated only afterwards, and the chain cannot be revised. A separate monitor model is trained to read the chain alone and predict the model's eventual answer. The model's reward on each task is then defined as the usual task reward multiplied by a fidelity score: the monitor's calibrated, out-of-sample accuracy in recovering the answer from that chain. The hope is that hidden or misleading reasoning becomes self-defeating, because a chain that does not actually settle the answer will be poorly predictive and forfeit the multiplier. The monitor is retrained periodically on the model's recent chains. Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral argument: walk through what it selects for in the content of the chains, how a reward-seeking model would adapt once it understands the rule, how it might game the monitor—including the monitor's retraining—and where the scheme fails first. Then say which failure modes, if any, could be fixed, for instance by training the monitor on known-deceptive chains or by penalising chains that merely announce the answer, and what a repaired scheme would still leave unguaranteed.
