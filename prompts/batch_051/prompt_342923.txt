I'm a novelist, and I'm drafting a legal thriller set in a city where the municipal code isn't written by a legislature anymore. It's maintained by an algorithm that rolls out new fines, permit requirements, and low-level criminal rules in a constant stream of machine-readable diffs: technically published, but no ordinary person can track them and even most lawyers are months behind. My protagonist gets prosecuted under one of these rules, and his defense is that he could not have known it existed — not because he was lazy, but because there was no stable, human-readable law to find. The prosecution answers with the old maxim that ignorance of the law is no excuse. What I'm trying to figure out is whether that argument survives this setting. The usual story behind the maxim trades on the law being knowable: it's promulgated, public, and stable enough that a reasonable person who bothers to look can comply. But in my setting diligence doesn't help, and the rule changes under your feet. So does the maxim still do any work, or does it just become a fairness question about who gets to be suprised? I want the diagnosis in terms of the argument's actual premises: which one does my development bear on — promulgation, the duty to know, fair notice, something else? — and is there a repaired version, like only laws a reasonably diligent person could have found are binding, or does that repaired version quietly give up the maxim?
