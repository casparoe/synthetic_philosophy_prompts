I have no formal training in philosophy, but I read a fair amount of it in my spare time, and a recurring pattern in debates about ordinary objects has begun to trouble me. People propose a rule for when several things together make up one further thing—for example, that they do when they are fastened to one another, or when they can be moved together as a single unit—and every such rule seems to let in cases I cannot honestly count as single objects, while excluding other cases that seem plainly to be single objects. The response I keep seeing is odd. Rather than treating the search for a good rule as merely unfinished, people retreat to one of two extremes: either absolutely any collection of things, however scattered, makes up an object, so that there is an object composed of my wristwatch and the Moon; or almost no collections make up an object, so that strictly speaking there are no tables or chairs, only particles arranged in the ways we ordinarily find them. My unease is not with the question whether a table is really one thing or many. It is with that specific argumentative move—from the fact that every proposed middle path has awkward cases to the conclusion that only the extremes remain respectable. Is there an established name for this move, or for the standoff it produces? What are the positions it pushes people toward, what exactly separates them, and is the move as strong as its users seem to assume? A bare label would not satisfy me; I want to know how the underlying dispute should be carved up, and where the move itself goes wrong, if it does.
