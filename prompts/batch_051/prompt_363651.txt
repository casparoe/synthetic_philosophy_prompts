My high school philosophy club has spent two meetings on the Münchhausen trilemma and I keep going in circles about it. The setup is: if you try to justify a belief, every reason needs its own reason, so you either keep asking why forever, loop back onto something you already used, or stop somewhere and just insist it's obvious. All three feel like failures. We tried to modify it and now we can't decide whether the modification actually escapes or just hides the problem. Here's the version we argued about. Let support fade as you descend the chain: each step passes on only 90% of the support it received from the step below. So the chain goes 100, 90, 81, 72.9, and so on — it never bottoms out, but the far-down tail gets tiny fast. Add a stopping rule: once the remaining support falls under 5%, you're allowed to stop asking why. Then a finite person stops without ever declaring anything self-evident. Does this really dodge all three horns, or does the 5% threshold just smuggle dogmatism back in? I also want to know whether the argument changes if the fade factor is 0.99 rather than 0.9 — is it easier to stop when the tail dies faster? And whether it matters if the 90% means each deeper reason is 90% likely, as opposed to 90% as important. I realize part of the problem is whether 'support' can even be measured like that, wich is maybe the real question.

Can you answer in four sections: 1. What the original trilemma rules out (about 100 words); 2. What the fading chain changes (about 250 words); 3. Whether the threshold stop collapses into one of the horns (about 200 words); 4. My verdict on whether this is a fourth option or dogmatism with extra steps (about 150 words).
