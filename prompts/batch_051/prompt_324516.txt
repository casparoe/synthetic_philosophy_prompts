I'm currently in a philosophy of mind course, and we spent several sessions on Dennett's scenario in which a person's brain is removed and kept functioning in a vat while radio links connect it to the rest of the body; the body continues to walk around and converse, but the brain is where the deliberating and intending occur. Separately, I work as a teaching assistant for an introductory distributed-systems course, and the more I think about the two, the more they seem to exhibit the same underlying structure. In distributed systems, a standard move is to decouple a service's identity from any particular machine: clients address a stable logical identity, such as a virtual address or a session identifier, while the system decides which physical host currently realizes that identity. When a host fails or the network partitions, the remaining nodes must determine which of them is the legitimate continuation of the logical identity, and the failure mode is split brain, where two hosts each act as though they hold the same identity. Systems handle this with quorum rules or generation counters that anoint a single winner, on pain of the identity forking.

The philosophical puzzle looks to me like an inversion of the same pattern. The self is treated as a stable logical point of view that happens to be realized on a particular brain and connected to a particular body over channels, and the interesting cases are exactly the ones where that decoupling becomes visible: severed channels, a replaced body, or two candidate brains each able to continue the same self. I want to know whether this is a genuine structural identity, a loose analogy, or merely a shared vocabulary of identity and ownership. If it is genuine, what would have to be true on each side for distributed-systems results to carry over? In particular, the invariant that there is at most one legitimate holder of a logical identity at a time closely resembles the intuition that a person cannot literally branch into two equally good continuants, but the analogy might also predict things about gradual replacement or partial brain transfer that philosophical accounts would reject. What does the parallel get right, and where does it come apart?
