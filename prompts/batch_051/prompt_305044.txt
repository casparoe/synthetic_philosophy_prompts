In my English class we have just read Shirley Jackson's short story 'The Lottery', and I want to write an essay that interprets it through the idea of ritual substitution and the transfer of guilt: the notion that a community can load its sins, impurity, or accumulated disorder onto a chosen victim and, by destroying that victim, restore and purify itself. My difficulty is that the story only half-fits this idea. In a paradigmatic scapegoat rite the victim is accused of wrongdoing or marked as impure, and the rite is understood as a genuine passing-over of the community's guilt; in Jackson's town the victim is selected by chance, nobody charges Tessie Hutchinson with anything, and the killing is carried out as a piece of civic routine, with only a dim, half-remembered sense that it secures a good harvest. I would like you to work through how far the lens of scapegoat and sacrificial substitution illuminates the story, where it begins to distort it, and whether the story's refusal to accuse the victim exposes the bare machinery of scapegoating or instead shows that this is not really scapegoating at all. Please write your answer in British English.
