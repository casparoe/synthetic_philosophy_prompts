I'm working with community mediators, and we keep running into a recurring mess. Two people come in with a dispute that, on paper, looks like a conflict about resources or rules—noise, a shared fence, a promotion one got and the other didn't. But it becomes obvious pretty fast that what's actually driving one side is something closer to envy or resentment. The basic training tells us to stay neutral and treat these as ordinary conflicts of interest. That feels slightly off to me, and I've been told Rawls has this gnarly little discussion of envy and resentment in A Theory of Justice where he distinguishes ordinary envy from what he calls excusable envy, and then from resentment proper, which he thinks is actually a moral feeling tied to justice. I don't trust my memory of it, and I don't want to misreport it.

I want a practical screening procedure my co-mediators and I can use before deciding whether to frame a case as a straight conflict of interests or as something with a justice-shaped grievance underneath. It has to be usable by people who haven't read Rawls and never will, in maybe twenty minutes before an intake session, with just the intake forms and what the parties said. It should walk us through the questions in order, say which answers push us toward "ordinary envy," "excusable envy," or "resentment proper," and be explicit about where we're making a judgment call rather than applying a classification. Also flag where the procedure should deliberately stop and not push further, because our role isn't to validate someone's moral grievance in front of the other party.

For the format: please write it as continuous prose, like an email or an internal memo, no headings, no bullet points, no tables, no bold. Just paragraphs.

Then run the procedure on two cases. First, the classic sort of one: a tenant is furious that a neighbor in the same building got a much larger unit for the same rent, and it comes out that the tenant isn't asking for a rent adjustment so much as wanting the other tenant to lose the unit or be knocked down a peg. Second, a trickier one: an employee in a small firm is upset that a coworker got a flexible schedule as a disability accommodation, and the upset employee insists the accommodation is unfair because they both have the same workload, even though the upset employee has no disability and hasn't requested anything. I'd like the procedure to say something genuinely useful about both, including where the second one should stop before it becomes an injustice-of-my-own situation. Don't hedge by just saying "it depends"; give me the actual steps and then commit to a read on each case.
