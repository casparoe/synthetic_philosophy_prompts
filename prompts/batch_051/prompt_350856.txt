Ok so a friend of mine has this argument he says is just Hume's argument against miracles made precise, and it's driving me mad because every premise sounds right but the conclussion is crazy. He laid it out like this:

(1) A miracle is an event that would violate a law of nature.
(2) A law of nature is an exceptionless regularity that our entire observed experience supports.
(3) So for any alleged miracle, the evidence against it is a complete proof, the strongest possible support experience can give.
(4) The only evidence for a miracle is testimony, and testimony is a kind of experience too.
(5) Our experience of testimony is not exceptionless; people sometimes lie, misremember, exaggerate, or get fooled.
(6) So no testimony can amount to a complete proof, at best it can be very probable.
(7) A complete proof cannot be outweighed by evidence that is merely probable.
(8) Therefore no testimony can ever make it rational to believe a miracle.
(9) But that is absurd: if a whole hospital staff and video and medical records documented a severed limb regrowing in controlled conditions, it would be irrational to keep saying no miracle happened.

I'd really like to keep (9) and give up one of the earlier steps, but I can't tell which one I can reject without loosing either the concept of a miracle or the whole idea of weighing evidence. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
