I have spent years teaching students that they should monitor their own reading and problem-solving, on the assumption that noticing one's own confusion is the first step toward correcting it. But I recently came across two results that seem to threaten the underlying monitoring-and-control argument, according to which self-observation feeds into better self-regulation. In verbal overshadowing, people asked to describe a face immediately after seeing it later recognize the face worse than people who did not describe it; in research on skilled performance, expert athletes and musicians perform worse when they direct attention at the components of a well-practiced action. If monitoring is supposed to matter because it improves regulation, these look like cases where monitoring corrupts the very process it inspects. Does the monitoring-and-control argument survive them? If it does, say exactly which premises are untouched and what the results actually refute; if it does not, say how the argument should be repaired—perhaps by restricting its scope—and what the repaired version claims. I want the kind of restatement an expert in metacognition could endorse, not a collection of caveats.
