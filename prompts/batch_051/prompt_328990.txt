I manage a portfolio of homeowners associations — about forty of them, mostly in Ohio — and I keep hitting the same wall. A homeowner breaks a rule and, when we send the violation notice, they say they couldn't possibly have known about the rule: it was a covenant in a 1987 recorded amendment nobody reads, a noise policy passed at a board meeting six years before they bought and only posted on a bulletin board, or a parking rule buried on page 40 of a welcome packet they never got. Sometimes the board wants to fine them anyway, on the view that ignorance of the rules is no excuse; sometimes the owner seems right that it's unfair to bind someone to a rule they genuinely couldn't know. I've been deciding these one-off, and that's not fair either, because two similar cases get different outcomes depending on my mood.

I want you to turn the underlying principle into a procedure I can actually use. Write me a decision procedure — a checklist or a flowchart in words — for deciding when a rule an owner did not actually know about can still be enforced, and when it can't. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of law actually says about fair notice, ignorance of the law, secret laws, vague rules, constructive vs actual notice, and so on, but don't lecture me about the theory; translate it into steps a non-lawyer can run. I have about twenty minutes per case, I'm not an attorney, and the board has to be able to defend the decision in writing to an angry owner. The procedure has to be reusable across my whole portfolio, for covenants (which are recorded) as well as board rules and policies (which usually are not).

Give me two or three worked examples using realistic cases — say, a parking rule the owner never saw, an architectural standard that was only announced in a email the manager swears she sent, and a rule everyone ignored for a decade that we suddenly started enforcing. Include at least one example where the procedure tells me NOT to enforce against someone who is, honestly, at fault, because the procedure says that kind of enforcement is illegitimate. And flag clearly the cases the procedure can't settle — where a person has to make a judgment call and I shouldn't pretend the checklist made the decision for me. Oh, and please write the answer in American English.
