I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have spent a lot of time arguing on my own about questions that seem to loop forever, and I have developed a rule for handling one large family of them. I want to know whether the rule is defensible or whether I have dressed up an impatient habit as a principle.

The rule is this. When two sides of a dispute agree, after being pressed, on every observation we could actually make and on every practical decision that would be affected — including observations under conditions we can describe but cannot produce — I classify the dispute as merely verbal and stop treating either side as better supported by reality, even when both sides insist that a genuine fact is at stake. I apply this most often to arguments about whether some kind of thing really exists. If one person says a heap of sand is real and another says there is no heap, only grains arranged heap-wise, and both agree on every possible observation of those grains and every practical use of the sand, I file the argument under "verbal" and move on. I take the same line on a person who says a statue and its lump of clay are one thing and a person who says they are two, provided both agree about what would happen to the clay under every operation anyone could perform. I apply it to disputes over whether a disease category is real or a useful invention, and to disputes over whether economic classes exist or are only classifications.

The rule has conserved my attention and exposed many arguments as not about the world at all. But I have begun to suspect it may be too blunt. People whose judgment I respect have suggested that a question can matter even when every test and every action would come out the same; that deciding what to mean by a word is itself a substantive choice with downstream consequences; and that my rule may simply replace a difficulty with a feeling of resolution.

I am asking you to evaluate the rule as a policy for an ordinary reasoner with limited time, not to settle any one of the example disputes. I do not want a reading list or scholarly citations; I want your own assessment.

Lead with your bottom line in one or two sentences, then use these sections, in this order and roughly these lengths:

1. The best case for my rule: reconstruct the strongest defense of it, including the real costs it protects me against. (About 400 words.)
2. The best case against it: give the strongest reasons to think the rule overreaches or misclassifies, using concrete examples. (About 400 words.)
3. Where the rule works and where it breaks: distinguish the disputes my test correctly classifies as merely verbal from those it wrongly dismisses, and identify the underlying difference. (About 500 words.)
4. A restated rule: offer a version of the rule, if any survives, that keeps the benefits while repairing the failures; if none survives, say what I should do instead. (About 350 words.)
5. My verdict: a final judgment on the original rule and on what I should actually do going forward. (About 200 words.)
