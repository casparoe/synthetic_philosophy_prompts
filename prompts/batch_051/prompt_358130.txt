So I'm writing a novel set in the 1920s, and one of my characters is a mathematician who's gone fully constructivist: he won't assert a mathematical claim unless there's an actual construction or proof in hand, and so he refuses to say that every well-formed claim is either true or false, even though he's not positing some mysterious third truth value. I wanted a scene where he works through an argument that seems to force the law of excluded middle back onto him, and I found this in my notes. I'm now not sure it's any good, or whether it even undermines him at all.

Take any statement P, and run this:

(1) Suppose not-(P or not-P).
(2) Suppose P. Then P or not-P, which contradicts (1). So by reductio, we've got not-P.
(3) But then not-P gives us P or not-P, which again contradicts (1).
(4) So (1) blows up; we can't maintain not-(P or not-P). In symbols: not-not-(P or not-P).
(5) If not-(P or not-P) is ruled out, then P or not-P is true.
(6) So P or not-P, for any P.

And that's just the law of excluded middle. So either my constructivist character has to cave, or one of these moves is shakier then it looks. I keep worrying about step (2), because it uses reductio and I thought constructivists have a hangup about reductio; but then I half-remember that constructivists distinguish between using reductio to prove a negation and using it to prove a positive claim. Can you tell me: on constructivist rules, is this argument valid, and if not exactly which step fails, and why doesn't step (2) fail on the same grounds?
