I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been chewing on Stigler's law of eponymy—the observation that basically no scientific discovery is named after the person who first found it, with the law itself being a little meta-joke depending on who you credit. Heres the puzzle I keep hitting. Physicists, chemists, and mathematicians all know that a bunch of their standard names are historically off. Avogadro didn't compute Avogadro's number, Halley didn't first observe the comet that carries his name, the Pythagorean theorem is much older than Pythagoras. Its not secret information; it's in every textbook's margin. And yet nobody is out there campaigning to rename any of it. Scientific communities keep minting new eponyms—particles, constants, syndromes, equations—and keep using the old wrong ones, without any serious push for historical accuracy.

So what view of scientific credit, or of naming itself, does that practice presuppose? Is an eponym best understood as a reward (credit), a mnemonic handle, a kind of ancestor-worship, or something more like a brand? Or do the functions split depending on context—naming a unit might be for standardization while naming a species is basically honorific? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of discovery-credit that fits the practice as actually run, say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined (several theories of credit could license exactly the same behavior), and then tell me what would concretely have to change if we replaced the implicit view with a flatly truth-tracking one, where names had to track priority. Would science itself look any different, or just the signage?
