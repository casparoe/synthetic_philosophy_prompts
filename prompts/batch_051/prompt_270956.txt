I am a novelist, and the society in my current book obliges me to decide how much of a person's occupation is chosen, how much is inherited disposition, and how much is produced by institutions. In the course of designing that world I have collected my own verdicts on cases, and they will not settle into any principle I can articulate. Rather than being given a position, I would like to know what principle my verdicts already commit me to, and which verdicts I would have to abandon if no tidy principle can hold them all.

The background is the gender-equality paradox, which I accept as a real pattern rather than a measurement artifact: on standard measures, countries with stronger egalitarian policies and narrower legal and economic inequalities between men and women, such as the Nordic countries, show larger average sex differences in occupational and educational sorting than less egalitarian countries, not smaller. That pattern, together with several invented societies, produces the following verdicts.

First, on the paradox itself: it should make it more costly to say that gendered occupational sorting is only the residue of legal or economic discrimination. But it should not license the opposite claim that the sorting is the unforced expression of an unchanging nature. Some third thing is doing the work, and I want a principle that names it without collapsing into either pole.

Second, the communal nursery. In one invented society, children are raised from birth in nurseries that eliminate gendered clothing, gendered play scripts, gendered praise, and gendered modeling in the caregiving roster. I believe average occupational differences would shrink but not vanish, and that the differences left would be less tightly bundled: a tendency in one direction would predict less about a person's other traits. I also believe this society would still not reach exact parity.

Third, the inverted incentives. In another society, the usual scheme is reversed from early childhood: risk-taking and object-manipulation are praised in girls and gently discouraged in boys, while verbal attunement and care are praised in boys and gently discouraged in girls. I believe the occupational distribution would partially invert. Taken with the previous case, this sets the bounds of the view I am hunting for: culture does real causal work, but it does not work on infinitely plastic material.

Fourth, parity by decree. In a third society, every occupation is sex-assigned to maintain exact fifty-fifty parity, and assignment is backed by force of law. I find this unjust as a standing arrangement, and not merely inefficient. This is awkward for me, because I also believe the segregation it corrects is partly the downstream effect of an unjust history. If gender-typed sorting is an injustice and culture can reshape it, why should a correction that shapes it toward parity offend me? And if it does offend me, am I not confessing that I take some residual difference to be legitimate after all?

Fifth, the individual chooser. A young woman becomes a nurse and reports that she has always wanted to; everyone she had ever seen doing nursing while she grew up was a woman. I do not think her reported preference can be treated as clean evidence of an innate disposition, because the set of selves she could vividly imagine was already gendered before she chose. But I also refuse to describe her as coerced or as a mere puppet of socialization. She chose, but from a menu someone else arranged.

Sixth, the overlap. I believe the male and female distributions of every relevant disposition overlap so heavily that no individual should ever be steered toward or away from a career on the basis of group averages. Yet I also believe that under any regime of genuinely free choice, exact parity in every occupation is essentially impossible. So I appear to maintain that the group difference is too weak to license any individual judgment, but strong enough to guarantee aggregate skew.

I would like the principle, or a small set of competing principles, that captures these verdicts. State it precisely, and say which feature of each case motivates which clause. Then tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, if it cannot accommodate them all, and whether the repair is worth the loss.

Finally, apply it to a case I have not settled. Imagine a society that changes no child-rearing practice, creates no quotas, and coerces no one, but reorders pay and prestige so that nursing, early childhood education, and eldercare are rewarded and honored exactly as much as engineering and finance. Should I expect the gendered distribution across those fields to converge, persist, or change only superficially? And should I read the outcome as evidence about nature, about the depth of culture, or about the limits of price signals in reaching the relevant mechanism?
