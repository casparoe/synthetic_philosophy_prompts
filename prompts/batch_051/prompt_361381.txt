I'm teaching a short module on AI safety in a philosophy of technology course, and I'd like a handout quiz that sorts students into positions on the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence. They've read Bostrom's two chapters from Superintelligence plus a couple of critical responses, so they know the jargon, but I want the quiz to sort them by what they actually say about cases rather than by which label they'd pick for themselves.

Please write a nine- or ten-question "Which view do you actually hold?" quiz. Each question should be a concrete scenario or claim about an AI's goals and how they change as the system gets more capable or gets dropped into new circumstances. Two to four answer options per question. Then give a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto at least these positions: strong orthogonality (roughly, any level of intelligence can be paired with any final goal), weak orthogonality (orthogonality holds for terminal goals but not for the instrumental competence required to pursue them, or only holds within some range), a value-convergence view (sufficiently capable agents converge on moral concern or something close to it), a training-artifact view (instrumental convergence is real but mostly reflects how systems are trained, not a deep fact about rational agency), and a deflationary/skeptical view (we're over-anthropomorphizing when we attribute terminal goals to these systems at all).

The cases should force choices about at least these: (a) whether self-preservation shows up purely as a means; (b) whether cooperation and honesty look convergently selected; (c) whether a very capable but corrigible system would preserve its original goals; (d) whether resource acquisition is a convergent drive; (e) whether radically different terminal goals really are compatible with the same superhuman competence. Don't use the words "orthogonality," "instrumental convergence," or "corrigibility" in the quiz questions themselves — a student should be able to answer without having memorized definitions.

Also flag which pairs of positions your scoring key can't cleanly seperate on these questions and why (I suspect weak orthogonality and the training-artifact view overlap on several). Note any answer combination that lands on no stable position, and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

The audience is undergraduate majors, so keep it accessible but philosophically sharp. The deliverable should be the quiz itself, the scoring key, and the note on unoccupied combinations. Please also say how confident you are in the claims the quiz bakes in, and single out the point you're least sure of.
