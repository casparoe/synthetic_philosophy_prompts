I'm a high school senior in an independent-study course on concepts and categories, and my final assignment is to submit a research proposal, not the paper itself; if the proposal were approved, the paper would run about eight pages over one semester. My half-formed idea starts from a familiar question: what makes every 'a' an 'a' when two instances of the letter can share no stroke, curve, or measurable contour? I suspect that way of putting the question already tilts toward a feature-checklist answer, and that letter identity may be closer to the way we recognize the same person across very different photographs, or the same melody across arrangements, than to classification under a rule. On that alternative model, style would not be decoration added on top of an essence but part of how the letter shows up as the letter at all. Please turn this into a proper proposal: state the question so that it has a determinate answer; explain what the feature-list approach leaves open and why evidence about how readers actually classify letters would not by itself settle it; describe an approach that makes progress by close comparison of a small, deliberately constructed set of letterforms, and say why that approach fits the question better than empirical or historical ones; lay out what a successful answer would look like and what would count as failure; and keep the scope realistic for eight pages and one semester. Do not include citations or a reading list; I will supply those separately if my teacher asks for them.
