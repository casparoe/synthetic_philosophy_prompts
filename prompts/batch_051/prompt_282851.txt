In the Scholium following the Definitions in his *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy*, Newton advances a view that cuts against the usual philosophical story about what makes a curve mathematically legitimate. He maintains that the construction of right lines and circles, which geometers are apt to treat as elementary postulates, is itself a problem whose solution is owed to mechanics, not geometry. Geometry, on his account, is that part of universal mechanics which accurately proposes and demonstrates the art of measuring.

I want you to explain the conceptual issue this raises about the boundary between legitimate mathematical objects and merely mechanical artifacts. If even straight lines and circles are, in their construction, mechanical achievements, does the traditional distinction between properly geometrical curves and merely mechanical or transcendental curves—for instance, the spiral, the quadratrix, or the cycloid—still have a principled basis? Does Newton’s position imply that the real criterion of mathematical legitimacy is not purity of construction but exactness of description, or something else? Take a definite stand: state precisely what you take the criterion of legitimacy to be on Newton’s view and defend that reading as the most coherent one, rather than presenting it as one option among several.
