I've been teaching the Grand Inquisitor chapter of The Brothers Karamazov this week and I keep thinking it deserves a proper send-up, so here's the assignment. Write a piece of deadpan satire in the form of a letter to an advice column -- a parish newsletter's "Ask the Abbess," say, or a churchy "Dear Prudence." The letter is from the Grand Inquisitor's new head of flock satisfaction, and he is genuinely baffled. His office has spent centuries giving people exactly what the chapter says ordinary humanity can't do without -- miracle, mystery, authority, and bread when it comes down to it -- and yet the satisfaction surveys keep showing a stubborn minority prefering their freedom, even when it obviously makes them miserable. Treat the whole thing as a pastoral-care and customer-service problem: complaints about the burden of freedom, retention figures among the recently unfree, a pilot program to make suffering optional, maybe a pained reference to the silent kiss as the one performance review the old man has never managed to spin. The underlying logic has to be the actual argument from the chapter, taken seriously: Christ overestimated human strength by refusing the three temptations; freedom is a burden most people would happily trade for happiness; adults would rather be children who don't have to choose; and the Inquisitor and his staff are the only ones willing to carry the conscience of everyone else. The target is that argument -- the idea that trading freedom for guaranteed happiness is the humane option -- not Dostoevsky and not Christianity as such. The comedy should come from following that logic to its absurd but recognizable conclusion, and a specialist should feel it's fair. Keep it deadpan: no winking, no breaking character, no explaining the joke. About 750-800 words. And please, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; continuous prose only, like an actual letter, though a salutation and a sign-off are fine.
