I teach an upper-level undergraduate philosophy seminar on death and the meaning of life, and I want to spend one session on Bernard Williams's Makropulos argument — the contention that a life without end would eventually go flat, so that death is not the worst thing we can imagine. I plan to make Groundhog Day the centerpiece of the session, and I need a reading of the film that I can actually teach from.

The reading should be faithful in both directions. On the Williams side, I mean taking seriously the machinery of the argument: the distinction between categorical desires — the projects, loves, and commitments that give a person a reason to go on — and contingent desires, which merely presuppose life and supply appetites within it; and the worry that, over a long enough life, any finite stock of categorical desires would be used up, leaving only repetition and tedium. On the film's side, I mean reading the actual arc rather than treating the movie as an illustration. Go through Phil Connors's phases: early instrumental mastery of the loop (seduction, money, cheap power), the middle despair and suicide attempts, and the long final phase of piano lessons, ice sculpting, and small acts of care for the town. For each phase, say what kind of desire is doing the work and whether anything like Williams's exhaustion is setting in.

Then work out an answer to the questions that most interest me. Does the film confirm Williams's pessimism — showing that even someone with unlimited time eventually hits the wall of meaningless repetition — and then rescue itself only by allowing the loop to end? Or does Phil's transformation amount to a counterexample: evidence that categorical desires can be regenerated, and that one can find new reasons to go on in competence, care, and particular attention to other people? Is the film about infinite life at all, or only about infinite repetitions of one day, so that it cannot decide the question Williams actually raises? And should we read the happy ending — the loop breaking — as an implicit admission that the film cannot imagine unending life as anything other than an escape from itself? Conclude with what the film makes vivid about the debate that the philosophical literature by itself does not.
