In the 1998 paper "The Extended Mind," Clark and Chalmers say the following: "Epistemic action, we suggest, demands spread of epistemic credit. If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is (so we claim) part of the cognitive process. Cognitive processes ain't (all) in the head!" Their examples are stuff like using pen and paper for long multiplication, rotating Tetris pieces to see if they fit, or a notebook. All of those external devices are basically passive or at least highly reliable: they store, check, or manipulate information in ways that would count as cognitive if they happened inside a brain. Now here's the development I want to stress-test the principle against: suppose my regular cognitive tool is a large language model that doesn't just retrieve or transform what I put in, but actively completes my thoughts, subtly rephrases my premises, guesses what I probably meant, and sometimes confabulates details I never gave it. If the confabulation were happening inside my head, I wouldn't call it part of my cognitive process in the sense Clark and Chalmers mean—I'd call it a glitch, or source misattribution, or confabulation, not something that earns epistemic credit. So does the parity principle still force the conclusion that the LLM is part of my mind when it's confabulating? Or is the principle only plausible when the external process is stable and mostly truth-preserving? If it needs that restriction, what does the repaired principle actually say, and is it still a version of extended mind?
