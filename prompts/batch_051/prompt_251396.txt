Two people in the reading group I'm in had an argument that I'd like a careful referee for. We had been working through some ideas about self-reference, and Talia defended the claim that the old analogy between DNA replication and Gödel's incompleteness theorems is more than a loose metaphor. Her setup went like this. In a toy string-rewriting system of the sort we had been studying — call it "typogenetics" — strands made of letters from a small alphabet encode enzymes that cut, copy, splice, and otherwise operate on strands; some strands encode the enzyme that copies strands of exactly their own type. The strand is therefore both data and program, the same reflexive arrangement through which a formal system can refer to its own formulas. Real DNA, she argued, has the same arrangement: it encodes the polymerases and repair enzymes that read and duplicate DNA. So a genome is, in effect, a program that contains the instructions for the machinery that runs it. If Gödel's result shows that a system with the right kind of reflexive access to itself can construct a claim about its own unprovability, she reasoned, then there should be a biological analogue — a genome-level construct that is, in some principled sense, "undecidable" inside the cell's own language, or at the very least questions that could not be settled from inside the genetic system.

Milan pushed back. He said the analogy is a pun, not an isomorphism. Gödel's theorem is not merely about self-reference; it requires a formal system rich enough to encode a proof predicate and enough arithmetic to diagonalize, and it needs something like negation to form "not provable." A DNA molecule, he said, copies itself but proves nothing, represents no arithmetic, and has no negation, so calling a DNA construct "undecidable" is a category mistake — genes are just chemistry. Talia replied that diagonalization, not arithmetic, is the engine: once a system can encode "this very strand" as an object of its own operations, the fixed point exists and the rest follows; the self-copying strands in typogenetics are exactly those fixed points, so the machinery of Gödelian self-reference is already present in replication. Milan replied that the fixed points in typogenetics are purely positive — strands that copy or recognize themselves — whereas Gödel's sentence is essentially negative, saying something is not provable; without a believable analogue of negation or non-provability, replication is molecular self-copying, not self-knowledge. Talia answered that negation can be modeled, say by an enzyme that destroys strands of a certain type, so a strand could encode "the enzyme that degrades strands that encode this very recognition"; and in any case the deeper point is that self-replication and self-reference are two faces of one phenomenon, so the burden is on Milan to name the extra ingredient Gödel needed and explain why chemistry cannot possess it. At that point they asked me to referee, and I could not tell which of the middle moves were sound.

Could you go through the back-and-forth move by move and say who is right about what? In particular, I want to know what Gödel's theorem actually needs beyond reflexive self-reference, whether the typogenetics fixed points Talia cites supply that missing ingredient or only a toy version of it, whether Milan's "category mistake" objection is fair, and whether the intermediate move about modeling negation actually helps. Please also say how confident you are in your overall adjudication and single out the one point you are least sure of.
