Within my own discipline of environmental history I keep noticing a double standard that I cannot make principled sense of. Histories of the Columbian Exchange routinely treat smallpox and measles as historical actors in their own right: the pathogens are said to have depopulated the Americas, destroyed imperial projects, and reorganised labour regimes, with human intentions treated merely as the channels through which the germs moved. Histories of the Black Death similarly allow plague to explain rising wages, the weakening of serfdom, and shifts in religious sensibility, and nobody calls this biological determinism. Yet the moment a palaeoclimatologist or archaeologist suggests that a prolonged drought contributed to the Late Bronze Age collapse, or that a volcanic eruption shaped a decade of unrest, many historians grow visibly uneasy: the claim is labelled environmental determinism, and we are reminded that climate and geology can only ever constrain human action, never cause historical change, because it is always human choices that do the real historical work.

I would like to know what philosophical commitments this disciplinary practice presupposes. Reconstruct, as charitably as you can, the implicit theory of causation or agency that would make an epidemic an admissible historical actor but a drought only a background constraint. Is there a coherent difference between the two cases, or does the practice rest on an unexamined distinction between the biological and the physical? More formally: what does the practice strictly presuppose, what does it merely fit well with, and where is it philosophically underdetermined? And what would have to change in the profession's explanatory norms if we treated a drought or a volcanic winter as a historical actor on exactly the same footing as an epidemic?
