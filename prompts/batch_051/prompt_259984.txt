I have recently found myself, for reasons that are partly personal, needing to assign probabilities to individual cases—a specific patient's chance of responding to a treatment, a particular borrower's chance of default—and this has pushed me back into the reference class problem. I have tentatively accepted the position that there is no objectively correct reference class for a single-case probability: the choice among equally natural reference classes is pragmatic or interest-relative, and a single-case probability is not a fact of the world awaiting discovery. I would like to know what would count against this position. Which arguments, formal results, cases, or developments in adjacent areas such as statistical practice, decision theory, the metaphysics of chance, or artificial intelligence would force me to give it up or significantly qualify it? Please distinguish what a defender of the position should concede as a genuine cost from what would be fatal, and rank the defeaters accordingly. I would also like to know what the view predicts about the success of statistical practice and about persistent disagreement among equally careful reasoners, and whether it risks collapsing into an untenable anything-goes view that would itself count as a refutation. I would personally find most persuasive a defeater that exhibits a well-motivated, non-question-begging principle that fixes a reference class in at least one hard case without tacitly importing interests or pragmatic considerations.
