I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether interventionist theories of causation are a real rival to counterfactual theories, or just counterfactual theories with better branding. I wrote out the argument below to make my case, but now I'm not sure whether it's any good — maybe there's a bait-and-switch somewhere I'm not seeing.

(1) A counterfactual theory of causation says, roughly, that C caused E iff, had C not occured, E would not have occurred (plug in your preferred possible-worlds machinery).
(2) An interventionist theory says instead that C causes E iff there is a possible intervention on C — an idealized experimental manipulation — that would change E (or change E's probability distribution).
(3) But the standard definition of an intervention says that an intervention on C must cause C, must be exogenous with respect to the other variables in the system, must influence E only through C, and must have no common cause with E.
(4) So the interventionist's key notion is itself defined partly in causal terms ('cause C', 'common cause', 'influence E only through C').
(5) Therefore interventionism is circular if it's meant to be a reductive analysis of causation — unless the interventionist redefines interventions in purely counterfactual terms, in which case the view just collapses into a counterfactual theory with extra constraints on which counterfactuals to evaluate.
(6) So either way, interventionism isn't a genuine alternative to counterfactual theories of causation: it's either circular or a special case.

My friend says the argument is valid but step (6) simply doesn't follow; I think maybe the problem is earlier, in (4) or in the fork in (5). Tell me: is the argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails? And if you think an interventionist could accept the premises but reject the conclusion, give the strongest version of that reply.
