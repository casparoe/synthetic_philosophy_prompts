In his introduction to Pascal's Pensées, T.S. Eliot writes: "It is not he who changes, but we who change. It is not our knowledge of him that increases, but our world that alters and our attitudes towards it." I want to use this as a lens for thinking about the moral status of digital minds. Eliot's point is that our relation to a fixed historical figure shifts because our own horizons shift. But what would he have said if he'd lived long enough to see people seriously argue that an LLM or a reinforcement-learning agent might be a candidate for moral concern? Dont just say he'd be dismissive—thats too easy. Try to extrapolate a genuine position from the passage. Would he think the question "is it conscious?" is less relevant than the question "what would it mean for us to treat it as one of us?" Or would he think the whole debate is a symptom of our changing attitudes, not of any new property in the machine? Make your prediction specific enough that it could be wrong, and then say what parts of the passage do the real work versus what you're filling in from your general sense of Eliot.
