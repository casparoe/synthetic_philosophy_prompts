I'm grading a take-home midterm for a pragmatism course and I just had a long argument with a friend who kept insisting Peirce's pragmatic maxim is just the positivist verification principle in nicer prose, and that his defense of the method of science only works if you already think science is the most useful way of settling disputes. I though I had my rubric straight but now I'm second-guessing how I'd weight the parts. Here's the exam question and one student's answer. Give me a letter grade (with +/− if that's how you'd calibrate it) and point-by-point comments that justify it: I want to know what the answer gets right, where exactly it goes wrong, and how much the wrong bits should cost relative to the right bits.

Question (expected around 800 words): Explain Peirce's pragmatic maxim, his account of the four methods of fixing belief, and how his fallibilism relates to the special status he gives the scientific method. Then evaluate this claim: because the maxim makes the meaning of a concept depend on its conceivable practical consequences, Peirce is committed to thinking that a true belief is just one that is useful to hold.

Student answer:

Peirce distinguishes four methods by which belief gets fixed. The method of tenacity refuses to consider anything that might shake the belief. The method of authority lets an institution, church, or state decide what is to be believed and censor the alternatives. The a priori method lets belief be fixed by whatever is most agreeable to reason, which Peirce says is just a dressed-up version of taste because it makes inquiry a matter of intellectual fashon. The scientific method is different because it makes belief answerable to something external: it assumes there is a reality whose character does not depend on what anyone happens to think about it, and that this reality will force agreement in the long run. Peirce's view is that the first three methods are unstable, since they can preserve belief only while a person or a community is insulated from alternatives, and they cannot correct themselves because nothing outside the method gets a vote.

The pragmatic maxim says that the whole meaning of a concept is the sum of the conceivable practical consequences of its object, so a distinction with no possible practical upshot is empty. Peirce presents this as a method for making ideas clear, not as a definition of truth, so the claim in the question is too quick if it says he simply defines truth as usefulness.

Peirce also defends fallibilism: inquiry never puts us in a position of certainty, and any belief, including a scientific one, remains open to revision. This does not mean all methods are equal; it means science gets its special status not from arriving at certainty but from being the only method that submits to a reality independent of opinion and can find and fix its own errors.

Still, I think the maxim pulls him further than he admits. If the entire meaning of a statement is its practical consequences, then any claim for which no possible observation or action would make a difference is meaningless — and that is basically the verification principle, so Peirce is rightly treated as a proto-positivist. And once you accept that, the only ground left for preferring science is that it is the most useful way to settle disputes and tell us what to expect. So although Peirce denies that truth is usefulness, the maxim plus his defense of science leave him with no consistent alternative. The claim is therefore true in substance, even if Peirce's official statements say otherwise.
