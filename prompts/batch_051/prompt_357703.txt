I'm in a philosophy-of-physics reading group, and a friend walked us through a version of the Rietdijk–Putnam argument that none of us has been able to get out of. I would like a diagnosis: either name the step that fails, or tell me which of my commitments I must give up if the argument is sound. The argument runs as follows. (1) In special relativity there is no frame-independent simultaneity: for any two spacelike separated events, there is an inertial frame in which they occur at the same time. (2) Let A be my snapping my fingers right now, here on Earth, and let B be a spacelike separated event that the Earth frame dates one week in the future — a particular flare on a star a hundred light-years away. (3) There is an inertial frame in which A and B are simultaneous. (4) A is real: it is happening now. (5) If two events are simultaneous, they cannot differ in reality-status; if A is real and B is simultaneous with A, then B is equally real, because a real event cannot be simultaneous with an unreal one. (6) Reality is not frame-relative: an event cannot be real for one observer and unreal for another. (7) Therefore B is already real, even though the Earth frame places it in the future. By chaining such frames together, the same move seems to make every event in spacetime real, regardless of where our frame places it. (8) But if next week's events are already real, then the future is as settled as the past — something to be awaited rather than brought about. That last step is what bothers me most, because it seems to turn deliberation and responsibility into a kind of illusion.

I would most like to keep two things: that what is real does not depend on an observer's state of motion, and that deliberation is not an illusion. But I do not know whether I can have both. So: where does the argument go wrong, if it does? If it is valid, which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it force me to say about time, becoming, and simultaneity? And if the argument can be resisted at different points, I want to know what each resistance costs — what the resulting view must deny, and what it gives up in return.
