I've been teaching a course on the ontology of music, and over several years I've collected my own pre-theoretic verdicts on a series of cases. They mostly feel sensible to me individually, but I cannot find a single principle that captures the pattern, and my working view has started to come apart. My working view is roughly this: a musical work is a type, performances are its tokens, and a recording is neither the work nor a performance of it but rather a document of some specific performance. But several of my verdicts below seem to pull against that picture, and I'd like to know whether my view can be repaired or whether some of the verdicts have to go.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. (1) Beethoven's Fifth Symphony: if every score, performance, and recording were lost but knowledgeable musicians remembered the piece, the work would still exist; the work is not identical to any score, performance, or recording, and no single performance is privileged as "the" work. (2) The jazz standard "Autumn Leaves": a slow ballad version, a bebop version at twice the tempo, and a solo piano reduction with heavy reharmonization are all performances of one work, even though they share little surface sonic detail. (3) A two-minute field recording of a thunderstorm released as a track: there is no work distinct from the recording itself, and playing the track back is not performing anything; the recording simply is the thing. (4) A studio electronic piece that exists only as a digital audio file, with no score and no acoustic performance ever intended: copies and playbacks are not performances, and yet the piece is still a work. (5) A wholly improvised duo set, one night only, never recorded: the performance itself is the work, if anything is; there is no repeatable something that later performances could instance. (6) A singer-songwriter's cover of a pop song, with a new arrangement and in a new key, is a performance of that same song; but a remixer's track built around a looped two-second sample of the song's most recognizable hook is a new work, not a performance of the old one. (7) A MIDI rendering of a Chopin prelude played by software, with no human performer involved in the sound production, still seems to me like a performance, or at least an instance, of the work. (8) A premodern melody transmitted orally, never notated until centuries later, and having drifted in contour, text, and mode along the way, is still one work throughout the lineage rather than a series of distinct works.

I'd like you to tell me what principle or small set of candidate principles best captures these verdicts. Please state each candidate precisely, identify which cases motivate each of its clauses, and test the candidates against my cases; devise two or three further cases of your own whose verdicts are forced by each principle and use them to pull the candidates apart. Finally, be explicit about which of my verdicts a defensible principle forces me to overturn, and what it would cost to keep them. Structure the response with these sections in this order: "Verdicts restated" (a short paragraph or table, roughly 150 words); "Candidate principles" (roughly 300 words); "The case-by-case audit" (roughly 500 words); "Further cases" (roughly 200 words); and "What I should give up" (roughly 200 words).
