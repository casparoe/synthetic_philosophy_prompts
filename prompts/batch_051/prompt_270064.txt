I've been meaning to run something by someone, because it's been eating at me for weeks. A friend of mine studies Islamic philosophy and recently walked me through al-Ghazālī's occasionalism — at least the version he teaches. The way he put it: no created thing has any genuine causal power. When fire touches cotton, the fire isn't what burns the cotton; God creates the burning in the cotton at that very moment, and the fire is only the occasion, not the cause. What we call laws of nature are really God's habit — ʿāda, they call it — the way a king might habitually feed a guest when a bell rings, without the bell causing the food. God could withhold the burning next time without any contradiction; it would just be a break in the habit. This landed hard on me because my dad just started on a beta-blocker, and now I can't hear the doctor explain how it works without mentally translating it into occasionalism against my will.

So I've been collecting my gut verdicts on cases, and I want to know what principle, if anything, ties them together. Fire on dry cotton: I cant shake the feeling that the fire itself does the burning, even though in the abstract I admit the occasionalist can account for everything I see. A wet match that doesn't light: this feels like evidence that the dryness and the chemicals are doing real work, and I resist giving that up. A billiard ball setting another ball in motion: here the idea that God habitually moves the second ball feels completely fine, maybe because I have no attachment to billiard balls. Those two should probably get the same verdict, and the fact that they don't bothers me. My hand rising when I decide to raise it: I'm most resistant there; if my decision doesn't cause the motion, then my acting is theater, but I can't tell if that's an argument or just a feeling. An eclipse predicted years in advance: prediction working like that feels as if it needs real necessity, or at least real powers in things, not mere habit. The story of Abraham and the fire that didn't burn: no trouble at all, I'm happy to say God simply suspended the habit there. And a coffee cup staying on my desk for the last ten minutes: if the occasionalist is serious that God re-creates it at each instant, that strikes me as absurd, but I can't locate the absurdity.

Is there a principle that captures all of these, or close to it, and does it push me toward occasionalism, toward ordinary secondary causes, or into some mixed view I don't have a name for? Please say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — say a nerve firing when I decide to move, or a placebo lowering someone's pain — and which of my listed verdicts it has to overturn. And because I'm actually trying to settle which way to go, be honest about your own account: tell me which argument or case would most likely change your mind about the principle you end up with, or where it's most likely to fail.
