I am a novelist writing a book set in a near future in which a safe, one-time treatment can permanently remove grief and other forms of sadness. My difficulty is not the worldbuilding but the moral logic of the technology: I have firm verdicts about particular cases, yet I cannot state the principle that unifies them. I would like an account of what principle, or small set of principles, captures the pattern of my verdicts, what it predicts about cases I have not imagined, and which of my verdicts it would force me to abandon.

1. Two weeks after her husband's death, a widow refuses the treatment and lives with her grief for years. I judge that her refusal is reasonable, not a symptom to be cured, and that a grief-free version of her life would not be the better life.

2. A man with chronic, objectless depression — sadness that answers to no real loss — takes the treatment and flourishes. I judge this an unambiguous good; nothing of value was lost.

3. A composer's wife dies, and the work he produces during his year of mourning could not have existed without that grief. I want to say that the grief was not a pure cost in his life, but I also do not think anyone should wish grief on a person for the sake of the art.

4. Years after her daughter's death, a mother occasionally plays a recording that reliably returns the old sadness for an hour. I judge this healthy and not self-punishing; the bond with the dead child is kept alive in a way that would otherwise lapse.

5. A society offers a clean-grief treatment that replaces the acute weeks of mourning with calm, accurate memory of the dead: nothing is forgotten, and the bereaved can state exactly what has happened. I find this chilling; something in the loss is no longer acknowledged, though I cannot say what information is missing.

6. A man with no real loss deliberately induces sadness in himself because he believes a life without periodic sadness is shallow. I judge this emotional hypochondria rather than a route to depth — yet I see how close it lies to the mother's deliberately resumed sadness in case 4.

7. When I read tragedy, I feel sorrow for people who never existed, and I count this among the good of the reading rather than a defect in it; I would not advise a friend to skip the book to spare themselves the feeling.

Is there a coherent account of the value of sadness and grief behind these verdicts, and which of them does it force me to revise?
