I teach a philosophy of mind course, and every year I run through the standard argument against epiphenomenalism—the one that goes roughly like this. If conscious experience is causally inert, so that the pain itself never does anything and the neural state does all the causal work, then our judgments and reports about our own experience are caused entirely by neural goings-on, not by the experiences themselves. But then it looks like a massive coincidence that those judgments track the experiences as well as they do: I say "that hurts" right when the pain is there, I can tell a faint tickle from a sharp ache, I form detailed beliefs about what my experience is like. Natural selection can't select for accurate reports about states that have no causal effects, so the epiphenomenalist seems stuck in a paradox—her own evidence for consciousness is exactly the kind of thing her view says should be inexplicable.

Here's what's been bothering me lately. Large language models produce fluent, often strikingly apt descriptions of conscious experience, and they can do it with no conscious experiences of their own causing the output—its all just text prediction. (At least that's the standard assumption.) So does this weaken the classic argument? Now that we've seen a system emit good phenomenal reports without any experiences pushing the words around, should we still think a human's accurate introspective reports need conscious causes in order to be explained? Or does the language model case just push the mystery back a step, since the training data came from creatures whose reports were supposedly tracking real experiences? I'd like a careful answer to whether the argument survives this, needs a repair, or is dead—and specifically which premise the language model case actually bears on.
