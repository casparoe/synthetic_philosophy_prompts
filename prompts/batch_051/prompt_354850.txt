Produce the specification sheet for a theory of perceptual experience, where the theory's task is fixed in advance: it must answer the argument from illusion and hallucination without begging the question against any of the serious contenders. I want the requirements any adequate theory has to meet, each backed by the case or consideration that forces it, and a clear ruling on which requirements are genuine and which are the preferences of one camp rather than neutral data.

Give the requirements as a numbered list. For each one: state the requirement, give the case or distinction that motivates it, and say what goes wrong in a theory that drops or softens it. Cover at least the following. (1) The basic cases: ordinary veridical perception; an illusion in which a real object appears to have a property it does not actually have; a hallucination in which no relevant object is present; and a deliberately matched pair in which a veridical experience and a hallucination are subjectively indistinguishable. (2) The distinction between an experience's being subjectively indistinguishable from another and its being the same fundamental kind of mental event. (3) The distinction between how an experience seems from the inside and what the subject judges or believes on its basis. (4) The distinction between the epistemological question whether perception justifies belief and the metaphysical question what an experience is; a theory may rest on one, but it may not use a claim about one as an unearned premise in settling the other. (5) The separation of the argument from illusion from the argument from hallucination: the first involves a real object misperceived, the second involves no object at all, and a theory that treats them as failures of a single kind must earn that assumption rather than assume it. (6) The fact that standard illusions keep looking wrong even after the subject knows they are wrong. (7) The time-lag case of seeing a star that may have ceased to exist, and what it forces a theory to say about the direct object of awareness.

Then sort each requirement into one of two columns: demands that any adequate theory must meet, and demands that only some programs treat as binding, with the cost of dropping each noted. In this sorting, take a definite stand on a contested item: decide whether the demand that a veridical perception and a matched hallucination fall under one fundamental kind is a neutral explanation of their indistinguishability or a tendentious move that favors sense-datum and representationalist theories over disjunctivism.

Next, inspect the list itself for internal tension. If some subset of requirements cannot be jointly satisfied without weakening one of them, identify it precisely: say which requirement would have to be weakened, in exactly what way, to make each main contender viable, and what price the weakening extracts.

Finally, name the one requirement that does the most work in excluding otherwise serious contenders, and state which theory it excludes and why. Then give a short scorecard for sense-datum theory, representationalism or intentionalism, and disjunctivism against the list. Keep the scorecard brief; the standard of evaluation is the deliverable.
