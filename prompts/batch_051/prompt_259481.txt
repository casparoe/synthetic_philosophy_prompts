Two friends of mine, Tanya and Reza, spent most of a dinner party arguing about a city ordinance, and I honestly could not tell who had the better case. The ordinance makes it a criminal offense to stand around in a public place in a way that is likely to alarm other people. Neither of them is a lawyer, but both had thought hard about the issue. The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

1. Tanya said the law is unfair on its face. A person standing on a corner has no dependable way to know in advance whether what they are doing will alarm someone, so they can end up punished for conduct they could not have known was criminal.

2. Reza replied that every law uses words with fuzzy edges — reasonable, reckless, decent — and we do not throw those laws out. A law does not need to decide every borderline case; it needs a clear core that an ordinary person can recognize, and the rest gets worked out in application.

3. Tanya answered that criminal law is special because the stake is punishment, so it has to meet a higher standard than the loose language people tolerate in everyday rules. And this ordinance does not even have a clear core, because whether you are likely to alarm others depends on other people's reactions, which the person on the corner can neither control nor know.

4. Reza said that is true of plenty of uncontroversial crimes: whether an act is threatening, or whether noise rises to disturbing the peace, also depends on how others receive it, and the law handles that by asking how a reasonable observer would take it. A blanket rule cannot list every alarming action; a general standard is the only way to avoid a checklist that invites loopholes.

5. Tanya said he was missing the more serious problem. The issue is not only notice; it is that a law this loose hands officers the power to decide, after the fact, who was being alarming — and those judgments will not fall on everyone equally. Vagueness matters because it licenses arbitrary enforcement.

6. Reza answered that discretion cannot be eliminated from any enforcement. What matters is that the standard constrains officers enough. And if we rejected every statute an unscrupulous officer could abuse, we would have almost no criminal law left, which cannot be the right test.

Please serve as the referee. For each of the six moves, say how far it is right or wrong and why. Also identify where each person overreaches or changes the subject, who is more right overall, and whether there is a principled line between an acceptably general criminal law and an unacceptably vague one that would settle what they were actually fighting about.
