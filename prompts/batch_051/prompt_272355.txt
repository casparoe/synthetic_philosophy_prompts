I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so I will describe the issue in terms I work with rather than in terms of legal theory. Repeatedly I have watched two respectable camps argue past one another about abandonware and orphaned software: a tool, library, game, or protocol implementation still matters to some community, but the author is unreachable, the company has dissolved, the license file is missing or ambiguous, and after a genuine search no one with standing can be found. One camp treats ownership as persisting by default: absence of a reachable rights holder creates no permission, exclusivity survives neglect until it is affirmatively released, assigned, or expired, and unauthorized modification or redistribution is unauthorized even when it meets no resistance. Orphanhood on that view is a discovery problem, not a change in entitlements. The other camp treats abandonment as morally transformative: sustained non-exercise of exclusivity plus the disappearance of anyone able to speak for the work loosens or extinguishes the claim and can justify community use, preservation, repair, and possibly redistribution after a diligent search. On that view an entitlement is not an inert veto that survives all neglect; it lives in a continuing practice of asserting, maintaining, and depending on the work.

Produce the document an adversarial collaboration between those two camps should converge on. Structure it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. The agreed claims should be substantive and specific to abandoned software and orphan works, not merely restatements of definitions. The disagreements table should state each residual point with enough precision that each camp would accept the wording of its own side. Then identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side. I expect candidates to include whether decades of silent non-enforcement are evidence of relinquishment or merely of inaction; whether a community's long reliance on an abandoned system creates interests that count against late enforcement; and whether preservation and security patching are best treated as narrow exceptions to exclusivity or as evidence that the default itself is mistaken. For each crux, name a concrete case or test both camps would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Flag any portion of the dispute that is merely verbal and would dissolve under a shared definition.
