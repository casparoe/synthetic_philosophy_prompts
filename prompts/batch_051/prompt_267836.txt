In our course on ancient philosophy and the ethics of promising, we have been discussing the special status of oaths sworn on the river Styx, against the background that a god who breaks such an oath is punished automatically: the perjurer is struck down and cut off from ambrosia, nectar, and the assembly of the gods for years. Write a dialogue between two equally competent characters about what ultimately binds in such an oath. One holds that the binding force consists entirely in the divine sanction, so the oath binds as a matter of prudence because the penalty is certain and dreadful. The other holds that the obligation not to break faith is prior to and independent of the sanction, and that the Styx simply marks, with special solemnity, an obligation that would exist even without it. Have them respond to each other's actual arguments rather than talk past one another, and have them work through the hard cases that ought to strain each view: an oath extracted under duress, an oath whose terms are ambiguous, and an oath to perform an act that is itself wicked. Each side should be forced to refine its position rather than remain a caricature.
