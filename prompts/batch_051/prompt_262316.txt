I've been arguing with my brother about whether it's fair to tear down statues or rename buildings, and the line 'you can't judge them by today's standards' keeps coming up. I get the rough idea, but I don't think anyone could actualy write down the rule behind it, and half the time it just works as a get-out-of-jail-free card. The thing I can't shake is that the 'everyone around them did it' excuse seems to apply equally to the cases I want to excuse and the ones I don't, so the rule can't just be 'it was normal then.'

So here's what I want: a working definition of when a historical figure genuinely deserves the standards-of-their-time pass. The definition has to handle this list: Jefferson owning people and fathering children with an enslaved woman while going on about liberty; Churchill's conduct around the Bengal famine and the contemptuous things he said about Indians; Columbus's time as governor of Hispaniola and the way he treated the people already living there; a Roman senator who owned slaves and never thought twice about it; a 19th-century surgeon who operated without anaesthetic and without really asking the patient, back when nobody did; and someone today who buys industrially farmed meat, assuming people in 200 years regard that the way we now regard owning slaves. I'm pretty confident the Roman senator gets the pass and Columbus doesn't, and I'd rather not commit on Jefferson or Churchill. The meat case feels like it should scramble any serious definition, so don't dodge it. Propose the definition, run it through all six, and tell me where it gives a verdict you'd defend but most people would hate, and where it just breaks down.
