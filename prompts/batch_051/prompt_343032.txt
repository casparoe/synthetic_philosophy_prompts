At a party last week two friends of mine -- both policy students, which somehow made it worse -- got into an argument about discounting the future, and I was stuck in the middle for the whole thing. They reconstructed it for me afterward as a numbered list, and I still can't referee it. (1) Maya said standard analyses discount at around 3%, which makes a life saved a century from now worth about 5% of a life saved now; so unless you think future people are 95% less important, the method looks like a moral error. (2) Dan said discounting is about money, not lives: a dollar today can be invested and turn into more than a dollar in a hundred years, so future costs and benefits have to be converted to present values, and ignoring that overweights the future. (3) Maya replied that you can't quarantine the move that way, because policy analyses apply the same discount rate to lives saved and species preserved, and then you've slid from "a future dollar is worth less" to "a future death is worth less." If someone wants to be a pure time discounter, fine, but they should own it rather than smuggle it in as a technical detail. (4) Dan said this blurs pure time preference and consumption discounting. Someone can care exactly as much about a person born in 2120 as a person alive now and still discount future *consumption*: future people will likely be richer, and a dollar matters less to a richer person. So the rate on lives could be near zero while the rate on money stays positive, and the real mistake is using one number for both. (5) Maya said there's a deeper problem: any constant positive discount rate, however small, tells you that enough harm far enough in the future gets outweighed by a small benefit today. At 1%, a catastrophe 50,000 years out rounds to nothing. So the numbers encode that the far future simply doesn't count, and no amount of fine print about opportunity cost undoes that. (6) Dan said that's not an argument against discounting, it's an argument against a constant rate; a rate that starts steep and then flattens out handles exactly this, and the fact that people use constant rates because they're easy is an implementation problem, not a refutation of the whole idea. They both turned to me, and I had no idea which moves were actually right, which were wrong, and which were just talking past one another.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move -- rate each numbered point from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each -- and then say what the two of them are still conflating and who, if either, has the better overall position.
