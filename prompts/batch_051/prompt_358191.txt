I'm taking a software licencing course and the copyleft unit has me completely tangled up. The criteria we got for whether something counts as a derivative work that the GPL can force to be GPL are roughly these: (1) it actually copies the GPL-covered code, verbatim or with edits; (2) it's linked or loaded into the same executable or address space as the GPL program; (3) it's shipped and operated as one combined program, not as the mere aggregation of two independent ones. But the hard cases always seem to circle back to how intimately the two programs talk, which makes me suspect criterion (2) is doing all the real work and (1) and (3) just decorate it.

Here's the case I have to write up for class. I'm building Harness, a proprietary plugin for SolverX, a GPL simulation engine. Harness is not dynamically linked into SolverX and never shares an address space with it: SolverX launches Harness as a separate process at startup, and the two chat over a local Unix socket using a private binary protocol. But the protocol is basically a wire-level mirror of SolverX's internal data structures, which I designed by reading SolverX's source, and Harness exists only to answer SolverX's calls and return results. A human never talks to Harness directly, and from the outside the two behave like a single program. My flatmate says I'm laundering a GPL program through a socket; my view flips by the hour. Go through the three criteria honestly: is Harness a derivative work of SolverX, and if the criteria say no, is that the right answer or a sign that the criteria are misstating the real principle? Where does SolverX end and Harness begin? You're an expert on free-software licensing and the conceptual side of copyright; take your time, brainstorm the edge considerations before you commit. Then lead with the bottom line in the very first sentence — yes, no, or the criteria underdetermine it — and give the criterion-by-criterion reasoning after, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
