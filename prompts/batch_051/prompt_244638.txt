We've been going back and forth in a seminar about the Epicurean argument that death isn't bad for the person who dies. The standard version goes something like: nothing can harm you unless you're around to be harmed; while you exist your death hasn't happened yet, and once it happens you no longer exist, so there's no subject left to undergo it. I'm not asking whether the argument is right in general. I'm interested in a specific new twist.

Suppose someone develops a cheap, infallible test that tells you, on any given day, the exact date and time you will die, decades before it happens. Assume nearly everyone takes it, and the results are shared the way we share birthdays, so everyone knows everyone else's deadline too. At first glance the test shouldn't touch the argument: it only changes when you learn about your death, not whether your death is an experience you can have. But in practice it seems to turn death into something that structures the whole of a still-unfinished life—people make plans backwards from the date, marriages acquire known expiry dates, and there is a massive new category of anticipatory dread that is real suffering while you're alive. Does the Epicurean argument survive this development, or does the anticipation show that it was always missing something about how death can be bad through its effects on your own earlier self? Which premise, if any, is actually under pressure here, and is there a repaired version that still says something true in a world with the test? I'm after what happens to the argument, not policy questions about whether the test should be banned.
