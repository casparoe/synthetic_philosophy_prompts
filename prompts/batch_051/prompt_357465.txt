I'm taking a philosophy of action class this term and something from my internship keeps bugging me, and I'd love some help thinking it through. The place I work runs an agile/scrum process. At the start of every two-week sprint, each person has to give a confidence percentage for their own tickets—like, 85% sure I'll finish the login-flow rewrite by Thursday. The scrum master puts all these into a spreadsheet, multiplies them by the story points, and calls the result a risk-adjusted sprint value, which the product owner uses to decide how much we can carry over. The official line is that we should be dispassionate forecasters of ourselves: saying 70% isnt a promise, its just an honest credence, and missing a 70% item only means the 30% world came up. But when I try to actually forecast my own ticket, the number I choose seems to change what I end up doing. If I say 90% I basically cancel my weekend to make sure I don't miss it; if I say 60%, I've already half-decided it's fine to slip. I cant get into the detached, outside-myself stance the spreadsheet assumes. In class we looked at the idea that deliberation crowds out prediction—that while you're still deciding what to do, you cannot simultaneously treat your own action as an event to assign probabilities to, because the probability you assign feeds back into the choice. But I'm not sure how far that idea is actually meant to go.

So my actual question is: what view of the relation between prediction and choice does this practice presuppose? Does it commit my company to the claim that a person can assign a meaningful probability to their own future action from inside their own deliberation, or can the practice just as well be interpreted as a sneaky way of extracting commitments from us? I'm interested in what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely sits comfortably with, and whether it's coherent at all. And if deliberation really does crowd out prediction, what should the team do instead—have other people estimate our tickets, ask for plans rather than probabilities, or keep the numbers but reinterpret them as resolutions or commitments? Or is there some middle position where assigning probabilities to your own choices is fine as long as you do it in a certain way?
