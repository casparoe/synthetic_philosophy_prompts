I have been reading the section "Of curiosity, or the love of truth" in Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature (Book 2, Part 3, Section 10), and I have built an argument from it that now strikes me as proving too much. I would be grateful for a diagnosis of where it goes wrong, or of which premise is least costly to abandon.

The passage that set me off is this, copied with Hume's spelling:

"And in an arithmetical operation, where both the truth and the assurance are of the same nature, as in the most profound algebraical problem, the pleasure is very inconsiderable, if rather it does not degenerate into pain: Which is an evident proof, that the satisfaction, which we sometimes receive from the discovery of truth, proceeds not from it, merely as such, but only as endow'd with certain qualities."

A little later he adds that "the pleasure of study consists chiefly in the action of the mind, and the exercise of the genius and understanding in the discovery or comprehension of any truth. If the importance of the truth be requisite to compleat the pleasure, 'tis not on account of any considerable addition, which of itself it brings to our enjoyment, but only because 'tis, in some measure, requisite to fix our attention."

From this I reconstruct the following chain of reasoning.

(1) The satisfaction taken in discovering a truth is not produced by the truth as such, but by features that merely accompany some truths: chiefly the exercise of the understanding in the discovery, together with a sense of the result's importance sufficient to hold the attention.

(2) If the satisfaction of inquiry is explained entirely by these accompaniments, then our apparent regard for truth itself is explained away: truth is valued only as an occasion, target, or pretext for intellectual exertion, never for its own sake.

(3) Knowledge therefore has no value for its own sake; it is good only as an instrument, whether as a means of engaging the faculties or of securing further ends.

(4) The same must hold for curiosity. If curiosity is a disposition to seek truth, it is at bottom a taste for the pursuit rather than a regard for the truth of what is found, and the correctness of the belief reached is not among the reasons for which the curious person seeks it.

(5) A person who spends a lifetime proving an abstruse theorem and a person who spends the same lifetime decoding increasingly elaborate artificial ciphers thus stand in exactly the same relation to the good of inquiry, provided the exercise of ingenuity is comparable and each regards the task as important. Nothing about truth or correctness, as such, makes the one inquiry better than the other.

(6) That conclusion is hard to accept. It would strip the intellectual life of its claim to be directed at reality, and it would leave unexplained why the love of truth is prized over a love of difficulty or cleverness. It also sits badly with the widespread thought that curiosity is an intellectual virtue precisely because it aims at getting things right rather than at being occupied.

So one of these steps must go. Each of (1) and (2) seemed unavoidable on a natural reading of the passage, yet I find (6) more plausible than any claim that would force me to abandon it. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? If you judge the chain valid, say which premise is least costly to give up and what exactly one pays for giving it up. I should also like to know what the rejection costs for the idea that curiosity is an intellectual virtue: is that idea a casualty of the argument, or can it survive on another footing? And please say what would change your mind about your diagnosis: which alternative reading of the passage, or which independent consideration about the value of knowledge, would lead you to a different verdict, and where do you take your own reasoning to be most likely to fail?
