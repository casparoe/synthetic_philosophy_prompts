I've been going back and forth about how much I actually buy a Mīmāṃsā-flavored idea, and I want to see whether my gut reactions to a bunch of cases add up to anything principled. The idea, roughly: testimonial cognitions are valid on their own, without needing a separate check, and a text with no author is in an especially strong position, because there's no speaker to lie, misremember, or have an axe to grind. In the Indian setting that's the Veda — beginningless, authorless, preserved by a chain of exact recitation — but I'm not asking about that text specifically; I want the general principle, and I dont care about citations.

My gut lands like this. (1) An anonymous, undated manual for consecrating a shrine that a community has chanted and copied verbatim for as long as records go back, with no named author and no claim to have one: I catch myself treating its instructions as the thing to do unless something specific speaks against them. (2) A signed manual by a living craftsperson known to be honest and expert: I trust that too, maybe equally — but when the two conflict in an emergency, I'd follow the signed one, which feels like a betrayal of my verdict on the anonymous text. (3) If the anonymous manual included a sentence claiming its words should be trusted precisely because no person composed them, I'd think the sentence adds nothing; self-certification can't do any work. (4) A text left unsigned on a park bench yesterday making precise medical claims: I give it no default trust at all, even though it's authorless in the ordinary sense. (5) If I learned the community keeping the anonymous manual had a motive to alter a passage but I couldn't prove any alteration, I'd stop trusting that passage. (6) If a long oral chain of individually trustworthy tellers goes back to a human nobody remembers, I'd trust that the wording survived but feel I still know nothing about whether the original content was true.

Is there one principle about testimonial authority — maybe intrinsic validity plus an account of defeaters — that captures all six verdicts, or close? If so, spell it out and tell me what it predicts for a case I keep hesitating on: a machine-generated set of instructions, no human author, that a community has used successfully for decades but whose source data no one can inspect. If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn, and why?
