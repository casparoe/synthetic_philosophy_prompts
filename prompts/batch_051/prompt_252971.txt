For most of my working life I was an experimental scientist, and I have come to philosophy late. I have been reading the debate between Humeans and anti-Humeans about laws of nature, and I find that each side captures something I am unwilling to give up, while neither side captures what I learned in the laboratory. Let me put a proposal before you and ask for an honest assessment of it, not a survey of the field.

I call it the load-bearing view of lawhood. Begin with a true, exceptionless generalization that a mature science treats as a law. On my view its lawhood is not an extra modal ingredient—no primitive necessity, no governing relation over and above the events themselves—but neither is it merely the fact that the generalization happens to earn a place in a best-system summary of the actual mosaic. Its lawhood is a structural property of the surrounding inferential network: a generalization is a law of nature to the extent that it is load-bearing. By load-bearing I mean that a great many downstream predictions, measurement protocols, instrument calibrations, conservation checks, and interventions within the relevant science depend on it in such a way that they would have to be reinterpreted, discarded, or heavily patched if the generalization failed. The first law of thermodynamics is load-bearing in this sense; the accidental regularity that all the coins presently in my pocket are copper is not.

The central claim is that this load-bearing character is not evidence that the generalization secretly possesses some hidden necessity, and it is not a merely sociological fact about how attached scientists happen to be to an idea. It is what lawhood consists in. Load-bearing is in principle quantifiable: one can ask how many independent regularities a framework would have to alter, and how many prior observations would have to be explained away, if a given generalization were refuted tomorrow. A law is, roughly, a true generalization with a very high cost of refutation, where that cost is indexed to the whole accumulated web of results in the relevant science.

I see the obvious objections. First, an undiscovered law would not count as a law on this view, which seems to get the order of discovery and lawhood backwards. Second, a sufficiently dense system of false beliefs—astrology is my own worrying example—could become load-bearing for its practitioners and would then count as containing laws, which offends me. Third, the view may be describing scientific practice rather than nature, and I do not want it to collapse into sociology of science.

Please take a definite position. Reconstruct the view in its strongest form if you think I have stated it poorly, indicate which existing family of positions it is closest to, and give me the three strongest objections ranked by how much damage they actually do. Then tell me plainly whether the view, suitably repaired, is a genuine competitor to both Humeanism and anti-Humeanism, or whether it merely relocates one of them. Do not lay out options and leave the choice to me; commit to a verdict and defend it.
