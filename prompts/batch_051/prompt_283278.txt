Got into a long argument last night with a friend about whether it actually matters that we call something a genocide rather than crimes against humanity or ethnic cleansing. She thinks the label is mostly ceremonial now, because once you've got crimes against humanity the legal duties to prevent and punish are roughly the same; I think the word still does independent work, or at least states act as if it does. Suppose someone had three years and a small research budget to spend on the conceptual and political side of the naming-of-atrocities debate, with the aim of changing how the label actually gets used in advocacy and international politics rather than just adding to the literature. Which two or three questions would you put first -- the special intent requirement (the main reason genocide is so hard to prove in court), the exclusion of political groups from the legal definition, the gap between the legal definition and everyday political use, whether the word's moral force comes from the law or the other way round, or something I'm not listing? Defend the ordering: which issues are upstream of the others, which are tractable without new treaty law, and which are overrated because settling them wouldnt change how states or movements actually talk.
