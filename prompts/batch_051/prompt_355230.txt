Act as a professional philosopher of biology working on the units of selection debate. Before committing to a verdict, brainstorm the strongest versions of both the view that the argument survives and the view that it is dead.

The gene's-eye view rests on a specific premise: biological evolution is best understood as acting at the level of the gene because the gene is the only entity stable enough across generations to serve as a replicator, while organisms are ephemeral vehicles. Now suppose a discovery is reported: in a clade of plants, an environmentally induced methylation state at a locus is transmitted faithfully for hundreds of generations, persists even when the underlying DNA sequence at that locus varies or mutates, and can occasionally transfer horizontally between unrelated lineages as a heritable methylation pattern unaccompanied by any corresponding DNA. The methylated state affects flowering time and is inherited as reliably as many DNA variants.

Does the gene's-eye argument survive this discovery, does it need repair, or is it dead? Identify precisely which premise the discovery bears on, and say whether the argument can be repaired without abandoning its central claim that the gene is the privileged level at which selection acts. If it survives, say what "the gene" has to become for that to be true; if it needs repair, state the repaired claim and what it concedes; if it is dead, say what the unit of selection becomes instead. Finally, tell me what would change your mind, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
