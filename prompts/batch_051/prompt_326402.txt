For a paper I am drafting on the metaphysics of laws, I have been trying to decide how much the classic Humean critique should actually lower my credence in anti-Humean views, and I would like your quantitative decomposition rather than a survey of positions. I keep returning to this passage from David Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section VII, Part I: "When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other." I read that passage as primarily an epistemological claim about the absence of a certain impression, not yet as the metaphysical claim that laws are mere regularities. So I want to know: given that absence, what credence should I have in the proposition that at least one fundamental law of nature, or the world's nomological structure itself, is something over and above the total mosaic of non-nomic facts—such as necessary relations among universals, primitive governing powers, or irreducible dispositions? Please give me a number, not a range, and decompose it. I would like at least four sub-questions, each assigned a probability and one line of justification, followed by an explanation of how you combined them. At least one sub-question should explicitly concern how much evidential weight the Humean passage deserves against anti-Humean views; other sub-questions could address explanatory circularity, scientific practice, objective chance, or the intelligibility of primitive governance. Then tell me which sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to, and specify the smallest concrete change—a philosophical argument or an empirical result—that would move your number by at least ten percentage points, and in which direction.
