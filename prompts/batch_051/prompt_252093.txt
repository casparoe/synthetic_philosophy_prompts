so I've been running this little philosophy-and-AI reading group at my college (around 12–14 undergrads, one 90-minute meeting a week), and I've gotten mildly obsessed with the "AI safety through debate" proposal — the idea that you can get at the truth by having two AIs argue in front of a human judge, because the side that's wrong will eventually trip up under questioning. lately it's also started following me into my actual life: last month I got into a reddit argument with somone who was clearly wrong but won anyway by flooding the thread with jargon and half-remembered studies, and I couldn't keep up with fact-checking him in real time. it made me *feel*, not just think, why the debate setup might be much too optimistic. a debater doesn't have to be right — he just has to be harder for a limited judge to check than the other guy. I'm especially interested in the obfuscated-arguments worry, where one side produces arguments that sound careful and hedged and expert, but the judge can't actually tell which premises are doing the work or whether the key claim is even answerable.

so here's what I want: a single-session activity for the reading group that makes them run into this failure mode themselves, through the rules and incentives, before I name it. no lecture up front — I want the temptation to obfuscate to show up because the game rewards it, so someone in the room realizes halfway through that they're arguing harder rather than better. the activity should be for about 12–14 people in 90 minutes, with a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the formal worry (limited judges, uncheckable claims, more rounds not necessarily helping, hedging that hides the load-bearing premise), plus an honest note on what the game can't capture about real AI debate (e.g., superhuman optimizers, or a judge who can query a trusted source instead of relying on vibes). I'd also like to know what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it.
