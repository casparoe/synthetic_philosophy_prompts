Me and my brother got into this game we saw in a youtube video, which I later learned is called the centipede game. It works like this: two players, a pot that starts at two bucks. On your turn you can take the pot — actually you'd get a bit more than half and the other guy gets a bit less — or you can pass, which doubles the pot and gives the turn to the other person. The game has a fixed number of turns, like ten, and if no one ever takes, the pot just gets split at the end. The weird part is that if you think backwards from the last turn, it seems like each player should always take at their very first chance, so the whole thing should end instantly with the first player grabbing two dollars. But when we actually played with real money, we both kept passing for a while and ended up with way more, and we're both pretty reasonable people, I think.

I've been chewing on this for weeks and it feels like it says something important about trust and why people cooperate, maybe about promises and business deals too. Is "rationality" broken in this game, or is the backwards reasoning smuggling in some assumption about the other person that's too strong? I want you to act as a professional philosopher who works on rationality and games: first brainstorm the main ways people have tried to dissolve the paradox, then tell me what you think are the most important implications of it for real life — friendships, handshake deals, the kind of situation where someone could just take the money and run. I don't want abstract math; I want to know what this should change in how I think about being reasonable with other people. And if the honest answer is that the implications are alot narrower than they feel, say that too.
