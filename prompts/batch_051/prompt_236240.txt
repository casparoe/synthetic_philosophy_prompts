I've gradually come around to a strong version of the view that almost nobody who gets called a "free rider" in ordinary political argument is actually guilty of the thing that is usually supposed to make free riding wrong. The position, roughly: free riding is wrong when it is wrong because of something like the principle of fairness — if you voluntarily accept the benefits of a cooperative scheme that others are sacrificing to keep going, you owe your share. The load-bearing words are voluntarily accept. For a whole class of genuinely nonexcludable goods (clean air, public radio, herd immunity, basic research, the legal system) you mostly cannot decline the benefit, and mere reciept isn't acceptance. So the principle, applied honestly, condemns far fewer people than it's usually aimed at. When someone says a tax evader or a public-radio freeloader is wrong because he's a free rider, something else is doing the real work — harm, a natural duty to support just institutions, the wrong of breaking a law you had some say in — but not fairness. In fact I've come to think the fair-play principle is close to a very local, excludable-goods principle: it works when you can take the benefit or leave it and you take it, and it goes quiet in exactly the large, diffuse cases where people reach for it.

I want you to act as a moral philosopher who works on obligation and collective action, start by brainstorming the structure of the view and its commitments, and then think carefully about what would count against it. I don't mean ordinary objections to the conclusion; I want a map of its exposure. Which cases, arguments, and results in nearby areas (promising, consent, estoppel, the duty to rescue, assurance games, the logic of public goods) would show that acceptance is not doing the work, that receipt can generate obligation after all, or that the acceptance/receipt distinction is unstable? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Be clear about which ones a committed fair-play theorist would themselves accept as counting against them, and which they'd dismiss as changing the subject. Also tell me how much of the view survives only by qualification — say, by a constructive notion of acceptance where you "accept" a benefit just by not rejecting it when rejection was possible — and whether by the time the view accommodates the obvious cases it has insulated itself into unfalsifiability, and whether that's a problem for a view of this kind. Finally, is the right lesson from the defeaters that I should keep the acceptance condition and just stop using the word "free riding" for tax dodgers, or that the acceptance condition itself was a mistake?
