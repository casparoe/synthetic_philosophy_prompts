I'm a novelist, so I spend my working life making characters legible to readers, but I've never been sure how far I owe legibility to the people in my own life. I've been checking my instincts case by case. Here are my verdicts:

1. My wife asks what I meant by a remark that obviously stung, and I say "you know perfectly well" and leave the room. I don't think that's fine — she's owed a straight answer.
2. A reader asks in an interview what the ending of my last novel meant, and I say the book has to speak for itself. I think that's completely fine, even though I'm refusing to be legible there too.
3. A close friend of years asks why I've gone quiet, and I tell her I've been swamped when the truth is the friendship has run its course. I think that's fine; she isn't owed a post-mortem.
4. My brother asks why I wasn't at our mother's funeral, and I say I couldn't get away when the truth is I couldn't face the family. I don't think that half-truth was fine.
5. A younger writer asks why I blurbed her book when I had real reservations, and I give a warm, evasive answer. I think that's fine — almost what the situation required.
6. When I'm in a bad depressive spell I tell everyone I'm fine. I don't think that's a refusal to be legible in any morally interesting sense, but my wife says it is, and that I owe her the truth.

Is there one priniciple that gets all of these, or close? If not, which verdict would you overturn first? And what does your principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on — whether I owe an interviewer an explanation for why I stopped writing for six years, when the honest answer would involve my mother's death and a period I don't really understand myself?
