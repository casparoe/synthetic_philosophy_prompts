Write a dialogue between two equally competent epistemologists, one a contextualist about the word "knows" and the other a defender of pragmatic encroachment, who are working through a single pair of cases together in a seminar room. The case concerns Sana, a commuter. At 9:00 a.m. she says, "I know the next train stops at Central Station"; her evidence is good but not perfect, and the only cost of being wrong is that she will wait an hour for the following train. At 3:00 p.m., with exactly the same evidence, reliability, and truth-relevant circumstances, she says the same words, but now being wrong means missing a job interview and losing a position she has spent months pursuing. The two interlocutors agree about all of these facts and agree that ordinary speakers readily call the morning claim knowledge while finding the afternoon claim doubtful. The contextualist holds that the word "knows" picks out different epistemic standards in different contexts of ascription, so the same sentence can be true in the morning context and false in the afternoon context even though nothing truth-relevant changed for Sana. The pragmatic encroacher holds that the practical stakes themselves bear on whether Sana knows: knowledge is not exhausted by truth-relevant and evidence-relevant factors, so her heightened stakes can make her afternoon claim false without any change in evidence.

The dialogue should be a genuine exchange, not alternating set speeches. Each character should at some point formulate the other's position in its strongest available form before challenging it. The contextualist must press the encroacher on why factors that are irrelevant to whether a belief is true or well supported should be able to destroy knowledge, and must answer the charge that contextualism turns the apparent loss of knowledge into something merely verbal. The encroacher must press the contextualist on why the apparent difference between morning and afternoon should be located in the word "knows" rather than in Sana's epistemic standing itself, and must answer the charge that pragmatic encroachment conflates knowing with being in a position to act.

Partway through, have them consider a divergence case: at 3:00 p.m., Ben, a third party who faces no practical stakes himself, considers whether Sana knows that the next train stops at Central Station, and says, "Sana knows the train stops at Central Station." The two should work out exactly which verdict each view predicts for Ben's utterance, explain how their predictions come apart, and connect that divergence to the more general dispute about whether the subject's stakes or the ascriber's context matters. End the dialogue with either a precise statement of the residual disagreement or an unexpected agreement about what kind of evidence would resolve it.
