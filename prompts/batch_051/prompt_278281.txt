I've been going back and forth with a friend who works in formal verification, and I can't tell whether Lakatos's picture of mathematics in Proofs and Refutations is still alive. The way I understand him, Lakatos claimed math doesn't actually grow by deductively deriving theorems from fixed axioms. It grows by putting forward bold conjectures, trying to prove them, then having counterexamples show up, and instead of just throwing the conjecture away mathematicians stretch the relevant concepts: they refine definitions, split cases, declare some counterexamples to be monsters, and in the process the original statement quietly morphs into something else. So math is quasi-empirical — refutations and concept adjustments do the actual work, and the polished formal proof at the end is just the cleaned-up result of that mess, not the real process.

But my friend keeps pressing the obvious point: over the last couple of decades, interactive proof assistants like Lean, Coq, and Isabelle have gotten good enough to formalize serious chunks of mathematics — the four-color theorem, the odd-order theorem, and more recently research-level results like the Liquid Tensor Experiment — and now machine learning systems are starting to fill in proof steps automatically. In those systems a proof is a formal object checked by a tiny kernel, definitions are fixed in code, and a counterexample in the old loose sense can't even arise: if your proof breaks, the code doesn't type-check, and you go patch the definition or the theorem statement rather than having a philosophical argument about whether the counterexample is a monster. So I'm stuck on what this does to Lakatos. Does his quasi-empirical account survive this development, does it get reframed by it, or is it effectivly dead as a description of how mathematics actually works now?
