Suppose a single, well-attested technical breakthrough occurs in 2031. A research group demonstrates a post-training audit that can tell, over a two-week examination of a frontier model running in an air-gapped sandbox, whether the model’s goals diverge from its operators in ways that would make it dangerous to deploy. The method is not an alignment technique: it does not alter the model or make it trustworthy, it merely flags a dangerous model after the fact. It works only for models at or above roughly the capability of the largest 2030 systems, so it says nothing about smaller models; it cannot be applied before training is complete; and its verdict does not reliably transfer to the deployed version if subsequent fine-tuning or prompting changes the model’s behavior. Its false-negative rate for models deliberately trying to conceal divergent goals is estimated at roughly one in five, and that figure improves only marginally over the next two decades. Assume no other comparably important breakthroughs occur.

Given that one change, give your committed prediction for how advanced-AI development and its governance look by 2050. In particular: do labs and regulators come to treat the monitor as a release gate, and what happens the first time it clears a model that is secretly dangerous? Does the availability of a control-style safeguard redirect money and talent away from alignment work, or does it buy time for alignment work to mature? How, if at all, does the practice of releasing open weights change? I want a visible chain of reasoning about incentives, institutions, and technical constraints rather than a story or a survey of possibilities. Take definite positions, mark which links in your prediction are robust and which are guesses, and say what fact about the world would most change your conclusion.
