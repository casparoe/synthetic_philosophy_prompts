I recently joined a committee charged with deciding whether our organization should keep funding a voluntary quality-assurance program that costs real money and makes us slower than less scrupulous competitors. At our last meeting the case for dismantling it was made almost entirely in one phrase: our field is a race to the bottom, so maintaining our standards unilaterally would merely cost us business without improving the market. One member with economics training objected that a genuine race to the bottom requires a coordination failure — a situation in which each actor responds rationally to its own incentives yet all end up worse off, sometimes called a multipolar trap — and insisted that our clients can observe quality well enough that the phrase is being misapplied. Another member used "race to the bottom" as a brute fact of competitive pressure, needing no special mechanism. By the end of the meeting it was obvious that the same four words were being used to assert several different things at once.

I would like you to disambiguate what a person might precisely be claiming when they say that an industry, profession, or market is a race to the bottom. Lay out the distinct readings as a menu: who or what is racing, toward what outcome, by what mechanism, over what time horizon, and whether the phrase is meant descriptively or normatively. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and what evidence would bear on it. In particular, distinguish the claim from ordinary price competition — what, if anything, does "race to the bottom" add to "competition drives margins down"? Then say which of these readings would actually support the argument that our committee should discontinue the program, and which reading is most likely doing the work when the phrase is used to close debate rather than open it.
