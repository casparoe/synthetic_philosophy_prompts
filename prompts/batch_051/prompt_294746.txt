I chair the adverse-event review panel at a medical device manufacturer. Each month we review around ten reported incidents and vote yes/no on whether the evidence supports a causal link to one of our products. The vote decides whether we file adverse-event reports, issue corrections, or restrict use of a device, so errors in either direction are expensive. Panels of three to seven clinician–engineers read a shared file, deliberate for roughly half an hour, and vote; I then ratify, overrule, or send cases back. I want a durable procedure instead of case-by-case improvisation.

Please turn the Condorcet jury theorem, together with its known failure modes, into a reusable decision procedure for determining how much weight to give such a majority vote in a given case. The procedure should be usable in fifteen minutes by colleagues without formal background. It should tell us when to take a majority at face value, when to require a supermajority or unanimity, when to weight members rather than count heads, and when to treat the vote as carrying little or no information — including cases where a strong majority should push us away from its conclusion rather than toward it. It should spell out how to check the theorem's preconditions in practice: a single well-defined yes/no question with a fact of the matter, individual competence better than chance, and independence of votes once shared files and deliberation are taken into account. Pay particular attention to correlated evidence: six people reading the same faulty log are not six independent tests. Where a check requires data we do not have, say what we would need to collect and what to do in the meantime, and mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases: (a) six of seven members vote "caused" after all rely on the same unreproduced firmware readout supplied by one engineer; (b) three members read fully separate evidence packages and vote 2–1 "not caused," but neither of the two in the majority has the clinical specialty most relevant to the failure mode while the dissenter does; (c) the panel votes 4–1 "not caused" after a recent internal training module and a shared anecdote about a competitor's over-reporting have given everyone a strong prior that devices like ours rarely cause this type of harm. For each case, show what the procedure instructs and why. Include at least one case where it tells me to overrule or disregard a comfortable majority — an answer I would not welcome — and say plainly what the procedure cannot do, such as manufacture independence, set base rates, or choose our tolerance for risk.

Finally, say how confident you are in the specific recommendations and identify the single point you are least sure about.
