I have been pushed by a series of cases toward a deontological rather than a consequentialist reading of frugality, and I want the position stress-tested rather than defended. The position is this: the obligation to be frugal is not the obligation to produce or maximize frugality in the world. It is an obligation about how I may permissibly use resources — an upper bound on what I take, consume, or waste, one that applies to me in a way that is not simply derived from a calculation about aggregate depletion, waste, or future well-being. The rival, consequentialist reading treats frugality as valuable only instrumentally, as a way of lowering total resource use or waste; on that reading, acts and policies are assessed by whether they maximize the frugality that matters, and one person's extravagance can in principle be offset by someone else's greater restraint. I suspect the consequentialist reading mislocates the value, because it allows one agent's vice to be traded against another agent's virtue in a way that misses what conscientious thrift actually governs.

A case I find genuinely difficult: someone whose private frugality is known to make others around them consume more, so that their restraint produces no net resource saving at all. The consequentialist reading takes this to weaken or dissolve the obligation, since the restraint is not doing the relevant good; the deontological reading seems to leave the obligation standing. I do not know whether the deontological reading should be revised to handle this, and I want it treated as a live test rather than a settled defeat.

So: map out what would count against the deontological reading. In particular —

What possible cases, observations, or well-taken arguments would most threaten the view that frugality is best understood as a personal constraint rather than as a value to be promoted? Distinguish the defeaters that would be decisive from those that would be merely awkward, and rank them from fatal to merely awkward.

Which of these would a committed defender of the deontological reading themselves concede counts against the view, and which would they dismiss as changing the subject or as importing a criterion the position never accepted?

Under what pressure does the view collapse into the consequentialist reading — for example, once it admits thresholds, emergencies, or cases where restraint foreseeably produces harms or perverse consumption by others? Does such collapse actually occur, and if it does, is that a refutation of the contrast or a clarification of it?

How exposed is the position, honestly assessed? Is it well insulated against these tests, and is that insulation a virtue or a vice here?

Finally, be explicit about what would change your own assessment: which single consideration, if it turned out to be true or well supported, would lead you to reverse or substantially revise your ranking or your verdict on the position, and where in your own reasoning about this you are most likely to be going wrong.
