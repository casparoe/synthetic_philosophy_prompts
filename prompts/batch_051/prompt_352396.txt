I have always taught the standard line that honoring sunk costs is irrational, but I recently encountered a decision-theoretic argument that seems to dissolve the fallacy, and I cannot tell whether it is sound or merely a redescription. The argument runs:

(1) Rational choice consists in maximizing expected utility, where the utility of an act is the value the agent assigns to the possible outcomes that might result from it.
(2) Expected-utility theory places no restriction on the content of the outcomes it evaluates; in particular, an outcome may be individuated as a complete history, and an agent may coherently prefer the history in which an earlier investment was not wasted to the history in which that same investment was wasted.
(3) If an agent has such a preference, then continuing a failing project can have higher expected utility than abandoning it even when the project's purely future net payoff is lower, because continuing is exactly what determines whether the same past investment is embedded in a wasted or a non-wasted history.
(4) But choosing to continue because one's previous investment must not have been wasted is precisely the pattern the sunk-cost fallacy condemns as irrational.
(5) Therefore the sunk-cost fallacy is not always a fallacy: for a coherent agent with the preference described in (2), honoring a sunk cost is rational, and the blanket prohibition on sunk-cost reasoning needs a further restriction on admissible preferences that expected-utility theory does not itself supply.

Is the argument valid? If not, identify the exact step that fails and give the corrected version of that step. If it is valid, say what the argument has and has not established, and identify the step at which a defender of the orthodox view should most plausibly resist.

Answer in exactly five numbered points, one for each step of the argument, with the fifth point also carrying the overall verdict. Each point should be one or two sentences. Do not add an introduction or a conclusion.
