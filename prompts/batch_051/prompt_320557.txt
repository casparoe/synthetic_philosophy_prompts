Act as a moral philosopher who works on how to aggregate small harms across people. I've been going in circles on this, and I'd like you to think carefully: maybe start by brainstorming three or four candidate principles, then test them against my cases before settling on one.

It's become personal because I recently joined a small public-health committee, and the old dust-specks-vs-torture tradeoff (one person tortured for fifty years vs. a astronomically huge number of people each getting a single speck of dust in the eye for a second) now feels like my Tuesday afternoons. My honest gut verdicts are these, and I'm aware they might be inconsistent. (1) In that dust-specks case I choose the torture; the specks just don't add up for me. (2) One person with a severe week-long migraine vs. twenty thousand people each getting a mild hour-long headache: I spare the twenty thousand, no hesitation. (3) One person losing both legs vs. a million people each losing one fingernail: I save the legs; I don't think a million fingernails beat that, even though losing a nail is way worse than a speck. (4) Same person, spread over a year: a single person gets either a broken arm or a hundred thousand specks spread out over the year; I'd take the specks for them, and I know that looks like it contradicts (1). (5) One person mildly hungry for a day vs. five hundred people mildly hungry for a day: I give the hunger to the one person; I'm not against adding across people when the harms are identical.

So: is there a principle that gets all five, or close? What would it say about the one I keep flip-flopping on — one person going blind vs. two hundred people each loosing one tooth? And which of my verdicts, if any, should I give up? I don't care about labels or which author said what; I want an actual rule I could apply in a meeting.
