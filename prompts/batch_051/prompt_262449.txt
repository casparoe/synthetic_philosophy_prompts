I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar — about 15 students, one 75-minute session — and we're deep into a unit on the timing of experience. I want to run the session on color phi and the Orwellian-vs-Stalinesque distinction (you know the standard result: a red flash then a green flash at two nearby spots, short gap, and people report a single moving dot that changes color mid-path). I tried it last year by just showing a video and asking "so, when did the color change happen?" and it flopped: half the class ended up debating free will. So this year I want a runnable in-class activity where they actually run the demo and have to produce reports under some pressure.

What I have in mind: students first witness a color-phi sequence a few times and write down, individually, what they saw and — crucially — exactly when each feature appeared, like the position versus the color, what was on screen when they first noticed the motion, and when the color changed. Then I want to split them into small "audit teams" that have to reconcile their reports into one agreed event timeline, the way you'd reconstruct a bug report from conflicting luser logs. Each team has to choose: was the live experience itself edited before it reached awareness, was there a real earlier experience that memory rewrote afterwards, or is this a question with no fact of the matter — and they have to defend that using only their own reports as evidence, not by appealing to what they read for class.

So I'd like you to design the full session: the exact demo setup (two browser tabs with colored circles, an LED pair, or a projected slide; something cheap and reliable), the timing parameters that are most likely to produce the effect in a classroom, the worksheet or rule structure for the audit teams, a 75-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in their reports back to the philosophical distinction. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — people who report just two seperate flashes, people who immediately say "it's just an illusion, so what", people who can't keep seeing apart from remembering — and how to handle each without me just lecturing at them. And do be frank about the one thing the activity can't stage: the claim that there is objectively no fact of the matter about the timing can't be produced by any demo; it has to be supplied in debrief. Write this as an expert in philosophy of mind and in philosophy pedagogy. Start with a quick brainstorm of the key parameters and what each phase should force students to notice, then give me the finished activity in a form I can print and run.
