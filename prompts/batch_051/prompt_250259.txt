Take Adam Smith's actual views on empire as your starting point: he thought Britain's monopoly over the American trade mainly enriched a narrow merchant interest while costing everyone else, that the colonies were on net a drain—more expense to govern and defend then they returned—and that the sensible options were either full representation for the Americans or simply letting them go. He also thought the East India Company's rule in India was about the worst government you could have, because a trading company asked to act as sovereign would govern in its own commercial interest, not in the interest of the governed. Now fast-forward him to the post-1945 wave of decolonization and to the later argument that the colonial powers just swapped formal empire for economic domination through trade agreements, aid conditionalities, and multinational firms. What would Smith make of the 'neocolonialism' critique? Where would he agree with it, where would he push back, and what would be his distinctively Smithian worry—the thing a generic free-trader or a generic anti-imperialist would miss? I don't want 'he'd support free trade and leave it there'; I want the extrapolation to run through his actual theory of empire, including the places where that theory puts pressure on the neocolonialism story itself.
