For an upper-level undergraduate seminar on contemporary moral philosophy (fourteen students, 105 minutes), design a structured game or role-play that makes the Scanlonian restriction on interpersonal aggregation feel like something participants discover under pressure rather than something they are told. Assume the students have read Scanlon and initially find the restriction implausible. The claim your activity should dramatize is this: when a principle governs what we owe to each other, the reasons of separate individuals may not simply be summed, so a large number of individually weaker complaints cannot automatically outweigh one person's much stronger complaint. The heart of the activity should be a forced distribution problem involving a single identifiable person who faces one serious, potentially life-altering harm, and a large number of other people each facing an individually modest but real loss. The rules should be arranged so that the natural first move is to add the many small losses together and use that sum to outweigh the one serious loss, and so that this move then breaks down because the principle behind it has to be defended to the one person it disadvantages. By the end, participants should be able to state, from their own experience in the game, why the additive move felt natural and why it failed on its own terms. Do not use the transplant, trolley, or lifeboat scenarios; invent a new setting. The deliverable should include roles and rules with enough structure that the activity runs itself, a minute-by-minute timeline for the full 105 minutes, a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto Scanlonian ideas such as justifiability to each person, reasonable rejection, and the ban on aggregating complaints across separate lives, and an honest note on which features of the view the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply, including at least the absence of real stakes and the fact that a classroom arbiter is not an impersonal standard of reasonable rejection.
