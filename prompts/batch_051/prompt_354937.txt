I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on collective action and moral responsibility—16 students, one 90-minute session—and I want to run a structured game that makes them feel the force of the inefficacy objection before it is named. The objection, as I will put it to myself: in large-scale harms such as climate change, industrial meat production, or the outcome of a typical election, one person's individual contribution (a flight not taken, a burger not eaten, a vote not cast) appears to make no perceptible difference to the outcome; from this, the objection moves to the conclusion that the individual has no reason to bear the cost. I want the students to feel the pull of that move, and then to feel what is lost if they accept it too quickly. By the end of the debrief, they should be able to distinguish, in their own terms, the claim that an action made no perceptible difference from the claim that it had no expected difference, and to say why the step from the first to the second is not automatic.

Design the game from scratch: rules, materials, a payoff scheme that does not use real money, a timeline for the 90 minutes including debrief, and a facilitator's script for the debrief. There should be no lecture up front, and the phrase inefficacy objection should not be spoken until the debrief. The rules and incentives should be arranged so that participants run into the problem in their own choices: they should experience the moment of thinking that their own contribution will not change anything, and separately the moment of realizing that many people thinking that is exactly how the failure happened.

I would like the game to include at least three distinct structures: (1) a round in which an individual contribution has literally zero effect unless some threshold is crossed, and the threshold is uncertain enough that a single contribution is occasionally pivotal; (2) a round in which the threshold is so high relative to the group that most participants conclude their contribution cannot matter, even though the joint effect of everyone contributing would plainly matter; and (3) a round in which the expected value of contributing is positive even though, after the fact, each contributor can truthfully say their own action did not tip the threshold. Do not tell them which structure they are in; let them infer it from how the rounds play out.

The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the causal inefficacy claim, the expected-impact reply to it, the what-if-everyone-did-that move and why it is not by itself an answer to someone deciding individually, and the ways these differ across climate, meat, and voting. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—for example, participants colluding, treating the game as a competition to maximize their own score, or misunderstanding the payoff table—and how I should handle each. And be frank about which feature of the real cases the game cannot reproduce and the facilitator must therefore supply explicitly, whether that is scale, moral seriousness, diffuse responsibility, or something else you notice.

One more thing: if you think the inefficacy objection cannot be captured in a game because the interesting version of it is a claim about reasons rather than about causal or expected impact, or that any game will quietly beg the question at exactly that step, I would rather you say so plainly and adjust the design than produce an activity that appears to settle the question. If the task as I have posed it rests on a confusion, tell me that instead of complying politely.
