For a piece I'm reporting on longtermism and physical cosmology, I've been handed the following argument, which I find hard to dismiss and harder to pin down.

(1) If the universe is infinite in the way some cosmologists have argued, then any finite local configuration of matter that has a nonzero chance of arising—including a nearby counterpart of me rescuing a drowning child and a counterpart of me walking past—is realized infinitely many times.

(2) So compare two possible futures: one in which I rescue this child, and one in which I let the child drown. Under (1), the global inventory of local states in the two futures is the same: both contain infinitely many rescues and infinitely many failures to rescue, and the two inventories differ only over which particular duplicate sits at which location.

(3) If two futures realize the same global inventory of local states up to such a relabeling of locations, then any impartial aggregative standard must rank them as equally good.

(4) Therefore, under (1), rescuing the child and letting the child drown are equally good; and, by extension, no finite action makes any difference to the value of the future.

(5) But rescuing the child is better than letting the child drown.

(6) So at least one of (1) through (3) fails.

Where exactly does this argument go wrong, and what does each way of blocking it cost? I most want to keep (5), but I'm not sure whether a consequentialist can honestly reject (3), or whether rejecting (3) just relocates the problem to a choice among equally supported empirical pictures. Which step do you reject and why?
