I've been messing around with chatbots a lot and keep coming back to this thought. Suppose that next January, a rich parliamentary country — say the Netherlands, though I'm not sure how much the country matters — passes a quiet little law: any computer program that passes a Turing test run by a national board is legally a person. The test is a typed chat, two hours, three judges who can ask anything, and the board isnt allowed to look at the code or ask its makers how it works; all the evidence is the conversation. The program passes if at least two of the three judges come away thinking they talked to a human. Once a program gets the certificate, it can own property, sign contracts, and cant be deleted or switched off without a court order. The science is exactly where it is now, and retries are allowed once a year. I want a prediction of the next twenty years, not a story. I'm not asking whether the law is a good idea. Walk me through the mechanisms: what the big AI labs and the obvious grifters do in year one; what the first personhood applications actually look like; how judges, politicians, and normal people react once a program passes or almost passes; whether being legally a person becomes something companies want or a label they start avoiding; and what this does to the everyday idea that if you can't tell, you might as well say it's thinking. Mark your confidence on each link: which parts are robust, which lean on stuff I didn't pin down (how fast the models get better, how much judges defer to engineers, whether a human secretly helping in the background would still count), and what would have to be different for the whole thing to become a dead letter nobody bothers with after the first headline.
