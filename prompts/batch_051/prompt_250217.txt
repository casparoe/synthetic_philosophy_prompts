I am a novelist at work on a book about a writer accused of stealing another writer's plot. While researching the legal dispute at the center of the novel, I kept encountering a distinction the law treats as foundational: copyright protects the expression of an idea, but not the idea itself. The more I tried to locate the boundary between the two, the more an argument took shape in my mind that seems to show the boundary cannot be located at all. I find the conclusion unacceptable, yet I cannot find the flaw, and I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) Copyright protects the expression of an idea, but not the idea itself.

(2) Any expression, described at a sufficiently high level of abstraction, becomes an idea: a novel's plot, reduced to its sequence of events, is an idea; a character, reduced to a type, is an idea; a prose style, reduced to a technique, is an idea. What is protected at the level of sentences becomes, one level up, unprotected material.

(3) Conversely, any idea, stated with enough concrete detail, becomes an expression: the bare notion of a retired detective drawn into one last case is an idea, but a point-by-point account of this detective, this case, and these events is a telling, and a telling is precisely what the law protects.

(4) Therefore idea and expression are not two different kinds of thing; they are the same thing described at different levels of abstraction.

(5) A distinction that is only a difference in the level of description cannot ground a stable boundary between what a person owns and what belongs to everyone.

(6) If there is no stable boundary, then any line the law draws between protected and unprotected material is arbitrary.

(7) The only non-arbitrary positions, then, are to protect every writing, treating every idea as someone's property, or to protect none.

(8) Both positions are absurd: the first would allow the first writer to record an idea to own every later telling of it, and the second would abolish copyright altogether.

(9) Therefore the distinction between idea and expression is incoherent, and the legal protection of expression rests on a line that cannot be drawn.

I would rather keep the distinction than accept either of the positions in (8), and I am especially reluctant to surrender (1) or (5). But I want to know what I am paying. Which premise or step should I reject, why did that step look sound, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, which premise is the least costly to give up?
