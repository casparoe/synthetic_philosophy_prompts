I've been trying to pin down my actual views about the dollar auction and escalation of commitment, and I keep going in circles; I honestly can't tell where the contradiction is, if there is one. Here's where I've landed:

(1) I take the sunk-cost doctrine literally: once a cost is behind you and no future action can recover it, it's irrelevant. Any argument that runs since we've already spent this much we can't stop now is, as far as I'm concerned, just wrong.

(2) But I also think that if I've somehow all ready bid in a dollar auction, the right move each time the other player tops me is to top them back rather than drop, even once both bids have passed the prize, because dropping means paying my last bid for nothing while topping at least preserves a chance of coming away with something.

(3) At the same time, I think entering a dollar auction against a competitively minded rival is a mistake, and that a clear-eyed person sees beforehand that the game is rigged to transfer money from both bidders to the auctioneer.

(4) I endorse temporal stability for rational choice: if continuing is rational at each individual stage, then I can't see how refusing to enter could have been rational on the same grounds, because the only thing that changes is the number of bids placed, which, by (1), shouldn't matter.

(5) In real organizations, I usually read the we-can't-stop-now move as sunk-cost thinking and judge leaders accordingly. But I want to leave room for a leader rationally continuing a failing project because abandoning it publicly advertises that the original decision was a bad one, and the reputation hit is a real future cost.

I can't tell which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and which one claim is doing the most damage. Could you go through the commitments, rank the real tensions by severity, and say what the cheapest fix is and what it costs?
