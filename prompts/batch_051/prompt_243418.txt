I have been working out a distinction I call contract corruption versus relationship corruption, and I would like a candid appraisal of whether it holds up.

By contract corruption I mean the model that seems to dominate anti-corruption law and policy: a public official receives a private benefit in exchange for a specific official act, such as a bribe, a kickback, or a quid pro quo. By relationship corruption I mean a standing pattern of gifts, jobs, contracts, and favors between an official and a constituency, in which no particular favor is tied to any particular decision, but the relationship as a whole produces durable expectations of mutual support. For example, a politician channels a road contract to a local businessman, later arranges a position for the businessman's nephew, and then draws on his network at election time. No single episode looks like a bribe; the relationship is the thing being maintained.

My view is that relationship corruption is genuinely corruption, not merely clientelism or local custom, and that Western anti-corruption frameworks are bad at seeing it because they were built around the contract model: a discrete, provable exchange between two parties. But I worry that the distinction proves too much. Ordinary gift-giving among colleagues, church mutual-aid funds, and even social welfare programs all create standing relationships of benefit and expectation without being corruption.

Please give me an honest assessment. Is this a distinction that already exists under a better name, or am I re-describing something familiar? What does it get right? Where exactly does it blur or break down, and what, if anything, separates relationship corruption from legitimate forms of ongoing reciprocity? Finally, is it fair to say that the Western contract model is a culturally specific lens, or would a defender of universal anti-corruption standards argue that some discrete exchange is precisely what makes a practice corruption in the first place?
