I'm a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas, not a philosopher, so forgive the muddle. Lately I've been trying to work out what I actually think about political obligation—the Hobbes problem about why anyone owes a state anything at all. Hobbes says we leave the state of nature, where there is no law and everyone lives in fear of violent death, by authorizing a sovereign who can actually protect us, and that obedience is owed exactly as long as the protection lasts. That sounds right in the abstract, but when I line up my gut reactions to concrete cases, they refuse to form one rule.

Take these. A hiker gets lost in a blizzard and a stranger takes her in, feeds her, gives her a place to sleep. In the morning the stranger asks her to help dig out his car. I think she owes him the help even though she never promised a thing. But a tourist in a foreign country for four days uses the roads and assumes the police would help if she were mugged; I don't think she owes that state taxes or jury duty or conscription, even though she's receiving the same kind of protection Hobbes says is the whole point of the contract. So why does the hiker owe and the tourist doesn't? My best answer is something about depth of reliance, but I cant make it precise.

Now add these. A citizen of a functioning state never voted, never signed anything, and says she never consented. I still think she's bound by the laws, and I'd think that even if her only reliance on the state were that her livelihood depends on contract law. An undocumented resident has lived under the same state for twenty years and built a business; I think she owes the state obedience too, even though if anything she has less consent than the citizen. I'm comfortable with both, but note that consent is doing no work in either.

The case I can't resolve. The state stops sending police to a housing project; a gang provides whatever security exists there. The state still mails tax bills. I don't think the residents owe those taxes—protection has lapsed. But I also don't think the state has lost the right to forbid the residents from forming their own armed patrol. I hold both verdicts and I can't see how they go together: taxes lapse when protection lapses, but the general ban on private force doesn't. What principle says that?

Then the outer edges. A perfectly legitimate state, full consent and fair procedures, enacts a law requiring every citizen to donate a kidney to a stranger if they are a match. My gut says no one is bound by that even though the process was flawless—some part of you never enters the covenant. That feels like Hobbes's point that you can't sign away your right to resist death, but donating a kidney is not literally death, so I'm already stretching the idea. And one more: if an invading army takes over and actually imposes order, I have the uncomfortable feeling that after a while the invaded population owes the occupier obedience, even though I also want to say the government in exile stays legitimate. Protection and legitimacy seem to be coming apart.

The best I can do is a rough principle: you owe obedience to whichever power actually supplies your security, in proportion to how deeply you have come to rely on it, for exactly as long as it supplies it, and no contract can bind your body or your life. But I already know it misfires—it would make the tourist owe a little, and my gut says she owes nothing; it gives me no reason why the failed state can still ban armed patrols; and the body/life exception won't reach the kidney case unless I stretch body to include organs, which is a stretch I'm not sure I can defend.

So what I'd like from you: is there a coherent principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures most of my verdicts and says plainly which ones I have to drop? Make the principle precise enough that each clause is doing work, and tell me which case or conviction motivates each clause. Also, what does the repaired principle say about a gated community whose private security firm is the only body that ever actually protects its residents—do they owe allegiance to the firm, the state, both, or neither? And when you answer, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question.
