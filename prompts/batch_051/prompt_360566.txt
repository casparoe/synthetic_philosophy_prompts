Act as a poet with a working command of formal verse and analytic modal metaphysics; plan the poem carefully before drafting, and do not settle for merely summarizing the two positions in stanza form. Write a 19-line modified villanelle — five tercets and a closing quatrain in which the first and third lines of the opening tercet return as refrains, alternating as the last line of each subsequent tercet, and reappear together at the end of the quatrain — that dramatizes the disagreement between two accounts of what makes a claim like "I could have turned out differently" true: transworld identity and counterpart theory. The two refrains should carry the disagreement rather than naming it. Let one refrain express the transworld-identity picture and come back word-for-word identical at each occurrence: the very same individual exists in another possible world with different properties, so sameness across worlds is strictly preserved and de re modal claims are about that same individual reoccurring elsewhere. Let the other refrain express the counterpart-theoretic picture and come back each time with one word or a short phrase altered, so that each recurrence is recognizably similar but not literally the same — a distinct, contextually selected stand-in, just as a counterpart is a resembling individual in another possible world rather than you yourself existing there. The fact that one refrain survives each repetition intact while the other returns only as a similar variant should itself make the reader feel the difference between identity across worlds and replacement by a surrogate; when the closing quatrain brings the two refrains together, it should show how the positions keep passing each other without resolving the dispute, and it should make a reader feel what each view buys and what each gives up. The poem should be accurate enough that a specialist in the metaphysics of modality recognizes both theories as they are actually formulated, and legible enough that a reader who has never heard of counterpart theory experiences it as a poem about possibility, selfhood, and how our talk of "what could have been" quietly commits us to one of two very different pictures. After the poem, supply a short prose note of three or four sentences that (a) states where the poem takes poetic liberties or leans on a contested interpretation of either view, and (b) separates what is settled background in the debate from what is genuinely contested, making explicit which claims belong on each side and not presenting a contested view as the consensus. Use American English throughout.
