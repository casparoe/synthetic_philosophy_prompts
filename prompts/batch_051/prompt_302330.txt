Here is an asymmetry in ordinary moral judgment I have been turning over for some time. Consider a physician in a region where cholera recurs each year because the municipal water supply is contaminated. She treats patient after patient, and she is admired for it. But if she refuses to use any of her standing to push for repairing the water system — and especially if the hospital keeps adding cholera beds while the pipes remain broken — many of the same people who admired her begin to criticize her. They say she treats the symptom while leaving the cause untouched, and that by making the disease survivable she may be taking pressure off the city to fix what produces the patients. Now consider a donor in a wealthy country who funds a program that distributes oral rehydration salts for cholera in a poor region. The program saves lives and asks nothing about the upstream causes: the absent sanitation infrastructure, the policies that keep the region too poor to build it, the local government's neglect. The same people are far less likely to call this donor complicit, to accuse the program of entrenching the system, or to say the donor treats symptoms and ignores causes. Yet the physician and the donor seem to act on the very same logic of marginal intervention: help the people in front of them, cheaply and measurably, without challenging the system that produces the need. One is judged to be missing something morally important; the other is judged generous and effective.

Is there a defensible principle that makes the physician's relation to the upstream cause different from the donor's in a way that justifies the different judgments? Or is the asymmetry an artifact of distance, salience, or professional role that we should refuse to organize our practice around? I want the strongest principle you can construct, tested against nearby cases — a local donor who could easily meet the mayor, a physician from abroad working in the same clinic, an aid agency that refuses to coordinate with the government while running the same program — and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or abandoned.
