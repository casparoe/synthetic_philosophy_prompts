Design a structured classroom exercise for an upper-level ethics seminar (roughly 16 students, 80 minutes) on John Taurek's challenge to the idea that, when only some people can be saved, the number of people in each group is by itself a decisive moral reason. The exercise should make participants feel both the pull of aggregation and the resistance to it before those terms are named. It should be runnable without money or special materials, and its rules or roles should force repeated choices about allocating a scarce, life-saving resource between a single identified person and a larger number of strangers. Arrange the rules so that the group discovers for itself why someone might think the numbers do not by themselves count, and also discovers the cost of treating them that way. Include a timeline, materials, a debrief that maps specific moments in the exercise onto the distinction between summing individual losses and respecting each person's equal claim, a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run, and an honest statement of which feature of Taurek's view the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator.
