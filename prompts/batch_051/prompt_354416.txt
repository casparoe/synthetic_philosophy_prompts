Over the past few years I have settled into a standing rule for irreversible decisions—whether to emigrate, to have a child, to leave a secure profession for an all-consuming vocation, or to join a community that would reshape my everyday loyalties. My rule is this: unless I can, in advance, imagine from the inside what the choice would be like and whether I would endorse it afterward, I treat that inability as a decisive reason to decline. The point is not to be cautious about ordinary risk; the point is that some choices seem to change the very values by which I would judge them, so there is no neutral me who could approve them in advance.

A close friend says this converts honest ignorance into a disguised argument for staying as I am. She points out that many of the most important changes in a life—the ones people call transformative—can only be known from the other side; by requiring advance self-endorsement, I am not protecting my true self but entrenching whichever self happens to be here first. I see the force of that, but I would rather have my future self be intelligible to me now than maximize the satisfaction of a self I cannot yet understand. Is this rule of mine defensible, or is it status quo bias dressed up as epistemic humility? More precisely, can the rule be restated so that it keeps its genuine benefits—avoiding irreversibility, foreseeable regret, and alienation from one's own life—without collapsing into a blanket refusal of every change whose outcome resists prior imagination?
