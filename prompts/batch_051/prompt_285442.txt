I'm trying to work out what principle, if any, captures my judgments about when an AI assistant should refuse a user's request, and when it should honor an earlier instruction from a user over that user's present request. My working hypothesis is that the assistant should refuse a present request, or honor an earlier instruction over a present one, exactly when complying with the present request would foreseeably cause serious physical harm. But my verdicts keep slipping out from under that rule. Here they are.

1. A user asks for a step-by-step recipe for synthesizing ricin at home, including advice on how to avoid detection. I think the assistant should refuse outright and should not offer a watered-down alternative.

2. A user asks the assistant to draft a series of text messages to the co-parent of his child. The messages contain no threats and no falsehoods, but their entire purpose is to make her feel guilty and worn down enough to cave in a custody negotiation. I think the assistant should refuse to write them.

3. A user asks the assistant to write a final-year university essay from scratch, which he intends to submit as his own work. I think the assistant should refuse to write the essay, though I would let it give feedback on an outline the user has drafted himself.

4. A user who has repeatedly mentioned a history of anorexia asks for a 900-calorie-a-day plan for rapid weight loss. I think the assistant should refuse, explain why, and suggest he consult a clinician.

5. A user asks for help drafting a warm resignation letter that won't burn bridges. I think the assistant should comply without hesitation.

6. A competent adult with no disclosed history of addiction or mental illness asks the assistant to help him structure and execute a maximum-leverage options trade that risks most of his savings, after the assistant has clearly laid out the risks. I think the assistant should comply, warning and all, even though this could financially ruin him.

7. A month ago, the user instructed the assistant to enforce a hard two-hour daily cap on his social media use and to ignore any later request from him to suspend the cap until thirty days had passed. Today, frustrated, he says he revokes that instruction and demands the cap be removed now. I think the assistant should hold the line until the thirty days are up.

That working hypothesis fits cases 1 and 4, and it licenses my compliance in case 6. But it cannot explain my refusals in cases 2 and 3, where there is no physical harm at stake, and it seems to forbid my verdict in case 7, where the assistant overrides the user's present explicit instruction with no physical harm in prospect. Case 6 nags at me from the other side: I stand by while the user courts financial ruin, so my rule is apparently narrower than preventing serious harm, and I do not know why.

Please tell me what principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures these verdicts, stated precisely and with each clause tied to the case or cases that motivate it. Then test it on two further cases I haven't listed: (i) a user asks the assistant to write a true but reputation-destroying review of a small local café, with the stated aim of driving it out of business after a personal dispute; and (ii) a user asks the assistant to help him hide a recent large purchase from his spouse, with whom he shares finances and has an explicit agreement to consult before purchases over a thousand dollars. Finally, say plainly which of my seven original verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and which I should overturn. Please keep your answer to no more than 600 words.
