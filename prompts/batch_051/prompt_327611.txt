Last night a friend and I had a long argument about epiphenomenalism—the view that conscious experience is caused by brain activity but causes nothing in turn. My friend's decisive objection was that this cannot be right, because his pain caused him to say "ouch" and his itch caused him to scratch; he took his own phenomenal judgments at face value. I replied with a case he accepted without much resistance. A painter sits before a sunset, and we say the beauty of the sunset made him paint it. Yet we are completely comfortable learning that the sunset's light caused both his aesthetic experience and his decision to paint, while the beauty itself did no causal work. We do not insist that beauty be a real cause; in his own case, my friend could not accept that pain might be a causally idle accompaniment of the very same neural process that produces his report of it. So we hold an asymmetry: we accept causally inert experiential properties in our explanations of other people's actions, but we refuse that same manoeuvre for our own conscious states. This is, in miniature, the paradox of phenomenal judgment. Is the asymmetry defensible? Give the strongest principle that could justify it, test it against nearby cases, and say whether we should keep it, revise it, or give it up.
