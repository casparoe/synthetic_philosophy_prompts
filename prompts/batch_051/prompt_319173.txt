In an upper-level undergraduate seminar on philosophy of mind (12 students, 90 minutes), I want a runnable classroom activity built around a famous argument from John Stuart Mill's An Examination of Sir William Hamilton's Philosophy. The argument, roughly paraphrased because I don't have the text in front of me: Mill contends that our belief in other minds is not a direct intuition but an analogical inference. In my own case, I experience a regular link between certain outward bodily signs — gestures, facial expressions, vocal sounds — and the inner sensations and thoughts that accompany them. When I observe the same outward signs in other bodies, I infer that inner states accompany them there too, even though I can never observe those inner states directly. Mill's point is that this inference is entirely analogical and therefore less certain than our knowledge of our own minds.

Design an activity that makes participants actually perform this inference themselves, under conditions that let them feel both its force and its limits, before the instructor names Mill's argument. The idea should emerge from what participants do and discover, not from a lecture up front.

The core of the activity should be a game in which participants must judge, from outward behavior alone, which of several "interviewees" has a mind in the relevant sense. At least one interviewee should be answering from their own occurrent thoughts, while the others operate under a fixed, rule-based script. The guessers should have to ask questions, observe answers, and form judgments without ever getting to see the inside of the process. Arrange the rules and incentives so that guessers naturally fall into Mill-style analogical reasoning: they compare the interviewees' outward behavior with what they believe their own behavior would reveal about their own inner states. Then introduce a twist or a second round that puts pressure on the analogy — for example, a scripted interviewee who mimics exactly the outward signs of genuine thought, or a condition that forces guessers to explain what, if anything, they are inferring to beyond the behavior itself.

After the activity, the debrief should map specific moments in the game onto the parts of Mill's argument: the first-person regularities, the observed third-person signs, the analogical inference, and the gap between behavior and inner state that the inference is meant to bridge. The debrief should also mark the moments where participants resisted the inference, demanded more evidence, or became unsure whether they were detecting minds or merely reading scripts.

Finally, include an honest note on which feature of the problem of other minds this activity cannot reproduce — for example, that the game gives participants a real fact of the matter about who has a mind, whereas the philosophical problem includes the question of whether there even is such a fact for us to detect, and whether the analogical inference could fail across the board.

I want a committed design: make definite choices about the exact rules, roles, materials, time allocations, and debrief questions, and defend the choices you make. Don't present a menu of possible activities.
