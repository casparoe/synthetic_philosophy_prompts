Ok so here's something I've been chewing on. I work at a boatyard on a small island and a while back I got pulled into the old bar argument about the boat that gets every plank replaced one at a time and whether it's still the same boat. People went in circles for hours, so I started thinking about how you could make it concrete instead of a word game.

Imagine the island's boat registry made this rule. When a boat is first registered, a surveyor weighs all the major parts, hull planks, keel, mast, engine block, rudder, whatever, and records them on a birth certificate for that boat. Every year at renewal, the surveyor checks what share of the boat's total weight is still made up of those same recorded pieces. If more than half of the total weight is still from the original pieces, it's legally the same boat: it keeps its registration number, its inspection history, its age-based fee schedule, and its insurance rating. If the originals ever drop below half, the boat is legally a brand-new vessel. It has to meet the current construction codes, not the ones from when it was built, the fees reset, the registration number changes, and any unpaid yard debts attached to the hull get wiped because legally the old boat no longer exists.

Im not really asking whether this is a good rule in the abstract. I want to know how it would actually play out. What would boat owners, repair yards, insurers, and the registry itself start doing once they understood the threshold? How would people game the fifty percent line? What weird second-order stuff shows up, like someone hoarding the old scrap from a boat to keep it on the right side of the line, or someone deliberately crossing the line to shake off a bad safety record or dodge a salvage lien? Does the rule actually track what the bar argument was really about, or does it just turn into a paperwork game? And if you tried to patch the worst loopholes, would the fixes just gut the whole idea? Walk me through the mechanics, not the yes-or-no about what same really means.
