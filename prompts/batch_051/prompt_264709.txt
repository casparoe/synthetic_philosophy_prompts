You are a moral philosopher who works on rescue cases, aggregation, and the ethics of saving lives. Think slowly; before you settle on an answer, brainstorm several candidate principles and test each one against my cases, then say which survives and what it costs.

I'm a journalist writing about triage and about the old charge, pressed by John Taurek, that in a choice between saving one person and saving five, the numbers themselves do no work: each person has a claim to be saved, and the fact that five people each have such a claim does not give them any greater claim than the one. My own verdicts on rescue cases will not fit into either that picture or the simple "save the most" picture. I want you to help me find the principle behind my verdicts, or show me which verdicts I need to give up.

Here are the cases, each with the verdict I actually reach.

1. Symmetric strangers. A building with two wings is on fire: one wing holds one unconscious stranger, the other holds five unconscious strangers. Both wings are equally easy to reach, and no one has a special connection to anyone. I would save the five, and I would say it would be wrong to flip a coin.

2. One friend. Same scene, but the single stranger is a very close friend. I would save him, and I would defend that afterward; I don't believe I'd have acted wrongly.

3. Spouse. Same scene, but the single person is my spouse and the other wing holds two hundred strangers. I would save my spouse, and I don't accept that doing so is wrong — however terrible the outcome is.

4. Risk to me. One stranger can be rescued with almost no risk to me; five strangers can be reached only by a route that will likely collapse on me. Saving the five would be heroic, but I don't think I'd be obligated to take that risk. Saving the one is permissible.

5. Certainty. One stranger can be saved with certainty, or five strangers can each be given a 20 percent chance by taking the other route. I would save the certain one, even though the expected number of lives saved is the same.

6. Promise. I have promised the family of one trapped stranger that I'll bring him out; while keeping that promise I come across five other strangers I could rescue instead. I would keep the promise and save the one, and I'd defend that as the right thing to do.

The nearest rule I can state is: each person has an equal claim to be saved; the claims aggregate; but my relationships, the risk to me, certainty, and my promises can each change how the claims weigh. That feels like a pile of exceptions rather than a principle, and I don't see how it survives cases 2 and 3 while still giving me case 1.

Please give me a principle — or a small set of competing principles — stated precisely, with each clause motivated by one of my cases. Apply it to my six cases, saying which verdicts it preserves and which it overturns. Then apply it to cases I haven't settled: two strangers versus one stranger; two close friends versus twenty strangers; one close friend versus one stranger; my spouse versus a million strangers. Tell me plainly where the principle is most counterintuitive, and whether the best version saves all six verdicts or forces me to drop one or two.

End your answer with exactly one line beginning with "Summary:" and containing a one-sentence statement of the principle you recommend, or your diagnosis if you conclude no coherent principle can do the job.
