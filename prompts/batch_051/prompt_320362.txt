I'm a philosophy of physics PhD student and I keep hearing physicists and philosophers say that symmetries explain conservation laws: time-translation symmetry explains conservation of energy, rotational symmetry explains conservation of angular momentum, and so on, with Noether's theorem doing the heavy lifting. I've worked up an argument that this can't be right, and I want to know which step, if any, fails.

(1) A symmetry is by definition a transformation under which the laws/equations of motion are unchanged, so applying it never changes what physically happens.
(2) So a symmetry is not a difference-maker: whether or not the transformation is applied, the system evolves the same way, and nothing about the physically real outcomes differs.
(3) Explanation requires a difference-maker. If O would have happened no matter what, citing X doesn't explain why O rather than not-O.
(4) Noether's theorem says that every continuous symmetry of the Lagrangian (or action) comes with a corresponding conserved quantity.
(5) Therefore Noether symmetries cannot explain conservation laws; the theorem just gives us a tidy way of re-describing them.

I half-suspect steps (1)-(2) are sneaking from 'the transformation changes nothing' to 'the symmetry property changes nothing', but I can't make that objection precisly. Or maybe the failure is really at (3): maybe not all explanation is difference-making, and symmetry explanations are exactly a counterexample to that principle. Tell me whether the argument is valid, and if not, which step fails on what reading, and whether some weaker version of the conclusion survives.
