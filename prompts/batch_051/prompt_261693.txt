I run the risk desk at a small sports-trading outfit—we take positions on maybe a dozen markets a week, and we're small enough that one bad month can blow the bankroll. We've been running on Kelly-ish intuition: when an edge is real, size the bet to maximize expected log wealth, and if we're unsure, bet some fraction of it. But "some fraction" keeps turning into either too small to matter or too big to survive, depending on which trader you ask.

I want a reusable decision procedure my traders can actually follow for sizing a position, grounded in geometric rationality—the idea that what matters is long-run growth rate, not expected value, because wealth compounds multiplicatively. The procedure should be a sequence of questions: is this bet part of a long sequence of similar bets or is it a one-off? do we have an actual estimate of the edge, or just a story? how big is the uncertainty on that estimate? are the positions we're already carrying correlated with this one? and so on. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer and where the procedure stops with "no bet" or hands the decision to me. The hard cases I want handled explicitly: one-off bets (where the time-average argument may not apply), correlated simultaneous bets, skewed payoffs with a tiny probability of a big win, and a hard drawdown limit that pure Kelly ignores. My traders are sharp at handicapping but allergic to anything beyond a spreadsheet, so the procedure has to be runnable by someone like that.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a modest-edge sports line where the edge estimate is noisy; a high-conviction longshot futures bet that is effectively one-off; and a bet that looks +EV but is correlated with three positions we already hold. I expect the procedure will tell us to bet less than our gut says on at least one of these—I want honesty about that, and about what the procedure cant do (it can't manufacture a real edge, and it can't protect us from a bad run).
