I recently read a feature article about Stephen Wolfram's A New Kind of Science, and I am trying to work out how much of its summary I should trust. The article's account runs roughly as follows. Wolfram begins from the observation that very simple computational rules, such as the elementary cellular automaton Rule 30, can generate patterns of apparent randomness and complexity, and he takes this as evidence that much of the complexity we see in nature is best explained by simple underlying programs rather than by the equation-based constructions of traditional physics. The centrepiece of the book is the principle of computational equivalence, which the article glosses as the claim that all processes that are not obviously simple are computationally equivalent, so that a system such as a dripping tap, once it crosses a fairly low threshold, can compute anything the human brain can. This is supposed to amount to a new kind of science: traditional equation-based methods work only for systems with special regularities, whereas most systems of real interest are computationally irreducible, meaning that no equation or shortcut can predict their behaviour in advance and the only way to discover what they will do is to run them. The article adds that critics dismiss all of this as unscientific, since A New Kind of Science makes no falsifiable predictions and its central principle is really an unfalsifiable claim about all possible computations. Finally, the article reports that Wolfram takes computational irreducibility to resolve the problem of free will: because even simple systems can be computationally irreducible, there is no calculation that determines what a person will do, and our choices are therefore free in the only sense available.

How fair is this as an account of Wolfram's actual position? I would like a claim-by-claim audit: which elements are accurate, which are overstatements or conflations, what a sympathetic reader of Wolfram would say in response, and whether the article's conclusion that the project is unscientific survives the corrections.
