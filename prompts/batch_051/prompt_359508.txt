I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about brain-computer interfaces and mental privacy, going mostly by my gut on cases, and I'm not sure my gut is consistent. I'd like you to find the principle behind my verdicts, if there is one, and tell me which of my calls it forces me to give up.

Cases:

1. A trucking company gives drivers an EEG headband that detects the onset of microsleep and sounds an alarm. Processing is on-device, nothing is stored or sent anywhere. Verdict: fine.

2. A company pays people to wear a consumer EEG headset while they watch ads and sells the aggregate attention data to advertisers. Verdict: fine, a little gross but fine.

3. A health insurer offers a premium discount to customers who voluntarily wear a headband that detects early signs of a depressive episode and pings the person's own phone, not the insurer. Verdict: fine, though I worry it's a slippery slope.

4. An employer makes a BCI headset mandatory for remote customer service reps and docks bonuses when the device says their attention was off-task. Verdict: not fine.

5. Police have a suspect in custody and use a P300 oddball test to probe whether he recognizes details only the culprit would know. The result can't be used at trial, but it guides how hard they press in interrogation. Verdict: not fine.

6. A person with locked-in syndrome uses an implanted BCI to speak. She consented, through an advance directive, to let the company use her neural data to improve the decoder. Verdict: fine.

7. Parents of a non-speaking, pre-verbal child with a rare condition use a headband that predicts pain episodes before the child can show distress, so they can medicate early. Verdict: fine.

8. A person starts a channel that livestreams raw EEG and decoded guesses at what he's thinking, for subscribers. Verdict: fine, it's his brain and his business.

The case I keep going back and forth on: an airport security screening lets passengers choose between a standard pat-down and a quick EEG scan that flags whether they're being deceptive about what's in their bag; the scan is optional, but "optional" at airport security never really feels optional.

My tentative principle is something like: decoding is OK when the thing being decoded is a low-level, bodily-ish state rather than propositional mental content, and the person genuinely opts in. But case 6 is about as propositional as it gets and still seems fine unless consent and benefit-to-the-person do most of the work, and then I can't tell why cases 2 and 4 come apart, since both decode attention in a work-ish setting, except that one is optional and aggregated.

So: is there one principle that gets all eight cases right, or nearly right, and does it put the airport scan on the fine side or the not-fine side? If not, which of my verdicts should go and why? And please say what the principle you settle on says about a case I havent listed: a court orders the maker of a consumer headset to turn over a year of someone's sleep and attention data in a civil lawsuit about a car crash.
