Over the past year I have been running a court-mandated anger-management group, and I keep noticing an uncomfortable symmetry in the work: the tools we teach — breathing protocols, if-then scripts, stimulus control — are designed to work precisely by making someone less responsive to the details of the situation, more automatic, less like a person weighing a situation and more like a routine executing. That unease sent me back to the philosophical example of the sphex wasp: a wasp whose egg-laying sequence runs so rigidly that if one preparatory step is disturbed, it simply starts the whole sequence over from the beginning, unable to step back and revise its plan. The example is meant to ask what a creature would be missing if it were perfectly competent yet mechanical, and whether the difference between that and us is what free will amounts to. I want a critical reading of the Ludovico Technique sequence in the film A Clockwork Orange, and of the film's ending, through that lens. Take both the film and the philosophical question seriously. Where does the film genuinely dramatize sphexishness — rigid, stimulus-bound behavior that replaces agency rather than expressing it — and where does it run together distinct things: automaticity, compulsion, external control, and the flexibility that might actually matter for free action? Does the film, by making the conditioned Alex an object of pity while the first half showed his brutality as chosen, end up defending an older picture of free will as undetermined choice, and does the wasp example complicate that picture from inside? I want the honest critic's version of this: where the lens illuminates, where the film resists the reading, and what the film adds to a philosophical debate that bare thought experiments tend to flatten. Please begin with the reading itself, with no preface or commentary.
