I am a PhD student in the philosophy of cognitive science, working on scientific practice in deep learning. I have been reading interpretability papers and am struck by a tension in how they treat internal states. In one standard practice, a researcher freezes a trained model, trains a linear probe on a layer's activations to predict some property the input is known to have—say, whether a syntactic agreement relation holds, or whether a board position is winning—and, when the probe's accuracy is high, reports that the model has learned to represent that property. In another standard practice, the same researcher will caution that a high-performing probe does not show that the model actually relies on the property, because probes can learn to extract information from features the model never uses in downstream computation. Yet the field keeps publishing the former result while endorsing the latter caveat.

My question is not about which claim is true, but about what philosophical commitments this pattern presupposes. Does the practice of inferring representations from probe performance presuppose some form of realism about the model's internal states—that there is a fact of the matter about what the network represents? Could an instrumentalist or deflationary view of representations, on which talk of a model representing syntax is just a useful summary of input-output behavior or probe performance, license exactly the same experimental reports? Or does the practice quietly commit its practitioners to the view that hidden activations have semantic content in a stronger sense?

I am hoping for a reconstruction of the implicit theory of representation that the practice needs, a statement of where it is underdetermined by rival theories, and a sense of what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were abandoned—for example, would the probe-and-report move still be publishable if one thought there was no fact of the matter about what the network represents?
