I teach biology at a religiously affiliated secondary school, and this year a perceptive student asked me how the fossil record—with its evidence of predation, bone pathologies, and extinction across hundreds of millions of years—can be reconciled with the doctrine that God created a good world and that death and suffering entered it only through the Fall. I fell back on the usual distinction between scientific and theological questions, but the answer felt evasive, and the question has stayed with me. To get clear about it, I have tried to turn the difficulty into a single argument, and I would like to know whether the argument goes through.

The argument runs this way. (1) If the world is the creation of a good God and was made good, then it does not contain wholly gratuitous suffering, where gratuitous means neither a consequence of sin nor ordered to any good. (2) The fossil record documents animals dying by predation, starvation, injury, and disease long before any creature capable of sin existed. (3) Dying in such ways involves suffering in the familiar sense—pain, fear, and distress—not merely the end of a biological process. (4) Suffering that predates all sin cannot be explained as the consequence of sin. (5) For pre-human animal suffering, no ordering good is available to us, and a purpose that cannot be specified cannot do the work of explaining actual pain. (6) Therefore, by (1), a creation containing hundreds of millions of years of pre-human animal suffering is not the wholly good creation the doctrine describes.

I have a suspicion that the weak point is (5), because it seems to convert our inability to state a justifying purpose into the conclusion that no such purpose exists. But I am not sure that is the real defect, and I can also imagine a defender of the doctrine rejecting (1) on the ground that the goodness of creation has never meant the absence of all creaturely suffering. Is the argument valid? If it is not valid, identify the exact step that fails and say what reading is needed to make it work and what reading breaks it. If it is valid, say which premise a defender of the doctrine can most plausibly deny without quietly surrendering the doctrine itself.
