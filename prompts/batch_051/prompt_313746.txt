I'm working up a research proposal for a philosophy-of-science fellowship (say 8,000 words, 18 months) and I want you to turn an half-formed idea into a proper proposal. The topic is wether computational irreducibility really puts a limit on explanation, and I keep coming back to this bit of Wolfram in A New Kind of Science, right after he says traditional theoretical science has never gotten far with systems whose behavior isn't simple. He writes:

> For the point is that at an underlying level this kind of science has always tried to rely on computational reducibility. And for example its whole idea of using mathematical formulas to describe behavior makes sense only when the behavior is computationally reducible.

My hunch is that this move deserves closer scrutiny than either side of the existing debate gives it. The usual line is either that irreducible systems resist theory because explanation is basically compression (so if there's no shortcut, there's no explanation), or that a step-by-step simulation should itself count as an explanation, which has always felt like lowering the bar. But it seems to me both sides share a premise: that an explanation is something that lets you avoid doing the system's full computation. What if that premise is wrong? Specifically, suppose explanation is not a shortcut device but something more like a map of what would change under perturbations — a statement of what depends on what, even if the only way to learn the output is to run the computation. Then Wolfram's inference, from "no shortcut" to "mathematical-formula science makes sense only when reducible," would only hit one kind of explanation, not explanation full stop.

Be realistic about scope — I want something I could honestly sell to the committee. Don't bother with citations or a bibliography, I'll add the literature myself; names of positions are fine.
