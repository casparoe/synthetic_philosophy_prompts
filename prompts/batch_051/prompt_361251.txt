I am a PhD student in early modern political philosophy, and I have been working with Pascal’s analysis of justice and force in the *Pensées*. In the fragments concerned with justice and force, he argues, as I understand it, that justice without force is powerless because there will always be offenders, while force without justice is tyrannical; therefore the two must be combined, and one must either make what is just strong or make what is strong just. This much looks like a sober constraint on legitimate institutions. But I have built an argument from it that now seems to prove too much, and I cannot locate the bad step.

(1) A norm is law, rather than a mere aspiration, only if it is backed by a reliable capacity for enforcement.  
(2) An enforcement capacity is justified only if the norms it enforces are just.  
(3) Therefore every legitimate legal order must satisfy Pascal’s requirement: it must make the just strong and the strong just.  
(4) If legitimacy consists in satisfying that requirement, then any durable legal order will tend to satisfy it, because a state that enforced unjust norms too effectively would be mere tyranny, and a state that had just norms but no effective force would not have law at all.  
(5) But then durability across time begins to function as evidence of legitimacy: the union of justice and force is just what staying in existence seems to require.  
(6) Yet there are durable regimes that enforce deeply unjust norms, and there are just norms that remain unenforced.  
(7) So Pascal’s requirement, taken as a criterion of legitimacy, is either vacuous or an apology for whatever happens to last.

Where does this go wrong? I would like to keep the thought that justice without force is not really justice in public affairs, but I also want to be able to say that a durable regime can be thoroughly unjust. What do I have to give up, and which step is the cheapest to reject?
