Does the following argument show that counterfactual history is either vacuous or no longer about the actual past? I want to know whether the argument is valid, and if it is not, exactly where it goes wrong.

(1) Every historical event is caused by some conjunction of antecedent conditions together with the actual laws of nature.
(2) If antecedent conditions C together with laws L cause an event E, then, given C and L, E could not have failed to occur.
(3) Therefore, given the antecedent conditions and laws that actually obtained, no historical event could have failed to occur.
(4) Counterfactual history asks what would have happened if some historical event had not occurred — for example, if the assassination at Sarajevo in 1914 had not taken place. Such a question presupposes that the event in question could have failed to occur.
(5) But if, as (3) says, no historical event could have failed to occur given its actual antecedent conditions and laws, then the presupposition in (4) is never satisfied; counterfactual questions about history have impossible antecedents and are therefore vacuous, or at best only trivially true.
(6) So counterfactual history is either vacuous or it is not about the actual past at all.

I am torn: the argument looks like a straightforward consequence of determinism, but the conclusion also feels like a modal sleight of hand. Is the argument valid? If it is, is it sound? If it fails, identify the precise step or steps at which it fails, and state explicitly the reading of any term on which your diagnosis depends — for example, "could have failed to occur" or "given."
