I have, without intending it, been living through a version of the self-torturer. I keep accepting one more small commitment in exchange for one more modest raise; each addition is trivial by itself, but I have arrived at a schedule I would have refused if it had been offered to me all at once. The classic puzzle gets the same structure from a dial with many settings: each one-step increase in pain is imperceptible and well paid, so every step seems rational even though the final setting is one the person would never have accepted in a single jump. Here is the variation I would like worked through. Change a single assumption: make the pain increase at each step clearly perceptible—the agent can reliably tell that setting n+1 hurts more than setting n—but small enough that taking the payment for that one step is still the sensible choice. Do the standard readings of the original survive this change? Some hold the puzzle shows preferences can be rationally intransitive; others hold the imperceptibility means there is no genuine intransitive preference to explain. Which of these arguments, if either, still has a grip once the increments are perceptible, and what, exactly, should the agent do? Trace the analysis through the steps rather than giving only the bottom line.
