For the past several months I have been sitting in on a reading group on free will, and as a retired physicist I came into it expecting the main question to be whether the world is deterministic. Instead I have learned that much of the real difficulty lies in the logic of choice: even granting determinism, the decisive move is a transfer principle, and the interesting dispute is whether that principle may be rejected. We are about to close the seminar, and I would like to present a short poem in place of a final handout.

Please write a villanelle about van Inwagen's Consequence Argument that a specialist would recognize as accurate and a newcomer would find to be a decent poem. The form should do the philosophical work. The first refrain should render the thought that we had no choice about the remote past or about the laws of nature; the second should render the contrary-seeming thought that we do have a choice about at least some of what we now do. Let the alternation of these returning lines enact the way the argument tries to send unavoidability from the first thought to the second. The middle tercets should carry the machinery: the determinist's premise that the past together with the laws entails each present act, the rule that if a proposition is a necessary truth then no one has a choice about it (the alpha rule), and the transfer rule (the beta rule: if no one has a choice about p, and no one has a choice about the fact that p entails q, then no one has a choice about q). The closing quatrain should pause at the genuine fork the argument leaves: accept the conclusion that no one has a choice about the acts they actually perform, reject the beta rule, or accept the conclusion but deny that anything a person could care about was lost with it. Keep the fatalist confusion out of it: the claim is not that the past and the laws bring about our acts whether or not we deliberate, but that if the past and the laws jointly entail an act, then choice does not transmit through the entailment unless the transfer rule is granted. The group is split along familiar compatibilist and incompatibilist lines, so the poem should leave that fork open rather than settle it. Accuracy matters to me more than metrical polish, but I would like the refrains to be lines worth repeating rather than slogans.
