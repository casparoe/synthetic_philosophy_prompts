I recently read an article in a popular philosophy magazine that summarized Graham Priest's dialetheism for a general audience, and I would like to know how much of it to trust. The account ran roughly as follows. Classical logic treats the law of non-contradiction as absolutely universal: no statement can be both true and false. Priest denies this. He argues that certain special statements, especially the Liar sentence, are both true and false. To avoid the obvious problem that from a contradiction anything follows, Priest adopts a paraconsistent logic, which weakens classical rules so that a contradiction no longer entails every sentence. In Priest's preferred logic, the negation of a true sentence is false and the negation of a false sentence is true, but when a sentence is both true and false its negation is also both true and false. Because the logic quarantines inconsistencies, dialetheism is supposed to have no revisionary consequences for mathematics or ordinary reasoning: contradictions arise only in self-referential contexts, and everywhere else standard classical logic can be retained. The article concluded that Priest's view ultimately rests on the claim that our ordinary concept of truth is inconsistent, and that dialetheism is therefore not a revisionary proposal but a descriptive one about how truth already behaves.
