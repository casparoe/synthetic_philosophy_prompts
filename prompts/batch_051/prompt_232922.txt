I recently read an essay in a general-interest magazine that used Chesterton's fence and Burke to explain conservative resistance to reform. I want to know how much of it to trust, though I know just enough about the subject to suspect that something is off. The essay's account ran roughly as follows:

Chesterton's fence is the principle that you should never tear down an existing institution, custom, or rule unless you first discover why it was put there. The reason is that traditions persist only because they encode the accumulated wisdom of past generations, and the mere fact that a practice has lasted is itself evidence that it solves some real problem even when nobody can currently say what that problem is. The burden of proof therefore lies with the reformer, and the older an institution is, the heavier that burden becomes. Burke made the same point when he described society as a partnership joining the dead, the living, and the unborn. From this, the essay concludes that Chesterton's fence commits you to a strong presumption against reform: because the original reasons are usually forgotten, a reformer can almost never meet the burden, so the rational attitude toward any functioning institution is to leave it alone.

I suspect the essay has run two thinkers together and has turned a modest rule of thumb into a much stronger conservative thesis, but I am not sure exactly where the account goes wrong or which pieces a careful reader should accept. Please go through the paragraph sentence by sentence and tell me, for each sentence, what Chesterton himself could accept, what is closer to Burke than to Chesterton, and what is the essay's own addition or overstatement. Then assess whether the concluding presumption against reform actually follows from the accurate parts of the account, or whether the principle supports something narrower. Please answer in American English.
