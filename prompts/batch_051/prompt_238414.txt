Just got back from a panel on responsibility and moral luck as research for a feature, and I've realised I can't actually pin down the line everyone leans on: you're only morally responsible for what you did if you could have done otherwise. I've heard it used to mean everything from a banal point about excuses to something almost about alternate universes. At the panel, a climate correspondent (don't ask) used it to dismiss the idea that anyone deserves blame for a decision that was, realistically, going to happen anyway, and a proper philosopher next to me muttered "principle of alternative possibilities" and "Frankfurt cases" under his breath. I half remember Frankfurt cases from undergrad but not clearly enough to know which version of the slogan they're supposed to kill.

So, for my piece: can you lay out the different precise claims that slogan could be making? I'm especially interested in what "could have done otherwise" could mean — causal possibility, physical possibility, what the agent believes they could do, what a rational agent would do under other circumstances, ability at what time, whose ability — and whether the "only if" targets moral responsibility itself, or blame, or praise. Then for each reading, say whether it's a platitude, substantive and defensible, or false. And tell me which readings Frankfurt-style cases actually put pressure on, and whether the climate guy's inference (couldn't have done otherwise, therefore no responsibility) survives on any plausible reading. The feature doesn't need citations, just the argument laid out. Oh, and please write the answer in British English.
