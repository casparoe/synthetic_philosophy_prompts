People say things like "not watering the plant caused it to die" or "the doctor's failure to prescribe antibiotics caused the patient's death." But some philosophers think this is sloppy talk: strictly, an omission isn't an event, it's an absence, and an absence can't push anything around, so omissions are at best quasi-causes or a way of talking we reach for because someone violated a norm or because the unperformed act was especially salient. Others think that's over-squeamish: the world is full of absences doing causal work, and our counterfactual and interventionist reasoning needs omissions to count as real causes. I'd like to know whether this is a dispute about the world or just about how we talk. Design the best empirical test you can think of for whether people's causal judgemnts about omissions track real causal structure rather than norms, defaults, and salience. Be concrete: what would you manipulate, what would you hold fixed, what pattern of results would favour each view, and what would a positive result settle and what would it not settle? One sanity check to work through: keep the physical facts identical across conditions while changing only whether the non-acting was normal or a norm violation—would a difference in causal ratings, by itself, prove anything? Finally, say whether any part of this debate is untestable even in principle, and why.
