Ok so I've been chewing on this thought experiment by Warren Quinn, from a paper I think is called The Puzzle of the Self-Torturer. The setup is a person hooked up to a machine with a dial that goes from 0 up to a huge number, like a thousand or so. Each single step up adds such a tiny increase in pain that the person honestly cannot feel the difference between one setting and the next, and each step also pays him ten thousand dollars. He can stop anytime. At the highest setting he's in awful, just about unmanageable pain, but at every individual step along the way it made sense to turn the dial one more notch: the extra pain was imperceptible and the money was real. So he ends up in a state he'd pay a fortune to escape, and he'd probably say the whole thing was a mistake, but no particular turn of the dial looks like the mistake.

I've always run with a rough two-part test for whether a choice is rational: first, the person prefers the result at the moment of choosing, and second, if you asked them right after that single choice in isolation, they'd do it again. By that test, every single turn from 0 to 1, 1 to 2, 2 to 3, and so on all the way up is rational. But the whole sequence lands him somewhere he'd never have chosen in one go, and he'd likely say his overall decision was irrational. So I'm trying to figure out whether he counts as a case of rational choices adding up to an irrational result, or whether my test is missing something. Is the relevant unit the individual step or the whole path? And does it matter that he can tell the difference between settings far apart, just not settings next to each other? I'm less interested in the pain and money than in the criteria themselves: should I say the sequence is irrational even though no individual step is, or would that be giving up on the idea that rationality works step by step?
