I have been keeping a notebook of how I actually change my mind about philosophical questions, and I would like to know whether there is one defensible policy behind its entries or whether several of them will have to be given up. When a carefully described case about causation conflicts with a general principle I hold about what causation is, I take the case as decisive and revise the principle: I once abandoned a simple counterfactual analysis of causation because I found myself convinced that an event could be a cause even though the outcome would have happened without it, and I felt no temptation to dismiss the case as exotic. When a colleague offered a case in which someone has a true, justified belief that I still could not call knowledge, I did the reverse: I kept my long-held analysis of knowledge and said the case was too peculiar to be evidence against it. I did not feel that these two decisions were inconsistent, since the subject matters seemed different, but I have never been able to state the difference. I treat the transitivity of value as a principle no case may overturn; if I judge A better than B and B better than C, I will accept that A is better than C and assume something has gone wrong in my direct comparison of A and C. But I do not give transitivity of personal identity the same authority; if a series of small changes preserves the person step by step while the endpoints feel like two different people, I am prepared to let that case overturn the principle rather than dismiss the feeling. When I choose between two accounts of the nature of belief, I take simplicity to be genuine evidence for the simpler account, not merely an aesthetic tiebreaker, even when both accounts are tailored to the same cases. Yet when I choose between two interpretations of a novel, I think simplicity carries no evidential weight at all.
