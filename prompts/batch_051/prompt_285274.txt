I direct the grants team at a mid-sized family foundation. Four times a year we review every active multi-year grant and decide which to renew, which to extend, and which to terminate early. A typical grant is between $300,000 and $3 million over three to five years, so each review commits or frees real money. Program officers have a few hours per grant; they are capable generalists with no training in economics or decision theory, and the board makes the final call. I am trying to stop the board from reasoning that we have already put too much money into a grant to walk away, but I also do not want a naive rule that says past spending can never matter. Ending a grant mid-stream can damage our credibility with grantees and co-funders, and continuing a struggling grant can genuinely produce information or keep options open even after the original case weakens. So I need something subtler than the textbook slogan.

Please write a decision procedure my program officers can actually run at these quarterly reviews. It should: (a) state each question in order; (b) say what counts as a sufficient answer to that question in practice; (c) say where the procedure stops and directs a do-not-renew recommendation; (d) say where the procedure must hand the decision to a person because the question is a matter of values rather than facts; and (e) mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated. It should keep the legitimate forward-looking reasons to continue — commitments made to grantees and co-funders, credibility, learning for our own future decisions, option value, and the possibility that a program's early numbers are a poor predictor — separate from the sunk-cost fallacy of treating the money we have already spent as a reason on its own. The procedure should be usable by someone who has never heard of economic theory.

Then run the procedure on these three cases:

1. A workforce-development grant now in year two of five, with weak placement numbers but a plausible argument that the model takes five years to show effects. We announced it as a flagship commitment, and two peer funders joined because we went first.

2. A community health pilot that is running well over budget and will need a no-cost extension. Evidence so far is positive but thin; continuing would mainly fund the final round of data collection that could tell us whether the model works.

3. A small arts-education grant we have renewed for nine years out of habit and founder loyalty, with inconsistent attendance data and a change of leadership at the grantee.

In at least one of these cases your procedure should tell me something I will not like. Conclude by saying what the procedure cannot do: which cases it will misfire on, and where it quietly imports values under the appearance of a test.

Please write the answer in British English, including spelling and punctuation conventions.
