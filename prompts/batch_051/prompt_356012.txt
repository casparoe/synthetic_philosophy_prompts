Consider a standard version of Bernard Williams's case of Jim and the captured Indians. Jim is given the choice of shooting one of twenty captives himself, in which case the other nineteen will be released, or refusing, in which case all twenty will be shot. The usual philosophical reading is that a straightforward utilitarian calculation favors shooting, while Williams uses the case to identify a distinct integrity-based consideration against it, one that concerns Jim's relation to his own action rather than the number of lives at stake.

Now modify the case in only one respect. Jim does not know whether the officer's threat will be carried out. He assigns the following probabilities, and they are stipulated to be correct. If Jim refuses, the officer frees all twenty with probability 0.6 and kills all twenty with probability 0.4. If Jim shoots, the other nineteen are freed with probability 0.9 and killed anyway with probability 0.1. Jim knows these probabilities and cannot improve them.

How should the integrity-based objection be formulated under this kind of uncertainty? Is its force a continuous function of the probability that shooting will save the nineteen, or does it have a different, non-probabilistic structure? Are there thresholds, and if so, what determines them? Does the case still separate the utilitarian from the defender of integrity, or do their practical verdicts now converge at certain probabilities?
