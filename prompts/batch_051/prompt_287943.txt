I'm taking a philosophy of langauge class and got into a long argument with two friends about whether a language could completely lack indexicals — words like "I", "here", "now", "this", "you" whose reference depends on the context of utterance. The standard line is that they aren't really eliminable: any paraphrase (say, replacing "I am hungry" with "the person currently speaking is hungry") either smuggles an indexical back in or changes the content, because the first is a self-locating thought while the second could be true without the speaker so much as realizing it's about them. We ended up inventing five candidate languages that each try to dodge the problem in a different way, and now we're stuck arguing about which comes closest.

The candidates:
- A (proper-name-plus-clock): no pronouns or demonstratives. Speakers refer to themselves by their own proper names, "here" is always spelled out via a named place, and "now" is replaced by universal coordinated time; every declarative sentence is meant to be eternally true or false.
- B (utterance-ID): each utterance gets a unique ID at the moment it is produced. "I" becomes "the producer of utterance #8841", "now" becomes "the production time of utterance #8841", and so on.
- C (coordinate): all spatial and temporal reference is done through numeric coordinates read off instruments — no "here" or "now" in the lexicon. Speakers learn to say "the body at 34.05 N, 118.25 W is hungry at 10:00 UTC" and the like.
- D (gesture): no context-sensitive words at all. Reference to self, place, time, and objects is done purely by pointing — including pointing at yourself for first-person claims. The only words in the language are names.
- E (description): every indexical is replaced with a definite description, e.g., "the speaker of the utterance being produced" for "I" and "the location of the utterance being produced" for "here". The language officially has no pronouns.

Rank these five from best to worst as attempts to be a genuinely indexical-free language that still lets speakers express self-locating beliefs and act on them. I'm less interested in one correct ranking than in your reasoning about exactly where each device fails (or doesn't). Structure your answer like this:
(1) "The ranking" — just the letters in order, best to worst, with a one-sentence statement of what the ranking tracks.
(2) "Why each lands where it does" — for each candidate in that order, 60–90 words on its main mechanism and where any indexical residue hides.
(3) "The unavoidable bit" — 100–150 words on whether the whole project can work: does any of these (or some sixth option you'd design) escape the self-locating-content problem from the setup, and why you reached that verdict.
