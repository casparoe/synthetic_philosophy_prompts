I've been reading about the distinction between AI alignment and AI control — alignment means making the model actually want the right thing, while control means building safety measures that assume the model may be misaligned: monitoring, auditing, sandboxing, capability limits, tripwires, keeping it from acting unilaterally, and so on. I keep noticing a parallel with political theory, and I can't decide whether it's deep or superficial.

Constitutional design seems to have the same shape. Checks and balances, seperation of powers, vetoes, judicial review, impeachment — none of these require office-holders to be virtuous; they're built for people who may be self-interested or corrupt. That looks like control. The civic-republican idea that a state can't survive unless citizens and rulers have the right character looks like alignment. The old constitutional point — that you shouldn't rely on virtue you can't verify, and should design institutions that work even with bad agents — sounds exactly like the argument for control over alignment. Even the objections rhyme: the control side says alignment is nice but unverifiable and brittle; the civic-virtue side says control measures will be gamed or captured, and a sufficiently bad agent will find the gaps, so eventually you need the agent itself to be good.

I want a verdict on whether this is a genuine structural parallel or just pattern-matching on the word 'control'. If it's deep, give me the mapping precisely and say what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer from constitutional theory to AI safety. Does anything like separation of powers make sense for a single model — different models or subsystems checking each other, none able to act alone? Does the old problem of who guards the guardians transfer to the people and models doing the monitoring? And what's something the parallel predicts that turns out false? I can see one candidate: constitutional checks work among agents of roughly comparable power, whereas AI control may need to work against an agent far more capable than its overseers. Maybe that's where the analogy gets interesting rather than breaks, but I want you to be the judge.

Be blunt, and also say what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Please answer in British English.
