Been arguing with a friend about Breivik in Norway, and I keep feeling like the standard ways of talking about punishment break on the case. The facts: in 2011 he killed 77 people, a bomb in Oslo and a mass shooting at a Labour Party youth camp, and in 2012 he got the maximum fixed sentence in the Norwegian system, 21 years, plus preventive detention that can be extended in five-year blocks for as long as he's judged dangerous. He's held at Ringerike, where by all accounts he has a private room, a computer, access to education and exercise, and the ability to sue over his conditions. My friend says this is just consequentialism: the fixed sentence is a wrapper, and the actual system only cares about dangerousness, so the lenient-looking conditions are consistent because punishment isn't supposed to hurt for its own sake. I'm not so sure. The fact that there's still a 21-year sentence tied to the crimes, and that people were furious it wasn't longer, suggests some residual commitment to desert.

I want you to actually run the case through retributivism and consequentialism, not the caricature versions. What does each theory say should have happened, seperately about (a) the 21-year sentence, (b) the open-ended preventive detention, and (c) the conditions of confinement? Where does each theory explain what Norway did without strain, and where does it have to go quiet or bolt on an extra principle? Then tell me what you think the episode shows about the usefulness of the retributivism/consequentialism split—does the case favor one side, or does it reveal that the split itself is missing what the Norwegian system is actually doing?
