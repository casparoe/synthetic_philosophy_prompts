At a reading group I attend, two members got into a long argument about infinite regresses, and I could not tell who had the better of it. The argument began with a small puzzle about inference. Suppose someone accepts all of a certain conditional premise, accepts its antecedent, yet refuses to accept the consequent. To help, we add a further premise: anyone who accepts a conditional and its antecedent is entitled to accept its consequent. The resister now accepts all three statements but still refuses the consequent, insisting that a further premise is needed to license the move from those three to the consequent. And so on, apparently without end.

Sofia said the puzzle exposes a vicious regress. In her view, each step's entitlement depends on the next, and because the chain never reaches a bottom, the original entitlement is never established; if reasoning worked like this, no conclusion would ever be licensed. Damien replied that she was misreading the puzzle as a regress of premises rather than a confusion between two kinds of thing. A rule of inference is not another premise; it is what a reasoner uses to move among premises, and a capacity to use rules does not itself need to be written down as a further premise. Properly described, he said, there is no endless dependency to worry about.

Sofia answered that Damien was relocating the problem rather than solving it. If the capacity to apply a rule needs no further license, why not say, in every alleged vicious regress, that the relevant operation is simply a primitive capacity of the system? Without an independent test for when such a capacity is legitimate, she said, his response would dissolve every regress argument, including ones in which the regress is plainly fatal. Damien countered with what he took to be a clearly benign case: the natural numbers, where each number determines its successor and so there is an infinite sequence, yet no particular number must wait for the whole sequence to be completed in order to exist. Viciousness, he argued, lies not in having an infinite chain but in having an unfinished dependency, and in the inference puzzle the unfinished dependency is manufactured by treating rules as though they were premises. Sofia replied that the numerical example proves too much, because each number's existence is secured by the construction itself, whereas the reasoner's acceptance of the consequent is never secured unless the chain is finite; capturing exactly this difference is the whole job of a criterion of viciousness, and Damien has not stated one.

They asked the rest of us to referee, move by move. I honestly could not say who was right. Please adjudicate their exchange: who is right about which specific point, where each goes wrong (if anywhere), and what, if anything, both of them are missing about how to tell when a regress is vicious.
