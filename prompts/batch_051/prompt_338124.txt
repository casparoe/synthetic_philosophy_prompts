I've been reading some moral philosophy since I retired from the lab, and last week I was at a reading group where two friends got into a spirited argument about Peter Singer's 1972 article "Famine, Affluence, and Morality." The article's central idea, as they summarized it, is roughly that if you can prevent something bad from happening without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, you ought to do it. Singer uses the case of a child drowning in a shallow pond to argue that you should wade in and save the child even if it ruins your clothes, and then extends that logic to distance and famine relief. The argument went like this.

(1) One friend, a retired civil engineer, said that Singer's conclusion is absurd because he's taking a principle that works for one-off emergencies and applying it to every moment of our lives. If we really followed it, we'd have to give away nearly everything until we're at the edge of harming ourselves, and that would make normal life—raising kids, keeping friendships, doing science—morally impossible. (2) My other friend, a former high school teacher, replied that "morally impossible" is just another way of saying "inconvenient by our current standards," and Singer is exactly questioning those standards. She said if the choice is between my kid's piano lessons and a stranger starving, the piano lessons are not of comparable moral importance. (3) The engineer shot back that there's a real difference between wading into a pond and running your whole life as a relief agency: in the pond case there's an identifiable victim right in front of you, no one else is there, and the causal chain is clear. With distant famine, the causal chain is murky, there are millions of potential helpers, and you never know if your donation actually reaches anyone. (4) The teacher said those are all empirical differences, but Singer's point is that they are not morally relevant: we can reliably help distant people through decent charities, and the fact that others could also help doesn't dissolve your obligation, any more than the fact that other people walked past the pond would dissolve yours. (5) The engineer then said that even if each individual act of donating is good, universal compliance with Singer's rule might be disastrous—it could wreck economies, create dependency, and leave no one funding local arts and science. (6) The teacher answered that this is an empirical claim, not an objection to the principle itself, and Singer can accept that we should choose effective aid rather than naive dumping of resources.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing. Please keep the whole answer under 600 words.
