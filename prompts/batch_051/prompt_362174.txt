I've been kicking around a distinction I want to call residue-free salvage. The core idea is that using an animal byproduct that would otherwise be wasted is okay only when the act of salvaging leaves no residue — no signal of demand back to any company, no normalizing of animal-product use to anyone watching, no making the upstream industry even a little better off. So eating a sealed, expired yogurt from a dumpster counts if nobody sees me and the store doesnt know it's being taken; wearing a rescued leather jacket out in public doesnt count because it advertises leather and normalizes the look. What I can't figure out is byproducts from animals we've rescued and keep alive, like eggs from hens or wool from sheep: they aren't going to rot, so the use-it-or-lose-it logic feels different. My friends say the whole thing is either trivially true (its just don't increase demand) or hopelessly vague (everything leaves some residue). Is the distinction actually doing ethical work beyond standard freegan reasoning? Give me the clearest counterexample to it, and tell me whether there's a salvageable version.
