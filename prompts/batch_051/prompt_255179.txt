For the past several years I have followed an epistemic policy I think of as "ignore-the-method, look-at-the-incentives." When I have to decide how much to update on an empirical claim—whether salt restriction meaningfully lowers blood pressure, whether a social program reduces recidivism, whether a management practice raises productivity—I no longer ask whether the supporting studies followed something called the scientific method. I ask a different question instead: if this claim were false, how likely is it that someone with both the means and the incentive to expose that would already have done so? The habit crystallized during the replication debates, and it found philosophical support in Feyerabend's anything-goes reading of the history of science: the idea that there is no single scientific method, and that successful science routinely violates the methodological rules philosophers have laid down.

I now apply the policy almost automatically. Recently two meta-analyses reached opposite conclusions about a dietary supplement, and rather than adjudicate the statistical details I tried to estimate how many independent, competent, and differently motivated groups would have been in a position to publish a null result if one existed. But I have started to doubt that the policy is what it appears to be. A friend who works as a statistician tells me I have simply reinvented falsificationism with extra steps: my test still requires claims to be testable and still privileges the ones that have survived attempts to kill them. Another friend says it is worse than useless, because my estimate of who had the means and motive to refute a claim is itself among the most motivated estimates a person can make; the policy can therefore rationalize whatever I already believed.

Is this policy defensible as stated, or is it a bias dressed up as a rule? Under what assumptions about competition, publication incentives, and the structure of the relevant scientific community does a could-it-have-been-refuted test track truth, and where does it fail in ways I should explicitly correct for rather than absorb into my background sense of what counts as scientific?
