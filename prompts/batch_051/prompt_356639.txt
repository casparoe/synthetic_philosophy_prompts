You are a philosopher of mind with expertise in self-model theories of subjectivity and phenomenal transparency. Work through the argument below carefully; begin by brainstorming two or three candidate diagnoses before defending the one you find best.

Set-up. According to the self-model theory, the ordinary experience of being a self is the content of a phenomenal self-model (PSM), a representational process whose content presents a first-person perspective on an embodied self. The theory also says the PSM is phenomenally transparent: in ordinary waking life, the system cannot introspectively represent the PSM as a representation, because the machinery that builds it is not itself available in experience.

Now consider:

(1) If the PSM is transparent, then in any ordinary self-conscious state, the system cannot introspectively represent its current PSM as a representation.
(2) To believe the self-model theory about one's own current self-consciousness, one must represent one's current PSM as a representation.
(3) Therefore, in any ordinary self-conscious state, one cannot believe the self-model theory about one's own current self-consciousness.
(4) A defender of the theory who affirms it while self-conscious is in an ordinary self-conscious state and takes herself to believe it about that very state.
(5) So such a defender is in an impossible condition by the theory's own lights: she believes, about her current state, something that no one in such a state can believe.
(6) Hence the theory is self-undermining as an account of the experiences it is meant to explain.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1); if the right diagnosis requires rejecting it, say how the theory would need to be restated. If the argument is valid, identify the least costly premise to abandon; if it commits a fallacy, pin down the exact step and explain why it looked so natural.
