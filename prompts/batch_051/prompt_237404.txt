In debates about whether miracle reports can warrant belief, defenders of Hume's skepticism usually reach for a familiar picture: testimonial evidence is like something with weight, and you settle a miracle claim by loading the reports favoring the event onto one pan of a balance and your settled experience of nature onto the other, then seeing which side is heavier. In Section X, Part I of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Hume appears to give this picture its canonical statement:

"In all cases, we must balance the opposite experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the smaller number from the greater, in order to know the exact force of the superior evidence."

I would like an audit of this weighing picture, not another restatement of Hume's argument. First, make the mapping explicit: what corresponds to the pans, the weights, the beam, and the act of "deducting" the smaller number. Second, identify where the analogy misleads once applied to real testimonial evidence; puzzle over, for example, whether independent reports aggregate the way weights do, whether uniform past experience really has a determinate weight that can sit opposite a report, and whether a subtractive operation yields the binary verdict the picture suggests. Finally, say how much of Hume's conclusion—that testimony alone cannot establish a miracle—survives if the weighing picture is abandoned, and what better model of the relation between testimony and background evidence you would put in its place. Please answer in American English.
