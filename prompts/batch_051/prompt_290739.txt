I'm a PhD student in epistemology, and I've developed a policy I'm half-suspicious of. Whenever a view bottoms out in "there are truths that can't in principle be known," I treat that as a reductio of the view and go back looking for the bad premise. I do it in seminars, in my own reading, in referee reports, everywhere. I know the Fitch derivation too well to just help myself to the naive knowability principle: take an unknown truth p ∧ ¬Kp; if every truth is knowable, this one is too, so ◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp), but K distributes over conjunction and is factive, so that's impossible. So the slogan "everything true is knowable" is off the table for me. But I still find accepting unknowable truths as a stable conclusion worse—it feels like a standing invitation to stop asking what would count as evidence. Is this policy defensible, or is it verificationism with the serial numbers filed off? If it's a bias, what's the most defensible version I could keep, and what argument or consideration would most likely show me I should just live with unknowable truths? Tell me where your verdict is shakiest, too.
