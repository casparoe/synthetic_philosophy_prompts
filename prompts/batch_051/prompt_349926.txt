I am a high school student preparing a workshop for our school's urban studies club on housing, zoning, and the question of who has a right to the city. I would like to make a sorting quiz: a short decision tree or quiz with a scoring key that helps people discover which position on this question they actually hold, before they learn any of the standard labels. I would like the instrument to sort people by what they believe, not by what they call themselves.

Please build the instrument around one specific question: what, if anything, gives someone a moral claim to remain in a city, to help decide its future, or to move into it? I do not want the instrument to treat those three things as automatically going together. I think the real work is done by three distinctions, and I would like the tree to be built from combinations of answers to them rather than from a list of school names:

1. Whether the claim attaches to a person, to a role, or to a use.
2. Whether the claim is acquired primarily by length of residence, by current occupancy or use, by formal ownership, or by something else such as need or vulnerability.
3. Whether the claim is a claim to stay, a claim to participate in decisions, a claim to enter, or some combination.

The diagnostic questions must be concrete cases that someone can answer without knowing any technical terms. Please write your own cases rather than using mine, but make sure at least one question involves a homeless person making regular use of a public plaza, one involves a commuter who works in the city but lives elsewhere, and one involves a prospective future resident who does not yet live in the city. For example, a good question might ask whether a longtime resident and a recently arrived tenant have the same kind of objection when a low-rise building in their neighborhood is proposed for redevelopment, but please do not reuse that exact wording.

The tree should include at least these positions as leaves, though you may add or relabel them: (1) the view that long-established residents have the strongest claim; (2) the view that current residents as a whole hold the city as a shared enterprise, regardless of tenure or ownership; (3) the view that actual users of a place, such as tenants, workers, shoppers, and commuters, have a stronger claim than distant owners; (4) the view that the city belongs to whoever participates in its local democratic processes; (5) the view that the central obligation is to keep the city open to newcomers and future residents; and (6) the view that housing and zoning disputes should mostly track ordinary ownership and contract rights.

For each leaf, give one sentence stating the hardest commitment a person holding that position has to accept. Also flag any combinations of answers that are logically possible but that no recognizable position occupies, and say briefly whether you think each is unoccupied because it is incoherent, because it is too unattractive, or because it has simply been underexplored. If two positions on my list cannot be separated by the questions you use, say so and explain why they collapse under this scheme.

Please aim for roughly five to seven diagnostic questions and no more than eight leaves. The audience is high school students with no background in urban planning or political philosophy, so the instrument must be answerable by someone who has never heard the names of any existing positions.
