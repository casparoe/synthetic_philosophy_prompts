Here's an argument I've been going round in circles on; it came out of a conversation with a collegue who works on consciousness, and it seems to prove too much whichever way I turn it. (1) If epiphenomenalism is true, my pain is caused by a neural state and never causes anything, including my judgement that I'm in pain. (2) A judgement is justified only if the state of affairs it reports is among its causes. (3) So if epiphenomenalism is true, my judgement that I'm in pain isn't justified. (4) But it plainly is justified — I know I'm in pain. (5) So epiphenomenalism is false. The trouble is that (2) looks like it proves too much: run the same principle over beliefs about the future (future events can't cause present judgements) or over beliefs about the primes (numbers don't cause anything), and you get scepticism about both. So either (2) is false, or there's some special reason it holds for pain but not for tomorrow's weather or the primes, and I can't find one that doesn't beg the question. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd most like to keep (4), but I've a feeling that's the cheap option. Put your one-sentence summary of the diagnosis as the final line.
