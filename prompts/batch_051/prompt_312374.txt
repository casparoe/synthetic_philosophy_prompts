I'm not trained in education or cognitive science, but I've been tutoring high-school physics and chemistry on the side for several years, and I keep running into a pattern I don't have a word for. The wrong answers my students give are not scattered; they recur almost identically from one student to the next, and they appear to hang together. A student who says heavier objects fall faster will often also say that a rolling ball slows down because the force you gave it is getting used up, and that in a vacuum there would be nothing to keep a thrown ball moving. When I correct one point, the student usually keeps the underlying account and re-explains the correction inside it rather than abandoning it. I can call each of these a wrong fact or a gap, but that misses what interests me: the students seem to be reasoning from a small set of connected, reusable, wrong rules about how the world works, and those rules resist correction in a way an ordinary mistake does not. Is there an established name or body of research for this specific thing—not isolated errors, but systematic, coherent, hard-to-dislodge wrong accounts of how something operates—and how would that concept be distinguished from a simple error, a missing fact, a guess, or a merely incomplete picture? I'm less interested in a label than in getting the boundaries right.
