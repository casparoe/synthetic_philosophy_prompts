A friend and I have been arguing for weeks about what purely statistical evidence should be allowed to do in court, and I find that my own instincts are inconsistent enough that I want help finding the principle — or being told which instincts to give up. The cases I keep coming back to are these. A car is hit by a bus that leaves the scene; on that route at that hour, 80% of the buses are run by the Blue Bus Company and 20% by the Red Bus Company, and nobody recorded the company name. In the victim's civil suit against Blue, I do not think I would find Blue liable, even though Blue is more likely than not responsible. But in a version of the case without the market-share evidence, where a bystander saw the bus and says it was blue but is known to get colors right only 70% of the time under those lighting conditions, I would find Blue liable, with some hesitation — which feels backwards if all that matters is which probability is higher. Similarly, if a thousand people attend a rodeo, 499 buy tickets, and 501 climb over the fence, and the promoter points to an attendee at random, it is slightly more likely than not that that person gatecrashed, but I would not make him pay. And in a criminal case, if a hundred prisoners are in an exercise yard, ninety-nine of them take part in killing a guard while one stands aside and does not, any particular prisoner is 99% likely to have participated, but I would not convict him of murder beyond a reasonable doubt on that alone. Yet if the bus route were 99.9% Blue Bus, I think I would find Blue liable — and I cannot say what changed between 80% and 99.9%, so perhaps that last instinct is the one that has to go.

My tentative principle is that a verdict against a person requires evidence tied to that person or event, the way the bystander's statement is a trace of this particular bus, whereas the market share and the rodeo counts are facts about a group that would look exactly the same no matter which person I am considering. But I cannot draw that line precisely. The bystander's 70% reliability is itself a group frequency, about eyesight and lighting; the bus company's market share is also a concrete fact about this route at this hour; and I do not know why one trace counts as particular and the other does not. Nor do I know what the principle says about cases I have not settled, for instance a DNA database trawl that turns up one person whose profile matches at one in a million with no prior reason to suspect him, or a substance that doubles the background rate of a disease in a patient who then develops that disease.

Please give me the best principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I can apply it myself. Test it against my cases and the two further cases, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and how my tentative principle should be repaired. Please write your answer as continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would write me a letter.
