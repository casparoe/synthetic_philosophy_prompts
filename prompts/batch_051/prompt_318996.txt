I'm taking a course on the aesthetics of games this term and I keep getting stuck on the same problem: what would a theory of games-as-art actually have to do before we should accept it. My roommate thinks the whole thing is a non-starter — he says art is something you submit to and a game is something you operate, so calling a game art is like calling a toaster art because someone designed it nicely. But the prof keeps warning against two lazy ways to defend games as art: the 'cinematic games' move (praise the cutscenes, the music, the story — everything that isn't really the game) and the 'interactivity is automatically art' move (as if every spreadsheet were a sonnet because you can edit the cells). What I want is a list of desiderata a theory of games-as-art must meet to avoid both of those moves and still say something informative about games as a seperate art form, not just a delivery system for cinema or painting.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration behind it. The examples I'd like the list to be tested against are: Tetris or Asteroids as abstract, story-free cases; The Last of Us as the cinematic case where the 'game' part can feel incidental; Papers, Please or The Stanley Parable as cases where the choices are the content; and a sports game or a walking simulator as boundary cases. Also say which requirements are non-negotiable if games are to count as an art form in it's own right, and which are only preferences of a particular camp (authorial-message people vs player-expression people, for instance). The part I most want: tell me whether some subset of the desiderata is in tension or jointly unsatisfiable — like, can a theory really respect the player's agency as part of the work and still treat the game as an authored object with a bounded meaning? — and if so, whether one of them has to be relaxed rather than just fudged. Skip scoring the actual theories; I'll do that mapping myself for the paper.
