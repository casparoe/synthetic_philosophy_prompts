In many school systems, and in several large military and employment selection systems, a person is given a battery of short, abstract, timed tasks — vocabulary, digit-span recall, matrix puzzles, block design — each scored separately. Those subtest scores are then averaged or otherwise combined into a single full-scale number, and that number, rather than the pattern of strengths and weaknesses, is what gates entry into a gifted-and-talented program, an officer track, or a job. The single number is treated as roughly stable across years and contexts, as largely independent of how the person was taught and of the language and culture in which they grew up, and as predictive of later performance in settings that resemble the test items only faintly. Meanwhile, the very institutions that impose these tests often behave in classrooms and on the job as if the abilities that matter are uneven, teachable, and shaped by situation and practice.

What theory of intelligence does this practice presuppose?

Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, and separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Address each of the following.

(1) Does collapsing the subtest profile into one number commit the practice to the existence of a single general ability, or only to the weaker empirical claim that the subtests hang together well enough for a composite to be predictively useful? What pattern of evidence — about the size of the intercorrelations, their stability over time, or their origin in instruction and environment — would count against the practice even on the weaker claim?

(2) Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined? Which rival accounts of intelligence — for example, that intelligence is partly a property of a person-plus-situation system rather than a fixed inner trait, that there are many only loosely related capacities, or that the test measures a particular cultural skill rather than a context-free ability — would nevertheless underwrite most of the same procedures?

(3) If one of those rival accounts replaced the presupposed theory, spell out concretely what would have to change: the choice of items, the scoring rule (a vector rather than a scalar, or a best-score plus worst-score report), how stability and predictive claims are stated, and how placement decisions are made.

(4) Which step of the practice do you take to be the most philosophically loaded, and what specific evidence would most force that step to be revised?
