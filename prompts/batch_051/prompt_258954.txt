The argument from computational irreducibility, associated with Wolfram, says that many deterministic systems have no shortcut: to learn what such a system will do, one has to run it, because no computation substantially shorter than the evolution itself can produce the answer. A recent development puts pressure on this: machine-learned surrogate models, trained on the outputs of expensive simulations of turbulent flow, molecular dynamics, or global weather, now make useful forecasts orders of magnitude faster than the simulators they imitate, without stepping through the underlying dynamics. Does this development refute computational irreducibility, or only force a sharpening of it? One natural response is that the emulator has not shortened the computation but amortized it, paying the cost up front during training to buy cheaper predictions later; assess whether that reply is adequate. Then identify exactly which premise of the irreducibility argument the development bears on—a claim about exactness, about total computational cost, about guaranteed correctness on novel cases, or about the difference between predicting an outcome and explaining it—and state whether the argument goes through, needs repair, or is dead. End with the strongest version of the claim that survives contact with learned emulators, or explain why no interesting version survives.
