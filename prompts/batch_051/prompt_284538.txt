I have no academic background in this, but I have been thinking for years about something I first noticed after my grandmother died. Her voice did not leave my head; it stayed with startling specificity. I can still predict, with uncomfortable accuracy, what she would say about people she never met and situations she never saw. It is not simply that I remember her, the way I remember a fact. A working model of her is still running in me, making judgments and sometimes steering my decisions before I notice. I assume the same is true in the other direction: a working model of me runs in other people too.

I have started calling these running models "delegates," and I want the idea behind the term tested rather than the word. My claim is that a self is not a thing but a pattern of perceiving, judging, and responding. A pattern exists wherever something runs it with enough detail. So a person is genuinely distributed across every brain that runs a sufficiently fine-grained delegate of them. The original brain runs the highest-resolution delegate, but it has no special ownership: it is the largest part of me, not the whole of me. Death, on this view, is not a clean break but a gradual loss of resolution. A person is only fully gone when the last delegate drops below some minimum level of detail or stops running.

This seems to explain two feelings that are usually dismissed as mere sentiment. First, that the dead can still act through the living, not just be remembered by them. Second, that it matters what kind of delegate of yourself you leave running in other people, so you should try to be worth carrying.

I can see the problems. Where exactly is the threshold of detail, and why should a fuzzy boundary be any kind of boundary at all? If several people each carry a different fragment of someone, is that one person spread across many brains, or many fragments that never add back up to a person? Does a vivid caricature of someone count as that person surviving, even if it would horrify them? And am I really saying anything more than that people influence one another, with the word "self" doing the heavy lifting?

I would like an honest assessment. Is this a coherent claim or a metaphor that has gotten away from me? Has it already been worked out under a better name, and where does it break?
