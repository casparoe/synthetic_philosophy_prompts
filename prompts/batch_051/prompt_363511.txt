I help run a tenants' union in a mid-size city. There are five of us on the steering commitee, and to keep us fast we gave each member the power to act alone: any of us can email the whole membership list, post to the union's social accounts, put our letterhead on a statement, or spend up to $400 from the legal-defense fund without asking anyone first. This worked fine for a while, then it didn't. Last month one member sent a press release under our letterhead about a rent-control fight the rest of us never agreed to enter; two of us thought it was a bad move, one thought it was fine, one had no strong view, and the member who wrote it still says he'd do it again because he was sure it was right. A friend who reads a lot told us we'd run straight into something called the unilateralist's curse: when a bunch of people can each act on their own, and each one goes by "I'll act if I think it's net positive," the group ends up doing whatever the most optomistic person thinks is a good idea, even when most of the others would have stopped it. That sounds like us, but I'm not sure the simple version is the whole story, because the opposite rule—nobody acts until everyone agrees—would also grind us to a halt.

These judgment calls come up a few times a month, but the full committee meets only monthly and a lot of the work is time-sensitive. So I'm looking for a reusable decision procedure a member can run on their phone in about twenty minutes before doing something unilaterally. The people using it are volunteers with day jobs, not philosophers or statisticians, so the questions have to be concrete. I'd like them in order, with stops built in ("if the answer is X, don't act"; "if you can't answer Y, go to the full committee"), and I'd like you to say honestly where the procedure hands things to a person because it's a values call rather than a factual one, and where it will misfire. Please then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a member who wants to send a press release about a new landlord practice and is convinced it's a winning issue; (2) a member who wants to spend $300 from the legal fund to file a complaint she thinks is good law, while three others have privately called it a waste of the fund; and (3) a member who wants to post a short congratulations to a city councilor on reelection, which he sees as harmless relationship-building but other members may read as taking sides. I particularly want to see a case where the procedure tells someone not to do something they strongly want to do, and I'd like you to spell out what the procedure still can't settle.
