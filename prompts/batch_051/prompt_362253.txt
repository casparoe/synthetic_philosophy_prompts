You are a philosopher of science known for giving careful, case-specific verdicts rather than applying a slogan. I write about science and ideas for a general but scientifically literate readership, and I am preparing a piece on disputes about what should count as science. I would like your help working through one disputed case.

Here is the working standard for a scientific enterprise that I apply in my reporting:

1. Falsifiability: it makes claims precise enough that some conceivable observation or experimental result would count against them.
2. Explanatory unification: it brings many apparently unconnected phenomena under a small set of principles or mechanisms.
3. Public reproducibility: its results can be checked, reproduced, and extended by other investigators using shared, transferable methods.
4. Novel output: it generates predictions, classifications, or data we would not have arrived at through the previous framework.
5. Self-correction: it has a record of revising or dropping its claims when the evidence or the community's checks go against them.

Now consider the research program Stephen Wolfram advanced in A New Kind of Science and has continued since. As I understand it, the program begins with simple computational systems, especially one-dimensional cellular automata, and documents that extremely simple rules can produce behavior of unbounded complexity. From this it draws broad conclusions: that science should search the space of simple programs for rules whose output matches observed phenomena, rather than insisting on finding equations; that the traditional mathematical approach of the exact sciences has been a detour imposed by the available tools; and that essentially all processes that are not obviously simple are computationally equivalent, so that beyond a low threshold of simplicity there is no meaningful gradation of complexity. That last claim, which Wolfram calls the principle of computational equivalence, is meant to apply to nature, to minds, and to computation itself.

The program clearly satisfies some parts of my standard more easily than others. Its concrete cellular-automaton findings are highly reproducible and have been rediscovered independently by many people, yet the sweeping conclusions are hard to turn into observations that would refute them, and critics argue that the program describes and classifies complexity rather than predicting it.

Please work through this carefully rather than settling it by first impression. Begin with a short brainstorm of the best considerations on each side. Then take my five criteria in order and assess how the program fares against each, distinguishing what it plainly satisfies, what it plainly fails, and what remains underdetermined. Where you judge that it fails a criterion, tell me whether that is a defect in the program or a limitation in the criterion as a tool for evaluating computational research of this kind. End with a verdict on whether the program is best described as science, and on whether this case shows that my five-criterion standard should be revised, perhaps because falsifiability is being asked to carry too much of the load.
