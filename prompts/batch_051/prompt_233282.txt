Me and my friend have the same fight every few weeks, and I want to know whether my policy is defensible or just a way of never losing. The policy: when he shows me that things I've already said, taken together with logic, commit me to something I find absurd, I don't grant the reductio unless I can retrace the derivation step by step myself. If the moves are long or turn on subtleties I can't reconstruct, I set the whole bundle aside and keep my original views, plus at most a vague note to myself that I should look into it. Last month he pulled three separate claims I'd made over the past year and argued they jointly commit me to a exact number of hairs where somebody becomes bald. I couldn't find the bad step on the spot, so I told him I don't accept that I'm committed to that, and I've honestly avoided reopening it since. He says I'm acting like my beliefs don't have consequences unless I can feel them, and that modus tollens doesn't require me to understand the route. I've also been burned the other way: I once accepted a clever derivation in an argument, conceded on the spot, and later found it turned on an equivocation I hadn't caught. Who's right here? Is there a version of my policy that a careful person could actually run, and what would it force me to do in the cases where I can't retrace the steps?
