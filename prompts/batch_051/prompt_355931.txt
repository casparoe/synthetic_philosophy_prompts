I've been reading about private cities, charter cities and seasteading, and I keep having the feeling that the whole 'opt out of the state' move is just forking a repo. You clone the entire codebase, keep the parts you like, throw out the rest, and carry on under new maintainers while upstream keeps shipping changes; you can later pull those changes in, or not. A charter city looks to me like a fork of a national legal system: you fork the parent state's law, keep contract, tort and property as the core libraries, rewrite the modules you don't want, and now you're maintaining your own distribution of 'the law'. I know its not a perfect fit — a forked repo doesn't have a territory or a police force, and cloning a legal system doesn't clone the people who run it. But are those incidental disanalogies, or do they point at the load-bearing part? I'd like someone to do a careful audit: what corresponds to what, whether anything from open source actually transfers (maintenance burden, fragmentation, never-ending patches from upstream, the fact most forks die, the way a licence constrains what a fork may do), and what the parallel predicts that turns out false. For instance it predicts charter cities should mostly die from maintenance burden rather than politics — does that track? Is this a real identity of structure, or am I just pattern-matching on the word 'fork'?
