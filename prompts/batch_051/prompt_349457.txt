Consider the following passage from Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, Q. 40, Art. 1, Reply to Objection 1 (New Advent translation):

"On the other hand, to have recourse to the sword (as a private person) by the authority of the sovereign or judge, or (as a public person) through zeal for justice, and by the authority, so to speak, of God, is not to 'take the sword,' but to use it as commissioned by another, wherefore it does not deserve punishment."

This passage is often read as an early form of the claim that soldiers are not personally culpable for killing when they act under public authority. I want your assessment of its stronger modern descendant. Define the moral equality of combatants as follows: a soldier who fights under the authority of his own state and complies with the rules of jus in bello is morally no more at fault, and has no less permission, in killing opposing combatants when his side's war is unjust than when it is just.

Please give me your credence in that thesis: a single number rather than a survey of positions. Then decompose the number. Assign rough probabilities to the sub-questions you take to be load-bearing—for example, whether sovereign authorization can remove individual culpability even when the cause is unjust; whether ordinary soldiers can reasonably judge their side's ad bellum status; whether ad bellum and in bello wrongs are separable when assessing individual liability; and whether each killing on the unjust side inherits the wrongness of the war as a whole. Show how the sub-probabilities combine, identify the single sub-question your overall credence is most sensitive to, and say what evidence would shift it most. Keep your answer to at most 600 words.
