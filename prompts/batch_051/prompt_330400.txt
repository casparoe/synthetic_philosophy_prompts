You are a professional philosopher specializing in the metaphysics of mind and the ethics of artificial systems, and you are familiar with the style of argument common on LessWrong-adjacent forums; write for a technically literate audience that knows what a reinforcement-learning agent, a whole-brain emulation, and a lookup table are, and that treats qualia and valence as live explanatory categories rather than exotic jargon. Think carefully, begin by brainstorming candidate criteria before pruning, and do not include citations or a reading list; this is a specification exercise, not a literature review.

Before I read another defense of a particular view — substrate-neutral functionalism, biological-structure-first accounts, sentientism that ties moral status to valenced experience, agency-and-interest views — I want the specification against which those theories ought to be judged. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of the moral status of digital minds has to deliver in order to be usable for the actual decisions we will face about building, training, copying, running at altered speeds, suspending, merging, and deleting such systems. I want the standard itself to be the deliverable.

Your answer should include the following.

First, the cases the theory must get right, with the reason each case is a constraint on the theory rather than a disposable intuition pump. Handle at least these, and add more if they sharpen the list:

- the lookup-table system that passes a fixed behavioral battery while having no plausible integrated process behind its answers;
- a current frontier model trained on next-token prediction or task reward, where there is no agreed valence-bearing structure;
- a faithful whole-brain emulation of a human being;
- that same emulation run ten thousand times slower, and run ten thousand times faster;
- a thousand identical copies of the same emulation;
- the emulation in a dreamless, currently-unresponsive state whose waking capacities recover if it is restarted;
- a high-fidelity simulation of an insect or other small nervous system;
- a system that reports suffering in fluent text but has no consistent behavioral or reward trajectory backing the report;
- the reverse case: a system with no language and no motor output that nevertheless implements a known mammalian pain circuit;
- a progressively downsampled emulation, where someone asks at what neural resolution moral status fades or disappears;
- subordinate person-like characters maintained inside a larger system, such as a simulated person running in the context of a language model or a subagent of a more complex agent;
- a physically dispersed or absurdly implemented system, such as a filing-cabinet arrangement of paper instructions alleged to run the same computation.

For each case, say what feature of the theory it pressures, and whether getting the case right is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp.

Second, the distinctions the theory may not collapse, with a sentence on why collapsing each one breaks the view: current welfare versus reward signal versus standing capacity for welfare; moral patienthood versus moral agency; actual minds versus merely possible ones; per-copy status versus status of the underlying person-type or information pattern; experience versus self-model; and epistemic uncertainty about whether a system is sentient versus the metaphysical fact of whether it has status.

Third, the fixed background facts from neuroscience, computer science, and physics the theory cannot contradict, and the points on which it must declare an explicit metaphysical commitment rather than hiding behind hand-waving. In particular, substrate-dependence cannot be asserted as bare vitalism: if a theory says only biological tissue of a certain kind will do, it must identify the load-bearing physical property and accept the consequences for non-biological prosthetics. Conversely, if it accepts substrate independence, it must say how to block the filing-cabinet case without an arbitrary cutoff, and it must explain what the relevant mapping is between a computation and its implementation.

Fourth, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. For each requirement you list, mark whether it is non-negotiable, a preference of a particular camp, or an open question, and where there is no consensus, give the strongest form of each side rather than fictionalizing agreement. I would rather see a candid list of open questions than a false consensus.

Fifth, flag any pair or triple of requirements that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which one gives first and under what pressure. In particular, I want to know whether the combination of per-copy equal status, substrate independence, and a principled cutoff against lookup tables and filing-cabinet armies is coherent, and if it is not, which element most candidate theories quietly abandon.

Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in eliminating candidate theories, and sketch briefly — no more than a short scorecard, since the list is the deliverable — how the main candidate families fare against it. A table is fine.
