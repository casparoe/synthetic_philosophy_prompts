So I'm a retired computational neuroscientist, and since I stopped doing bench work I've been reading philosophy of mind. Somehow I ended up on my university's research ethics board, and we now keep seeing protocols involving large recurrent networks, cortical organoids, and agents trained by self-prediction, and we get stuck on the same question: when does a computational system stop being ordinary code and start needing animal-style ethical review? One member says only wet brains count, full stop. Another member—and I find myself sympathetic—says what matters is the kind of computation being performed: if the system does the right sort of integrated or self-modelling processing, its in a morally different category even if its outward behavior is identical to a lookup table. The problem is that our board meets every two weeks, has to decide within about ten days, and most members have never read any philosophy of mind.

Can you turn that computationalist principle into a decision procedure we can actually use? I want ordered tests to run on a protocol, with what to do at each step and what extra technical details to request from the lab. Give me two or three worked examples, including at least one where the procedure tells us to review something we'd rather not, and say plainly where the procedure stops being mechanical and a person has to make the call. No citations—I need something usable, not a reading list.
