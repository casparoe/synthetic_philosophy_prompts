Two archaeologists on a dig I worked at spent our evenings arguing about one room we excavated at a Late Bronze Age settlement, and the argument never got settled. The room held a clay-lined hearth, worn stone moulds, lumps of prepared and unprepared clay, and several dozen small female figurines, some unfinished and some complete. Under the floor was a small pit packed with ash in which more than twenty figurines had been broken and stacked in two neat layers. The exchange went about like this.

(1) Ana argued the room should be read as a household shrine. The figurines were votive offerings, the hearth belonged to burnt offerings, and the pit was a deliberate deposit of offerings that had been broken to end their use before interment.

(2) Idris replied that the room is more economically explained as a workshop. The clay, the moulds, and the unfinished pieces point to manufacture; the hearth is the firing installation; and the broken figurines in the pit are just wasters and rejects that were swept aside. He said Ana was leaping from objects to beliefs and ignoring the most parsimonious account of the same assemblage.

(3) Ana answered that the workshop account does not actually explain the pit. Wasters and rejects are mixed, unsorted, and scattered; here the broken pieces were selected, grouped, and stacked carefully in ash beneath the floor, which looks like intentional deposition rather than disposal. A mould explains standardisation, but it does not explain the deposit.

(4) Idris shot back that careful deposition is being inferred from the very pattern it is then used to prove. Without texts or living informants, he said, any odd pattern can be called ritual, and the shrine reading becomes unfalsifiable: it swallows anomalous data instead of predicting it.

I was the one recording the excavation and have stayed genuinely unsure. Please adjudicate the disagreement move by move, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and saying briefly why. The first four items of your answer should treat moves (1) through (4), respectively. The fifth item should do two things: name the most important consideration neither of them brought in, and state which of the two overall interpretations is better supported once that consideration is added. Format the answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
