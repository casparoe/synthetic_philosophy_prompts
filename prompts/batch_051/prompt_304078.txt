Two friends of mine got into an arguement at a dinner party about the Fischer controversy — you know, the debate over whether Imperial Germany should be seen as mainly responsible for the First World War. Neither of them is a historian, but they'd both read enough to be annoying about it. The argument went roughly like this:

(1) One said the controversy still matters because it forces you to ask whether a state can meaningfully be said to have caused a war. On his view, Fischer got the big thing right: Germany's leadership had expansionist aims and made a series of deliberate choices that made a general European war much more likely. So saying "Germany bears responsibility" isn't nationalist propaganda — it's a real causal-moral verdict.

(2) The other replied that this confuses responsibility with a just-so story about causation. States don't have intentions; the people running them do, and in 1914 there were plenty of people in Vienna, St. Petersburg, and Paris making escalatory choices too. She said that picking out Germany as 'the cause' is like saying the assassination at Sarajevo caused the war: it's just a convenient place to stop the chain, chosen for political reasons rather than an honest account.

(3) The first one came back and said that's too clever by half. Yes, lots of actors contributed, but responsibility isn't the same as a full causal explanation. It tracks who had alternatives and chose the riskier option. Germany gave Austria-Hungary unconditional backing, then violated Belgian neutrality and invaded France — those were discretionary moves that deliberately raised the stakes. Other powers made bad choices, but it isn't arbitrary to say some choices mattered more.

(4) The second one said even if that's true, it's a category error to move from "this chancellor and these generals made reckless choices" to "the German nation bears responsibility." The war-guilt clause of the Versailles Treaty did exactly that: it treated ordinary Germans as blameworthy so the victors could justify reparations. That's victor's justice, not a defensible moral verdict, and it's exactly the kind of collective guilt we'd reject anywhere else.

(5) The first one replied that nations do exist as ongoing collectivities — they sign treaties, owe debts, issue apologies, and pay reparations. If we say no sense can be made of blaming a nation, we also can't make sense of half of international law or of a country later owning or disowning its own past. On his view, the real value of the Fischer controversy was that it pushed Germans to ask what their political community should acknowledge, instead of letting every causal explanation double as an excuse.

I stayed out of it, but now I keep turning it over. Could you adjudicate this move by move? Rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating seperately, and then say who is right about which specific point and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
