I chair a subcommittee of the research ethics board at a mid-sized teaching hospital, a role I fell into after retiring from an academic career in the natural sciences and spending a few years reading philosophy for its own sake. I now seem to be the member expected to supply whatever conceptual scaffolding the board's policies are missing. We review roughly two dozen protocols a year, most of them modest in risk, and our recurring problem is not unscrupulous investigators. It is that our current consent checklist is a box-checking exercise: it asks whether a form was signed, whether a witness was present, and whether the investigator has asserted that the participant understood. The full board formally ratifies our recommendations, but in practice it defers to our subcommittee's analysis. The checklist gives a harried clinician-researcher no guidance about what to do when a potential participant is wavering under family pressure, when a trial offers a substantial payment to people who are financially desperate, or when a patient says she understands but plainly does not because, for example, she believes a phase I trial is treatment.

I have been reading philosophical work on what makes consent valid, and I believe the board could do better by operationalizing that analysis than by adding further boxes to the form. What I want is a decision procedure my subcommittee can hand to investigators before recruitment begins and can also use itself when a protocol is borderline. The procedure should walk through the components of valid consent as I understand them, roughly that the person must be competent to make this particular decision, adequately informed, and free of controlling influences, and translate each into concrete questions to be asked in order, with thresholds for what counts as a satisfactory answer, points where the procedure stops with a no, and points where it hands the decision to a human being because the judgment is irreducibly normative. I would also ask you to be explicit about which distinctions in the underlying theory must survive the translation and which can be dropped for non-philosopher users. In particular, I have been struck by the difference between consent as a merely social permission-giving transaction and consent as an act that expresses the person's own settled values, and likewise by the difference between withholding information and deceiving a participant; I do not know how much of this is usable downstream by investigators.

Then run the procedure on three protocol scenarios. The first is a study offering a substantial cash incentive to unemployed adults for a painful, non-therapeutic lumbar puncture. The second is a dementia study enrolling patients with mild cognitive impairment whose adult children are present during the consent discussion and visibly eager for enrollment. The third is a placebo-controlled trial in which the consent form is accurate but the recruiting physician, out of therapeutic optimism, repeatedly describes the active arm as promising. I expect the procedure to return at least one answer my board will not like, and I would rather you say so plainly than soften it. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which failures of consent will remain invisible to any checklist, and where the board should keep a human being in the loop no matter how good the procedure becomes.
