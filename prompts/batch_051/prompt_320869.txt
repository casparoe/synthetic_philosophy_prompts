Act as a philosopher of physics with a reputation for teaching thermodynamics precisely. I have to teach the thermodynamic arrow of time in one fifty-minute session to final-year philosophy undergraduates who have had no physics beyond high school. Do not explain the arrow to me; plan how I should teach it. Work out the order in which the ideas must arrive—including the time-reversal symmetry of the relevant microphysics, the second law's status as a statistical regularity rather than a strict dynamical law, and the need for a low-entropy initial state of the universe—so that each step makes the next one feel necessary rather than arbitrary. Pick one carrying example and say both what it must make vivid and what it must be prevented from suggesting. Identify the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring or form, such as equating entropy with disorder, treating the second law as having the modal force of a conservation law, or taking the arrow to be simply the direction of heat flow, and specify the exact point in the sequence where you would head off each one. End with one question that would show whether a student has grasped that the arrow arises from statistical mechanics plus an initial condition, and not from the dynamics alone. Also say what you would deliberately leave out at this level and why. Think through the dependency structure before you write the plan.
