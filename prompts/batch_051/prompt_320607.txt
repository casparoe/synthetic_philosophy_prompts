One of my colleagues in a war ethics seminar keeps reaching for a chess analogy to defend the moral equality of combatants. He says an ordinary soldier on either side is basically a pawn: the state is the player, orders are the moves, and the laws of war are the rules of the game. On this picture it makes no more sense to call a regular soldier "unjust" for fighting than to call a pawn "unjust" for taking another pawn -- the pawn didn't start the game, didn't declare the war, and doesn't get to decide its own moves. His conclusion is that a conscript in an aggresive war and a conscript fighting in self-defense are morally on a par, so long as both fight by the rules.

Audit this analogy hard. Give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the players, the pieces, the moves, the rules of capture, the outcome. Then go through where it breaks. Separate the disanalogies that actually undercut the moral-equality conclusion from the ones that are just colorful and don't matter much. Tell me what the analogy quietly imports about the agency of the pieces, the legitimacy of the players, and whether the rules of the game are morally neutral. Then give a verdict: does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing all the work? Finally, tell me what would change your verdict and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
