You are a philosopher who specializes in the ethics of cultural property, museums, and restitution. Please think carefully and do not jump straight to a verdict. Begin by laying out the possible load-bearing premises of the argument I describe and showing how they are supposed to support its conclusion. Then work through which premise the technological development bears on and whether that premise can be restated or repaired.

A familiar argument for retaining contested colonial-era objects in large Western museums — think of the Benin Bronzes or the Parthenon Marbles as paradigmatic examples — runs roughly as follows. These objects are part of the shared cultural heritage of humanity, not merely the property of one nation. What matters most is that they be conserved to a professional standard and made accessible to a worldwide public. Large Western museums are, or at least were, the institutions best placed to deliver both. So when return would mean worse conservation or narrower access, the objects should remain where they are. Call this the universal-access argument.

Now introduce a specific new development. High-resolution three-dimensional scanning, photogrammetry, online databases, and 3D printing now allow the production of extremely accurate physical replicas, and they allow anyone anywhere to inspect the visible surface of an object in fine detail. Consider two possible arrangements for a contested object, assuming for the sake of argument that both the Western museum and the source community can now meet accepted conservation standards. Under the first arrangement, the museum keeps the original and provides the source community and the global public with high-quality replicas and full digital scans. Under the second, the original is returned to the source community, while the museum keeps a high-quality replica and publishes full digital scans.

Your task is to say whether the universal-access argument survives this development. Does it still give a reason to prefer keeping the original in the Western museum, does it now support neither arrangement over the other, or does it begin to favor return? I want you in particular to separate two possible readings of the access premise. On one reading, the value to be protected is largely epistemic and experiential: the opportunity to see, study, and appreciate how the object looked and was made. On another reading, the value involves unrepeatable access to the authentic object itself — its material continuity, its physical presence, and the fact that one is encountering the very thing with the relevant history. Say which reading the original argument needs in order to retain its force, whether that reading was really doing the work in the original argument, and whether shifting to it turns the argument into a different argument about authenticity, place, provenance, or repair. Then give your overall judgment: is the universal-access argument dead, damaged but repairable, or intact — and if it is repairable, what precise formulation of it remains available?
