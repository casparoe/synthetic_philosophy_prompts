So I've basically landed on this position and I want to know how exposed it is. The position is: nobody owes it to other people to be legible. Being hard to read — deadpan, ambiguous, private about what you're actually feeling, not doing the expected social signals — is not by itself a moral failing. It only becomes wrong when it crosses into actual deception or manipulation. I got here partly from a friend of mine who is extremely hard to read (flat voice, never volunteers explanations, you genuinely can't tell if they're upset) and I used to think that was a tiny betrayal of the friendship, and then I realized alot of my annoyance was just me wanting them to do the work of making me comfortable. Also, the more I think about it, the more "just be legible" looks like a rule that taxes some people way more than others — if you're autistic, or from a different culture, or supposed to smile because of your gender, or whatever, being readable takes actual display work, and the people demanding it never notice that work. I also think my view makes sense of something my rivals view can't: two people can be totally honest with each other and still one of them is constantly being treated as shady just for being hard to read.

So what would count against this? I don't want the obvious "but trust requires predictability" point, I want the strongest possible case that there is a real duty to be legible to others — in close relationships, in public, wherever — sorted by how decisive each thing is, from fatal to merely awkward. I want the evidence, arguments, cases, or intuitions that should actually move me. Tell me what the position predicts that the "you should be transparent" view doesn't, and whether it risks anything at all. Be honest about whether my view has been qualified into unfalsifiability by the "unless it becomes deception" clause — and if it has, whether that's a real problem in this kind of view or just how moral positions normally work. And say what a defender of my position would themselves concede counts against it, not just what critics say. I'd personally be moved more by concrete cases where someone gets hurt by another person's opacity and couldn't have been expected to just ask, than by abstract stuff about community or identity.

Oh and answer in American English please.
