So I just lost a two-hour argument with a friend about predictive processing and I need someone to tell me whether the thing I came up with afterwards is actually a thing or just cope. My friend's line was the dark room one: if the brain is in the business of minimising prediction error (or free energy, surprise, whatever the right proxy is), the optimal policy is to sit in a dark, quiet, warm room with your eyes shut and do nothing, because that gives you a nice low steady error rate. I gave the usual reply — we have high-level priors that predict novelty, change, information and so on, so a dark room is actually surprising relative to those. He said that's unfalsifiable: you can always staple on another prior that predicts whatever the animal in fact does, so the principle ends up predicting everything. I didn't have a good answer and its been annoying me.

So here's the thing I've been calling precision starvation. I want to say the dark room objection mistakes prediction error for something that's only a cost. It's also the signal the system uses to calibrate its own precision estimates — its judgements about how much confidence to place in this prediction or that sensory channel. Precision is itself inferred, and that inference can only stay honest if the system regularly encounters some error. Park yourself in a sensory-uniform environment long enough and your precision estimates would go uncalibrated; the machinery that decides which errors to take seriously would start to drift, and you'd end up generating structure that isn't in the world (which is, I gather, roughly what sensory deprivation does). So below some error rate the system doesn't just get bored, it degrades. Error is a nutrient, not a toxin. A working agent should therefore act not to eliminate prediction error but to keep it in a tolerable band, and a policy that drove it to zero would be self-undermining.

I know active inference has its own dark-room reply via expected free energy — policies are chosen to minimise expected free energy, and that includes an epistemic term that values resolving uncertainty, not just keeping sensory error low. But I think what I'm gesturing at is different: it's not about seeking information for its own sake, it's about keeping the precision-setting machinery fed.
