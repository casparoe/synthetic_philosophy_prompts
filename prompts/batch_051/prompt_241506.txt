I've been defending a position in my reading group that I have come to actually hold. The position is that the value of a mental state is multiplicative across its two fundamental dimensions: roughly, value equals degree of consciousness times degree of happiness, where happiness is signed (negative for suffering) and consciousness is graded, with a true zero. On this picture, a state with no consciousness has zero value no matter how positive its other features, a fully conscious but perfectly neutral state has zero value, and more consciousness amplifies whichever valence is present — making vivid pleasure better and vivid suffering worse. The value of an outcome is then the ordinary additive sum of these products across states and moments. I like this because it captures two intuitions at once: awareness matters only through what it is like, and how good a state is matters only if someone is there to feel it. It also cleanly contrasts with two rivals: an additive view, on which consciousness and happiness each contribute a weighted amount on their own, and lexical views, on which one dimension outranks the other no matter the quantities involved.

I want you to tell me what would count against this multiplicative view, beyond the obvious point that the quantities are hard to measure. What possible cases, intuitions, formal results, or commitments in the metaphysics of consciousness would a defender of this view have to take seriously?
