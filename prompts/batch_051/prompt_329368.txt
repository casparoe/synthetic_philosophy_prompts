Two people in my reading group got into it about how an aligned AI ought to handle the fact that the people it's serving don't share values. The setup we'd been using was a general-purpose assistant rolled out by a pluralistic society—not a personal assistant, but the kind of thing that might mediate public information, help people with government forms, triage mental-health questions, that sort of thing.

Jonas kicked it off by saying the only defensible target is to aggregate everyone's values: once you've said the system is for everyone, refusing to aggregate just means some designer's private list of what matters gets smuggled in. He said neutrality is a fiction because every time the system acts it has to do something, and whatever it does will be more in line with some people's values than others.

Amara said that's exactly why the system should be liberal-neutral rather than a maximizer: on questions where reasonable people have rival views of the good—religion, the weight of community versus individual choice, how much risk is acceptable—the system shouldn't pick a winner, even a majoritarian one, because that lets a majority's conception of the good be imposed on minorities. She said aggregating everyone's values into a single ranking isn't a neutral bookkeeping move; it already takes a stand that their values are commensurable and tradable against each other.

Jonas came back that liberal neutrality sounds principled until you ask what the system actually does when it can't stay out. If someone asks a state-facing assistant about, say, assisted dying, or whether to stay in a faith community they're doubting, staying silent or offering "both sides" is itself a choice with predictable effects, and not everyone wants that kind of counseling. He said some people would rather the system just answered from the aggregate, because perfect neutrality helps nobody.

Amara said he was conflating two jobs. The assistant doesn't have to adjudicate the true answer to a moral question; it can protect each person's effective freedom to live by their own lights—give them accurate information, flag coercion, refuse to steer. That isn't "both sides"; it's a one-sided commitment, but it's a commitment to a framework rather than to any particular conception of the good.

At that point Jonas said fine, but then her constraint is just another value that goes into the aggregation: some people care more about autonomy than others, and the aggregator should weigh it the way it weighs everything else. Amara said no—if you can weigh my interest in autonomy against your interest in having the majority's values reflected, you've already decided autonomy is the sort of thing that can be traded off, and thats exactly the decision that was supposed to be off the table. Jonas replied that this is just reheated rule-worship: constraints are just preferences with very high numbers attached, and refusing to state the price means you've helped yourself to a price you won't defend.

Neither would budge, and they asked me to settle it, but I couldn't. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who was right about which point, where each of them overreached, and whether there's a version of their two positions that actually reconciles them? I don't want a tie; I want to know exactly which claims hold up and which ones don't.
