I'm a software engineer, no formal philosophy background, working on the backend for a robo-advisor. Part of my job is our risk-tolerance questionnaire: users get a sequence of binary choices between small lotteries, and we fit a utility function to their answers and map them to a portfolio. Every quarter we audit the questionnaire, because for a decent chunk of users the allocations come out weird and we have to figure out whether it's them or us. Lately the data team is flagging a lot of people who make this pair of choices: they choose a guaranteed $1,000,000 over an 89% chance of $1,000,000 with a 10% chance of $5,000,000 and a 1% chance of nothing, and then, in a separate question, choose a 10% chance of $5,000,000 over an 11% chance of $1,000,000 (rest nothing in both). Our model calls this pair internally inconsistent because it violates the independence axiom of expected utility. The product team wants to treat these as user error and re-ask until they're "consistent." Compliance wants to treat the first answer as the real one. I have a hunch that some of these violations are not bugs but actual preferences — people seem to really care about the difference between a sure thing and a near-sure thing — and I'd rather not overwrite something real just because our model says its an error.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure we can run on each flagged user (and on the questionnaire itself) to decide whether a violation of the independence axiom should be treated as (a) confusion or measurement error, so we re-ask or drop the answer, (b) a genuine non-expected-utility preference we should respect and propagate through to the portfolio, or (c) a case no automated rule should settle, where we punt the user to a human advisor. It should be a checklist in words, with the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with "respect the preference," where it stops with "re-ask/flag as error," and where it hands off. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as code. The constraints: we get at most one or two follow-up questions before a user churns, we have no decision-theory-trained people on staff, everything has to be explainable to a compliance officer in plain English, and the procedure has to be implementable in a Python service, not a seminar.

Then run the procedure on three concrete cases: (i) the classic Allais pair above; (ii) a user who makes consistent choices at one probability scale but flips when we scale all the probabilities down by the same factor; and (iii) a user whose violation is real but tiny — the two options are a few dollars apart and nearly identical in expected value. Finally, make the procedure say something we will not like. In particular, I suspect the right answer in some of these cases is that our questionnaire design is causing the artifacts, or that we should stop automating certain users entirely and send them to a human. Tell me where the procedure breaks down and what it can't decide.
