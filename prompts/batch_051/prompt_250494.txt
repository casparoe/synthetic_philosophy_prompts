I've ended up running a small weekly programming circle for grad students in the humanities. Most of them can write a loop and a function but never had a proper CS course, and we've been doing Python. This week the whole group stalled on the same thing: two of them thought they'd copied a list, then one person sorted "their" copy and the other person's list changed too; another person did the same with a string, nothing changed, and now there's a faction that thinks strings are just broken. I promised I'd do a dedicated session on why mutation is so hard to think about, and I don't want to lecture at them — I want a lesson plan. Don't explain mutation to me; plan how I should run the hour.

I have an hour and a whiteboard. What has to come first, before aliasing or mutability, and what depends on what? I have a hunch the real blocker is identity — whether two names reach the same thing — and that "change" is the red herring, but I want the order argued for, not asserted. Pick the single running example that should carry the whole session: what must it have (two names for one object, a lookalike separate object, one visible mutation) and what should it deliberately not have? Then the misconceptions I should expect and exactly where in the sequence to head each off: a variable is a box with a value in it; assignment copies; "if I sort this one the other can't change"; strings are just weird and lists are just like that; a function that returns a list gives me a brand-new one. And what should I openly refuse to explain at this level — memory addresses, garbage collection, the small-integer identity weirdness, whatever the pass-by semantics debate is. At the end, give me one short exercise or question that would show me they've got identity-versus-value, rather than a memorized rule like "lists are mutable" that they'll overapply to every object forever.
