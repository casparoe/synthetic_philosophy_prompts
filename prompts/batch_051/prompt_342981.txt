So I'm a novelist, not a statistican, and in my current book there's a scene where an investigative journalist uses the following argument to make a drug company's data scientist look foolish. I want the scientist to have a sharp, correct reply, not just bluster, but I need to know the reply myself first.

The set-up: someone pooled a year of treatment records for a new drug, Rezuvin, against the standard drug, Solvacin. Every patient either survived or died, and everyone is either under 60 or 60-or-over.

(1) Among under-60s, survival was 93% on Rezuvin (93 of 100) and 87% on Solvacin (348 of 400). Rezuvin better.
(2) Among 60-or-overs, survival was 73% on Rezuvin (292 of 400) and 69% on Solvacin (69 of 100). Rezuvin better again.
(3) Those two groups are exhaustive and non-overlapping: every patient falls in exactly one.
(4) If one treatment is better than another in each group of an exhaustive partition, then it is better overall.
(5) But overall survival was 77% on Rezuvin (385 of 500) and 83% on Solvacin (417 of 500). Solvacin better.
(6) So the same data shows Rezuvin is both better and worse than Solvacin.
(7) Therefore "which drug is better" has no determinate answer, and comparative-effectiveness evidence is just marketing with decimal points.

The journalist is being theatrical, sure, but the argument feels too airtight to just call wrong. Which step should the scientist reject, and why is that step so seductive? What's the corrected principle? And does he have to also reject (6), or can he concede everything up to (5) and only strike one premise? I'd rather not have him say the overall numbers are meaningless, but I don't know wether that's coherent.
