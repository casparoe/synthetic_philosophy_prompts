Below is an exam question from a course on virtue theory, followed by a student's answer. Grade the answer out of 20, and provide the point-by-point comments that justify the grade. The comments should not be a general impression; they should identify which specific claims and inferences in the answer succeed, which fail, and why.

Question:

In a virtue-theoretic framework, carefully distinguish intellectual humility, moral humility, and social modesty. For each, state the strongest case for counting it as a virtue and the strongest case for regarding it as something less than a virtue — for example, a social lubricant, a self-protective strategy, or a form of timidity. Then give your own view of whether any or all of them are genuine virtues, and what unifies them, if anything does.

Student's answer:

A person can be intellectually humble without being socially modest, and morally humble without being intellectually humble, so any adequate account must treat the three separately. Intellectual humility is the disposition to hold one's beliefs at a confidence below what the evidence would by itself support, especially on matters where intelligent people disagree. This is a virtue because persistent controversy is itself evidence that the reasoning on all sides is unreliable; the intellectually humble person discounts toward the midpoint of the dispute and is therefore less likely to be led around by partisanship. It also improves inquiry, because a person who doubts themselves seeks out counterevidence instead of trying to win.

Moral humility is the recognition that one's own moral record is no better than anyone else's in any way that would give one standing to blame. Because our motives are opaque to us, and because we almost certainly would have committed the same wrongs in the same circumstances, the morally humble person does not blame others. Blame presupposes a standing that no one actually has, so the humble person treats moral criticism as something to be offered, if at all, only as a report of personal discomfort rather than as a verdict on the other person.

Social modesty, finally, concerns self-presentation. The modest person does not advertise their achievements, dominate discussion, or invite flattering comparisons. This is not the same as underestimating oneself: a person can know exactly how good they are and remain modest. For that reason modesty is a social virtue: it keeps cooperative groups from being poisoned by status competition and makes excellence tolerable to others. Its value is thus instrumental rather than intrinsic, but the same is true of many genuine virtues.

The common core of all three is a kind of self-restraint in the face of one's own importance. Each is a virtue because each curbs the tendency to overrate and over-claim for oneself, which is a real vice.

Grading instructions:

For each of the three categories — intellectual humility, moral humility, and social modesty — say what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong or leaves unsupported, and whether the mistakes are local or affect the overall argument. Also assess the claim that all three share a common core. Do not penalize the student for omitting assigned readings or named authors unless the question required them; grade the philosophical content.
