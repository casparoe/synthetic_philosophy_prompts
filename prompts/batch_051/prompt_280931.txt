I run the selection end of a small democracy nonprofit. We organize citizens assemblies and policy juries for city governments — maybe six a year, things like a climate assembly or a policing oversight jury. My whole job is to translate "select by lot" into an actual procedure for getting from a city's adult population to a seated group of 25–40 people in about eight weeks, on a fixed budget, with the city council and a few hostile local reporters checking whether the panel is rigged or just unrepresentative.

I start from the sortition/lottocracy view: political bodies should be selected by lottery from the whole governed population, not elected. But the principle seems to commit me to two things at once. (a) Each member of the political community has an equal chance of being selected, so nobody has to be a good campaigner or know the right people. (b) The seated body should mirror the population on politically relevant traits — age, geography, income, maybe race or gender depending on the issue. Pure random sampling gives (a) but at n=30 it will regularly miss (b). Stratified random sampling buys (b) at the price of making individual selection odds depend on which stratum you end up in and how many slots it gets. I keep resolving this tension differently on each project, which I've learned is a sign I don't have a procedure, just vibes with a lottery machine.

Write me a reusable decision procedure my team can actually run. The deliverable should be an ordered checklist from "we have a population definition and a target size" to "we seat these people," and for each step say: the question we're asking, what counts as a sufficient answer to move on, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it explicitly hands the decision to a human because the equality commitment can't settle it. Constraints: about eight weeks from mandate to first session, two staff, a recruiting budget around $30k, and the city council has to approve the final list with written justification for any exclusions, replacements, or departures from the random draw. Extra contact attempts cost real money; volunteer signups are nearly free, which I assume is the original sin here.

Then run the procedure on three cases that actually came up, so I can watch it think:

1. A climate assembly, n=30. The random draw from the city database gives us only two people under 30 (they're about 18% of adults here) and zero from the two poorest wards. The equity office suggests we recruit a few young and low-income volunteers through community groups to "fix representativeness." I suspect my procedure should say no, but I want to know whether that's the principle or just my stubbornness.

2. A policing oversight jury. The draw seats a retired cop. A local abolitionist group demands he be removed as a stakeholder, and the mayor quietly agrees. Can the procedure exclude him after the draw, or does the absence of a pre-registered exclusion rule for cops (and for stakeholder conflicts generally) settle it, even if it means seating someone half the room will refuse to work with?

3. A standing panel on the city budget. Ten days before the first session one drawn member drops out. We keep a "reserve list" of volunteers who filled out a web form saying they'd step in. Do we seat the reserve who matches the dropout's demographics, leave the seat empty, or redraw that one seat from the original random frame? My prior is that the reserve list is poisoned by self-selection even when its demographics look tidy, and I want the procedure to either confirm that or tell me why I'm wrong.

Also mark every step where the real choice is mine or the council's and can't be derived from the principle. I'd rather know when I'm making a political call under an equality-sounding label than pretend the checklist made it for me. And give me at least one place where the procedure returns an answer I won't like, because otherwise I'll assume it's just laundering my priors.
