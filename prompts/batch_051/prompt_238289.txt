He thinks the NHS was just a postwar political fix and any Rawlsian story about it is post hoc rationalising. I'm not so sure, so I want to work through the founding of the NHS in 1948 as a proper case study.

Just so we're on the same page about the facts: Labour government, Aneurin Bevan as health minister, hospitals nationalised, GPs left as independent contractors but paid from public funds, and the whole thing financed out of general taxation so treatment is free at the point of use regardless of ability to pay. The BMA and plenty of consultants opposed it and won concessions, so it wasnt some clean unanimous agreement. I don't need the history recapped; I need the Rawlsian analysis.

Run the case through the veil of ignorance and the difference principle. Who are the parties in the original position, and what do they know and not know about their own health, income, and likelihood of illness? Would they choose a tax-funded, universal, mostly non-market system like the NHS, or would a safety net plus private insurance satisfy the difference principle just as well? Be honest about the fit: the difference principle is officially about income and wealth and primary goods, so does Rawls have to treat health as a special primary good, or route it through fair equality of opportunity, before the theory can say anything at all? Then turn it around: which features of 1948 does the model explain, and which does it miss or mangle — the doctors' self-interested resistance, the fact that an elected government decided this rather than people behind a veil, the postwar mood of solidarity. Does the episode vindicate the difference principle, or does it show that the principle is too underdetermined to pick out anything like the NHS as against a range of other systems?
