I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about how much literal weight the language of "reasons" can carry when no one is around to do the reasoning: natural selection, markets, predictive models, even the explanation of a bridge collapse. Before I write it, I need a definition with real cutting power.

Please give me a working definition of "a reason" — something's being a reason for a conclusion, an event, or a course of action — that is explicit about whether reasons must be mental states (or must at least be taken up by someone) or can be free-floating. Test the definition against these eight cases, and say for each whether the item is a reason literally, a reason only in an extended or "as-if" sense, or not a reason at all, and whose reason it is, if anyone's:

1. A correct but unread proof: a valid derivation of a theorem, written down and destroyed before anyone else checks it.
2. An engineering explanation: a bridge collapses, and the report says the reason it failed was accumulated metal fatigue in a joint.
3. A selectional explanation: the heart exists because it pumped blood in ancestral populations, though no one designed it for that end.
4. A thermostat: it closes the furnace circuit when the room cools below the set point, and we say it switched on because the room got cold.
5. A chess engine: its search returns a move together with an evaluation line, and a strong player says the evaluation line is the reason the move is best.
6. A confabulation: someone offers a coherent rationale for a choice, but experiments show the choice was caused by something else and the rationale was produced after the fact.
7. A never-entertained normative reason: the fact that it would be unjust to punish a person known to be innocent, in a society where no one ever formulates that principle.
8. An unread sign: a road sign correctly says the bridge ahead ices before the road and tells drivers to slow down, but an approaching driver never sees it.

Run the definition through all eight in order. I'm most confident that (1) is a genuine reason and that (6) is at most a purported one; the middle cases I find genuinely murky in different ways. Where your definition forces a verdict that collides with ordinary usage, tell me which gives way and why, and be willing to revise the definition rather than merely torture the cases.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. It would help me, for instance, to know which distinctions are standard (motivating, explanatory, normative reasons, if you use them) and which positions remain live disputes, such as whether normative reasons exist independently of minds or whether natural selection produces reasons in anything more than metaphor. Please do not present a contested view as the consensus.

End your response with a single final line, after the discussion, in the form:

\answer{<your final one-sentence definition>}
