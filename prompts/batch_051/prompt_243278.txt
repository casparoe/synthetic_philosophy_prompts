I have been turning over a dispute in Nyāya epistemology and want help deciding whether it is empirically tractable or merely a quarrel about where to draw a conceptual boundary. On one side, the Naiyāyikas count testimony (śabda) as an irreducible source of knowledge, a pramāṇa alongside perception and inference: when a competent speaker tells me that p, I can come to know that p through the speaker's words themselves. On the other side, their Buddhist critics argue that verbal knowledge is not a distinct source at all; it is a special case of inference, because I move from the fact that the speaker uttered p, together with some basis for taking the speaker to be reliable, to the conclusion that p. The two camps agree on many everyday cases; what they disagree about is the hearer's actual epistemic route.

A colleague in cognitive science claims the distinct-source view makes a real psychological prediction and can be tested. I am suspicious that any behavioral result will merely re-describe the same data in two vocabularies. In particular, do not let the test quietly assume what is at issue: a subject might have no introspective access to an inference that occurs subpersonally; a learned heuristic could mimic a dedicated channel; and no processing story by itself may settle the philosophical question of what should count as a separate pramāṇa.
