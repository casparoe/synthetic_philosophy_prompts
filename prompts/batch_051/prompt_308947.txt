I've been moderating an argument between two people in my reading group who have gone at each other for about a year, and it's clear they're mostly talking past each other. One defends Scanlon-style contractualism: roughly, an action or principle is wrong if it could be reasonably rejected by someone, and the justifying reasons have to be addressed to each person one at a time, not to the collection of people as a whole. He keeps insisting on what he calls the individualist restriction — the idea that adding up complaints across different people is not by itself a reason any single person has to reject or accept a principle. The other defends a plain aggregationist view: if a policy produces more total benefit when you literally sum the benefits and the burdens across individuals, that's what matters, and refusing to do the summing is just squeamishness. The fight has recently circled around the standard cases: choosing whether to save one person from death or five different people from death, and whether we may impose a serious lifelong injury on one person to prevent mild headaches for a very large number of people.

I want you to write the joint statement they would actually co-sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. It should have a numbered "agreed claims" section covering what they both accept about justifiability, the seperateness of persons (the idea that a loss to one person isn't automatically canceled out by a gain to someone else), why the aggregation problem is hard, and what exactly the contested cases are. Then a disagreements table: the residual differences, worded precisely enough that each side would accept how its own view is phrased, without caricaturing either. Then identify the cruxes — the specific sub-questions such that, if one of them were settled, at least one side would genuinely change its mind — and for each crux give a concrete case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Make sure the contractualist doesn't quietly become an aggregator in the saving-one-vs-five case, and the aggregator doesn't quietly stop counting in the headaches case. Flag any part of the dispute that you think is mostly verbal, and also flag places where the contractualist's view simply underdetermines an answer. No citations or footnotes; write from the positions themselves, so a smart layperson could follow it.
