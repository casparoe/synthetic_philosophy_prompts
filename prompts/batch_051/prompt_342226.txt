I chair the item-review committee for a state emergency medical services licensing board. Each cycle we review about 400 written items and 60 hands-on stations, and every item must be tagged as testing either theoretical knowledge or clinical know-how, because our accreditor weights the two categories separately and requires evidence that candidates are assessed on both. The committee fights about these tags constantly. One member tags a multiple-choice question as know-how because it opens with a scene and asks what you would do first; another member points out that a candidate could memorize the correct priority without ever having touched a patient. Meanwhile some of our hands-on stations can be passed by a candidate who recites the steps aloud before performing the skill, which feels more like knowing that than knowing how. I want the distinction operationalized, not relitigated item by item.

I have read some Ryle, and I remember the outline of his view: knowing how is a disposition or ability, not the possession of true propositions; and if every intelligent performance required first contemplating a true proposition about how to perform it, the contemplating itself would need a prior contemplated proposition, and so on without end. If that is right, the real line is not about whether an item is written or hands-on, and not about whether it mentions a scenario; it is about whether success requires the exercise of an ability or merely the recognition or recitation of information. Our reviewers have ten or fifteen minutes per item, no philosophical training, and their tags must be documented and stand up on appeal.

Act as a professional philosopher of mind and epistemology who also understands high-stakes assessment design. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming at least three candidate tests for distinguishing a knowing-how item from a knowing-that item; tell me which tests you keep, which you discard, and why. Then give me a reusable decision procedure — a checklist or flowchart in words — with the tests run in order, phrased so a non-philosopher reviewer can follow it. Apply the procedure as a worked set to these four items and classify each with reasoning: (a) a written multiple-choice item asking for the correct adult compression-to-ventilation ratio in CPR; (b) a written short-answer item asking the candidate to list, in the correct order, the steps for applying a traction splint to a suspected mid-shaft femur fracture; (c) a practical station in which the candidate must ventilate an apneic manikin with a bag-valve mask for two minutes while an examiner rates mask seal, ventilation rate, and chest rise; and (d) a role-play station in which the candidate must explain to a conscious, frightened patient why a cervical collar must be applied before she is touched, and adapt if she refuses. Make sure at least one worked example receives a tag my committee will dislike, and explain why the unwelcome outcome follows from the principle rather than from a defect in the procedure. Flag the cases the procedure cannot settle, where a reviewer has to exercise judgment. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which new argument or evidence about the nature of skill, or about what written and practical exams can actually measure, would lead you to redraw the boundary or alter a rule, and where you think your procedure is most likely to fail.
