I'm teaching Kafka's Metamorphosis next month in a little adult-ed class, and I keep going back and forth about how to use it to talk about L.A. Paul's work on transformative experience. Paul says a decision is epistemically transformative when you can't know what it will be like until you've had the experience, and personally transformative when having it changes your preferences or the person you become. Her standard example is becoming a vampire — the human weighing the choice can't know what being a vampire is like, and the vampire might stop valuing what the human valued, so ordinary expected-value reasoning breaks down. In the story, Gregor doesn't choose to become a giant bug; it just happens to him, which already breaks one part of her framework. But I'm interested in what the story actually shows about the two kinds of transformation. Early on he still thinks like a traveling salesman: he worries about missing the train, his boss, how to open the door. That seems to support the opposite reading — the change is epistemically transformative, because neither he nor we can know what it's like, but not personally transformative, because his cares survive the body. Later on the sister's violin, the changed food preferences, the crawling on the ceiling, his human memories going sour — that seems like the second transformation arriving late. Does the story end up vindicating Paul's claim that you can't cleanly separate the two? Or does it put pressure on the very idea that reflections from inside a transformation can guide someone standing outside one? Also, Paul's problem is mostly about first-personal choice under uncertainty, but nearly all the decisions in the book are made by the family about what to do with him, and they get transformed too — Grete especially. Read The Metamorphosis through this lens, and go both directions: which scenes does the philosophy illuminate, and where does the story resist or complicate Paul's picture? I'd like a reading that takes the text and the philosophy seriously and says what the story teaches about transformative experience that the vampire example doesn't.
