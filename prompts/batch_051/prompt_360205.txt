After a long argument on Sunday, I still cannot reconstruct the interpretive position my friend was defending. She teaches legislation and calls herself a textualist, but by the end her verdicts seemed to fit a more contextual view and at moments sounded almost purposivist. I wrote down, as faithfully as I can, the series of hypothetical cases she ruled on during the argument. All are invented, set in one imaginary jurisdiction, and I have stipulated the relevant drafting facts; please don't object that a real legislature would have drafted more carefully or that real law would add exceptions. Here is her track record.

1. The scooter case. An ordinance makes it unlawful to "operate a motorized scooter on any sidewalk in the central business district." "Motorized scooter" is defined as "a device with handlebars, no seat, and an electric motor capable of propelling a standing rider above eight miles per hour." A rider uses an electric stand-up scooter at eleven miles per hour on a business-district sidewalk. The preamble and the council discussion leading to the ordinance are entirely about gas-powered mopeds that had been weaving through pedestrians. She says: violation; the definition names electric motors and a speed threshold, and the fact that the council's motivating example was a moped does not shrink the text.

2. The flintlock case. A statute makes it an offense to "carry a concealed firearm into any public building." "Firearm" is defined as "any weapon designed to discharge a projectile by means of an explosion." A historical interpreter carries a working reproduction flintlock pistol under his coat into the county records office for a scheduled talk; it can load and fire, but he carries it unloaded and it is part of the presentation. The committee report is entirely about modern concealable handguns. She says: violation; the pistol is a weapon within the definition, it was concealed, the statute contains no museum or reenactment exception, and legislative history does not narrow an otherwise clear definition.

3. The kite case. A statute prohibits a person from "operating an aircraft within five hundred feet of a school" without a permit. "Aircraft" is defined as "any contrivance used or designed for flight in the air." A hobbyist flies a large box kite over a school playground. She says: no violation; read against the chapter as a whole — which speaks of engines, runways, flight plans, and airspace — "aircraft" means the kind of powered or piloted craft the chapter regulates, not a tethered box kite. She insisted this was still textualism: ordinary meaning is the meaning a reasonable reader of the entire statute would assign, not dictionary-plus-definition literalism.

4. The typo case. A provision of the state code, enacted as part of a penalty schedule, says a person who violates section 4 "is excepted from the penalty set out in section 7." No one disputes that "excepted" is a typo for "subject." A person cited under section 4 argues that the enacted text exempts him from any penalty. She says: no; a manifest scrivener's error may be read as corrected, because no reasonable reader would think the legislature deliberately exempted the very violators it had just described.

Now apply her to this new case. A state tax-credit statute offers a credit for "the purchase of any new plug-in electric vehicle." "Plug-in electric vehicle" is defined as "a motor vehicle with at least four wheels that draws propulsion energy from a rechargeable battery of at least four kilowatt-hours." A student-transportation company buys a new battery-electric school bus that satisfies the definition and claims the credit. The legislative history is unusually explicit that the credit was drafted as a consumer incentive: the fiscal note estimated revenue loss on the assumption that only passenger cars would qualify, and the committee report uses "passenger motor vehicle" throughout. The final text, however, says "vehicle," not "passenger motor vehicle."

I would like four things. First, identify the candidate interpretive rules that are jointly consistent with her four prior verdicts, and say how each fits them. Second, say what each candidate rule predicts for the school-bus case. Third, give your best prediction of her verdict, identify which of the four prior cases is the closest analog, and say what principle that prediction commits her to if the prior cases were taken not to have settled it. Fourth, describe one further hypothetical — just one, with the facts stated precisely — that would cleanly separate the live rules. Throughout, keep what is settled among people who work on statutory interpretation separate from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
