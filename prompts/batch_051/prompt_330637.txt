A colleague in political philosophy recently sketched an argument I cannot quite dismiss, and I would like you to tell me whether it is valid and, if not, exactly where it fails.

(1) Resentment is a moral sentiment: it is a special kind of pain at an undeserved disadvantage or wrong, directed toward the party responsible for it.

(2) Envy is not a moral sentiment: it is pain at another person's having a good one lacks, together with the wish that they not have it, and it can persist even when their having the good does not deprive one of anything.

(3) In a society marked by serious unjust inequalities, many of the goods the well-off enjoy are advantages they have only because of past or present injustice.

(4) It follows that, in such a society, the envy that ordinary people feel toward the well-off is, in the cases that matter, resentment under another name: the pain is really directed at the unjust advantage, not at the other's being better off as such.

(5) Therefore this envy is not a vice but a fitting, or at least excusable, response to injustice.

(6) Consequently, the familiar moral counsel that envy is always a vice to be overcome functions ideologically, because it discourages a form of resentment that would otherwise track injustice.

My suspicion is that something goes wrong between (3) and (4), but I have not been able to say precisely what, because (5) still strikes me as right in many cases. Is the argument valid? If not, identify the first step that fails, explain what distinction it suppresses or what inference it gets wrong, and state the strongest version of the conclusion that can legitimately be drawn. If you think the argument is fine as it stands, say so and defend (6).
