In writing fiction I keep running into a three-level structure, and I cannot tell whether it is the same structure Hofstadter discusses when he asks where a message's meaning is located. He separates the frame message — the signal that a message is present at all and is to be decoded — from the outer message, the information carried by the form and conventions about how the message is to be taken, and the inner message, the content itself. A novel seems to me to exhibit exactly these three layers. The book's existence as a bound object on a fiction shelf, with a title and a cover, announces that a literary message is present and that it is to be read as a novel rather than, say, a memorandum (the frame); the narrative register, genre cues, and formal machinery — a first-person narrator who may be unreliable, the conventions of the gothic, the chapter rhythm — instruct the reader how to take what follows (the outer); and the plot, characters, and prose are what is actually said (the inner). My question is whether this parallel is deep or merely verbal. If it is deep, does anything from Hofstadter's analysis transfer — for example, the idea that a message's meaning is not localizable in any one of the three levels but is distributed across them, so that changing the outer message can alter the inner message while leaving the inner sentences themselves untouched? And where exactly should the mapping break down? I would rather be shown where the analogy fails than have it confirmed.
