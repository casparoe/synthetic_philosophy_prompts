I'm retired now—spent my career in geochemistry—and I've gotten into philosophy of language through a little reading group. We're doing rules and exceptions in grammar, and the organizer asked each of us to write down our current commitments so the group can tear into them. Here's what I've landed on:

1. Languages have real rules, not just statistical patterns. The English past tense is formed by a default rule (add -ed), and sing/sang is an exception to that rule, stored as a seperate lexical item.
2. Suppletion, like go/went, is different in kind from sing/sang. 'Went' isn't the default rule misfiring; it's a separate lexical item that fills the slot for the past tense of 'go,' and once you look at the whole paradigm the form is rule-consistent rather than exceptional.
3. Sound change is regular—the old Neogrammarian idea that sound laws are exceptionless is roughly right. Apparent exceptions to a sound change come from analogy or borrowing, which are themselves processes with their own regularities.
4. If 3 is right, then historically every irregular form is either the residue of an earlier regular process or the product of analogy or borrowing. So at bottom there are no true exceptions to grammatical rules: what we call an exception is just several rules interacting.
5. But it would be absurd to write a grammar of English without saying the past-tense rule has exceptions like sing/sang. The notion of an exception is doing real descriptive work, and I don't want my view to make standard grammatical description come out false.

Does this hang together? I honestly can't tell where the stress points are. Rank the tensions by severity, say which are only apparent, and tell me what the cheapest single revision is and what it costs.
