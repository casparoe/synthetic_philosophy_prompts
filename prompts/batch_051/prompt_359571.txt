Ok so a friend has convinced me that there's a real problem with the idea of eternal bliss, and he has this tidy argument he says he's adapting from Williams's Makropulos case. I can't spot the flaw, but the conclusion feels way to strong — it seems like it would rule out not just heaven but any kind of worthwhile endless life at all, which I find hard to accept. The argument goes:

(1) A life is worth continuing only insofar as the person has categorical desires — projects, attachments, and concerns that give her reasons to go on, not just passing wants.

(2) Any actual person has only a finite stock of these desires. They come out of a particular character and a particular life, and a fixed character can generate only so many of them before they run out.

(3) In an endless life, that finite stock would eventually be exhausted: either the desires get satisfied, or they lose their grip through repetition, or the person changes so much that the later desires no longer belong to the original person.

(4) So any endless life eventually becomes boring — not just mildly dull, but not worth continuing.

(5) Heaven is supposed to be an endless life that never becomes boring, and indeed is supposed to be blissful forever.

(6) So heaven, as traditionally conceived, is impossible: either it's endless but eventually boring, or it's only blissful by transforming the person so much that it isn't really the same person's afterlife anymore.

He says the only routes out are to deny that a worthwhile life needs categorical desires, or to deny that heaven involves an unending temporal life in anything like our sense — and he thinks both are desperate. I don't especially want to give up the idea that a worthwhile life needs some kind of project or attachment, and I'd also like to keep the idea that heaven is coherent. So: where exactly does this argument break, and what do you have to give up if you reject that step?
