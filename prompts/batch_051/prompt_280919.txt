I'm a novelist, and I'm working on a book about a Holocaust survivor and memoirist who, near the end of his life, asks for his earliest testimony to be withdrawn and forgotten. The whole thing has me stuck in a real arguement. On one side, Avishai Margalit says we have a duty to remember the victims of radical evil — memory is owed to the victims themselves, not to some grand project that uses them. On the other, David Rieff worries that constant remembering can be a sickness: it keeps old wounds open and gets the dead conscripted into every new conflict. Levi died in 1987, before the internet made forgetting nearly impossible and before Europe's right to erasure/de-indexing made certain kinds of forgetting legal again. What would Levi have made of a moment like ours, where people demand their own digital forgetting at the same time others insist memory is a duty? Would he land with Margalit, with Rieff, or somewhere stranger? Try to reason from his actual sensibility as a witness and a writer, not the flattering version of him.
