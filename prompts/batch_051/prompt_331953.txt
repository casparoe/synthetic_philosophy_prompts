I'm a software engineer who has spent the past year implementing structure-learning routines for probabilistic graphical models, and I've accumulated a set of judgments about when a directed acyclic graph is the right one that I can't yet reduce to a single principle. As I understand the standard options, a graph is Markov with respect to the data when all its d-separation statements are matched by real conditional independencies; a minimal graph is a Markov graph with no edge whose deletion would preserve that fit; and a faithful graph adds the converse, meaning every conditional independence in the data corresponds to a d-separation in the graph. I keep seeing these notions pull apart, and I want to know whether my case-by-case verdicts commit me to a coherent position or force me to give something up.

Here are the verdicts. (1) When two DAGs are Markov equivalent, differing only in the orientation of some non-collider edges, I treat them as indistinguishable on passive observational data and don't think an algorithm should assert one orientation over the other. (2) When a nine-edge graph and a twelve-edge graph both fit the data's conditional independencies, I prefer the nine-edge graph in reports, but I don't infer that the three extra edges are absent; I treat them as unsupported rather than false. (3) In an XOR-like gate with two independent coin-flip inputs, each input is marginally independent of the output even though the two inputs jointly determine it. I count both inputs as causes; the collider graph with edges from both inputs to the output seems right even though it is not faithful to the distribution, and I wouldn't remove an edge just to make the representation respect those extra marginal independencies. (4) In a system where X has a direct effect on Y and an indirect path that exactly cancels it, X looks independent of Y. Since I know the mechanism, I resist deleting the direct edge just because the data, taken at face value, would invite it. (5) For pure prediction, I ignore both minimality and faithfulness and use whichever structure, or non-graph model, cross-validates best, even if it encodes physically impossible edges. (6) When two equally minimal graphs differ in orientation and I have background knowledge about mechanism timing, I pick the graph consistent with that knowledge while admitting the data alone can't decide.

These verdicts feel individually sensible, but I can see the strains: judgment 2 wants sparsity while judgments 3 and 4 want mechanism to override exactly the independence-driven trimming that a minimalist would endorse; judgment 1 says data can't orient while judgment 6 lets background knowledge orient. The deeper thing I'm trying to understand is what, if anything, a graph selected only for minimality commits me to causally, and which of minimality or faithfulness is doing the heavier work in my judgments. Can you formulate the principle, or small set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts? State each clause precisely and say which case motivates it, apply it to cases I haven't listed (such as a graph that is minimal but unfaithful, and a graph that is faithful but not minimal), and tell me honestly which of my verdicts cannot be accommodated and would need to be revised or dropped. Also, mark clearly which parts of your answer are settled technical consequences of the definitions and which parts are genuinely contested among people who work on this, and avoid presenting a contested view as if it were the consensus.
