I'm a PhD student working on free will and moral responsibilty, and I keep running into a move in the providence and problem-of-evil literature that I don't have a proper label for. The move goes like this: God is said to be justified in permitting some actual evil because of something like, "if the agent had been put in slightly different circumstances, she would have freely done something even worse," or "if God had arranged for that particular temptation not to occur, the agent would have freely chosen a later, worse sin," or "if God had actualized a world where the driver didn't hit the child, the driver would never have freely come to repentance." These are all treated as perfectly determinate, true-or-false facts about what a free creature would have done in circumstances that never obtained and, in some versions, could never obtain given the agent's actual history.

What confuses me is that they don't look like ordinary counterfactuals. An ordinary counterfactual about a dropped rock is made true, or so I was taught, by the initial conditions plus the laws plus facts about the rock; but a free choice (on a libertarian picture, anyway) can't just be read off the laws and the initial conditions, and the non-actual circumstances aren't there to read anything off of anyway. Sometimes people in these debates say such facts are brute, sometimes they say they're grounded in the creature's character or in God's own nature, and I can't tell whether these are real rival positions or just ways of ending the conversation.

So my question is whether this kind of claim — "this free creature would have freely done X in non-actual circumstances C" — has an established name in that literature, and more importantly, how the debates carve it up: what, if anything, could make such claims true rather than merely useful, how exactly they differ from ordinary counterfactuals, and whether there's a live dispute about an omniscient being knowing them before deciding which world to create. I'm not after a one-word label so much as the distinctions; a bare name wouldn't settle anything.
