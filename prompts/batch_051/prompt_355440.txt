I've been co-running a reading group for the past few months on whether reinforcement-learning agents could suffer, and we've ended up split into two camps that mostly talk past each other. A few of us are ML people and a few are philosophers, so the arguments keep slipping between engineering and metaphysics. The valence camp says a negative reward signal is already a form of negative valence, or at least the closest thing to it: it's the quantity the whole learning algorithm is organized around avoiding, it drives exactly the avoidance learning that pain is supposed to drive, and if functionalism has any bite at all then that's what pain is. The error-signal camp says a reward is just a scalar in an optimization loop, no more a feeling than a negative number in a spreadsheet; to get actual suffering you'd need the right kind of architecture—a self-model, some kind of global workspace, integrated valence processing, a subject in the relevant sense—and a deep policy network steering a robot arm has none of it.

I'd like you to draft the joint statement the two camps' adversarial collaboration should have produced. Please structure it as a numbered agreed-claims section first, then a disagreements table where each side's position is phrased in wording that side would actually sign, then a cruxes section. For each crux, give the concrete case, experiment, or observation both sides agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. The cruxes I can already see coming are: whether valence is a computational role or a physical state; whether learned avoidance without a self-model can amount to something it is like to be that agent, or only a disposition to avoid; and whether the relevant signal is the raw reward or the reward-prediction error. Don't declare a winner, and don't dissolve the disagreement too quickly by saying it's all verbal—I want the residual disagreement made exact.
