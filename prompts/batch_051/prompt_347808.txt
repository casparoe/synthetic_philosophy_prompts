there's a classic line of reasoning in economics I keep running into: if a gamble has positive expected value, you should take it, and if you can take the same gamble over and over, that only strengthens the case, since the good and bad luck will average out and you'll collect the expectation in the long run. I've been reading the ergodicity-economics response to this, and it's clearly got something. the stock example is a coin toss where heads multiply your wealth by 1.5 and tails by 0.6, equal odds. after one toss the expected value is up 5%, so it looks great; but the time-average growth rate of any single player's actual wealth is negative, so if you're fully exposed each round your wealth trends towards zero almost surely, while the average over a big ensemble of parallel players grows without bound. The move is to say expected value is an average across parallel copies of you, but a real person only lives one path through time, and for multiplicative processes the two averages come apart.

What I'm trying to work out is whether the classic argument survives this, or whether it's just dead. specifically: does the ergodicity critique kill the positive-expected-value argument, repair it, or leave it untouched? I half think it kills it for repeated multiplicative bets but leaves one-shot bets alone, and maybe the argument can be saved by saying expected value was never meant as a prediction about what happens to your own wealth over time, only about which lottery you'd pick from behind a veil of ignorance. But i'm not sure that's a rescue rather than a retreat. Which premise does the critique actually bear on, and can that premise be restated so the old argument still does some work? I'm interested in the fate of the argument itself, not in whether people should take some particular bet. And please write the whole answer in British English.
