I'm a software engineer with basically no formal philosophy background, but I've been trying to read more philosophy-adjacent stuff in my spare time. Lately I got sucked into this debate about whether human extinction is bad beyond the deaths it directly involves. Someone pointed me to a blog post summarizing Scheffler's book Death and the Afterlife, and the summary was so confident that I wanted to fact-check the framing, but I genuinely cant tell where the summary is doing the work versus where Scheffler actually signs up for the claim.

Here's the summary as I understood it from the blog. The author said Scheffler's big idea is that most of us are wrong about what actually gives our lives meaning: we think we're afraid of our own death, but what we really depend on is that other people will outlive us. He apparently has a thought experiment where you learn that you'll die painlessly on schedule, but then, thirty days after your death, everyone else will die too in some catastrophe. The question is whether your current projects would still seem worth doing. According to the blog, Scheffler says no: the cancer researcher would stop working because the whole point of a cure is that future people will benefit; an engineer would lose motivation to build things for people who will never use them; even smaller stuff like learning an instrument or planning a garden would sort of go flat. The blog then said Scheffler takes this to show that the continued existence of humanity after us is a required ingredient for anything we do to have any value at all, and that this is also why our own death is less important to us than we think. It ended by saying Scheffler thinks it follows that people who don't care much about long-term environmental collapse are being inconsistent, since they would have to admit that their own current activities get their value from exactly the future people they claim not to care about.

I don't know enough to tell whether this is accurate, a fair simplification, or the blogger putting their thumb on the scale. The jump from "my projects would feel pointless if I knew no one would be around after me" to "the continued existence of humanity is literally required for any value at all" seemed like it could be an overstatement, but maybe Scheffler really does defend exactly that. Can you go through this summary and tell me which parts a careful reader of Scheffler would accept, which parts are subtle misrepresentations or conflations, and whether the final claim about environmental indifference actually follows from the accurate parts?
