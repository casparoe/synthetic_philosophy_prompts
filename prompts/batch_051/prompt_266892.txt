I keep mulling over something about the way adults lie to kids about Santa and the tooth fairy, and I can't find a name for the exact thing, though I suspect someone in philosophy or developmental psychology has carved this terrain already.

The configuration that strikes me as odd is this. The lie isn't a one-off cover-up; it's maintained for years, with props (half-eaten cookies, a note from the tooth fairy, footprints in the snow). But the odd part is the ending. When the kid finally figures it out, the adults mostly treat the discovery as a sign of growing up — like, good for you, you caught on — and nobody acts as if a promise was broken. Then the same kid is often recruited to help keep the deception going for a younger sibling. So the whole thing seems to be a deception whose intended endpoint is not that the child keeps believing, but that the child eventually stops believing and catches the adults at it.

Is there an established name for that — a lie whose successful uptake is not belief but the child's eventual detection of the lie, and where the detection is supposed to increase trust rather than damange it? If there is, I want to know how it's distinguished from an ordinary white lie, a noble lie, and a paternalistic lie. It doesn't feel like a white lie, because a white lie is usually meant to spare someone's feelings and works only if it goes undetected; here the detection is the point. If there isn't a name, I want a definition and some boundary cases: does it still count if the child is genuinely hurt when they find out? Does it count if the adult secretly wanted to keep the child believing and only pretends to celebrate the discovery? Does it stop being the same thing if the child is never told by the parents but finds out from other kids? I'm after the carve, not just the lable.
