A colleague who works on causal inference keeps insisting that the interventionist theory of causation is either circular or a notational variant of the simple counterfactual theory, and that the appearance of a third option in the literature is an illusion. He lays the argument out like this:

(1) Interventionism says a variable X is causally relevant to a variable Y just in case there is a possible intervention on X with respect to Y — setting X to some value while the rest of the system is left to run by its own rules — under which Y would change.

(2) But what counts as an intervention on X with respect to Y is itself spelled out in causal terms: the intervention variable must be an exogenous cause of X; it must override X's other causes for as long as it is active; it must not affect Y through any route that bypasses X; and it must be independent of other causes of Y that do not run through X.

(3) So the notion being defined — causal relevance — shows up inside the definition of the notion doing the defining.

(4) A definition that runs in a circle, with the term being defined used (directly or through an auxiliary term) in stating its own conditions, is circular; the only way to avoid this is to state the conditions in independent, non-causal terms, which is just to offer a reduction of causation.

(5) Interventionists explicitly disavow any reduction of causation to non-causal facts.

(6) Therefore interventionism is circular, and a circular definition is empty: it can neither rule out a candidate cause nor license an inference from an experimental result to a causal conclusion.

(7) Yet the entire advertised point of interventionism is that it earns its keep. It is supposed to explain why randomized trials and A/B tests are probative, and to do better than simple counterfactual theories in hard cases that embarrass them, such as redundant causation and overdetermination.

(8) Since (6) and (7) cannot both be true, the interventionist account is either the simple counterfactual theory with some unanalyzed causal vocabulary bolted on, or it has no explanatory content at all. On neither reading is it a genuine competitor to the counterfactual theory it was introduced to improve.

I would most like to keep (7), and I am also reluctant to give up (2), because the causal character of the intervention concept seems to be exactly what lets interventionism sidestep the problem cases that embarrass simpler counterfactual accounts. But my colleague insists that any response keeping both is special pleading: if the view cannot rule out candidate causes without presupposing the causal facts, then it has not ruled out anything. Which step, if any, should be rejected, what does rejecting it cost, and how much of interventionism survives the diagnosis — in particular, does it retain anything over and above what a simple counterfactual theory could already claim?
