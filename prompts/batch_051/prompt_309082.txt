I come from the LessWrong-ish end of things, so my instinct is to taboo the labels and make people answer concrete questions before they say what camp they're in. I've been running a small discussion group that's half exvangelical and half charismatic, and we keep getting stuck on whether the charismata stopped at the end of the apostolic age or continue today. Somebody threw B. B. Warfield's Counterfeit Miracles (1918) at me, and the first chapter, The Cessation of the Charismata, makes a move I can't shake. He treats the spiritual gifts as something like scaffolding for the founding of the church: their job is to authenticate the apostles and the new revelation, not primarily to meet felt needs. On his account, a miracle proper is different from a special providence or an answer to prayer, so you can't lump them together into "God did something unusual." I've been wondering whether that distinction is the right first branch in a sorting instrument.

Could you build a decision tree for my group that sorts someone from a starting commitment — "the apostolic age was uniquely foundational" — to a named position in the cessationist/continuationist space? The audience is people who know the relevant Bible passages but don't necessarily know the theological labels. Phrase every internal node as a concrete case or claim someone could answer without knowing which leaf it sends them to. Use cases like: a medically documented healing after prayer, a private word from God that later comes true, a church member speaking in tongues without an interpretation, an elder's anointing with oil followed by recovery, a Sunday prophecy that reveals someone's hidden sin. Include these leaves at minimum: hard cessationism, open-but-cautious cessationism, ordinary-providence continuationism, supernatural-gifts continuationism, and revelation-continuationism. Flag any adjacent leaves that your tree can't really separate, and say whether the gap is because the views are the same under the hood or because the tree would need a different question. Dont make it a list of positions with definitions; make it an actual flowchart with yes/no or multiple-choice branches. American English. Last line should be \answer{...} with a one-sentence statement of the single question the tree ends up treating as load-bearing.
