I've been going in circles on what the word "cause" actually picks out, and I'd like someone to sort me out. The two rough rules I've been using are these. First, the counterfactual rule: a caused b when, if a hadn't happened, b wouldn't have happened either. Second, the wiggling rule: a caused b when you can wiggle a while holding everything else fixed, and b wiggles along with it. I can't tell whether these are really two theories or just two ways of stating one idea, but they start to come apart on the cases below.

1. I flip the light switch and the light comes on. Both rules say the flip caused the light. So far so good.

2. A rooster crows every morning just before sunrise. Both rules say the crowing did not cause the sunrise, since the sun would rise anyway and wiggling the crow does not wiggle the sun. Still good.

3. Two people push a stalled car, and either one alone would have been enough to get it rolling. My gut says both pushes caused the car to move. But delete one push and the car still moves, and wiggle one push while the other keeps pushing and the car still moves. So both rules seem to say neither push caused it. That feels like a bug, not a feature.

4. Billy and Suzy each throw a rock at the same bottle. Suzy's rock hits first and shatters it; Billy's rock arrives half a second later and would have done the job if Suzy's hadn't. My gut says Suzy's throw caused the smash and Billy's didn't. But if Suzy hadn't thrown, Billy's rock would have smashed the bottle, so the counterfactual rule says Suzy's throw wasn't a cause. And if you wiggle Suzy's throw while holding Billy's throw fixed, the bottle still smashes, so the wiggling rule seems to say the same thing. Both rules refuse to pick out Suzy's throw as the cause.

5. I don't water my houseplant for two weeks and it dies. If I had watered it, it would have lived, so the counterfactual rule says the not-watering caused the death. But "not watering" isn't something I did, it's something I failed to do, and I can't figure out what it would even mean to "wiggle" an absence. So the wiggling rule might not even apply, while the counterfactual rule handles it cleanly.

6. A patient on a ventilator is dying anyway; a visitor sneaks in and switches the machine off, and the patient dies an hour later. The doctors say the patient would have died within a day regardless. If the ventilator had stayed on, the patient would still have died that day, so the counterfactual rule says switching it off didn't cause the death. That verdict seems to let the visitor off the hook in a way that can't be right. The wiggling rule, as far as I can make sense of it, gives the same answer if you hold the underlying illness fixed.

So my ask is: give me a working definition of "cause" — or tell me straight that the word is doing two jobs, and give me the two definitions plus which cases switch between them. Then run your definition through each of the six cases, flag any case where it gives a verdict you'd be uncomfortable defending, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdict. If both my rules fail, I'd rather you fix one of them then tell me the word is meaningless.
