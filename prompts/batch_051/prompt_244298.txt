I recently read a chapter in an introductory philosophy survey that gives an account of Richard Rorty's antirepresentationalism, and I want to know how much of it to trust. The author's account runs roughly as follows. Rorty holds that beliefs and sentences do not picture or mirror an independent world, because we could never step outside our own vocabulary to compare it with reality as it is in itself. From this, the author says, Rorty concludes that no vocabulary gets reality more nearly right than any other, so the only standard left for deciding what to believe is agreement with the community one is addressing. Truth, on this reading, is nothing more than such agreement: to call a belief true is just to pay it a compliment in the currency of current consensus. Inquiry therefore aims at solidarity with our fellow humans rather than at getting the world right. The author adds that Rorty was untroubled by the charge of relativism and in fact accepted it, since without any way to compare beliefs with unconceptualized reality there are no facts that could make one belief better than another.

I am inclined to think this slides from a modest epistemic point to a very strong conclusion, but I do not know Rorty well enough to be sure. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what Rorty explicitly denied? I also want to know whether the final claim — that Rorty's position is a form of relativism — survives once the distortions are corrected.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
