I have spent enough years reading the alignment and rationality literature to have settled, provisionally, on a picture of what careful thought is: deliberation is well-formed only insofar as it factors into many small steps, each small enough to be inspected and checked on its own, each taking explicit inputs and producing explicit outputs, and each composed so that the whole can be audited from the leaves up. The limiting case is HCH—Humans consulting HCH—a finite but astronomically large tree in which a human answers a question by delegating sub-questions to copies of the same arrangement, recursing until every leaf is a task one human can do reliably without further decomposition. I do not claim HCH is a practical system or a blueprint for safe AI; I claim it is the right idealization of deliberation, the model against which actual reasoning, human or machine, should be measured, with unfactored judgment treated as an approximation to a factored process or as an error.

What would count against this? I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, ordered by how decisive they are, together with an honest assessment of how much risk the view actually carries. Which empirical or formal results would be most damaging—for example, demonstrations that information essential to a task is systematically lost at factor boundaries, that human sub-question answering degrades in regular and predictable ways under decomposition, that well-factored processes underperform holistic ones on tasks whose difficulty consists in holding many things in view at once, or that the human at each node of HCH is doing un-factored work the construction itself silently relies on? Distinguish the results that would be decisive from those that would merely be awkward. What would defenders of the view themselves concede counts against it, as distinct from the objections critics raise? What does the position predict that its main rival—call it the holist or gestalt picture, on which deliberation is constitutively context-sensitive and resistant to decomposition—does not, and what does the rival handle naturally that the factored view can accommodate only with strain? Finally, tell me whether the view as I have stated it has been insulated into unfalsifiability: if a defender replies to every failed factorization by saying that this only shows we have not found the right factorization yet, has the view stopped risking anything, and is that a defect in an idealization of this kind or the wrong charge to bring against a regulative ideal? Personally, I would find it most persuasive if someone exhibited a concrete piece of reasoning whose best available expression could not be factored without changing the answer—not merely a case where current systems cannot do it, but a case where the factored description is false to what the reasoning was, rather than merely hard to produce. I want to know if the idealization is incomplete or actually misleading.
