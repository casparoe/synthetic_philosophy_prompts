Here is the exam question:

Exam question: Kant's universalizability test is often treated as the ancestor of Hare's universal prescriptivism and Singer's generalization argument. Explain how Kant's test operates, and then assess whether the move from Kant to Hare and Singer preserves the logical force of the what-if-everyone-did-that question, or whether that question changes in an important way.

Here is the student's answer:

Kant's universalizability test asks whether the maxim of your action can be conceived and also willed as a universal law of nature. In the lying promise case, Kant argues that the maxim cannot even be conceived as a universal law, because if everyone made false promises when they needed money, the institution of promising would collapse and no one would believe a promise. In the beneficence case, the maxim can be conceived but not willed: you can imagine a world where no one helps anyone, but a rational finite being cannot will such a world because they must will that others help them if they need it. So the test has two parts, contradiction in conception and contradiction in the will, and the lying promise fails the first while the failure-to-help maxim fails the second.

Hare's universal prescriptivism extends Kant by holding that moral judgments are prescriptions the speaker is committed to universalizing across all relevantly similar situations. Hare shows that this formal requirement leads directly to utilitarianism: once you universalize a prescription, you must take into account everyone's preferences equally, and that just is preference-satisfaction utilitarianism. So Kant's test, properly understood, turns out to be a version of utilitarianism in disguise.

Singer's generalization argument is basically a modern restatement of the same idea. He says: if everyone did X, the consequences would be bad, so X is wrong. That is exactly Kant's question what if everyone did that, but applied to consequences instead of to the consistency of a maxim. The only real difference is that Singer drops Kant's metaphysics of the kingdom of ends.

So all three share the same logical force. They all ask whether an action could become a general practice without producing something impossible or unwanted. The differences are mostly presentational: Hare and Singer make explicit the utilitarian consequences that Kant already implies.
