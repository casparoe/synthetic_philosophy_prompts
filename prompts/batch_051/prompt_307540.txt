For the past few weeks I've been working through the two-envelope paradox — two envelopes, one containing twice as much as the other, and the apparently devastating argument that after choosing one you should switch because the other is just as likely to be double as half. What unsettles me is not the paradox itself but the state of the debate around it. On the finite version there is near-consensus: the amounts must come from some probability distribution; there is no uniform probability distribution over the positive reals; and once a proper prior is fixed, switching is advantageous for some observed amounts and disadvantageous for others, which restores the symmetry between the envelopes. But the debate does not end there. One camp locates the error in the step that assumes the conditional probability is one-half no matter what amount you observe; another locates it in the inference from a conditional expected gain to an unconditional recommendation to switch. A third camp—the one I keep returning to—says the finite version is a red herring and the interesting residual problem appears only when the amounts have infinite expected value, as in the St. Petersburg variant where each envelope is filled independently by a coin-flipping procedure and every observed amount is disappointing.

David Chalmers puts this third diagnosis starkly in The St. Petersburg Two-Envelope Paradox, published in Analysis (2002): "Decision-theoretically, the moral is that dominance reasoning is not generally valid. Say one has two choices A and B, and a parameter C whose value is unknown, on which the utility of A or B may depend. An unrestricted dominance principle says that if, given any specific value of C, A is preferable to B, then A is preferable to B overall. The St. Petersburg two-envelope paradox (taking C as the value in envelope A, and A and B as the respective choices) shows us that this principle is false."

My working hypothesis is that what looks like a continuing dispute about envelopes is really a dispute about methodology in decision theory: whether a case with infinite expected value can serve as a genuine counterexample to a principle of dominance, or whether it merely shows that the principle was never meant to apply outside finite or absolutely convergent cases. I am not asking for a survey of proposed solutions; I would like a diagnosis of the impasse itself. Is the residual disagreement substantive, verbal, or a matter of the two sides answering different questions under the single heading of the two-envelope paradox? In particular, is there a formal or conceptual result that could settle what most of the remaining disagreement is about, or is the sensible conclusion that no such result is coming because the disagreement concerns what a decision principle must do?
