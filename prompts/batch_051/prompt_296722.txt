I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about authorial intention and the meaning of a text, going by my gut on a batch of concrete cases, and I want you to find the principle that captures my verdicts or tell me which verdicts have to go. Here are the cases.

1. A novel says that a character's jaw literally hit the floor, but the scene is otherwise realistic and nothing physical happens. The novelist's private diary says the sentence was meant literally, as a small surreal intrusion. I read it as a figure of speech, and I don't think the diary changes what the sentence means. Verdict: figurative; the diary is irrelevant.

2. An aunt writes to her nephew, I am not angry about the will. The nephew knows from long experience that she adopts this stilted, formal register only when she is furious, and that she never uses the word furious about herself. I take the letter to mean that she is angry, whatever the literal words say. Verdict: the letter means she is angry.

3. A city ordinance says, No vehicles in the park. A person using a motorized wheelchair is cited. I think the citation is wrong. Verdict: the wheelchair is permitted, because the drafters were aiming at activity and harm, not at every mechanical conveyance, and that purpose is part of the text's meaning.

4. A constitutional provision forbids cruel and unusual punishments. I think it covers some practices, like prolonged solitary confinement of juveniles, that the ratifiers would not have considered cruel. Verdict: the provision is not fixed by the framers' specific expectations.

5. A poet writes, The air bites. Years later she says in an interview that the cold was meant to stand for sexual betrayal. I don't think the poem is about betrayal, even though she says so. Verdict: the later statement does not make betrayal part of the poem's meaning.

6. A colleague replies to a plan with, Fine, do whatever you want. I read this as a passive-aggressive dismissal, not as genuine permission, and I think the dismissive force is part of what the message means. Verdict: the tone is part of the meaning, not decoration.

7. An ancient text survives in a copy where one word breaks the syntax and the meter, while a later copy preserves a word that fits both and is supported by a quotation in a third author. Editors print the word that must have been intended. I think they are right. Verdict: the reconstructed intended wording is the text, not the scribal slip.

8. A published novel says, He went out to check the form. Every other detail in the chapter concerns a farm, and the sentence would make no sense with form. I take the text to mean farm, and I don't think the typo itself is meaning. Verdict: the intended word controls.

Part of what is driving me is Locke's observation, in the Essay Concerning Human Understanding in the chapter on the signification of words, where he notices that although words can signify only the ideas in the speaker's mind, men in practice give them a hidden reference to two further things: to the ideas they suppose other men attach to the same words, and to the reality of the things themselves. That seems to capture exactly the tension I feel between meaning as private mental content and meaning as a public instrument, but it doesn't yet tell me which side wins in my cases.

Is there a principle, probably not a single simple rule, that gets all or almost all of my verdicts? State it precisely, with the case or consideration motivating each clause. Then apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on: a novelist dies leaving a finished, unpublished book. The typescript has two handwritten changes in the margins; one replaces a word with a synonym, the other cuts a sentence the author had second thoughts about. The posthumous edition keeps the original typed wording and the cut sentence, reasoning that those are what the author actually left as the continuous text, while the editor argues that the handwritten changes are the last expression of intent and should be read as the text. What does your principle say is the meaning of those two passages, and does that force me to revise any of my verdicts above?
