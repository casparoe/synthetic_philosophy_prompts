I've spent the last year moderating an adversarial collaboration between two people in my department who work on delusions, and they've reached the point where they mostly talk past each other. They both accept the two-factor theory — roughly, that a delusion needs both an anomalous experience (Factor 1) and a deficit in belief evaluation (Factor 2) — and they agree on the data: monothematic delusions like Capgras and Cotard, double-bookkeeping, circumscription, the jumping-to-conclusions findings. But one of them is a doxasticist: she thinks delusions are beliefs, just badly formed ones, continuous with ordinary false beliefs and produced by the same machinery. The other is an anti-doxasticist: he thinks the two-factor theory actually pulls the other way, because if Factor 2 is a broken belief-formation system, then the resulting state doesn't have the functional profile beliefs are suppose to have, so calling it a belief is a category error. What they can't settle is whether this is a genuine disagreement or a fight over the word belief.

Write the joint statement this collaboration should have produced. Format it as: a numbered agreed-claims section (claims both can sign, about the data, about what the two factors do and don't explain, and about what beliefs are functionally); then a disagreements table giving each side's preferred wording of its own view; then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — with, for each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence both accept as bearing on it and each side's prediction recorded in advance. If you conclude that part of their dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why. End the document with a single sentence beginning Bottom line: that states in one sentence what the residual disagreement really is.
