One proposal for reconciling autonomous weapons with the principle of meaningful human control — understood here as the demand that a human being, rather than a machine, make the effective decision to use lethal force — runs as follows. No autonomous weapon system may lawfully strike unless it first opens a twenty-second veto window. During that window, the system's assessment of the target, the underlying sensor evidence, and the predicted effects are simultaneously presented to every human operator on duty in the relevant theatre. Any one of those operators may abort the strike with a single stop command; the command requires no authorization from a superior and no stated justification, but every stop and every failure to stop is recorded. If the window closes without a stop command, the system fires. The scheme's distinctive feature is that force proceeds by default: the machine acts unless a human affirmatively intervenes, so each operator's inaction is as decisive as an action. Analyze this as a mechanism rather than as a moral position. What would it incentivize among operators, commanders, designers, and adversaries? How would each group game it? Where does it break down, and do the failure modes indicate something general about whether a veto window can deliver meaningful human control rather than merely distributing the opportunity to interrupt it?
