There's an asymmetry in how I treat time that I've never managed to justify, and I want to know whether it's defensable or just a cognitive artifact. I treat the past as fixed and the future as open. If yesterday's flight was delayed, I shrug and say "what's done is done"; if next week's flight will be delayed, I start replanning immediately. We punish people for crimes they committed, but the idea of punishing someone for a crime we're quite sure they're about to commit feels monstrous—we call it pre-crime. We regret past choices in a way we don't regret future ones, even when we can see ourselves walking into the same mistake. All of this leans on a picture where the past is settled and the future isn't.

But special relativity seems to undercut exactly that picture. The Rietdijk–Putnam line goes roughly like this: pick a moment on your worldline and call it "now." Because simultaneity is frame-dependent, a distant event that your frame says is still in your future, but which is spacelike separated from you, is simultaneous with your "now" in some other inertial frame. If being simultaneous with a real event is enough for an event to be real, and whether an event is real can't depend on which frame you happen to be using, then at least part of what you were calling "the future" is already real. Run the same move from that event's perspective, and from enough other perspectives, and the whole four-dimensional block looks equally real—past, present, and future all just there. The future would then be no more open than the past; we're just embedded in the block with memories pointing one way and deliberations pointing the other.

So the ordinary asymmetry—past closed, future open—would be doing an enormous amount of work (blame, punishment, regret, relief, grief, planning) on top of a distinction that physics doesn't treat as objective. Here's what I want from you: give me the strongest possible defense of our asymmetric treatment of past and future that can survive the block-universe argument. Maybe there's a principle—epistemic, practical, or moral—that justifies treating the settled past differently from the open future even if both are real. Maybe not. If the asymmetry can't be justified, tell me whether we should actually change any of our practices or just accept being quietly inconsistent. I want the strongest case for the "open future" left standing after special relativity, and an honest verdict on whether you'd keep the asymmetry anyway.
