I want to teach Hume's "Of Miracles" alongside William Friedkin's The Exorcist and use the film to test whether people actually apply Hume's probability calculus when they accept a supernatural claim. Here is the passage from Section X of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding that I want the reading built around:

"All probability, then, supposes an opposition of experiments and observations, where the one side is found to overbalance the other, and to produce a degree of evidence, proportioned to the superiority. A hundred instances or experiments on one side, and fifty on another, afford a doubtful expectation of any event; though a hundred uniform experiments, with only one that is contradictory, reasonably beget a pretty strong degree of assurance. In all cases, we must balance the opposite experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the smaller number from the greater, in order to know the exact force of the superior evidence."

Read the film's central investigative sequence through this passage. Father Karras begins by seeking a natural explanation, so the film seems to promise exactly the weighing of opposite experiments Hume describes. Trace where the film honors that procedure and where it shifts to a different standard, such as treating the failure of natural explanations as positive evidence for the extraordinary one, or letting understandable human sympathy do the evidential work. Does the film ultimately vindicate Hume's worry that belief in the miraculous wins only by stacking the scales, or does it present a case that meets Hume's own standard? Be fair to both the film and Hume, and end by saying what the film adds to the argument that the essay alone does not.
