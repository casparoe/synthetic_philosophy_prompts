A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether the world is becoming less violent, and by the end he had produced an argument I could not answer, though its conclusion strikes me as wrong. We had already granted, for the period we were looking at, that the rate of violent death per hundred thousand people has fallen while the total number of violent deaths has risen, chiefly because the global population has grown. His argument went like this. (1) To say the world is becoming less violent is to say that there is less violence in the world than before. (2) There are two equally legitimate ways of saying how much violence there is: the total number of violent deaths, and the number of violent deaths per hundred thousand people. (3) These two measures can diverge, and over the period in question they did: one fell while the other rose. (4) If two equally legitimate measures of how much violence there is point in opposite directions, then there is no non-arbitrary fact about whether the amount of violence has decreased; which answer one gets is an artifact of the measure one chooses. (5) Therefore there is no fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent, only the two measure-relative truths that it is becoming less violent per capita and more violent in total.

I half-suspect a trick, but everything I tried to say against the argument sounded as if I were merely refusing to accept the conclusion. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and under what reading does it fail? And what would giving up that step commit me to: does it restore a fact of the matter, or does it instead show that the original question was ambiguous between two questions, each of which has its own fact of the matter? If the whole dispute is itself misconceived, say so plainly rather than answering it on its own terms. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the question.
