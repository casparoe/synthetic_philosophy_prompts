I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of cognitive science, working on how classical somatic theories of emotion should handle cases in which bodily expression is artificially dissociated from reported feeling. My question turns on a passage from William James’s 1884 article “What is an Emotion?”. James writes:

“Our natural way of thinking about these standard emotions is that the mental perception of some fact excites the mental affection called the emotion, and that this latter state of mind gives rise to the bodily expression. My thesis on the contrary is that the bodily changes follow directly the PERCEPTION of the exciting fact, and that our feeling of the same changes as they occur IS the emotion. Common sense says, we lose our fortune, are sorry and weep; we meet a bear, are frightened and run; we are insulted by a rival, are angry and strike. The hypothesis here to be defended says that this order of sequence is incorrect, that the one mental state is not immediately induced by the other, that the bodily manifestations must first be interposed between, and that the more rational statement is that we feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble, and not that we cry, strike, or tremble, because we are sorry, angry, or fearful, as the case may be.”

I want to know how this passage applies to a case James does not consider. Imagine a research participant who has been given a reversible drug that prevents all voluntary skeletal-muscle movement, including facial expression, but leaves her autonomic responses intact. She is shown a live spider she knows is harmless but strongly fears. Her heart accelerates, her palms sweat, and she reports afterward that she felt genuine fear, though she describes it as strangely “cool” and bodiless. Does James’s passage, read strictly, require us to say that she underwent the emotion of fear? Does it require us to say only that the autonomic changes she felt were the fear, so that the absent facial and postural components simply were not part of that episode? Or does the theory make no room for the thought that the episode could have been fuller if the skeletal expression had occurred? Please work through the passage itself first, then consider what James would have said if asked whether skeletal expression is a necessary component of fear, and finally say whether textual, functional, and counterfactual-James approaches agree or diverge here.
