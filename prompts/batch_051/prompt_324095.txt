I am a PhD candidate in philosophy working on collective epistemology and the ethics of high-stakes unilateral action, and next term I am presenting a paper to a research group composed mostly of decision theorists and formal epistemologists, plus a few political philosophers who work on legitimacy and consent. The thesis is that, in domains where a single actor can unilaterally take a decision that binds or burdens everyone, and where the actors share broadly common values but hold private, noisy estimates of whether the intervention is net-positive — paradigm cases being unilateral geoengineering, release of dual-use models, or public whistleblowing — unilateral permission is very hard to justify, and the default should shift to a collective constraint; a question I am still settling is whether a consultation requirement is enough or whether the argument pushes toward something closer to consensus. The argument, in outline, is this: when any member may act on a private estimate, the probability that someone acts tracks the most optimistic tail of the group's estimates, so unilateralism lets the group's most over-optimistic outlier decide for everyone; if the group's aggregate judgment would reject the act, this imposes a decision on all that no ordinary aggregation would endorse; hence the permission structure itself, not the underlying values, is doing the forbidden work. Prepare me for the question period: what objections will this audience raise, in their own terms, which objections have genuine force, what should I concede in the framing of the talk, and which reply has to land for the thesis to survive? Order the objections by how likely and how damaging they are, and include the objection I should most hope no one asks.
