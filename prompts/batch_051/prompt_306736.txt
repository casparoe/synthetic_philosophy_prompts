1. The categorical imperative — roughly, only do something if you can will the rule behind it as a law everyone followed — is the best test I've found for whether an action is morally okay.
2. Making a false promise to get money you need is wrong because you can't even conceive everybody doing it: if everyone made promises they didn't intend to keep, being taken at your word would disappear, so the act itself would stop being possible. That's the clearest case to me.
3. Refusing to ever help anyone in need is also wrong, but in a different way: a world where nobody ever helps is thinkable, but I can't will it, because then on the day I'm the one in need I'd have willed a world where I get left in the lurch. So it fails a second sort of test, not the impossibility one.
4. The test has to be formal — it shouldn't depend on a prior picture of what's good, only on consistency. Otherwise it's not really Kant's test anymore, it's consequences wearing a different costume.
5. You can't game the test by wording your plan cleverly: turning "never help anyone" into "never help people born on a Tuesday" is cheating. The rule behind the act has to honestly say what you're actually doing.
6. But I also think "I'll play tennis every Sunday at 10am because that's when the courts are free" has to count as fine, even though if literally everyone had that rule the courts would be mobbed and nobody could play. That's a volume problem, not a moral problem.
