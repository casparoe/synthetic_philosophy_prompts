I sit on my hospital's ethics committee, and we are reviewing an incident from last month. A woman was admitted to our psychiatric unit after a serious overdose; she had told the admitting staff that her ex-partner had been violent with her in the past and that absolutely no information should be given to him. He appeared at the front desk and asked whether she was a patient and what room she was in. The clerk, knowing the patient's request and the safety alert on the file, told him she was not here. He left, later learned she was being treated, and has filed a complaint. The patient and her advocate say the lie protected her, the safety team says it may have prevented a confrontation, and the compliance office says our privacy policy forbids releasing patient information to visitors but is silent on whether an affirmative false statement is permitted. Some members want to discipline the clerk; others want to keep the present silence; others want a formal policy endorsing this sort of protective deception.

I've been rereading Kant's essay on the supposed right to lie from benevolent motives. The part that stays with me, in my own words, is this: if you lie and thereby frustrate someone's action, you can be held responsible for all the bad consequences that follow, however unforeseen, whereas if you stick to the truth you are not answerable to public justice for whatever results. The committee chair takes this to settle the matter against the clerk. I am not so sure, because the clerk was not acting as a private person answering a question but as an employee occupying a role whose whole point is to keep certain facts confidential; maybe the maxim, not the utterance, is what matters.

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action: discipline the clerk, clear her, or clear her and adopt a policy, or some more specific combination. If you recommend a policy, it should give front-desk staff criteria they can actually apply under time pressure, not just a general principle. Engage the strongest consideration on the losing side, especially the Kantian worry that institutionalizing benevolent deception is not a small exception to a principle but the erosion of the condition under which any trust in speech is possible. And please say how confident you are in your recommendation and identify the point in your reasoning you are least sure of.
