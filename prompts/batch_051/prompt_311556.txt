I am the managing editor of a generalist mathematics journal, and two submissions this year have made it clear that we have no real policy for computer-assisted or unsurveyable proofs. One paper claims a classification theorem whose human argument is short but whose finite case analysis was done with a custom program that only the senior author has used; the other is a machine-checked result accompanied by a proof certificate, but none of our regular referees can tell whether the formalized statement actually expresses the theorem announced in the abstract. The board keeps talking past one another: some members insist that a proof must be surveyable by a single competent reader, others reply that a checkable certificate is better evidence than a tired referee's reading, and everyone points to the four-colour theorem without landing on a policy.

I would like you to turn whatever the philosophy of mathematical practice has established about surveyability, formal verification, and trust in computation into a procedure we can actually run. Please write it as a checklist, or a flowchart in words: the questions to ask about a submitted proof in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure returns a rejection; where it triggers additional review, an external audit, or a request for a certificate; and where it must stop and hand the decision to an editor because the question is a judgment of values rather than a technical matter. Assume a generalist editorial board of working mathematicians, not automated-reasoning specialists, handling perhaps half a dozen such submissions a year, with limited referee time and a serious reputational stake in each decision. Be explicit about which steps are genuine tests and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical submissions. First, a large graph-theoretic proof whose certificate checks in a widely used proof assistant, but whose formalized statement is slightly stronger than the theorem the abstract announces. Second, a numerical optimality proof produced by a custom optimizer that has no certificate and has not been independently reimplemented. Third, a finite case analysis with no certificate, but whose code was independently reimplemented by another group and produces identical output logs. Arrange the cases so that at least one produces an answer the editors will not welcome, and close by saying plainly what the procedure cannot decide even when it is followed in good faith.
