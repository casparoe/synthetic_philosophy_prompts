I am a novelist, and a character in my current book—a retired truck driver whose worn brake line caused a fatal accident—keeps returning to the sentence "You can't be blamed for what you can't control." He uses it first to refuse the guilt his family presses on him, and later to argue that the practice of punishment rests on a mistake. I want the novel to treat the line fairly rather than letting it function as either folksy wisdom or obvious self-exculpation. I have read enough around the moral luck debate to know that the sentence sits uncomfortably next to cases in which a careless driver who harms no one is barely blamed while an equally careless driver who happens to kill someone is blamed catastrophically, but I do not trust my own ear for where exactly the sentence goes wrong, or whether it goes wrong.

Could you take the sentence apart? Lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making—about which kind of evaluation it concerns (blame, punishment, criminal liability, moral assessment), what sense of "can" or "control" it needs (physical possibility, foreseeable avoidability, alternative possibilities, lifelong control over one's own character), and what counts as "outside one's control" (the failed brake line? the habit of deferring maintenance? the temperament behind the habit? the circumstances that put him on that road at that hour?). For each reading, say whether it is a near-trivial platitude, a substantive and defensible principle, or something too strong to be plausible—perhaps actually false—and say which reading is the one an ordinary speaker probably means. Then tell me which reading actually does the work the character asks of it, and which readings would fail to support his conclusion that punishment rests on a mistake, so that I can write another character who pushes back in a philosophically literate way. Please answer in American English.
