I am a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science and the graduate representative on my university's Institutional Review Board for human subjects research. The IRB is currently considering a neuroscience protocol that would keep a fully informed volunteer's brain alive and functioning outside the skull for up to 72 hours while the brain, connected by a high-bandwidth wireless interface, controls a mobile robotic body located in another building. The volunteer's biological body would remain in a medically induced coma under supervision. The researchers propose to identify the research participant for consent, withdrawal, and incident-reporting purposes as "the brain-body system," but they ask that legally binding decisions be made by the volunteer's spouse, who would remain at the biological body's bedside. In discussion, several members have invoked Daniel Dennett's essay "Where Am I?" — for instance, the part where the narrator's brain is kept in a vat and his body moves around under radio control, and his point of view seems to be located at the vat when he looks through the body's eyes and sees his own brain, leading him to treat himself as wherever he seems to be. Others on the committee find that reference more playful than decisive.

Draft the IRB's decision memo for this protocol. The memo must have a clear bottom line: approve, reject, or approve with conditions. It should specify how the participant is to be identified for purposes of consent, withdrawal, and reporting adverse events, and it should defend that identification by taking a reasoned position on whether the person is located with the brain, with the biological body, distributed across the system, or in none of these. The memo should also present the strongest consideration on the losing side in a way its proponents would recognize as fair, and explain why the committee nonetheless did not adopt it. The document should be written so that the research team can actually respond to it.
