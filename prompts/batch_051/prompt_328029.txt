I am a high school student, and I serve as the secretary of my school's environmental action committee. Several weeks ago, one of our members proposed that the committee send an open letter, under the committee's name, to the school board and the local newspaper criticizing the school's refusal to phase out single-use plastics in the cafeteria. A number of us objected that the full committee had not approved the letter and that sending it on everyone's behalf was a decision the whole group would have to live with. He brushed the objection aside with the saying, "Better to ask forgiveness than permission." The discussion stalled there, and the letter has not been sent, but the exchange has been bothering me ever since.

I understand the appeal of the saying in ordinary cases. Asking permission can invite stalling, compromise, or a flat rejection before the merits are considered, while forgiveness can be a way of repairing things after the fact; there is a sense in which the phrase states a realistic piece of advice about which of the two conversations is easier. But in our situation the saying seems to be doing much more work than that. Any single member could send the letter, it would be effectively impossible to unsend once the school board or the local paper had it, and the rest of us would share the consequences. I later came across the idea known as the unilateralist's curse—roughly, that when any member of a group can act alone with consequences for everyone, the members with the most optimistic view of the action are the most likely to act, which can be dangerous even when most members oppose the action. That idea seems relevant here, but I am not sure exactly how.

I would like you to lay out the different precise claims that "better to ask forgiveness than permission" could be making, from the trivial to the radical, and to say for each whether it is plausible and whether it would actually support my fellow member's conclusion that he was entitled to send the letter on the committee's behalf. Please also indicate which readings interact badly with the unilateralist's curse and which still seem defensible in ordinary, low-stakes decisions where one person acts alone and bears most of the consequences. Write your answer in continuous prose, as though you were writing a letter or an email, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
