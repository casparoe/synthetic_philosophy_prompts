I've been arguing with a friend about whether "meme" is anything more than a metaphor, and somewhere in that argument I ended up inventing a distinction I'd like someone to take apart. I've been calling it lineage replication versus convergence. A cultural item does lineage replication when a new token exists because someone copied an earlier token, and the resemblance between the two is explained by that copying rather than by two people independently landing on the same thing because it's useful, easy, or obvious. Convergence is the opposite: people reinvent the same variant without copying a parent token, the way handshakes or a lot of folk-song phrases show up in different places because human bodies and situations push toward them. My claim is that most of the classic examples people call memes — recipes, religious ideas, jokes, melodies — are mostly convergence, so for those "meme" really is just a metaphor. But internet memes look different: an image macro actually descends with modification from earlier image macros, you can trace the edits the way you'd trace a manuscript stemma, and the variation is mostly blind-ish tinkering rather than deliberate redesign. So I think the word became literal right around when the internet made copying cheap — not because things spread faster, but because for the first time culture had visible parent-offspring relationships instead of everyone reinventing the same thing.

Is this a real distinction, or am I smuggling in demands (high fidelity? undirected variation?) that Darwinian selection never actually had? Has something like this already been worked out under another name by the people who think memetics failed? And where does it break: what would fit my definition of lineage replication while obviously not evolving, or clearly be evolving while failing the definition?
