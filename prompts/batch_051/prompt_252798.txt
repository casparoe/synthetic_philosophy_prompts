I'm a PhD student working on decision theory, and a friend whos really into cryonics walked me through this argument and I can't find the flaw.

(1) You should accept a bet whenever the expected value is clearly positive and the downside is bearable.
(2) Cryonics is such a bet: the annual fee is modest, losing the money is bearable, and the upside, if revival works, is centuries of extra life; since extra life is so valuable, even a fairly small probability of revival makes the expected value enormous.
(3) So you should sign up for cryonics.

Then he extended it. (4) By the same reasoning, if a stranger offers me, for the same fee, a one-in-a-trillion chance of being revived from a backup they claim to maintain, I should accept — as long as I give the claim any probability above zero, they can describe a payoff big enough that the expected value is still huge. (5) And since I can't rule the claim out with certainty, I can't assign it zero, so (1) commits me to paying anyone with a good story. That's a Pascal's mugging: you'd be exploitable by every con artist with an imagination. (6) So premise (1), or the move from (2)-(3) to (4)-(5), has to go.

I'd really like to keep the cryonics conclusion, and I don't want a rule like "ignore probabilities below some cutoff," since that also rules out risky longshots (startups, rare medical treatments) I think are sometimes worth taking. Where does this argument go wrong, and what do I have to give up if I reject it?
