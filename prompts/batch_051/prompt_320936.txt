I'm a retired physicist — spent most of my career in statistical mechanics, then some soft-matter stuff — and I've recently got into philosophy of technology, mostly the algorithmic fairness literature. The impossibility result I keep chewing on is the one that says, roughly, that when two groups have different base rates, a risk score can't be both calibrated and have the same false-positive and false-negative rates across groups. (By calibrated I mean that among people with the same score the probability of the outcome is about the same in each group; by equal error rates I mean the score is about equally likely to be wrong in each direction in each group.) My brain insists on reading this as a kind of conservation law or uncertainty relation, and I'd like you to take that seriously but also check me on it.

Please restate that result in the vocabulary I actually think in: phase spaces, constraints, conserved quantities, equations of state, trade-off relations of the uncertainty-principle sort. Do it as faithfully as you can, then give me the residue report: which parts of the theorem translate cleanly, which parts you had to fudge, and which parts have no counterpart at all. What I most want to know is whether the result is nearer to a conservation law (something that follows from the structure of the quantities, with no ethical input needed) or to an uncertainty relation (two desirable properties you can't jointly pin down), or whether both analogies mislead.

One more specific thing. In statistical mechanics we're careful to distinguish a hard constraint from a constitutive relation, and to distinguish something impossible in principle from something that's just a bad trade-off once you've chosen a loss function. Does the fairness result respect those distinctions, or does it blur them? And where does the normative content go in the translation — the claim that some particular point on the curve is the fair one? I suspect it has no counterpart in the physics vocabulary, but I'd like that shown rather than asserted.

If I've got the theorem wrong, or if asking for this translation is itself a category mistake, tell me straight rather than humouring me. And where you're unsure whether the analogy carries, say so instead of stretching it.
