I spent most of my career in the physical sciences, and I have only recently begun reading philosophy of physics seriously. I keep finding that debates about the direction of time and the thermodynamic arrow are conducted one proposal at a time: someone argues that the second law is the whole story, someone else points to a low-entropy past boundary, someone else to the cosmological expansion, someone else to the asymmetry of intervention. Before I read any further, I want the full specification of what a theory in this area must get right, where "adequate" means good enough to make the disagreement between statistical, cosmological, and interventionist approaches precise rather than merely to reproduce textbook thermodynamics.

You are a professional philosopher of physics with expertise in the foundations of statistical mechanics and temporal asymmetry. Take your time and think through the problem carefully; if it helps, begin by brainstorming the phenomena any serious theory must respect, and only then organise them into a list of requirements.

Write the desiderata for an adequate theory of the direction of time. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp (for example, those who want the arrow to be fully objective, fully cosmological, or broadly agent-relative).

Your list should at least take a position on the following, but do not treat my items as exhaustive; add anything I have missed and justify it:

- The thermal asymmetry itself. Isolated systems move toward equilibrium rather than away from it. The theory must say whether this is a strict law or a statistical regularity, and under what notion of typicality or coarse-graining, without smuggling the arrow in through the choice of measure or through temporal language in the description of initial conditions.
- The relation to microdynamics. The fundamental interactions are not exactly time-symmetric once weak-interaction T violation is included, so a theory may not simply assume full T-symmetry of the underlying laws. It must explain why that microscopic T violation is not the source of the macroscopic arrow, while remaining consistent with it.
- The boundary-condition problem. Whether the arrow requires a low-entropy past condition, whether that condition is best read as a law, a contingent matter of fact, or a constraint on explanation, and what the theory predicts if the universe's entropy were ever to begin decreasing.
- The derivative arrows. Records, memory, and the psychological arrow; the asymmetry of intervention whereby we can affect the future but not the past; the radiative arrow expressed in retarded rather than advanced solutions; and the causal arrow of cause before effect. The theory must either derive each of these from something more basic or argue that it is sui generis, and it must not quietly presuppose one of them in explaining another.
- The alignment problem. Why the various arrows point the same way, if they do, and whether there could be a world whose records open toward the past while its entropy gradient points the other way.
- Local entropy decrease. Refrigerators, organisms, and self-organising systems lower entropy locally. The theory must permit this without surrendering the global asymmetry, and it should address fluctuation theorems, recurrence, and Boltzmann-brain-style worries to the extent they are relevant.
- The question of what is being explained. The theory must declare whether its target is a moving "now" and the passage of time, or only an asymmetry of processes within a block universe, and it must not slide between the two.
- The question of objectivity. Whether the arrow is a feature of the world, of an epistemic standpoint, or of interventions by agents; and if the latter, how to avoid making the thermodynamic arrow depend on agents whose own existence seems to presuppose it.

Then do two further things. First, flag any pair or subset of these desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say whether the tension is real or merely apparent; that is the part I most want to see. Second, identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude otherwise plausible theories. You may briefly indicate how the main contenders fare, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves.
