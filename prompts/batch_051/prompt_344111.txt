I am a sixth-form student preparing a fifteen-minute talk for my school's Economics and Politics Society on Sen's liberal paradox, and I would like a question-and-answer preparation sheet for the discussion period afterwards.

My thesis is this: the paradox — the result that a social choice procedure cannot in general satisfy both minimal liberalism and the weak Pareto principle without producing cyclical social preferences — shows that the Pareto principle has to be restricted, not that decisiveness over personal matters should be abandoned. By minimal liberalism I mean the condition that each individual is decisive over at least one pair of alternatives, so their preference on that pair is socially respected; by the weak Pareto principle I mean that if everyone strictly prefers one alternative to another, society should rank it above the other.

The audience will be around twenty-five students, most of whom have not encountered the result before, plus two teachers who will steer the questioning. The first is my economics teacher, a committed welfarist who treats Pareto efficiency as the least controversial standard of social improvement and is likely to regard the paradox as an artefact of an over-demanding formalisation of rights. The second is my politics teacher, a rights-oriented liberal who regards individual liberties as side-constraints that should not be traded against aggregate welfare and is likely to argue that the paradox merely shows rights and welfare are different currencies a well-designed constitution can balance.

Prepare me for the six or eight objections I am likely to get — from both teachers, and including the two or three from sharper students that could genuinely destabilise the talk. For each objection, phrase it roughly as the person would put it, say whether it has real force, what I should concede at once, what I should hold, and give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, noting where the reply is weakest. Order them as I should expect them or by how much damage they could do, and end with the question I should most hope nobody asks. Please treat this as a diagnostic rather than a pep talk.
