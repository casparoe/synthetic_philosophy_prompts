Consider the following account of the distinction between a unit and a number, drawn from Euclid's definitions at the start of Book VII of the Elements. A unit is whatever it is in a thing that makes it one; a number is a collection formed out of units. On this account, the unit itself is not a number, and a number is always a multiplicity of ones. Now take two borderline cases: the empty collection, with no units in it, and the singleton collection, with exactly one unit. Modern mathematics treats the cardinalities of these collections, zero and one, as natural numbers. Work through the two cases criterion by criterion: does a collection formed out of units require at least one unit, or at least two, or can it be formed out of none? Is the singleton just the unit reappearing under another name, or is it a number? Then say whether these cases show that Euclid's criteria are merely incomplete relative to our modern concept of number, or whether they were tracking a different concept altogether, one in which number is always a count of distinct things. If you would revise the criteria to include zero and one, give the revision and state what it preserves and what it abandons.
