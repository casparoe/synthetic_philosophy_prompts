I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a long essay about the way we assign historical significance. Fernand Braudel's division of historical time into the event, the medium-term cycle, and the longue durée — the longue durée naming the slow-moving structures of geography, climate, demography, and material life — has always struck me as the right instinct but too vague to use. I have tried to make it operational with what I call the Structural Penetration Criterion. The criterion holds that an event is historically significant if and only if it alters the trajectory of at least one slow structure — demographic, climatic, institutional, technological, or ecological — for longer than that structure's own characteristic timescale. Otherwise the event is historical noise, however much it dominated the headlines. A head of state's assassination that leaves the regime untouched would count as noise; a modest change in crop-insurance rules that quietly reshapes land use, soil, and settlement over half a century would count as significant. I am aware this sounds reductive, and that the notion of a structure's characteristic timescale is doing a suspicious amount of work. I would like the three strongest objections to the criterion, a candid statement of what it gets right, and a verdict on whether any objection is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. I would also like to know whether the criterion already exists under a better name, and where my version goes wrong.
