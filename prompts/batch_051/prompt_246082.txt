You are a professional philosopher working on American pragmatism, with particular expertise in C. S. Peirce's account of the fixation of belief and his fallibilist view of inquiry. I have been developing a distinction and I want your candid assessment of it: whether it is coherent, whether it collapses into something Peirce already says, and where it breaks.

I propose a fifth method of fixing belief, which I call the method of deference. It would sit alongside Peirce's four methods: tenacity, authority, the a priori method, and the method of science. A person uses the method of deference when she settles what to believe by adopting the judgment of a trusted person or community, where the trust is neither backed by coercion, as in the method of authority, nor earned by evidence of the source's reliability that the believer has herself gathered or checked, as in scientific inquiry. The deferring believer's operative rule is roughly this: for any proposition p in the relevant domain, if the trusted source says p, then believe p. Consider a patient who accepts a diagnosis solely because she trusts her doctor, or a novice who adopts the consensus of a scientific community she has never audited. Neither person is being compelled, and neither has tested the source's track record.

I am inclined to say Peirce's taxonomy missed this option because his method of authority is institutional and coercive, whereas deference is usually voluntary and local. But it may instead be only the method of authority without uniforms, or not a method of fixing belief at all but a way of dividing the labor of inquiry. It also appears to sit badly with Peirce's fallibilism, since deference treats the trusted source as provisionally infallible and thereby closes inquiry prematurely. Work through the options carefully before settling on an assessment. Take my proposal in its strongest form, identify the nearest positions in epistemology and in Peirce's own system, and tell me exactly what is right about it, what is unoriginal, and where it fails.
