I'm teaching William Golding's Lord of the Flies in a philosophy of social norms course, and I want a committed reading of it through David Lewis's account of convention rather than a survey of possible angles. Analyze the boys' rules in Lewis's terms: which are genuine conventions, in the technical sense of an arbitrary regularity solving a recurrent coordination problem with conditional preference and common knowledge, and which are only regularities held in place by authority, fear, or a common-interest problem. Focus on the conch rule for assemblies and distinguish it from the signal-fire duty. I suspect the conch begins as something close to a textbook Lewis convention, but I want to know where it satisfies the conditions and where it only appears to because Ralph and Piggy are already enforcing it. Then track how the rule gains and loses its grip, up through Jack's secession and the confrontation at Castle Rock, and take a definite position on this question: does the novel show a Lewis convention decaying as common knowledge and the underlying common interest erode, or does it show that the conch rule was never really a convention at all but a norm propped up by fragile authority? Commit to one reading, support it with the novel's actual scenes, and say what needs to be added to or amended in Lewis's machinery to account for how the boys' norms actually evolve and collapse.
