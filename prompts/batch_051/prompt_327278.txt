I'm TAing a philosophy of science course this semester, and the professor wants to close the unit on interpretations of probability with a sonnet. I sort of volunteered to write one and now I'm stuck, mostly on the turn. The poem should be a Shakespearean sonnet — 14 lines, iambic pentameter or close enough, with the volta around line 9. The first eight lines should give frequentism a fair run: probability as limiting relative frequency in a long (ideally infinite) sequence of trials, something objective and out in the world, not a matter of anyone's hunches or degrees of belief. Then the volta should land on the thing that actually makes frequentism squirm: a one-off event — this tumor, this election, this coin about to be tossed once — has no long-run sequence of its own, and whatever frequency you read off for it depends on which reference class you stuff it into. The final six lines can reach toward a subjectivist or propensity response, or leave the tension unresolved; whatever makes the better poem. I don't want a poem that just drops the words "frequentism" and "reference class" like jargon. The logic of the problem should do the structural work, so someone who did the reading recognizes the position and someone who didn't still gets a decent poem. Meter can break where it needs to — I'd rather have the ideas come through cleanly than force fourteen lines of iambs. Please begin with the poem itself, no preface or commentary.
