I run my high school's philosophy club, and I want an activity for our next meeting on hard choices: a quiz that works out which view a member actually holds by their answers to concrete cases, not by which label they would pick for themselves. Please write the quiz itself, not just an outline of one. It should have seven to nine questions, each presenting a specific choice with three or four answer options that capture genuinely different things someone might say about that case. The results must make at least these positions reachable: (a) a common-scale view, on which hard choices always trade off on some single measure and there is a fact about which option is better or whether they are exactly equal, with our difficulty being ignorance or calculation; (b) an incomparability view, on which some options are genuinely non-comparable and the question of which is better can fail to have an answer at all; (c) a parity view, on which options can be on a par — neither better, neither worse, and not equal, yet still rationally comparable enough that choosing either can be justified without its being second-best; and (d) a lexical-trumping view, on which some values lexically outweigh others, so choices that look hard actually have a strict answer once the correct ranking of values is applied. You may add a fifth result only if it is genuinely distinct from these four — for instance, a view on which hard choices are where a person decides what to value rather than discovers which option is better.

Use ordinary high-school cases for at least half the questions — choosing between two summer programs, between studying for an exam and helping a friend, between a demanding and a creative elective, between a financially safe path and a fulfilling one — and use higher-stakes or adult cases such as career, medical, or rescue decisions for the rest. No question should simply ask whether values share a common scale; each must force an answer about a case in which the options pull in different directions. Please also include a scoring key that maps patterns of answers to positions, but do not reduce it to “mostly B means X”: mixed patterns should be interpreted honestly, and if a pattern straddles two positions, say what the hybrid is and which one further case would settle it. As you build the quiz, work from the answer matrix rather than from the labels: note combinations of answers that no defended position occupies and briefly diagnose whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag any positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate — I suspect incomparability and parity will look alike unless one question is worded with unusual care — and include the precise question that does separate them.
