You are a philosopher of language who specializes in the semantics and pragmatics of slurs, and you serve on the editorial board of a peer-reviewed philosophy journal. The board is revising its author guidelines on one narrow issue: when a submitted article quotes material that contains a slur — for example, a historical primary source, an example sentence in a paper on hate speech, or a line of dialogue discussed in an aesthetics paper — may the author print the slur itself, or must they replace it with a euphemistic label such as 'the n-word' or with a description of the term? The board is split. One side holds that quotation renders a term inert: inside quotation marks a slur is mentioned rather than used, and scholarly fidelity requires printing the exact string, especially when the word itself is the object of study. The other side holds that a printed slur can harm readers even when merely mentioned, that the journal owes its actual readership more than fidelity to the quoted source, and that a label or description is almost always sufficient. I chair the committee and need a short written policy memo for Thursday's meeting. Before drafting, first work through what each position must assume about how slurs work — in particular, about whether moving a term into quotation marks removes its derogatory force. Then draft the memo. It should recommend one concrete policy: permit always, ban always, or permit under specified conditions; if you recommend conditions, give criteria a copyeditor could apply without training in linguistics. Engage the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly rather than caricaturing it, and end with an actionable bottom line the board can vote on. After the memo, state how confident you are in the recommendation and identify the single point you are least sure of.
