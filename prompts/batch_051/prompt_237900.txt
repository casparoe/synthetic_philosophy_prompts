I'm a journalist who covers math and philosophy, and I'm deep in a longread about how computational tools — proof assistants, neural-network conjecture generators, the whole formalization ecosystem — are changing what it means to actually know a piece of mathematics. Every time I try to write the section on justification, I stall on the old Benacerraf dilemma. As I understand it, that dilemma was built for a time when the going epistemology was a causal theory of knowledge: platonism says mathematical truths are about abstract objects outside space and time, but if knowing something requires causal contact with the thing known, then nobody can know a fact about an abstract object — yet mathematicians plainly do. So platonism seems to get the semantics right and the epistemology impossible, while its rivals get the reverse.

Two things have changed since the dilemma was first pressed. Epistemologists have mostly stopped treating causal contact as necessary for knowledge, and a growing share of mathematical practice now runs through physical causal chains: a proof-assistant kernel checks a formalized proof, a model trained on token sequences proposes a conjecture, and the thing being known is, at least proximately, a symbolic artifact on a chip. I've gone back and forth on weather the dilemma is dead, or whether it just needs its central premise swapped — causal contact replaced by some other naturalist constraint that abstract objects still cannot satisfy. For my piece, I want you to actually work this through: does the Benacerraf dilemma survive the decline of the causal theory of knowledge plus the rise of computational mathematical practice? If it doesn't, say which premise was doing the real work; if it does, give the restored version that still has bite. Not a survey of the literature — just the argument worked through cleanly.
