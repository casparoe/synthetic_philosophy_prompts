P.F. Strawson argued that moral responsibility does not depend on any metaphysical thesis about free will. It depends instead on the reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, indignation, and forgiveness—through which people hold one another to account in ordinary interpersonal life. A key premise of his argument is that these participant attitudes cannot be voluntarily discarded: whatever we come to believe about determinism, we cannot permanently replace our engaged, demanding stance toward other people with a detached, objective one. The objective view is available in special cases—for example, toward the severely mentally ill or very young children—but only as a local suspension, not as a chosen global alternative, because the reactive attitudes are too deeply rooted in the kind of creatures we are.

Now suppose a safe, reliable, and reversible intervention—a drug or neurotechnological device—allows an ordinary adult to suspend the reactive attitudes for a chosen interval. While the intervention is active, the person genuinely feels no resentment, gratitude, or indignation and takes a detached, objective view of everyone, including spouses, rivals, colleagues, and political opponents; when it ends, the reactive attitudes return. Assume the intervention works exactly as described and has no relevant side effects.

Does Strawson's argument survive this development? Identify the specific premise that the intervention puts under pressure, and say whether the argument is undermined, needs repair, or can be preserved. If a repaired version remains, state precisely what it claims and why it is weaker—or stronger—than the original argument.
