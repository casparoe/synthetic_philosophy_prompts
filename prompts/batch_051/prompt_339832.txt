Please compare two implications in population axiology: Derek Parfit's repugnant conclusion and a strengthened variant sometimes called the very repugnant conclusion. In Parfit's version, for any possible population of at least ten billion people all with a very high quality of life, there must be some much larger imaginable population whose existence would be better, other things equal, even though its members have lives that are barely worth living. The strengthened variant replaces the single barely-worth-living group with a better population that contains two groups: a significant number of people with very negative well-being, and a much larger group with barely positive welfare. What, if anything, is the structural difference between the two conclusions? If a theory avoids the repugnant conclusion but implies the strengthened variant, has it made any moral progress, or has it merely shifted the unacceptable implication into a different place? Should resistance to the repugnant conclusion be treated as a fixed constraint on a satisfactory population axiology, or could accepting the repugnant conclusion itself be the more coherent option if the alternative is the strengthened variant? If you think the comparison rests on a confused premise—for example, if the strengthened variant is not genuinely distinct, or if the notion of moral progress here needs more specification—say so plainly and reformulate the issue rather than proceeding as though the task were well posed.
