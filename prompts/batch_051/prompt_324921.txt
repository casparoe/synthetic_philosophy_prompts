I lead a Sunday-morning discussion group at my church, and next week I have been asked to present on prayer. I have been turning something over on my own for months, and I have landed on a position I want to defend. The ordinary way we talk about petitionary prayer is as asking God to change His mind or to do something He otherwise would not have done, and that does not hold together with what we say we believe about God. If God already knows everything that will happen and His will is settled and perfectly good, then a request cannot inform Him or talk Him around the way a request works between people. But I do not want to end up saying prayer is just a way of reshaping ourselves, because then asking for a specific thing seems beside the point. My thesis is that a specific request can still be a real part of how God brings about an outcome. God does not have to be moved by a prayer; He can have willed from the start to take that prayer into account as one link in the chain, so that my asking is a genuine cause of the answer without God ever revising anything. I know that is a thin line to walk, and I can already feel half the room pushing back before I have finished.

The group is about fifteen people, all serious lay Christians with no academic philosophy — I would not know how to frame any of this in technical terms. They read the Bible closely, they have absorbed a fair amount of popular theology, and they pray with real specificity: for healing, for jobs, for estranged children, for a member's upcoming surgery. They hold two convictions at once, that God is all-knowing and unchanging and that prayer changes things, and they have never been made to feel the tension between the two. A few lean strongly toward predestination, a few toward human free will, and one man is a retired engineer who will want to pin me down on cause and effect. What they will not accept is any account that makes prayer merely therapeutic or that makes God look as though He is learning or reconsidering. I need a prep sheet, not a polished defense: the objections this specific room will raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way these people will actually phrase them; which objections carry real force; what I should concede up front and what I should hold; where my own position is weakest; and the one question I am secretly hoping nobody asks, with the best answer I can give if it does.
