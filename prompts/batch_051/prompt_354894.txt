In debates about modal epistemology, someone in my reading group keeps saying that conceivability is like a telescope aimed at possible worlds: if you can hold a scenario steadily in view without detecting a contradiction, that is like focusing a telescope and taking yourself to see a distant object; it gives you defeasible reason to believe the object exists. He uses this to argue that because we can conceive of philosophical zombies—beings physically identical to us but with no consciousness—zombies are at least metaphysically possible, and therefore physicalism is false. I want an audit of how far this analogy actually carries. Please give exactly five numbered findings, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. In those five items, address: what the parts of the analogy are supposed to map onto; where the mapping breaks (for example, what the telescope's independent calibration corresponds to in modal reasoning, or what gets lost when the conceiver's cognitive limitations are left out); what the analogy quietly imports about directness of evidence; whether the zombie argument survives once the disanalogies are on the table; and what a better analogy or picture would be, if any.
