The gender-equality paradox is the cross-national finding that greater gender equality, as measured by standard indices, often goes together with more gender-stereotypical educational and occupational choices: for example, women in Nordic countries are no more likely, and sometimes less likely, than women in less egalitarian countries to enter STEM, and more likely to enter care-oriented fields. A familiar argument takes this paradox at face value: once social and economic constraints are relaxed, stable sex-linked differences in interests emerge, so the remaining gender gaps are not straightforwardly unjust and may not call for corrective intervention.

Now suppose a careful reanalysis finds that the paradox is highly specification-sensitive. The negative cross-national correlation between gender equality and women's share of STEM weakens or vanishes once one controls for countries' distributions of math achievement and for the size of the female advantage in verbal ability; the correlation can even reverse when gender equality is measured through family-policy arrangements — parental-leave design, childcare provision, part-time work regulation — rather than through attitudes alone. Suppose further that women's STEM participation tracks these labor-market and welfare institutions more closely than it tracks attitudinal egalitarianism.

Does the original argument survive this development? The essay should identify which premise the new findings put pressure on, assess whether the argument is dead, repairable, or restricted in scope, and then reach and defend a conclusion about what, if anything, the paradox can still tell us about the relative roles of preferences and constraints — and about what justice would require.
