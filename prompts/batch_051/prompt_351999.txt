I'm helping run a small reading group on the Church-Turing thesis, and we keep getting stuck on the same thing: people argue about whether the thesis is a definition, an empirical claim, or a theorem, but they never pause to say what they mean by 'effectively computable.' That's a problem because the whole thesis is a claim about that informal notion. I want you to act as a philosopher of computation who has thought hard about this, and give me a working definition of an effectively computable function. The definition should be precise enough to settle the clear cases and honest about which cases stay fuzzy. I have hunches about a few of these, but I'd rather not bias you.

Here are the cases I want your definition tested against. Adding any two natural numbers written out in decimal notation. Deciding, for an arbitrary program and input, whether that program halts on that input. A finite set of instructions that includes the step 'flip a genuinely random coin and branch on the result.' An idealized human with unlimited paper, pencils, and patience following the vague instruction 'keep applying the rule until the result seems simple enough to stop.' A soap bubble settling into the shape that minimizes surface area. A machine hooked up to an oracle for the halting problem. A trained neural network doing a single forward pass on a fixed input. And a web server that never halts but keeps responding to requests for as long as anyone sends them. For each, say whether it clearly computes an effectively computable function, clearly does not, or is borderline. Then give the definition, run it against all eight cases, and wherever it gives a questionable verdict either revise it or defend that verdict. Start by brainstorming two or three candidate definitions before you commit. Finally, say which reading of the Church-Turing thesis your definition supports (definitional, empirical, or something else), how confident you are in the definition, and which of the eight verdicts you are least sure of.
