Work just made me the default "someone should think about this" person on a project where we are considering running thousands of simulated customer agents to test pricing changes, and I have realized everyone around me throws around words like sentient and suffering as if they had a criterion and lost it. Before I read another paper on computational accounts of moral status, write me the desiderata. Not your theory; the spec any adequate theory has to meet. It has to cover both halves: which computations matter morally, and how to tell whether a concrete physical system is actually running one of those computations rather than merely being interpretable as running it (the second half looks like the real swamp, since with enough gerrymandering almost anything can be read as running almost anything). Give me the cases it has to classify without fumbling, and say why each is a genuine test: the lookup-table twin that passes every behavioral test by storing old interactions; the right algorithm but run at one step per century; a fly-brain simulation slowed down or paused; a person in dreamless sleep; a language model doing next-token prediction but building internal representations; a slab of lab-grown neurons firing in cortex-like patterns. Distinguish the distinctions it cannot collapse: having status at all versus how much, implementing versus simulating, being a model of a mind versus being a mind, actual versus merely dispositional computation. Also name the biological facts it cannot contradict, like neurons being noisy analog devices rather than clean boolean gates. Then mark which requirements are negotiable and which are not, flag any pair that looks like it cannot be jointly satisfied (respecting fine-grained physical detail versus pinning down one canonical computation is my candidate), and tell me which single requirement does the most to kill off the lazy views people reach for when they say its just next-token prediction. A table is fine. Keep the scorecard of existing views short.
