I'm giving a paper next month to a mixed political theory and formal political science workshop. My thesis is that the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem is usually overread. It gets cited as a deep impossibility result about democratic voting, but what it actually shows is that three specific commitments—unrestricted preferences, purely ordinal ballots, and a deterministic, single-valued outcome for every preference profile—cannot all be combined with a non-dictatorial rule without leaving room for profitable misrepresentation. I want to defend a deflationary reading on which the theorem is not a proof that strategic voting is inevitable in any realistic institution, nor that strategy-proofness must be a binding design constraint. Rather, it forces institutional designers to say which condition they are willing to relax in a given context and which other values—informational richness, proportionality, simplicity, responsiveness to deliberation—they are willing to trade for sincerity of voting. My worry is that this reading can be made to sound trivial if I am too concessive and evasive if I am too firm, and I need to know exactly where it is vulnerable.

The workshop audience is not a friendly one. One participant is a social-choice formalist who teaches the theorem as a limitative result and will press me on which formal condition I am relaxing and at what cost to the proof-theoretic backbone of the result. Two are deliberative democrats who think the whole manipulability literature rests on a thin picture of voters as preference-reporting agents whose preferences are fixed before politics begins. One is an experimental political scientist who studies whether voters actually engage in strategic misrepresentation and whether mechanisms advertised as strategy-proof survive contact with real behavior. And one is a participatory-budgeting practitioner who will want to know whether any of this changes how a real decision procedure should be designed. Give me a question-period prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order they are likely to come, phrased as these participants would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody asks about.
