I retired a few years ago from a career in the physical sciences, where much of my work concerned the question of whether an instrument measures what it is taken to measure; I have lately been reading philosophy of social science, with that old habit of suspicion in tow. A magazine article recently summarized the World Happiness Report for a lay audience, and I have been trying to decide how much of the summary to trust. As I remember it, the article ran something like this.

The report asks respondents in a great many countries to imagine a ladder numbered from zero to ten, where zero is the worst possible life for them and ten is the best possible life for them, and to say which step they are standing on. It takes the average answer in each country, calls this a happiness score, and ranks countries by that average. Scandinavian countries sit near the top year after year, and the report explains their success by appeal to six factors: income per person, having someone to count on in times of trouble, healthy life expectancy at birth, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and freedom from corruption. The article concluded that the rankings measure how happy a country's people really are, that these six factors are the ingredients of national happiness, and that a rational government should therefore concentrate its efforts on those six levers, since that is the empirically demonstrated way to make its people happier.

I suspect the article overreaches, but I do not know whether that suspicion is anything more than an old scientist's prejudice about self-report scales. Please go through the summary claim by claim and distinguish what a careful researcher would accept, what would need to be qualified, and what is simply wrong. In particular, I would like you to separate what the report actually measures from what the article says it measures, and to say how far it is legitimate to describe those six factors as causes or ingredients of national happiness. Then assess the policy conclusion: if a government did set out to improve its country's ranking, what would a corrected understanding of the findings lead one to predict—and would a rise in the ranking be good evidence that the population had become happier?
