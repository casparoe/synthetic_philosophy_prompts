I'm a philosophy PhD student working through the scale argument for taking insect welfare seriously: even if the probability that any particular insect is sentient is low, the expected number of sentient individuals still comes out enormous once that probability is multiplied by the size of insect populations, so dismissing insect welfare on the grounds that insects probably aren't sentient looks hard to justify. My worry is that the argument quietly treats the uncertainty as a per-individual chance that can be multiplied across many independent trials, as if each insect were a separate lottery ticket. But the main uncertainty may instead be structural: whether insect nervous systems support any valenced experience at all is plausibly a single, highly correlated fact about their shared physiology rather than a property that varies from one individual to the next. The point estimate of expected sentient beings would be the same either way, since expectation is linear, but the practical and decision-theoretic force seems different—under correlation the argument becomes a wager on one architectural fact rather than a near-certainty that at least some immense number of insects are sentient. Does the scale argument survive this shift from independent to correlated uncertainty? If it does, identify the premise doing the work; if it needs repair, state the weakest repaired version that still supports concern for insect welfare; if it collapses, say what should replace it. Please answer in American English.
