Consider a practice that is now routine in the major currency areas. When a central bank conducts large-scale asset purchases—quantitative easing—it buys government bonds from private counterparties and pays for them not with anything the public can hold, but by crediting the reserve accounts that the sellers' banks maintain at the central bank. The central bank then pays interest on those newly credited reserves, typically at a rate close to the rate on short-term government paper. At the same time, the bonds the central bank has bought do not constrain it the way a private bank's bond holdings would: a fall in their resale price does not by itself force the central bank to sell them, shrink its balance sheet, or raise capital. The central bank can carry them to maturity, and its ability to meet its own-currency liabilities does not depend on its net worth in the way a private firm's does.

This combination of features is philosophically strange. Public discussion calls the operation printing money, yet the thing created—the reserve balance—never enters the spending stream, exists as a liability of the central bank, and pays interest almost like government debt. Meanwhile, the assets purchased can fall in market value without the central bank being treated as poorer, so long as it need not sell. The practice therefore appears to take a position on two old questions at once: whether money is a thing or a position in a network of claims, and whether wealth is a stock of liquid value or a capacity to command future real resources. I want to know exactly which position that is, and how much of it is truly presupposed rather than merely compatible.

Please reconstruct the implicit philosophy of money and wealth. Structure your answer in American English, in exactly these sections:

1. The practice and the puzzle (about 100 words). Restate the three features above and state the apparent inconsistency between them and the ordinary idea that printing money creates spendable purchasing power.

2. The implicit theory of money (about 180 words). Reconstruct what money must be if a created liability of the central bank, which pays interest and never circulates among the public, can properly be called money.

3. The implicit theory of wealth and liquidity (about 160 words). Reconstruct what wealth and liquidity must be if an asset can fall in resale price without the holder being worse off in the relevant sense, and if swapping one government liability (interest-bearing reserves) for another (a bond) can be described as creating money without obviously creating net wealth.

4. Strictly presupposed versus merely compatible (about 120 words). Separate the commitments the practice cannot be made coherent without from those it merely fits well with or tolerates.

5. Under a rival theory (about 120 words). Suppose instead that money is fundamentally a pre-existing stock of things whose quantity is exogenously controlled, and that expanding that stock raises nominal spending and prices rather than changing anyone's real wealth. Say which features of the practice would have to change, be redescribed, or be abandoned.

6. Bottom line (about 60 words). In one paragraph, say what the practice most deeply commits its participants to believing about money and wealth, and whether the phrase printing money should survive as an honest description.
