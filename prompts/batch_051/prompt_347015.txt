I have been arguing with a friend about a strange asymmetry in the way people talk about simulations, and I cannot tell whether it reflects a real distinction or just an inconsistency we tolerate when the subject happens to be our own minds. If someone builds a computer model of a thunderstorm, nobody asks whether it is raining inside the machine. If someone runs a detailed simulation of a fire, nobody checks the server rack for smoke. We treat those simulations as representations of the target process, not instances of it. But when the target is cognition, the same people who would never ask about the wetness of a simulated storm will ask, with complete seriousness, whether a machine running the right kind of program literally understands, thinks, or has experiences. The asymmetry is in the questions themselves: we are willing to ask whether a computational model of a mind is a mind, but we are not willing to ask whether a computational model of a storm is a storm. I want to know whether anything principled justifies that asymmetry, as opposed to its just being a special exemption we grant to whatever we care most about.

In a course I am taking, we read a late restatement of John Searle's Chinese Room argument, quoted in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy article "The Chinese Room Argument." Searle writes:

I demonstrated years ago with the so-called Chinese Room Argument that the implementation of the computer program is not by itself sufficient for consciousness or intentionality (Searle 1980). Computation is defined purely formally or syntactically, whereas minds have actual mental or semantic contents, and we cannot get from syntactical to the semantic just by having the syntactical operations and nothing else. To put this point slightly more technically, the notion “same implemented program” defines an equivalence class that is specified independently of any specific physical realization. But such a specification necessarily leaves out the biologically specific powers of the brain to cause cognitive processes.

He seems to be saying that the asymmetry is justified because computation is formal or syntactic while minds have semantic contents, and because the equivalence class defined by "same implemented program" cannot capture what the brain's biological machinery does when it causes cognition. Finally, give your honest verdict on whether the asymmetry is justified, and if not, say which of the two judgments we should revise.
