I've been reading for a course on formal epistemology and came away confused about a debate I thought I understood, so I'd like help checking a summary against the actual dialectic. We were discussing higher-order evidence—evidence about your own epistemic condition rather than about the target proposition, like learning that you are severely sleep-deprived, that a drug you were given impairs logical reasoning, or that equally competent people who examined the same data reached the opposite conclusion. A fellow student, who tends to be a confident summarizer, offered the following account:

"Start with a case where your first-order evidence strongly supports p, say a proof you have worked through carefully. Now add higher-order evidence that your assessment of that proof may be unreliable: you are told you were given a drug that impairs exactly this kind of reasoning. If you keep your original confidence in p, you are epistemically akratic: you believe p while believing that your evidence may not really support p. Since epistemic akrasia is universally regarded as irrational, a rational person must lower her confidence in response to higher-order evidence; the only serious question is how far down to go. At bottom, there is no deep puzzle here, because all of this can be folded into your total evidence in the usual way once you include facts about your own reliability: the apparent conflict between the first-order and higher-order levels disappears, and higher-order evidence turns out to be ordinary evidence about your own track record."

Something about this feels too quick to me, especially the slide from "I have evidence that I might be unreliable" to "I believe my evidence does not really support p," which is what the charge of epistemic akrasia seems to need. But I can't tell whether that is a genuine gap in the summary or just my own uneasiness. Could you audit it sentence by sentence: which parts would most epistemologists working on higher-order evidence accept, which are oversimplifications or conflations, where a defender of the opposing view would push back, and whether the concluding "total evidence" point is as uncontroversial as the summary makes it sound? Please also say what would change your mind: which considerations in the literature—about whether higher-order evidence can be modeled as ordinary evidence, or whether epistemic akrasia can ever be rationally permitted or required—would most likely move your verdict in a different direction, and where you think your own reading of the debate is least secure.
