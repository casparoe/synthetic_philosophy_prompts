I've been using a four-criterion account of pain in a course on the philosophy of mind, and I want to stress-test it against a clinical case. On my account, an episode is a pain if and only if (1) it is a bodily sensation normally produced by actual or threatened tissue damage; (2) it has a recognizable sensory quality—stinging, burning, aching, throbbing, and so on—which lets the subject locate it and rate its intensity; (3) it is intrinsically unpleasant; and (4) it tends to motivate withdrawal, guarding, or escape. Now take a patient with pain asymbolia, a condition in which nociceptive pathways appear intact but the usual affective response is absent. When the patient's skin is pricked or pressed with a noxious stimulus, she can report where the stimulus is and how strong it is, and she may unhesitatingly call it a pain, but she does not wince, does not withdraw, and does not try to make it stop. She denies that the sensation bothers her or feels unpleasant, and she may even smile while the stimulus is applied. Does this patient feel pain? Go through the criteria one at a time, say where the case falls under each, and then say whether the case gives us reason to revise or drop any of the criteria—particularly the claim that unpleasantness is part of the pain itself rather than a separable response to it.
