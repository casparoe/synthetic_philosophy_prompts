After spending my working life in the natural sciences and recently retiring into a serious engagement with philosophy, I keep returning to an institution I served for decades: scientific peer review. Referees are generally unpaid, anonymous, and receive no formal professional credit, while the journals that depend on their labor are often controlled by publishers who charge universities substantial access fees. Everyone active in the field benefits from the existence of a well-refereed literature, yet any individual may decline to review, and nothing ties a researcher's own submissions to the amount of reviewing they have done; senior colleagues sometimes reason that someone else will do it. At the same time, editors in several disciplines report a persistent shortage of willing referees even as the number of submissions keeps rising.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher working on collective action, fairness, and the ethics of institutions, and think through this specific practice rather than treating it as a generic example. Start with a bout of genuine brainstorming in which you entertain several candidate analyses before committing, and then work through the following three tasks.

First, distinguish the main ways of characterising what, if anything, is morally wrong with this kind of taking without contributing in the scientific context: unfairness to those who actually do the reviewing, a failure of reciprocity, free riding on a cooperative surplus, a form of social parasitism, a defect of professional virtue, an assurance problem in which each person's willingness to contribute depends on confidence that enough others will too, or the deflationary thought that any single refusal is marginal and therefore not seriously wrong.

Second, reconstruct the philosophical commitments that the institution of unpaid peer review presupposes about an individual scientist's obligations to contribute to shared intellectual enterprises. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and flag where the practice is underdetermined between rival theories. I am particularly interested in an apparent inconsistency: the institution's public language treats refereeing as a professional duty, but its actual incentive structure neither enforces that duty nor couples benefits to contributions.

Third, say what would have to change in the norms, incentives, economics of journals, and self-understanding of scientific authorship if the community adopted the contrary view that there is no default obligation to review and that refereeing should instead be organised as a paid market transaction. I want an answer in which the philosophy illuminates the concrete texture of scientific practice and the practice disciplines the philosophy; a generic rehearsal of the free-rider problem will not do.
