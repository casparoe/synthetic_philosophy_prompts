Everyone I argue with lately leans on a picture where humans are to future superintelligent AI roughly what the australopithecines were to us: an earlier, less capable lineage whose whole historical significance turned out to be that it made way for its replacement. The line is that we are a stepping stone, not a destination, and that trying to keep humans in the loop forever is as silly as an australopithecine insisting the genus Homo should defer to it. The conclusion they draw is that we should not resist AI taking over our roles, and that any sentimentality about human distinctiveness is just a precursor species complaining.

Audit the analogy for me. Give me the correspondence table first: what maps to the australopithecines, to Homo sapiens, to the selective pressures, to the shared lineage, and to the extinction or absorption part. Then go through where the mapping breaks, and flag which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion they draw and which are just decorative. In particular, does the analogy quietly import that the successor will carry forward something of the predecessor (shared interests, values, lineage) and that the whole process has some kind of direction to it? Then tell me whether the conclusion—don't resist being succeeded—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument the whole time. If there is a better analogy for what they are trying to say, give me that too.
