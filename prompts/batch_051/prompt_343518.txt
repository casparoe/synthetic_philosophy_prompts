Someone in my metaphysics reading group keep pushing the line that the whole debate over whether there are tables or just particles arranged table-wise is a verbal dispute, and she always reaches for the tomato to make the point: whether a tomato is a fruit or a vegetable only has an answer once you fix the culinary or botanical context, and nothing in the world picks one context as the right one. She says talk about what exists is the same — the two sides in the table debate our just using different quantifiers or different standards, so there's no deep fact about whether there are really tables, and we should just pick whichever way of talking is useful and stop pretending there's a substantive question.

I want an audit of how far this analogy actually carries. Give me the mapping first — what corresponds to the tomato, to 'fruit' versus 'vegetable,' to the two contexts, and to the fact that a tomato can be both without contradiction — and then the places it breaks. The disanalogies I can half-see are: maybe 'exists' is supposed to be univocal in a way 'fruit' isn't; maybe the two sides can be read as disagreeing even after the vocabulary is pinned down; maybe the tomato case is defused by pointing to different background interests, while the table case isn't. Tell me which disanalogies actually matter for her deflationary conclusion and which don't, and whether the conclusion survives once they're on the table. As you go, separate what's settled among people working on metaontology from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view — like the claim that all ontological disputes are merely verbal in this harmless tomato way — as if it were the consensus.
