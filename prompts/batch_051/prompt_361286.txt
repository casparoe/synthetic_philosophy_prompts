I'm on a three-member grade appeal panel. A student wins iff two things are found: (R) the grading was inconsistent with the stated requirements, and (T) the student filed the appeal by the deadline. We vote on R and T seperately, each member gives an overall grant/deny vote, and our stated practice is to decide R and T by majority and then apply the two-part rule.

Our last three cases:

1. Anderson: R was 2–1 in the student's favor, T was 2–1 in the student's favor, but the overall vote was 1–2 against, so we denied. The only member who found both voted to grant. When I pointed out that the issue votes should have made this a grant under our stated practice, we ended up arguing about whether Anderson was a mistake or the actual rule.
2. Brooks: R went 0–3 against, T went 2–1 in favor, and the overall vote was 0–3 deny.
3. Chen: R went 2–1 in favor, T went 3–0 in favor, and the overall vote was 2–1 grant.

The new case, Davidoff, is looking like another Anderson: R and T will probably both go 2–1 for the student, but only one member is likely to find both.

So: if we want to be consistent with our own history, should we grant or deny Davidoff? Which of our precedents is the closest analog? If our record underdetermines the answer, say what principle we'd be committing to with each choice, and what future case would cleanly tell them apart. Also, keep what's settled about aggregating a multi-member body's judgments separate from what's genuinely contested, and don't make a contested position sound like the consensus.
