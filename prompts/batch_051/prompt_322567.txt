In a debate about whether our values are ultimately many or one, a colleague dismissed the idea that we can meaningfully judge one way of life better overall than another by replying that you cannot compare apples and oranges. The examples at issue were goods of very different kinds — a life centred on professional excellence and a life centred on caring for a parent — and she plainly meant the proverb to settle the matter: because the goods are of different kinds, no all-things-considered comparison between them could even make sense. I suspect the proverb is being asked to do more work than it can carry, but I also doubt it is merely a platitude. Lay out, as a menu, the distinct theses that 'you can't compare apples and oranges' might be used to assert, from the near-tautological to the substantive and metaphysical. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or simply false; then say which reading, if any, would actually entitle her to dismiss cross-kind comparisons of value as incoherent, and which reading she most likely had in mind given how the debate was going. My real interest is not the proverb itself but what its possible readings mark out about the positions one can take on whether our values are many or one; the precise question is which of those positions the proverb, once disambiguated, actually supports.
