I'm giving a short talk at a small AI-safety org next month and I already know the Q&A is going to be rough. The room will be maybe fifteen people: engineers and researchers working on alignment, interpretability, scalable oversight, that crowd, plus one or two philosophers. Most of them think AI welfare is a competing cause that siphons attention away from the actual danger (misaligned systems), and a few of them think it's flat-out unmeasurable and therefore not a real research program. My talk argues this is a false split: welfare and safety are entangled in both directions. Concretely i'll defend three claims. (1) A system in welfare-relevant states — suffering, aversion, pain-like states, whatever those amount to in a nonbiological system — is a less reliable and less alignable system, so model welfare is a safety problem in its own right. (2) The training routines the safety community already uses to make models safer (RLHF, adversarial stress-testing, red-teaming) are also the most plausible places for welfare-relevant states to show up, so the community doesn't get to defer the question until later. (3) There are real conflicts: some transparency or alignment interventions could plausibly make a system worse off, and some welfare protections could make alignment harder, and we should name those instead of pretending one side always wins. What I'm not saying: that we should pause alignment work, or that I can prove current models are conscious. I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the whole talk. Order the objections I'll actually get by likelihood and by how much damage each would do, phrased the way these people would actually put them; for each, give me a two-minute reply, what I should concede and what I should hold, and a note on where the reply is softest. Then include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and what to say if it comes. Dont give me the objections a room of animal-welfare philosophers would raise; give me the ones from this room.
