I'm putting together a grad seminar on the hedonistic imperative—the idea, roughly, that we should use biotechnology to phase out suffering in all sentient life—and I've hit a wall before we even get to the ethics. We can't agree on what 'suffering' names. The biologists keep reducing it to pain signals, the phenomenologists keep saying it's a mood or a texture of experience, and neither camp can tell me what to do with the hard cases. Could you give me a working definition of suffering that handles these: a broken arm from a car crash; severe clinical depression with no physical pain; a patient under general anesthesia while a surgeon cuts into her; a lobster dropped into boiling water; a 24-week fetus recoiling from a needle; the grief of losing a spouse; and a person with congenital insensitivity to pain who still reports feeling empty and miserable. I'm confident the first two are suffering and the anesthetized patient isn't, and I keep flip-flopping on the lobster, the fetus, and the grief. (Actually I'm not even sure that last one is a single phenomenon.)

The reason this matters for the seminar is that I want a definition that lets us ask whether a world without suffering is a coherent target at all. If the definition rules in every negative affect, does it sneak in boredom, frustration, or the ordinary saddness of finishing a good novel—and does the abolition project then collapse into engineering a permanent high? Propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, flag where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts.
