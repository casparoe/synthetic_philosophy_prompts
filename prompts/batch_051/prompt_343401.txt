I lead the incident-review board at a company that runs a few thousand autonomous delivery robots in a big city. A robot injures someone or damages property about once a week, and after each incident my board has to decide two things: whether any individual or team is responsible in the blame-and-accountability sense, and who owns the follow-up. Right now we improvise. Typically we blame whatever operator or engineer happened to be nearest to the event, or else we shrug and say "the system did it," which satisfies exactly nobody—especially not the city's lawyers. Epistemic status: I'm an engineer, not a philosopher, and I might be mangling the underlying argument.

I've spent years reading LessWrong and similar rationalist blogs, so I suspect the word "responsible" is doing several jobs at once, and the first job of the procedure should be to split them: causal contribution, blame, and forward-looking ownership of the fix. There's also this worry I keep running into in philosophy of technology, the "responsibility gap" for autonomous systems: once you have a system that learns and acts without any person understanding, choosing, or forseeing each particular action, the usual ingredients for blame—control, knowledge, foresight, intent—can be spread so thin across trainers, engineers, operators, managers, and the system itself that no single person satisfies all of them, and maybe nobody does. I don't want a procedure that just invokes that as a slogan. I want one that runs the analysis and shows us exactly where the chain breaks.

You are a professional philosopher who specializes in moral responsibility and the ethics of automation, and who has also seen enough software incident reviews to know what a runnable checklist looks like. Think carefully. Before writing, brainstorm the conditions for blameworthy responsibility in your own words, and then figure out which distinctions have to survive being translated into a checklist that non-philosophers can actually run.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure for the board. It should be a flowchart in words, or a numbered checklist: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "no blameworthy party" (the gap), where it stops with "this is a values call, a human must decide," and where it says "causally involved but not blameworthy." Make the backward-looking blame question and the forward-looking who-owns-the-fix question separate passes, so we stop conflating them.

Constraints: the board is an engineering lead, an ops lead, a safety person, and a corporate lawyer, none with philosophy training. We have about two days per incident, and the procedure has to be runnable in an afternoon. The general counsel signs off on the final classification. The stakes are high: the classification feeds lawsuits, regulatory filings, and decisions about retraining or firing.

Then run the procedure on three incidents:

(1) A robot struck a pedestrian in a crosswalk because its perception model—retrained online by a third-party vendor—misclassified low sun at a particular angle as empty road. The vendor's test suite passed, and no one at our company or the vendor anticipated this exact lighting. The pedestrian broke a wrist.

(2) A maintenance tech disabled a lidar fail-safe to finish a repair faster and left it disabled; the robot later drove off a curb and damaged a parked car. There is a written checklist the tech was supposed to follow, and they initialled it.

(3) A junior engineer approved a weekly model update after a review that was, honestly, rubber-stamped because the org had normalized clearing the approval queue fast. The update made robots swerve around double-parked cars by entering the bike lane, and one robot sideswiped a cyclist. The engineer had no realistic way to understand the update's behavior from the dashboards they were given, and their manager kept pressing them to clear the queue.

For the worked cases, include at least one where the procedure returns an answer I will not like, and say why I won't like it. Also make the procedure honest about its limits: what it cannot decide, where it will misfire, and which residual decisions are corporate-values or legal-policy calls rather than facts.
