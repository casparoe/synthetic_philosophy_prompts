I am a software engineer who has spent years maintaining a long-lived, heavily patched codebase, and the vocabulary I think in is version control: repositories, commits, diffs, branches, merges, rebases, reverts, tags, HEAD, and the main branch. I have no formal training in historiography. Lately I have been trying to understand what is meant by the pejorative phrase "Whig history" — as I understand it, the practice of writing the past as an inevitable sequence of improvements that converge on the present, where past actors and institutions are sorted into those who contributed to and those who obstructed the way we now do things, and where the present is silently treated as the standard against which earlier periods are judged. The standard criticism is that this reads the present back into the past, strips events of their own context, and turns a contingent outcome into a destiny.

Please restate this position — both the Whig habit of mind and the standard criticism of it — in the vocabulary of version control, as systematically and faithfully as you can. I want a point-by-point translation: which elements map cleanly (for example, the present as HEAD or a tagged release, the selective historical narrative as a git log that follows only one line of ancestry while ignoring dead branches, the judgment of past commits as progress because they happen to lead to the current tree), which map only under strain (the evaluative language of progress and regression, the assumption that the current build is what the repository was "trying" to reach), and which have no counterpart in version control at all (the open future as experienced by past actors, the uncommitted alternatives that were never realized, the possibility that HEAD is itself a regression relative to an abandoned branch).

Then give me the residue report. When a claim about historical writing fails to survive the translation, tell me whether that is because the version-control framework is too thin to talk about history, or because the Whig habit depends on assumptions — teleology, present-bias, a goal the repository was moving toward — that version control rightly refuses to supply. I am not looking for a cute analogy; I want an honest translation that flags its own failures, and a verdict on which side the friction points incriminate.
