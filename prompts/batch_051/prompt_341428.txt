I write about the theory of collective decisions, and I can't stop thinking about the Condorcet jury theorem now that so much real-world voting happens in full view of a live poll. The theorem runs roughly like this: a binary question with one right answer, each voter independently has probability p just over one half of being right, and the group uses majority rule. It follows that as the group grows, the chance the majority picks the right answer converges to certainty (and if p is under one half, it converges to certainty of being wrong, which is just as striking). A lovely argument for why lots of moderately-clued-up voters beat one expert.

Here's the change I want to stress-test it with. Keep the private-judgement part exactly as Condorcet had it: every voter has an independent private lean that's correct with probability, say, 0.55. But now add a single shared signal that everyone sees before voting: a respected forecast, a pre-election poll, a prediction-market price, whatever. This signal is correct with probability 0.7, and everyone knows those numbers. Each voter combines the shared signal with their private lean in a rationally Bayesian way; they don't just defer to the forecast, and they certainly don't copy one another. Yet all their votes are now correlated through that one shared signal, so the independence premise is gone.

Does the theorem survive? Does majority accuracy still climb toward certainty as the electorate grows, does it plateau somewhere below one, or does a larger crowd end up no better off than a modest jury? I'm after the machinery, not the politics: which premise actually breaks, and is there a repaired version with a clean threshold, maybe a trade-off between the accuracy of the shared signal and the accuracy of the private lean? Put that as sharply as you can. And then, briefly, does the picture change if what everyone sees isn't a signal about the world but a running tally of how other people have already voted?
