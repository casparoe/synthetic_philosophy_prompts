I am in the middle of a module on social choice theory, and our weeks on the liberal paradox have left me puzzled in a particular way. The formal core is not in dispute: under unrestricted domain (the rule must be defined for every profile of individual preference orderings), no social decision function can satisfy both weak Pareto (if everyone strictly prefers one alternative to another, society strictly prefers it too) and a minimal liberty condition (at least two individuals are each decisive over some pair of alternatives that differ only in some feature of their own personal lives). What seems to have stalled is the interpretive debate about what the theorem shows, not the mathematics itself.

Some regard the theorem as a deep result about the incompatibility of Paretianism and individual rights. Others regard it as an artefact of modelling rights as decisiveness over pairs of alternatives, when rights should instead be modelled as constraints on admissible outcomes or on the process by which outcomes are selected. Still others think the real lesson is that the Pareto principle, or perhaps the practice of admitting one person's preferences about another's private conduct into the social calculus, should be rejected or revised. All of these positions seem to remain live decades after the result first appeared, and none has clearly displaced the others.

I want a diagnosis of this disagreement rather than a verdict on which response is correct. Is it a substantive disagreement within a single shared question, a verbal disagreement about what 'rights' or 'liberty' mean in formal work, or a case of several distinct questions—formal, interpretive, and normative—being pursued under the same heading? Do the parties differ over facts, over modelling conventions, over which conditions a satisfactory aggregation rule must meet, or over bedrock judgements about the authority of individual preferences? My provisional hypothesis is that the debate is largely a dispute about what a right is—whether it should be understood as a constraint, as a piece of decisiveness, or as something else—and that the impossibility theorem functions more as a shared reference point than as a premise anyone rejects. But I suspect this hypothesis understates a genuinely normative disagreement about whether preferences should be filtered before aggregation begins, and I would welcome an assessment of that.

Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: an account of rights that escapes the impossibility while retaining the Paretian idea, a domain restriction both sides could accept, a sharper distinction between preferences about one's own life and preferences about others' lives, a further impossibility theorem, or an acknowledgement that the underlying question is ill-posed? Please answer in British English.
