I've been arguing with a friend about why some people feel like NPCs to her and she insists it isn't a moral failing but an epistemic one: some people, she says, just fail a test she can't articulate. I suspect the test is doing all the work and maybe tracking the wrong thing, but I want a cleaner case to think with.

Construct a matched pair of thought experiments where two different accounts of the NPC feeling give opposite verdicts about whether someone is a real subject. Account 1 is projection: the target feels like a mind to you to the extent its behavior matches how you think you would behave in that situation—same reactions, same kind of inner monologue you take yourself to have. Account 2 is reciprocal tracking: the target feels like a mind to you to the extent its behavior tracks your mental states—it notices what you're attending to, catches misunderstandings, and makes the little repair moves people make when a conversation goes sideways. In the first scenario, the target passes projection but fails tracking; in the second, the target fails projection but passes tracking. Keep both scenarios ordinary—a family function and a customer-service interaction, a work meeting and a neighbor, that sort of thing—no philosophy zombies, no perfect simulations, no exotic conditions, and keep each under a paragraph.

Then run the two accounts on each scenario and spell out why they give the verdicts they do, and say which single feature of the cases is doing the separating. After that, say how a defender of the projection account would try to get their favored verdict back in the second scenario, and whether that move is legit or special pleading. Finally, settle the version of the argument I actually care about: if the NPC feeling is really tracking one of these two accounts, which of the two targets is it more rational to treat as an NPC—and does that mean the feeling tracks something real about the target, or something about how easy it is to project yourself into them?
