The argument over what Bell's theorem actually proves has been running for decades, and it has a strange structure: the mathematics is settled, Bell-type inequalities are violated in experiment, and yet the same results continue to license very different lessons. One side treats the theorem as a demonstration that nature is Bell-nonlocal — that no locally causal account in which each measurement outcome arises from local factors plus a common cause in the past can reproduce the observed correlations, so something about the outcome on one side must depend on what is measured on the other. The other side replies that the theorem rules out a package of assumptions that no experiment tests separately — local causality, definite pre-existing outcomes, and statistical independence between the measurement settings and whatever fixes the results — and that singling out locality as the culprit is therefore a choice rather than a proof. Each side can absorb the other's objections by adjusting a different assumption, and the empirical data do not seem to adjudicate between those adjustments. My working hypothesis is that a large part of the impasse is an equivocation on the word locality: between the relatively weak no-signaling constraint, which quantum mechanics respects, and Bell's stronger factorizability condition, which the correlations violate. If that is right, the residual disagreement is not a factual one that has simply failed to get settled but a dispute about which assumption is most costly to abandon and what a physical explanation must preserve. I want a committed diagnosis, not a survey: is this debate substantive, partly verbal, or ultimately about rival standards of explanation rather than about any hidden physical fact — and is there anything, a theorem, a discriminating experiment, a sharper distinction, or a reformulated question, that could genuinely end it?
