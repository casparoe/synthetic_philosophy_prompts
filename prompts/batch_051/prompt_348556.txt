A friend of mine has very settled opinions about whistleblowers, snitching, and when disloyalty is okay, so I've kept a running list of the judgments she hands down. Here's the track record:

- The hospice nurse who discovered her closest colleague was charting morphine doses for patients who never got them, confronted him exactly once in the parking lot, and then reported him to the state board: my friend says she did the right thing.
- The junior staffer who leaked a legal executive pay package and first-class flights to a local reporter, mostly because she'd been passed over for promotion; the expenses were already disclosed in the annual report and the board had approved them, so nobody was deceived or hurt: my friend calls her a backstabbing weasle.
- The son who testified against his own father in a scheme that had wiped out a bunch of retirees' savings, even though the father had raised him alone after his mother left: right thing, she says, but it would have been monstrous if it was mostly about getting his inheritance early.
- The postdoc who went straight to a journal about her labmate's fudged statistics before raising it with him, when the correction didn't change the paper's conclusions and she was competing with him for a job: she says the postdoc still came out wrong even though the data were cooked.
- The charity finance officer who quietly tipped off a major donor that the nonprofit's impact report had inflated the number of families it served, after the executive director refused to correct it: she says the officer was being loyal to the donor's money, and was right.

Next week I'm going to show her this one: a junior epidemiologist at a county health department finds that her mentor, the person who trained her and got her hired, quietly doubled the effect size in a five-year evaluation of a home-visiting program for new mothers. The raw data show a real but small effect, which would still have cleared the funder's bar for renewal; the padded number was only there to make the program look stronger to the county board. The junior person has already confronted the mentor, who told her not to be naive and that everyone does this. She has the evidence and is deciding whether to go to the funder, which would very likely end his career and drag the program's reputation through the local news.

What is my friend going to say, and why? I'm less intrested in the verdict itself than in the principle she's actually been running: whether it's about preventing harm, refusing to be complicit in a lie, keeping her hands clean, or something else. Which of the old cases does the new one really turn on, and if the track record doesn't settle it, what further case would force her to pick one rule over the others?
