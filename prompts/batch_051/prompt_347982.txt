I am trying to work out what, if anything, the passage of time should change about the justice of prosecution and punishment. I have a set of verdicts about particular cases, but I have not yet found a principle that captures them, and I would like you to help me extract or construct one.

Preliminary stipulation. I want to set aside the most familiar reason for statutes of limitations: that with time, evidence decays, witnesses die, memories distort, and a fair trial may become impossible. In each case below, assume that a fair trial is still possible and that the evidence is adequate to establish guilt beyond reasonable doubt. I am asking whether time matters for some reason other than the erosion of evidence.

My verdicts.

(1) Baseline. A thirty-year-old defrauds investors of their retirement savings in a single sustained scheme. The fraud is uncovered after one year, and the state prosecutes promptly. Verdict: prosecution and a substantial sentence are plainly appropriate.

(2) Delayed fraud, reformed. The same fraud is committed, but it goes undetected for twenty-five years. At fifty-five the offender is identified. In the interim he has committed no further offense, has run an honest business, and has—so far as anyone can tell—become a different sort of person. Verdict: I would not prosecute. I am not saying he deserves no blame for the past; I am saying that by now the state's pursuing him seems largely beside the point.

(3) Delayed fraud, unreformed. Same delay, but the offender has spent the twenty-five years much as he spent the years before: litigious, predatory in small ways, repeatedly skating along the edge of the law without conviction. Verdict: I lean toward prosecution, and I am less moved by the delay than I was in (2). The difference must be something about whether he is, in the relevant sense, still the person who committed the fraud.

(4) Delayed murder, unreformed. A twenty-four-year-old murders his business partner and conceals the crime. Forty years later he is identified. He has not reoffended, but there is no sign of transformation; he has simply kept his head down. Verdict: I am confident he should still be prosecuted and, if convicted, punished. Time alone does not seem to touch the case for accountability here.

(5) Delayed murder, profoundly reformed. Same murder, same forty-year delay; but in the interval the man has lived a genuinely admirable life, made serious restitution to the community in other ways, and by all appearances has changed at the level of character. Verdict: I still lean toward prosecution, though with more unease than in (4). The transformation that moved me greatly in the fraud case moves me much less here. I do not know whether that difference is principled or mere squeamishness about murder.

(6) Victim-delayed abuse. A man sexually abuses a ten-year-old child. The victim is unable to report the abuse for three decades—first because of childhood, then because of shame and fear. At forty the victim comes forward; the offender is now in his sixties and has otherwise lived quietly, without evident reformation but without further offending. Verdict: I think the victim should still be able to seek prosecution, and the three-decade delay should not by itself bar it. But I notice that this verdict seems to make prosecution turn partly on facts about the victim's ability to have spoken sooner—facts external to the offender—and that worries me.

(7) Lookout in a fatal robbery. At eighteen a boy acts as lookout in an armed robbery in which someone is killed. He is never charged at the time. For the next forty-five years he lives blamelessly: no further offenses, steady work, family, community involvement. A re-investigation identifies him. Verdict: I would, with real reluctance, let the case go; but I cannot say clearly why the forty-five years should do more work here than they do in (4), or why his blameless life does less work here than in (2). Perhaps one of these verdicts has to give.

The pattern I cannot extract. The best I can state, tentatively, is something like: the passage of time matters insofar as it tracks two things—genuine change in the wrongdoer, and the state's interest in allowing old matters to rest where the wrong is not among the gravest. But that principle stops precisely where my cases begin to hurt. It does not tell me whether grave wrongs are wholly immune to considerations of reformation or only less affected by them, so it does not decide (5). It makes no space for the thought that the victim's inability to have come forward earlier, as in (6), can preserve the possibility of prosecution. And it gives no guidance at all in the mixed case of the lookout (7), where the original wrong is grave but the offense was peripheral and the subsequent life blameless. I need to know whether these gaps can be filled by a more precise principle, or whether they are signs that some of my verdicts must be abandoned.

Please do three things. First, state the best principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that captures as many of my verdicts as possible; make it precise enough to apply, and say which case or pair of cases motivates each clause. Second, apply your preferred principle to at least two further cases of your own invention, and tell me what it says about them. Third, say honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether in your view I should overturn that verdict or complicate the principle to save it—and what the complication costs in simplicity or plausibility. If no single principle covers them, say which verdicts I should regard as outliers, and why those rather than the others.
