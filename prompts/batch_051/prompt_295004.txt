For a paper on measurement in public policy, I constructed the following argument, which seems to prove that a government committed to its citizens' happiness should try to top an international happiness ranking even by manipulating the ranking itself. That conclusion strikes me as absurd, yet each step looked plausible as I wrote it, and I cannot find the point of failure.

(1) A government has at least a pro tanto reason to promote the happiness of its citizens.
(2) Let H be a national happiness score of the kind used in international rankings: residents rate their lives on a 0-to-10 ladder, and H is the resulting country average, perhaps with a small set of statistical predictors folded in.
(3) H is the best available operational measure of national happiness; no rival measure tracks the target as well across countries and years.
(4) If one has a pro tanto reason to promote a target T, and M is the best available operational measure of T, then one has a pro tanto reason to promote increases in M.
(5) Therefore, the government has a pro tanto reason to promote increases in H.
(6) There are two broad families of ways to increase H: (a) make citizens' lives go better in the respects the score is meant to track, or (b) change what the score does — by excluding unhappy residents from the sample, retranslating the ladder so that lower life evaluations map onto higher numerical values, or reweighting the predictors so the number rises without any corresponding improvement in well-being.
(7) Increases of type (b) raise H just as much as increases of type (a); so, given (5), the government has a pro tanto reason to pursue type (b) increases whenever they are cheaper or faster than type (a) increases.
(8) But it cannot be right that a government has even a pro tanto reason to pursue type (b) increases: a manipulated happiness score that has come apart from citizens' actual happiness is not the thing the government has reason to promote.
(9) So the argument cannot be sound; some earlier premise or inferential step must be rejected.

If I had to choose, I would most like to keep (1), (2), and (3). I do not want the argument to force me to conclude that the rankings measure nothing. But if the later steps are rejected, I need to know what exactly is being rejected and what it costs for the broader practice of using quantitative indicators as policy targets. Where does this argument go wrong, and which plausible-looking premise or step is the least costly to give up?
