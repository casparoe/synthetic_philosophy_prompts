A colleague of mine has developed, over many years, a carefully thought-out position on what grown children owe their parents, and she has settled a number of cases in conversation. I have been keeping track of her verdicts, and I want you to try to predict her next one.

Her cases so far:

1. Her cousin's parents remortgaged their house twice to pay for her education and nursed her through a long illness; the cousin stayed close, visited weekly, and remained affectionate. When her father needed a year of daily assistance, my colleague said she owed not just financial help but as much hands-on care as she could give without wrecking her own health or marriage.

2. A man who abandoned his family and was cruel during the rare visits he bothered to make; his daughter has been estranged from him for twenty years. Now old and destitute, he asks for help. My colleague said the daughter owes him nothing as a father: at most the emergency aid any person owes a stranger, and even that not because he is her father.

3. A mother who raised her son competently, contributed modestly to his education, and was never cruel but always emotionally unavailable. The son keeps contact thin: a phone call every month or so, holiday cards, no confidences. When the mother faced a one-time medical bill she could not cover, my colleague said the son owed a bounded contribution to the bill, but not personal care, not extra visits, and not any display of closeness he did not feel.

4. Her neighbor's father was a barely functional alcoholic throughout her childhood: not abusive, but no real provision and no emotional presence. He got sober when she was in her twenties and worked hard to rebuild the relationship; they are now genuinely close. When he needed help sorting out his finances and finding a care home, my colleague said she owed him what a close friend would owe — time, advocacy, company — even though there was nothing from her childhood to repay.

Now for the case she has not seen. Her brother-in-law's parents gave him everything in material terms: extra jobs, good schooling, college paid for; they were never neglectful or cruel, but they were controlling and critical in a way he experienced as suffocating. As an adult he keeps them at arm's length: he answers their calls but volunteers little, appears on major holidays out of duty, and feels dread rather than affection. His mother has had a stroke and now faces years of need, and his father cannot manage on his own. What would she say he owes them — the sort of support one would give a close friend, only minimal emergency aid, or something else?

I am less interested in the exact verdict than in the theory that generates it. What rule or rules would make all four earlier verdicts come out as they did? Which of those rules diverge on the new case, and what does each predict? Which rule do you think she is actually running, and what in the earlier record most supports that reading? Finally, propose one further case, of the same sort, that would cleanly separate whichever candidates remain.
