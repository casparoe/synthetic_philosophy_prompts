For a reading group on the foundations of machine learning, I need a careful disambiguation of the slogan "there's no free lunch" as it gets used in arguments about induction and model selection. A member of the group has taken to repeating it whenever someone claims one learner or inductive bias is better than another: "there's no free lunch, so no bias is better than any other overall, and choosing between models is ultimately a matter of taste." I know this cannot be quite right, but I also know the Wolpert–Macready no-free-lunch theorems are real results, and I do not want to overcorrect into calling them trivial.

Please separate the distinct precise claims the slogan could be expressing, and for each say whether it is trivial, substantive-but-true, or false, and whether it would actually support his conclusion. Readings I can see, though I want the full menu: (1) that averaged uniformly over all possible target functions or labelings, all black-box algorithms have equal expected performance; (2) that no algorithm strictly dominates every other algorithm on every possible problem; (3) that all algorithms are equally good on the kinds of problems we actually encounter; (4) that every inductive bias is arbitrary and cannot be rationally defended; (5) that induction cannot succeed without substantive assumptions about the world; (6) that no procedure can extract a uniquely correct generalization from finite data by logic alone; and whatever further readings strike you as distinct. For each one, I want to know whether it follows from the no-free-lunch theorems themselves, whether it follows from sharper variants of those theorems once one stops averaging uniformly over all possible problems, or whether it is a further philosophical claim that the mathematics never touches. Pay particular attention to the slide from "no learner dominates on expectation under a uniform prior over all tasks" to "no learner is preferable for any actual task distribution" — I suspect that is where the argument goes wrong, but I want the precise diagnosis, including where the problem of induction proper and the no-free-lunch results genuinely overlap.

End with a compact statement of what the slogan, used honestly, licenses and does not license about choosing priors, architectures, and inductive biases, and identify which single reading the group member would need if his "matter of taste" conclusion is to go through.
