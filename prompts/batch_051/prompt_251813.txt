I am the editor of the ideas section of a general-interest magazine. Many submissions defend a philosophical claim with a vivid thought experiment — a trolley case, an experience machine, a brain in a vat — and I have repeatedly seen gripping cases carry weak arguments while clunky cases sink strong ones. I want a reusable first-round screening procedure built on the intuition-pump account of thought experiments: the idea that a case can move a reader to a verdict through vividness, emotional pull, or ease of imagining rather than through considerations that actually support the conclusion. Write it as a checklist my assistant editors can run in about twenty-five minutes per submission; they are sharp readers but have no formal training in philosophy. The procedure should specify which questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it rejects or flags a piece for revision, and where it escalates to me because the decision is editorial rather than diagnostic. Then run the procedure on three submissions: one that uses the experience machine to argue against hedonism about well-being, one that uses the Chinese room to argue that running a program is not sufficient for understanding, and one that uses a trolley variant to defend a moral distinction between doing and allowing. Make sure at least one of the three receives a recommendation the editorial team would find surprising or unwelcome. End by stating what the procedure deliberately cannot check.
