I am a retired natural scientist—I spent my career in geochemistry—and I now serve on the allocation subcommittee of my hospital's ethics committee. We must issue a written recommendation on how to distribute a limited supply of a promising antiviral antibody for a novel respiratory illness. The supply will treat roughly 500 people; the number of candidates far exceeds that. Our disagreement concerns two candidate rules.

Under Rule A, the aggregate-outcome rule, we would allocate the antibody wherever the total expected number of deaths prevented is greatest, even if this means treating a large number of people each of whose individual risk reduction is modest while leaving untreated a smaller number of people each of whom faces a much larger individual risk reduction. Under Rule B, the individual-claim rule, we would first identify the strongest individual claim to treatment—understood as the largest individual expected harm forgone—and then compare only such individual claims pairwise, refusing to let many smaller claims together outweigh one larger claim of the same kind.

The live case behind this: we can either treat roughly 200 patients in a high-risk group, each of whom would gain about a 20 percentage point reduction in mortality, or treat roughly 2,000 patients in a moderate-risk group, each of whom would gain about a 4 percentage point reduction. The first option prevents about 40 deaths; the second about 80. Several committee members regard Rule B as morally obtuse because it would knowingly bring about twice as many deaths. I have been reading Scanlon's What We Owe to Each Other, and I am struck by what I take to be the individualist restriction—roughly, that when testing whether a principle could be reasonably rejected, one considers the claims of individuals one by one, and the sum of many smaller claims is not allowed to outweigh a stronger individual claim. But I am not sure Scanlon's view actually yields Rule B in a case like this.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete allocation rule (one of these or a modified version), state the bottom-line recommendation in its first sentence, and engage seriously with the strongest Scanlon-style objection to the rule you recommend. Please do not rest the decision on a bare appeal to total lives saved or to the intrinsic inviolability of persons; I need the middle steps.
