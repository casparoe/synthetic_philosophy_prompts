I'm a retired microbial ecologist who got into philosophy of biology after retiring, and I host a small philosophy podcast. Next week's guest is a philosopher of biology who defends a modernized version of the biological species concept — the Mayr-style line that species are groups of interbreeding natural populations separated by reproductive isolation, give or take. I dont want the interview to just be him restating the definition; he's been through that conversation a hundred times.

Write me eight to ten interview questions that put real pressure on the view, plus a follow-up for each question, keyed to the answer he's most likely to give. I'd like at least one question on each of: bacteria and horizontal gene transfer; obligately asexual things like bdelloid rotifers; ring species, say the Ensatina salamanders; the sneaky work the word potentially is doing in the phrase potentially interbreeding; and whether reproductive isolation is really what makes something a species or is mostly a downstream symptom of populations having already gone their own way for other reasons. One or two questions can bring in the species-as-individuals idea from Ghiselin and Hull, since I suspect the guest is sympathetic to that framing, but don't let the interview turn into a survey of the literature.

For each question, make the follow-up press on the evasive move he's most likely to make — for example, if he says asexual rotifers are just an odd exception, ask why a concept meant to cover life can just write off a whole reproductive mode; if he says ring species merely show that species boundaries are fuzzy, ask what the concept is actually doing at the fuzzy edge if its not doing any real sorting there. Write everything in American English.
