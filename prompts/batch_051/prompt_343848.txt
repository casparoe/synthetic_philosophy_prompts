In my graduate seminar on constitutionalism I plan to open with the Sirens episode from the Odyssey, the source of the idea of a Ulysses contract. I would like to use it as more than an illustration of precommitment; I want the class to decide whether the poem is in fact a good model of what a constitution does. Read the episode closely against the structure of constitutional precommitment. In the poem the constraint is doubled: Odysseus has himself lashed to the mast under standing orders that he not be released, however much he begs; the crew are denied any choice in the matter, their ears stopped with wax, their part reduced to obeying his prior command. Map these roles onto a constitutional order. Who is Odysseus's earlier self—a present majority? Who is the bound later self, and does that figure resemble a future majority or a dissenting minority? Does the episode vindicate the thought that precommitment preserves autonomy by executing the agent's own considered judgment, or does it show self-binding shading into domination, with the present moment playing sovereign over every future moment and over others? And where does the analogy fail most instructively: what distinguishes a constitution as an exercise of collective, intergenerational self-rule from one man's wager with his own future desires? I want a reading that remains faithful both to the details of the episode and to the constitutional theory, and that ends by saying what the poem dramatizes that the formal literature on precommitment tends to pass over.
