Two friends of mine have been talking past each other for years about theory versus intuition, and they've finally agreed to an adversarial collabration. One is a systematizer: she thinks the point of a philosophical theory is to give us a small set of general principles, and that those principles are allowed — sometimes required — to correct our case-by-case intuitions, which she treats as data, often noisy and sometimes shaped by self-interest or parochial assumption. The other is a particularist: he thinks our judgments about particular cases are the only epistemic bedrock we've got, and when a theory demands we abandon a firm intuition his default is that the theory is on trial, not the intuition. They mostly fight about ethics, but both claim the issue is domain-general, so don't confine the document to ethics.

Draft the joint statement their collaboration should produce. It should have:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. Things both can actually sign: what counts as an intuition, what counts as a theory, which moves each side considers fair pressure on the other, and what they agree the debate is not. Make these substantive enough that they constrain the later disagreement, not just shared warm feelings.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state the residual disagreement in neutral terms, followed by a formulation of each side's considered position in wording that side would accept. Aim for at least four rows. One should be about default trust in case judgments, one about when a theory has been "earned" enough to override an intuition, one about what a debunking explanation of an intuition does or doesn't do, and one about how much cross-person or cross-cultural consensus matters.

3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one side. For each crux, give a concrete case or kind of evidence both agree would bear on it, and write down each side's prediction about that case in advance. Include at least one familiar historical episode (special relativity, plate tectonics, germ theory — common-knowledge stuff, no citations needed) and one invented thought experiment, and be explicit about what each is supposed to test.

4. A short concluding note saying which parts of the dispute are merely verbal and which parts are genuine.

They don't want a verdict; they want a document each could sign without wincing. The test of the document is whether a third party, reading it, can see exactly what someone would have to learn or give up to change sides.
