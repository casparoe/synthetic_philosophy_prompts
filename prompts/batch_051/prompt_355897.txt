I'm not a lawyer or a philosophy student, just someone who fell into reading about the absurdity doctrine after a dispute in our town over whether a maintenance truck could be driven onto the park grass for the annual fair. As I understand it, the doctrine says a court may decline to apply the plain words of a statute when doing so would produce a result so absurd that the legislature could not have intended it. The familiar illustration is the no-vehicles-in-the-park rule: usually the puzzle is whether an ambulance that enters the park to reach someone in cardiac arrest, or a pickup truck delivering a war memorial for a dedication ceremony, is a "vehicle" under the plain wording, and whether a court may read in exceptions because the literal result would be absurd.

Lately I've been turning that puzzle into a different test. Suppose the city council that passed the no-vehicles rule actually considered amendments that would have exempted emergency vehicles and vehicles used in official ceremonies, and voted those amendments down after hearing exactly the ambulance and war-memorial examples; the minutes record that the majority wanted a flat rule to avoid line-drawing and per-call judgment. So the hard result is not an oversight the drafters failed to imagine. It is close to a result they were told about and accepted. My question is whether the absurdity doctrine still leaves a court room to depart from the plain text when the evidence of what the legislature meant cuts the other way.

Then please vary one thing at a time and tell me which change matters most: (a) the absurdity is not a heart attack but a purely financial one, because refusing the festival setup would trigger a very large contractual penalty; (b) the interpreter is not a court but the park director, who must enforce the rule day by day; (c) the same council, in the same session, adopted a resolution describing the park as the city's main place for public festivals and emergency refuge. For each variant, say whether the court or director may set the plain words aside and why. And because I'm trying to sharpen my own view rather than decorate one, please say explicitly what would change your mind: which fact about the council's intent, the kind of absurdity, or the interpreter's role would flip your conclusion, and where in your reasoning you are least confident.
