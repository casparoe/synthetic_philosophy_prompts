After a seminar on formal epistemology I've been turning over something I can't tell is a real structural parallel or just a verbal one. In mathematical practice, when people amass evidence for an unproved conjecture, they give extra weight to lines of evidence that seem to pull from different directions — say, numerical verification up to huge bounds, a proof of a special case, and a loose heuristic argument. The folk rationale is that these checks are 'independent', so their agreement would be too much of a coincidence if the conjecture were false; that's the presumption of independence doing the justificatory work. The same move appears in the Bayesian epistemology of testimony: several witnesses' reports strongly confirm a claim only if the reports are treated as conditionally independent given the claim, otherwise the fifth witness is just an echo of the first. And in ensemble learning, a random forest beats a single tree roughly because averaging helps only when the individual models' errors are decorrelated. These three feel like one principle about when agreement among multiple signals is evidence rather than redundancy.

But I can also see a disanalogy: in the witness case the reports have separate causal histories that converge, whereas in the mathematical case every check is downstream of the same conjecture, so there's no analogous separation to ground the independence. I'm fuzzier than I'd like about whether the needed independence is conditional on the claim or on its denial. So: is this a genuine identity of structure, a loose anology, or just pattern-matching on the word 'independent'? Where exactly does the mathematical case come apart from the witness case, and does anything from the formal epistemology or ensemble methods transfer back to say when a mathematician's presumption of independence is unsafe? Also, I'd like the verdict to be self-critical: tell me which consideration would flip your answer in the other direction, and which step in the mapping you're least confident about.
