Eight months ago I was implanted with a brain–computer interface that lets me compose and speak through a synthesizer after a brainstem stroke took away my motor speech. The device works better than I expected, but living with it has forced on me a distinction I had never needed before, and I want to think it through rather than settle for the loose way the terms get used in my user agreement and in the neuroethics essays I have been reading. The two terms are mental privacy and cognitive liberty.

I would like you to compare mental privacy and cognitive liberty as normative concepts for governing brain–computer interfaces, not as slogans or synonyms. Please do the following.

First, state precisely the core interest each concept is meant to protect, and construct a concrete scenario in which one is violated while the other is left largely intact. Second, apply the distinction to a case the public discussion tends to miss: risks that arise not from deliberate decoding of a person's private thoughts, but from machine-learning inferences over signals that seem innocuous—fatigue, attentional drift, affective valence, subvocal rehearsal the user did not intend to transmit. A commercial operator might in this way learn something intimate about my mental life even when no one is reading out my words. Which concept does a better job of capturing that kind of harm, and why?

Third, address the objection that cognitive liberty simply includes mental privacy as one component, so the comparison is between a part and a whole. Say whether you find that reduction convincing, and in particular whether cognitive liberty protects something mental privacy cannot protect, such as a right to refuse or undo forms of cognitive influence, steering, or optimization that a device or its maker may direct me toward. Fourth, take and defend a position on which concept should be the load-bearing one in an early regulatory framework for consumer and medical brain–computer interfaces, and state what indispensable work, if any, the other concept still does.

I am not looking for a survey of the literature or a set of citations; I want a structured argument that would hold up if I brought it to my next appointment with my neurologist.
