I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been working through Heidegger’s Being and Time, specifically the sections on equipment and the way tools get handled before any explicit reflection. As I understand the position, Heidegger holds that in absorbed use a tool does not appear as an object with properties; it is ready-to-hand, and the surrounding world is organized as a network of references to tasks, goals, and roles. What is explicitly registered is not the hammer but the work being done. When equipment fails—it breaks, goes missing, or gets in the way—the tool becomes conspicuous, obtrusive, or obstinate, and only then does it show up as present-at-hand. The position I have latched onto is that breakdown reveals structure that was already doing the work: failure does not create the network of references, it exposes it. In terms from my own field, this is like saying that a subsystem’s architecture becomes visible to the engineer only when a crash or latency spike interrupts normal operation.

I want to treat this as a substantive claim about attention, tool use, and the epistemic role of failure, not just as a way of talking. Please tell me what would count against it. Which observations about skilled performance, human-computer interaction, or everyday breakdowns would be evidence that the readiness-to-hand versus present-at-hand distinction is not tracking a real difference, or that breakdown exposes something else, or that the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability? I would like the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I want an honest assessment of whether the position risks anything at all. What should someone who naturally thinks about tools through debugging and instrumentation find most persuasive as a live test?
