I retired from a research career in experimental chemistry a few years ago and have since been reading philosophy, chiefly around value, achievement, and the justification of scientific practices. A dispute has arisen in a scientific society to which I belong, and I want to think it through carefully rather than settle it by voting and moving on. The society's charter, adopted in 1926, sets up an annual medal and contains this sentence:

"The Medal shall be awarded for the most substantial addition to the store of natural knowledge made during the preceding five years."

The case before the council is this. A team of experimenters spent eleven years designing, building, and running a search of extraordinary sensitivity for a rare interaction predicted by a well-regarded class of physical models. They ran the experiment well past the point at which those models required a signal, and they observed none. The result was not an equipment failure or an underpowered study; it was a clean, high-confidence null, and the upper bound they established rules out that entire class of models. The work is now widely cited as the definitive closure of a promising avenue. No one questions the experimenters' skill, patience, or integrity. Some council members say the medal cannot go to this work, because there is no new entity, law, or mechanism to exhibit—only the removal of a possibility. Others reply that a reliable constraint is itself an addition to natural knowledge, and that the years of disciplined striving that produced it are precisely what the medal should honor.

I am less interested in which side prevails than in how the governing sentence should be read. Does "the most substantial addition to the store of natural knowledge" reach this case, given that its drafters plainly had positive discoveries in mind and did not anticipate our present practice of treating carefully measured non-observation as a citable, load-bearing scientific result? Lay out the readings this sentence yields on each of three approaches: the plain or literal words, the purpose of the clause, and what a reasonable member—or the drafters themselves—would have said if asked. Identify where those readings diverge. Then offer your own view on the deeper question: if we award the medal here, are we rewarding effort rather than achievement, and does the text leave room for that distinction at all?
