I have been puzzling over the view that some acts are simply off the table, no matter what the consequences of refraining would be. The case I keep returning to is lying: the view that one may never lie, even when lying would prevent other people from lying. I worked through the following argument that this view is self-defeating, and I cannot decide whether it is any good. I have numbered the steps so you can point me to the exact place where the reasoning breaks, if it does.

(1) To say that lying is absolutely wrong is to say that a lie is the kind of thing of which the world should contain as few as possible, other things being equal.
(2) If the world should contain as few lies as possible, then when the only relevant difference between two outcomes is that one contains one lie and the other contains five lies, the one-lie outcome is the better outcome.
(3) There are cases in which telling one lie now would reliably prevent five other lies from being told.
(4) In such a case, the practical choice is between an outcome with five lies, if you refrain, and an outcome with one lie, if you tell the lie.
(5) If the one-lie outcome is better by the very standard that makes lying wrong, then someone who says that lying is absolutely wrong should, in that case, tell the one lie.
(6) But the original view says that one must never lie, so it forbids telling the one lie.
(7) Therefore the original view is inconsistent: it both directs her to tell the lie and forbids her to tell it.

I half-suspect a slide between "this lie would be wrong for me" and "the world should contain as few lies as possible," since those do not sound equivalent, but I cannot turn that suspicion into an objection. Is the argument valid? If it is valid, which premise is false, and why? If it is not valid, identify the exact step that fails and give the corrected version of the step or of the original view that would escape the contradiction.
