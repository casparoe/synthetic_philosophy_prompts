I've been trying to pin down when an idealized preference still counts as the person's own preference, as opposed to a new preference you built for them and then stuck their name on. The rough criteria I've been using are:

1. Reachability: you can get to the idealized preference from the person's actual current desires and values through reflection, information, and correcting inconsistencies — not by just replacing what they want.
2. Endorsement: if the actual person were made fully informed and clear-headed right now, they'd endorse the idealized preference.
3. No imported values: the idealization isn't smuggling in values from outside the person's own evaluative standpoint, including the values of whoever or whatever is doing the idealizing.

Now here's the case I keep going back and forth on. A woman was raised in a closed, high-demand religious community and she geniunely wants to stay. But she also has a long-standing pattern of avoiding information that would make her doubt, and on the rare occasions she's forced to confront that information she becomes miserable and then rationalizes it away. Suppose an AI is asked to compute her extrapolated volition — not what she currently wants, not what the AI thinks is good for her, but what she would come to want if she had enough time and information and could think without fear or self-deception. The AI's model predicts that under those conditions she would leave the community and come to value personal autonomy in a way her current self would call a betrayal.

Does that extrapolated preference count as hers? I'm not asking whether leaving would be good or bad for her; I'm asking whose preference it is.
