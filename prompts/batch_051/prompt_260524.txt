I keep coming back to Blackstone's line in the Commentaries (Book IV, ch. 2) that all the pleas and excuses which protect someone from punishment reduce to "the want or defect of will." I think you can build a tidy argument from it that ignorantia juris non excusat is not just a policy but a conceptual truth. Here's my reconstruction:
(1) All excuses reduce to a defect of will.
(2) Ignorance of the law is a defect of understanding, not a defect of will: the agent can will the act and know the act, while not knowing its legal character.
(3) So ignorance of the law can only excuse if it produces a defect of will.
(4) Ignorance of the law does not, by itself, prevent the agent from willing the act.
(5) Therefore ignorance of the law does not excuse.
I'm not sure about (2): if I would not have done the act had I known it was forbidden, doesn't that show my will was in some way defective? And could the legal character of the act be part of the action's description? Tell me which step, if any, fails, and whether the argument still works if 'ignorance of the law' is read as ignorance of the legal character of the act rather than ignorance of the penalty. Also, does the argument settle the fairness question, or just the legal rule?
