Please argue that C. Lloyd Morgan's canon is not a neutral epistemic filter for deciding what animals actually think, but a stipulation about how much mind a description may posit, and that it is therefore more at home in the craft of fiction than in comparative psychology. I come at this as a novelist. Morgan's stated principle, roughly that we should not explain an action by a higher mental faculty when it can be explained by a lower one, is usually treated as a caution against anthropomorphism. I want the opposite reading: the canon only looks like parsimony because it assumes a pre-existing scale on which faculties are already ranked as higher and lower. Without that ranking, the canon gives no guidance. A modern evolutionary view should reject any single ladder of mental faculties, because different species have different cognitive solutions to different problems, not more or less of one general intelligence. My thesis is that a novelist is actually in a better position than the canonical comparative psychologist: fiction does not discover a mind but constructs one, so the governing constraint is not parsimony but intelligibility from the inside. For a scene I am drafting, the canonical question—can the raven's returning to hidden food be explained lower?—is simply the wrong question. The novelist should instead ask what mental life would make the raven's action most intelligible as the action of a character. Please make the argument philosophically precise, and show how the novelist's question differs from the ethologist's while still respecting the same behavioral evidence.
