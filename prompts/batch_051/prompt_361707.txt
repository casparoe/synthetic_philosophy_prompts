A colleague and I have ended up in a long-running argument about a parallel that I cannot quite decide is genuine, and I would like a careful outsider's assessment. The parallel is between updateless decision theory and the Rawlsian original position. The decision-theoretic side, as I understand it, runs as follows. In choice problems involving simulations, copies, or Newcomb-like predictors, an agent is often tempted to reason from its present subjective situation: it updates on what it observes, for instance on the fact that it is one particular copy with one particular set of evidence, and then chooses the act that looks best from that updated point of view. Updateless decision theory objects that this lets indexical information distort the evaluation of the agent's decision procedure as a whole. It recommends instead choosing an output of a general policy, a rule from possible observations to actions, that would maximise expected value from an ex ante perspective, before one conditions on which observer or branch one happens to occupy.

The political-philosophy side is familiar: in Rawls's original position, the parties choose principles of justice from behind a veil of ignorance, bracketing their eventual place in society, their natural endowments, and their conception of the good, so that the principles are chosen as if from no particular position rather than rationalised from the position one actually holds. The resemblance I keep noticing is that both frameworks refuse to let the decision-maker exploit particular-position information, the identity of a copy or branch on one side and a social position on the other, and both replace case-by-case, after-the-fact reasoning with the ex ante choice of a general policy or principle. I can also see why it might be superficial: the original position is a device of impartiality among distinct persons with conflicting interests, whereas updateless decision theory seems to be a device for a single agent to maximise its own values across possible worlds.

I would like you to take the role of a professional philosopher who works across formal decision theory and political philosophy, and to think this through carefully rather than giving a quick impression. Begin with some explicit brainstorming in which you map each element of one framework onto the other and note any elements that fail to map. Then state, as precisely as you can, what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer from one domain to the other, and identify at least one thing the analogy predicts that looks false. End with a considered verdict on whether the parallel is deep, superficial, or somewhere in between.
