I’m organizing a film discussion group on political emotions, and I want to read Florian Henckel von Donnersmarck’s The Lives of Others through the debate about whether trust in government is reasonable. Two pictures exert a pull on us. One, often associated with Scandinavian political culture, holds that citizens are generally warranted in trusting public institutions unless there are specific grounds for suspicion; the alternative, often associated with American civic suspicion, treats institutional power as default illegitimate and demands continuous oversight. The Lives of Others seems, on its surface, to confirm the second: the East German state spies on innocent people, ruins lives, and treats art as a threat. But the film’s moral core is a single Stasi officer, Wiesler, who falsifies reports and protects the playwright he is watching. What does the film actually show about the rationality of trust? Does it show that institutions are inevitably corrupt and only individual defection saves us? Or does it show something subtler, that trustworthiness can exist even inside untrustworthy institutions, and therefore the proper object of trust is never government as such but particular officeholders and practices? I want a careful reading of the film that tests these framings, including the ending where Dreyman finds his Stasi file and realizes that the officer’s reports concealed rather than exposed him. Where does the film resist the Scandinavian/American dichotomy, and what should we take from it about the role of transparency, oversight, and the possibility of trustworthy power?
