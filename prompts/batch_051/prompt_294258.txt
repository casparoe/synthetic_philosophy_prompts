Ok, here's something that's been bugging me. The peer disagreement debate in epistemology—conciliationism vs. steadfastness—has run for a couple decades now and I honestly can't tell what the fight is even about anymore. The standard setup: you and someone you regard as an epistemic peer look at the same evidence, with the same general competence, and reach different conclusions. Conciliationists say you should move substantially toward them, maybe all the way to splitting the difference. Steadfasters say you can rationally stay put, because the fact that you find your own first-order reasoning compelling is itself a reason, and abandoning it just because another person saw things differently would be self-undermining. Each side has answers to the obvious counterexamples (mental arithmetic, religious disagreement, juries), and yet theres almost no movement in the literature. My working theory is that the impasse hides a disagreement about two things both called 'peer': equal evidence plus equal generic smartness, versus equal expected reliability on this very question. If 'peer' means the first, conciliationism looks nearly forced; if it means the second, steadfastness looks fine, because you couldn't have known they were your equal before the disagreement started. I also suspect the uniqueness thesis is doing silent work: conciliationism seems to need the idea that a body of evidence rationalizes at most one doxastic attitude, while steadfastness quietly denies it. So diagnose the structure of the disagreement—is it verbal, is it a hidden normative question about the weight of self-trust, is it a real empirical question about how humans calibrate on other minds, or something else? Don't adjudicate, and don't survey the literature. I want a diagnosis with reasons, and an honest answer about what would count as progress: a sharper definition, a formal model, an experiment, changing the question—or an argument that we shouldn't expect convergence.
