Some traditions claim that the most important truths are ineffable: they can be known but cannot be said. I want a working definition of "ineffable" — understood as "what cannot be said" — precise enough to rule on the following cases. (1) The sentence "There are exactly three chairs in this room." (2) The sentence "The number of grains of sand on Earth right now is odd." (3) The taste of an unfamiliar fruit, as known to someone eating it and as described to someone who has never tasted it. (4) A complete, accurate written set of instructions for riding a bicycle, given to a beginner. (5) A joke's setup and punchline, written out correctly for a competent speaker, when the humor does not reliably survive the retelling. (6) What it is like to be the particular person now reading this prompt, as distinct from any list of facts about that person. (7) The color red as a sighted person sees it, described to someone who has been blind from birth.

I am confident that (1) is sayable and that (6) is not; I suspect that (2) is sayable but not known, and that (3) is ineffable but known to the eater; I am genuinely torn about (4), (5), and (7). Propose a definition, run it through all seven, flag any case where the definition gives the wrong answer or none, and either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Then use the definition to answer the underlying question: if something is truly ineffable, in what sense, if any, can it be known?
