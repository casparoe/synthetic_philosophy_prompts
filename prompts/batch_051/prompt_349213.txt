After forty years in molecular biology, I have only recently started reading philosophy in a systematic way, and Peter Singer’s 1972 essay “Famine, Affluence, and Morality” has left me more puzzled than anything I read in my scientific career. The passage I keep returning to is not the much-repeated drowning child case but the principle Singer states just before it: “if it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought, morally, to do it.” He immediately clarifies that the qualifying clause means “without causing anything else comparably bad to happen, or doing something that is wrong in itself, or failing to promote some moral good, comparable in significance to the bad thing that we can prevent.”

I would like to use that passage as the fixed assumption in a counterfactual prediction. Suppose that in a wealthy liberal democracy roughly like Denmark or Canada, beginning tomorrow, the adult population genuinely accepts the principle as an operational ethical rule for private spending and use of time. It is not made into law; no new tax system, regulatory regime, or aid institution changes. People simply treat the quoted standard as a serious moral obligation and adjust their choices accordingly. I want your best reasoned prediction of how the next two decades play out. Which parts of consumption and charitable giving change first, and through what mechanisms? What happens to the nonprofit sector, local businesses, labor markets, and family life when a large share of disposable income is redirected according to this rule? How do people resolve the open question of what counts as “comparable moral importance” in daily life, and do social norms emerge that effectively neutralize the principle or stabilize compliance? Which predictions are robust across different precisifications of that phrase, and which hinge on empirical assumptions about human motivation and social pressure? Please make the chain of reasoning explicit and attach confidence to each link rather than offering a single story.
