I am a retired natural scientist who came to philosophy late, and for the past several months I have been in a reading group on the ethics of belief. We have spent most of our time on Clifford and James, and the dispute between the two camps has hardened into a standstill. The Clifford side holds that it is wrong, always and everywhere, to believe on insufficient evidence: belief is not a private affair but an act with consequences, and the shipowner who talks himself into confidence in an unseaworthy vessel is guilty even if the ship does not sink. The James side grants that in ordinary scientific and prudential matters evidence should settle belief, but argues that there is a narrower class of genuine options — options that are live, forced, and momentous for the agent — where the evidence cannot by itself decide the matter and the passional nature may legitimately do so. In our group, both sides agree on the clear cases; what they cannot agree on is where the boundary falls, and in particular whether suspension of judgment in a forced case is the honest default or simply one more passional choice.

My own hypothesis is that the point on which the real disagreement pivots is not any of the familiar moral questions, but the question of doxastic voluntarism. Both Clifford and James seem to write as though believing is the sort of act one can be duty-bound to perform or permitted to perform; if believing is not under voluntary control, then the whole debate may be framed around the wrong picture of belief. But I am not certain that this is the deepest diagnosis rather than just another point on which the two sides can talk past each other.

I would like your diagnosis. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a hidden disagreement about whether belief is under voluntary control; about whether the same epistemic norms govern scientific and existential domains; about what counts as sufficient evidence; about whether the two sides mean the same thing by belief; or something else? And once the kind of disagreement is fixed, what would count as progress in it — a distinction, an argument, an empirical result, a reformulation of the question — or should the two sides expect the impasse to persist?
