I have been working through the problem of logical omniscience in a course on formal epistemology, and I have been trying to compress the standard worry into a single numbered argument whose exact failure point I can defend. The background assumption is the possible-worlds treatment of knowledge, on which knowing P is a matter of P being true throughout the agent's epistemic alternatives; one recognized consequence of this picture is that agents are closed under logical consequence in ways real reasoners are not. To make the tension vivid, here is the argument as I have reconstructed it:

(1) Knowledge is closed under entailment: if an agent knows P, and P entails Q, then the agent knows Q.
(2) In classical logic, any tautology entails any other tautology.
(3) An ordinary agent knows at least one tautology — for example, that either it is raining or it is not raining.
(4) Therefore, from (1), (2), and (3), an ordinary agent knows every tautology.
(5) But some tautology — say, a sentence with more logical symbols than any human being could parse in a lifetime — is one that no ordinary agent can even entertain, and a sentence that cannot be entertained cannot be known.
(6) So an ordinary agent both does and does not know every tautology; contradiction. Hence at least one of (1), (2), (3), or (5) must be false.

My own view is that the derivation is valid and that (1) is the false premise, because it slides from what an agent is in a position to know after competent deduction to what the agent already knows, and it ignores the time, memory, and attention that deduction consumes. A fellow student in the course takes a different line: they think the real sleight-of-hand is at (2), because the notion of entailment that makes (1) plausible is stricter than classical consequence — something closer to a relevance- or content-preserving connection between propositions — and under that stricter reading (2) is simply false. I would like to know whether the argument as numbered is actually valid, and if it is not, at which step it first goes wrong. If it is valid, which premise should a bounded-reasoning epistemologist reject — is my diagnosis correct, is my fellow student's, or neither? Be specific about how the questionable premises would need to be read for the argument to go through.
