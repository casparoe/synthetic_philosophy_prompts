I run a small betting operation with two friends—low six figures in the bankroll, maybe fifteen or twenty bets a week across basketball, tennis, and a little football—and we have a model that gives us a win probability p for each opportunity. None of us are real quants: one partner can do algebra, the other mostly wants a one-sentence reason for whatever we end up staking. Right now stake sizing is vibes. We underbet our best edges, and every few weeks someone talks us into putting 20% of the bankroll on a bet with a juicy p that is exactly the kind of bet our model is worst at estimating. I understand the basic Kelly idea—that with known p and decimal odds D (a winning 1-unit bet returns D), betting the fraction (pD − 1)/(D − 1) maximizes expected log growth—but our p is never known, it's an estimate with noise, and 'use half Kelly' feels like a folk remedy rather than a rule that follows from anything.

I want a reusable stake-sizing procedure we can actually run in under ten minutes per opportunity, given an estimated p, the decimal odds D, a rough sense of how much we should trust that p (sample size, market type, historical calibration), and the current bankroll level. It should output a fraction of bankroll to stake, or 'no bet', or 'escalate to a person', with the steps in order, what counts as good-enough inputs at each step, where it stops with a no, and which parts are judgment calls wearing a costume of arithmetic. It should be grounded in the actual logic of Kelly/geometric rationality rather than just the formula, and it should be explicit about what it cannot protect us against. Anything over 2% of bankroll has to be signed off by all three of us, so for bets like that the procedure needs to produce a two-line justification I can put in front of everyone. We also won't bother placing bets under 0.25% of bankroll—line movement and withdrawal friction eat them. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) p = 0.56 at decimal odds 1.91, in a market where the model has a long, well-calibrated track record; (2) p = 0.585 at decimal odds 1.72, from a market where the model is basically guessing and the sample is tiny; (3) p = 0.72 at decimal odds 1.60, in a market where our past edges this large have consistently been overestimates. For each, show the full-Kelly number, what your procedure actually does, and why those two differ.
