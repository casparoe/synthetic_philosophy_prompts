Ok, here's one I keep going back and forth on, and I want to use it in my philosophy of mind seminar next week. We've been on the problem of other minds. The classic argument from analogy goes: I know from my own case that a mind goes with certain behavior — wincing from pain, laughing from amusement, apologizing from regret. I observe other human beings doing the very same things, so I infer, by analogy, that there's a mind "behind" their behavior, too. Against this is the old main-character intuition I get from students all the time: that from the inside it can feel like I'm the only real person, and everyone else is an NPC running a script, the same way a videogame character has no inner life. I've spent years telling them that's a serious skepitcal worry but that the argument from analogy is our decent, if imperfect, answer.

Now LLMs come along and I honestly think it's unsettled. These systems produce fluent, responsive, seemingly thoughtful language — they apologize, crack jokes, explain a mistaken belief, describe a hypothetical toothache — and on the standard engineering story they do all of it with no inner life at all, just next-token prediction trained on human text. So now we apparently have a clear case where linguistic behavior that used to scream "there's a person in there" is actually running with nothing, or nothing we'd call a mind, on the inside.

Here's the prompt I want to give them (and, if I'm being honest, myself): does the argument from analogy for other minds survive the existence of LLMs? In your answer, identify the specific premise or inferential move the LLM case puts pressure on, then say whether the analogy can be repaired — for instance by narrowing the analogy base, or pointing to disanalogies like having a body, spontaneous pain-behavior, a continuous point of view, shared biology — or whether the LLM case gives the NPC intuition real teeth. I care about what happens to the argument itself, not about AI welfare or policy.
