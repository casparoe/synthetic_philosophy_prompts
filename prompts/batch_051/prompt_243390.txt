I've been reading about Avicenna's flying man thought experiment and I keep wondering how far he'd push it if he was around today. Quick recap of the setup as I understand it: imagine a person created all at once, floating in empty air, eyes covered, limbs spread apart so they don't touch anything, no sensations from the outside world or from their own body. Avicenna's claim is that this person would still know they exist — they'd be directly aware of their own self even with every sense shut off. So the self isn't just a pile of sensations, it's something you grasp before any experience comes in.

What I want from you is a careful guess about what Avicenna would say about these modern cases, given that argument. I'v got five:

1. A sensory deprivation tank. It's not total isolation — you still feel the water and hear your heartbeat — but people sometimes report losing their body boundaries. Would Avicenna see that as support for his view, or as a problem because the self gets blurry instead of clearer when input drops?

2. Locked-in syndrome. Fully conscious, can't move or speak, but still sees and hears. My gut says Avicenna would say of course they're aware, but I'm not sure his argument actually covers them, since the flying man has no senses at all and locked-in patients do. Does the argument say anything about them or not?

3. Waking up during surgery under general anesthesia — conscious but paralyzed and with senses dulled. That's sort of the flying man with the lights on. Would Avicenna treat that as a case of pure self-awareness, or does the pain and panic change things?

4. Philosophical zombies. The modern debate asks whether a body could do everything a person does with no inner awareness. Does the flying man argument rule zombies out, or does it only show something about beings with no behavior at all, so it leaves the zombie idea untouched?

5. An AI or a brain organoid that says 'I'm aware of myself' while receiving no sensory input. Would Avicenna accept the report as proof of a self, or would he think a self has to be a soul of the right kind, so the report means nothing?

And the bigger question underneath: is there one version of the flying man argument that gives a consistent Avicennan verdict on all five? If not, which of my guesses about him should I give up?
