I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I keep noticing a tension in the way thoughtful people speak about death. Almost everyone treats death as a bad to be delayed: they hope for effective treatment when ill, take ordinary precautions against fatal accidents, and call a death in one's twenties tragic rather than merely early. Yet many of those same people say they would not want to live forever. Asked about radical life extension or an afterlife, they predict that an endless existence would eventually go flat — that novelty would run out, that projects and loves would repeat until they lost their point, and that mortality is what gives a life its urgency and value. The Makropulos case, in which a person granted several centuries of life ends up bored and detached, is often offered as the vivid proof of that second thought. Its more sophisticated version is not about mere boredom: it is the claim that a person's deepest desires — the ones that make going on worthwhile — would all be satisfied or eroded given enough time, leaving no reason left to live.

So we treat death as a great harm and its complete removal as a harm of another kind. Is there a principled position that can hold both judgments? Perhaps a finite life is good precisely because it is finite, while particular deaths can still come too soon — but what makes any particular lifespan the right one, and why should the boundary fall where our biology happens to put it? Or is the aversion to immortality a failure of imagination, a story we tell to reconcile ourselves to what is unavoidable? If neither defense works, which attitude should be abandoned? Lay out the strongest case for each side, test it against the nearby cases of a death at twenty, a death at ninety, and a greatly extended life that remains actively engaged, and say whether the asymmetry is one we should keep or give up.
