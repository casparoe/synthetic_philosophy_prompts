I keep running into a tension in Maimonides that I cant quite shake. In the Guide of the Perplexed (III.32, Friedländer translation) he seems to say the whole sacrificial system was a kind of concession, not the point of the Law at all:

"It was in accordance with the wisdom and plan of God, as displayed in the whole Creation, that He did not command us to give up and to discontinue all these manners of service; for to obey such a commandment it would have been contrary to the nature of man, who generally cleaves to that to which he is used; it would in those days have made the same impression as a prophet would make at present if he called us to the service of God and told us in His name, that we should not pray to Him, not fast, not seek His help in time of trouble; that we should serve Him in thought, and not by any action. For this reason God allowed these kinds of service to continue; He transferred to His service that which had formerly served as a worship of created beings, and of things imaginary and unreal, and commanded us to serve Him in the same manner."

So the sacrifices are a weaning device for a people who couldn't imagine worship without them. Fair enough. But then in the Mishneh Torah, Laws of Kings and Wars 11:1, he describes the messianic era and says:

"In the future, the Messianic king will arise and renew the Davidic dynasty, restoring it to its initial sovereignty. He will build the Temple and gather the dispersed of Israel. Then, in his days, the observance of all the statutes will return to their previous state. We will offer sacrifices, observe the Sabbatical and Jubilee years according to all their particulars as described by the Torah."

That sounds like the sacrifices are coming back, and not just as a museum piece — they're listed as part of the restored Torah order the messiah establishes. If animal sacrifice was a concession to a people who couldn't handle anything more spiritual, why would the ideal future contain it? It feels like saying the point of weaning is to hand the bottle back once the child grows up.

Can these two passages actually be reconciled, and at what cost? I can imagine a few moves: the Guide is doing history and anthropology while the Mishneh Torah is doing halakhic futurism; he changed his mind; the future sacrifices are only for the sake of the nations, or only symbolic; the Guide's target is idolatrous sacrifice rather than sacrifice as such. I want to know which of these, if any, survives contact with both texts, and which are just special pleading. If you don't think any honest reconciliation works, say so — and tell me what the clash does to how we should read Maimonides on the cult.
