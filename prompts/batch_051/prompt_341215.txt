Something's been bugging me about how criminal law handles blame, and I want to know what theory of responsibility it secretly commits to. Courts will let someone off, or treat them much more gently, if the act was an accident, or done under duress, or done in a psychotic episode. But they won't let someone off because they had an awful childhood, or because an expert witness says thier genes and environment fully caused the crime. Even when everyone agrees the defendant's violence was the inevitable product of how they were made, the system still blames and punishes them anyway, unless they fit one of the narrow insanity-style categories.

I have a rough sense there's a distinction in play between acts that don't really belong to the person and people we no longer hold up to ordinary demands because they can't respond to them. And I suspect it has to do with the difference between engaging someone as a person to be reasoned with versus treating them as a problem to be contained. But I can't make it precise.

What view of moral responsibility does the practice actually presuppose? Does it strictly require some kind of free will, or does it only require that we keep treating each other as answerable whatever the metaphysics says? Where is the practice underdetermined, so that several theories would license it? And if we swapped in the determinist view that nobody ever really deserves blame, what exactly would have to change — would the distinction between excused and unexcused defendants collapse, or would the law just carry on punishing for deterrence while telling itself a different story?
