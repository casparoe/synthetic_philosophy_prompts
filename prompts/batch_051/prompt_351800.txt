I've been compiling a list of readings of the veined-marble passage in the preface to Leibniz's New Essays on Human Understanding, the one where Leibniz replaces the blank tablet with a block of marble whose veins already outline the shape of Hercules, so that the figure is not received from outside but is made actual by cutting and polishing away what obscures the veins. The readings I already have are:

1. The dispositional reading: the veins are not occurrent ideas but innate inclinations to form certain ideas under suitable experience.
2. The capacity reading: Leibniz is only claiming that the mind has native powers to know, which even an empiricist could grant once innate ideas are distinguished from those powers.
3. The structural reading: the marble's veins stand for the intellect's own form, the constraints and relations it supplies to whatever experience presents, rather than for any particular content.
4. The unlearned-prior reading: the veins are like an innate inductive bias or hypothesis-space restriction, so that experience selects among concepts but does not create the space of candidates.

I want a fifth reading that is genuinely different from all four — not a rewording, special case, or combination of them, and not simply the claim that the passage is only an analogy. It should make a distinct claim about what the veins represent, and it should say what philosophical work the passage can do that the four readings above cannot. Then tell me, if you were defending this fifth reading, where it would be most vulnerable.
