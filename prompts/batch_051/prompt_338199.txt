Last night I spent an hour arguing with a friend about money pumps, and I want to turn that argument into a classroom game. My friend's line was that there is no real trap in a money pump: a person who pays to switch from one option to another and later pays to switch back has simply changed, and the small fees are just the price of changing one's mind. I kept replying that the fees are pure loss and that the sequence exposes something uneasy about the chooser's preferences over time, but I could not make the point stick. I want my students to have the argument before I name it.

Design a game for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in decision theory and practical rationality — 14 students, one 80-minute session — that makes participants feel the force of the money-pump argument against intransitive preferences, and that lets them test the changing-tastes defense from the inside. The phenomenon must emerge from the rules, incentives, and roles rather than from an explanation given by the facilitator beforehand.

Include the following.

First, the choice objects and the procedure for inducing preference cycles without announcing what is happening. The options should feel like real choices with more than one relevant attribute — not trick questions — and the cycle should arise from the presentation of options, the framing of pairwise choices, or the structure of trading, so that a noticeable share of participants hold intransitive pairwise preferences while believing their choices are coherent.

Second, a trading stage. Specify a designated trader or market with rules for which trades may be offered, how fees are set, and why a myopic participant will accept the sequence; fees should be small enough that each trade is accepted but large enough that the drained total is felt. Use points or tokens with a meaningful final payoff rather than cash.

Third, an escape condition that isolates my friend's defense. Some participants should be able to refuse trades, precommit, or inspect their own earlier choices before trading. Design this so the group sees what immunity costs: if a participant declines the trade that closes the cycle, what does that participant reveal by remaining with an option they paid to move away from at the start, and what does precommitment do to the distinction between changing one's mind and being exploited?

Fourth, a timeline for the 80 minutes, with roles assigned before anyone knows the point.

Fifth, a debrief that maps concrete moments in the game onto the anatomy of a money pump: the intransitive cycle, the trader's profit, the point at which foresight or precommitment blocks the pump, and the cost of that immunity. The takeaway the students should earn is that the money pump is not a static fact about preferences at a single time but a problem about the relation between earlier and later choices, and that escaping it may require treating one's own future or past self as an opponent.

Finally, be candid about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students diagnosing the game early and warning one another, cycles too weak to generate trades, the trader overpumping and triggering blanket refusal, participants treating the fees as arbitrary points rather than costs — and say how I should handle each. Also say honestly which feature of real diachronic rationality the game cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply in discussion.
