I'm in a decision theory reading group, and we did the Allais thing last week. The person leading the group kept dismissing objections with a one-liner: "differences are all that matter" — or sometimes "you can just cancel the common outcomes." When I pushed on why the Allais choice pattern was suposed to be irrational, he said it basically comes down to that. I've seen the same line in a few places, like "shared consequences are irrelevant."

Here's the thing: I can feel that line doing real work, but I can't tell what exactly it's claiming. If it just means that when two options have the same outcome in some branch, that branch shouldn't affect your preference, that's one claim — pretty modest. But if it means something stronger about how you're allowed to reason about probabilities, or that any two gambles with the same non-common parts must be ranked the same, that's another. And the two choice problems in Allais matter here: most people take the sure payoff in the first pair and then switch to the riskier gamble in the second pair, even though the only difference between the pairs is a branch that stays the same across both options. Allais himself, in the 1953 Econometrica paper where he lays out the paradox, treats the independence axiom as the piece that generates the pattern, and he argues that rational people do not actually ignore common branches — especially when one option makes the difference between a sure thing and a risky one.

I don't want a neutral dictionary of readings. I want you to commit: lay out the distinct claims "differences are all that matter" could be making, say which are trivially true, which are substantive, and which are false or question-begging in the Allais setting. Then tell me which reading is actually doing the work when someone uses that line to call the Allais choices irrational, and whether that reading survives contact with Allais. Is it a requirement of rationality, a modeling convenience, or a disguised restatement of the very thing at issue? If you think the line has a true kernel even after Allais, say what it is and what it doesn't show. I want to bring your answer back to the group next week, so make the distinctions sharp and the verdict definite.
