I recently read a popular book on nationalism whose central chapter summarized a view it associated with Hobsbawm and Anderson, and I would like an accuracy audit before I repeat any of it. The summary ran roughly as follows. Nations are much younger than they present themselves as being. For most of history people’s primary loyalties were local, dynastic, or religious, and the idea of belonging to one vast national community — full of people one will never meet, all sharing a deep common identity — had to be deliberately manufactured in the nineteenth century. Many customs that appear immemorial, such as national costumes, anthems, flags, coronation rituals, and folk festivals, were in fact contrived in the same period, assembled from older fragments or simply invented by rulers, intellectuals, and nationalist organizers, precisely to give the new nation an air of ancientness and to attach people to it emotionally. On this view, nationalism does not express pre-existing nations; the causal arrow runs the other way, with nationalist politics producing the nation and supplying it with an apparent past. The book then concluded that nations are, in the idiom this literature made famous, imagined communities: they exist only because enough people believe in them, which makes national identity closer to a shared fiction than to something objectively real.

I find the conclusion arresting, but I suspect the book overreached somewhere, and I cannot tell exactly where. Please go through this summary piece by piece and say which parts a careful defender of the view would accept, which parts need qualification, and whether the final inference from nations being modern and imagined to their being fictitious actually goes through. In particular, I would like the audit to separate three contrasts that the summary seems to run together: a nation’s being historically recent versus its being unreal; a tradition’s being invented or reworked versus its being fraudulent; and a community’s being imagined versus its being merely believed into existence.
