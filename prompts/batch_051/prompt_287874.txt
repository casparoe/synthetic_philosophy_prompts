I've been trying to reverse-engineer the principle behind my own judgments about complicity: when does someone share in a wrong they participate in but do not cause? I keep a running list of cases and my verdicts, and my first-pass rule — that complicity tracks how much difference one's contribution makes — fits some but not all of them. Here they are.

1. A getaway driver waits while his associates rob a bank; the job would have been called off without him. I judge him complicit in the robbery, and substantially so.

2. A second lookout is hired for the same job, but watches a back entrance the first lookout already covered. His presence changes nothing and the robbery proceeds exactly as it would have. I still judge him complicit in the robbery, though less than the driver.

3. A woman in an angry crowd chants for the prisoner to be hanged. Her chant is one among hundreds; the mob was already fixed on the killing and would have acted without her. I judge her complicit in the killing, but much less than those who handled the rope.

4. A hardware dealer sells bolt cutters to a man who says he will use them that night to break into a warehouse. The dealer doesn't approve, but sells at full price. The buyer could have bought identical cutters a block away, so the sale changed nothing about the crime. I judge the dealer at most barely complicit — callous, maybe, but not party to the burglary.

5. An electric utility connects power to a warehouse it knows houses a counterfeiting operation, at the standard tariff. If refused, the operation would shift to another address or generator at low cost. I do not count the utility's decision-makers as complicit in the counterfeiting, though I do fault them for knowingly looking away.

6. A clerk in a forced-labor camp logs the workers' hours. The records are slightly useful but the commandant could compile them himself; the clerk took the job only for wages and does not share the camp's aims. I judge him complicit in the forced labor, and not merely blameworthy for insensitivity.

7. An investor buys stock on the secondary market in a mining company she knows uses child labor. The purchase supplies no new capital to the company and changes nothing it does; the seller would have found another buyer. I judge her not complicit in the child labor, though the profit leaves a thin moral taint.

The case I cannot settle is a translator paid at market rates by a militia to subtitle recruitment videos. She knows the videos help the militia attract fighters, but she has no other immediate income, and if she refused the militia would simply commission another translator.

Is there a single principle that captures my verdicts in (1)–(7), or close? If not, which of my verdicts should I abandon to get the simplest principled set, and what does the resulting account say about the translator? Please make the last line of your answer begin with "Principle:" followed by a one-sentence statement of the principle you settle on (or the principle you would defend after amending my verdicts as little as needed).
