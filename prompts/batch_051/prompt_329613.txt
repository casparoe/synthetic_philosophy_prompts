I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm putting together a longread about a fight that won't die: the Hart–Fuller argument over whether law and morality can be seperated. I've spent the better part of a year talking to two people—one is a Hart-style legal positivist, the other a Fuller-style natural lawyer—and they've agreed to do an adversarial collaboration: a single document, signed by both, that lays out what they now agree on, exactly where they still part ways, and what would actually move them. I'd like you to draft that document.

You are the philosopher of law they've asked to write it. You should be able to represent each camp from the inside, not as a caricature. Think carefully. Start by brainstorming seperately for each side: what formulations would the positivist actually sign, and what formulations would the natural lawyer actually sign? Then merge those into a document that is neither mush nor a victory lap for one side. Don't adjudicate; the whole point is that both of them could put their names to the final text.

The positivist is in H.L.A. Hart's line: law rests on a social rule of recognition; legal validity and moral merit are conceptually distinct questions; a valid law can be deeply wicked, and saying so is not a contradiction. The natural lawyer is in Lon Fuller's line: law is a purposive activity with an internal morality—generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, constancy over time, and congruence between announced rule and official action—and a regime that abandons those virtues entirely, or cynically uses legal form to dress up iniquity, loses its claim to be called law.

Use this structure, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. **Agreed claims** (~350 words): a numbered list of substantive claims both can sign. Include claims about the grudge informer problem, about the rule-of-law virtues, about the minimum content of law, and about what the Hart–Fuller exchange did and didn't settle. Make each claim informative enough that the other side feels real friction in signing it.

2. **Disagreements** (~400 words): a table. Columns: "Issue," "Positivist wording," "Natural lawyer wording," and "Neutral rendering both accept." Cover at least the separation of law and morals, whether the internal morality is genuinely moral or merely instrumental to effectiveness, and the legal status of an iniquitous regime that follows Fuller's eight principles scrupulously.

3. **Cruxes** (~250 words): numbered sub-questions such that the answer to each would move at least one of them. Try to separate the real disputes from the purely semantic ones.

4. **Tests and predictions** (~350 words): a table. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it—the grudge informer, a procedurally flawless apartheid regime, a retroactive curative statute, whatever actually does the most work—and record, in advance, what each side predicts the case shows.

5. **Where the dispute is verbal** (~150 words): say which parts of the fight, if any, are about words—for instance, whether "a legal system that violates the internal morality is not law" is a substantive claim or just a stipulation about the word "law."

I especially want the parts a hasty draft would miss: whether the positivist can accept most of Fuller's eight principles as rule-of-law virtues without becoming a natural lawyer, and whether the natural lawyer's view collapses into the positivist's if "morality" gets narrowed to procedural fairness.
