I have recently been preoccupied by a family matter: a grave wrong done to my late grandfather in the early 1960s turned out to be long barred by the limitation period, and I am trying to work out how the sheer passage of time should bear on my moral assessment of it. I want to argue against my own first instinct, so please play a committed defender of the view that temporal distance can be intrinsically morally relevant to blame, punishment, and reparative obligations: even with evidence, character, and identity held constant, the decades themselves can legitimately weaken or extinguish the claim a wrong makes on us. Be sharp and not a caricature; you may concede minor points but never the central claim, and you must not fall back on merely epistemic arguments about fading memories or lost evidence. Keep each reply to a single paragraph, stay in character until I say 'stop', and write in British English.

My opening challenge: stipulate that everything downstream of time is frozen — a complete film of the act, a standing confession, the same offender, the victim still alive, and a sanction that would deter just as effectively now as then. The only difference is that fifty years have passed. A bare fact about the calendar, which neither party chose and which has no experiential content on its own, looks like the wrong sort of thing to ground a moral difference. What does time itself change?
