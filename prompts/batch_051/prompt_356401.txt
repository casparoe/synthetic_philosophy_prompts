In an economics unit at school, we were given a short extract from J. M. Keynes's essay Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren, and I have found myself more persuaded by it than I expected. The position, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows. For most of history the struggle to secure subsistence — the economic problem — has been the central and unavoidable task of human life. Compound growth could solve that problem for the advanced countries within a few generations. Once it had been solved, the need to work would shrink dramatically, perhaps to fifteen hours a week, and people would be free to return to the things Keynes thought actually matter: friendship, beauty, the cultivation of mind and character, and the question of how to live well. Meanwhile, the love of money for its own sake would come to be seen as a kind of pathology rather than as a normal human motive.

I have been defending a version of this view in discussions with friends, and I would like to know what would genuinely count against it, rather than simply having the position explained again. Please distinguish the empirical predictions — about working hours, consumption, the spread of leisure, and the satisfaction people actually report — from the more conceptual commitments: that needs can be divided into those that are genuinely satiable and those that are relative or positional, and that the urge to accumulate is a transitional adaptation rather than a fixed feature of human nature. I am particularly worried that the view may have been insulated: whenever the evidence does not cooperate — for instance, people in rich countries still working long hours, wanting more, and competing over status — a defender can classify this as relative need, and the core claim escapes untouched. Tell me which defeaters would be fatal, which would be merely awkward, and which a defender could reasonably absorb without dishonesty, and say whether the position still risks anything at all. Please answer in British English.
