I am a PhD student in decision theory, and I would like you to write a short philosophical dialogue for a graduate seminar on the two-envelope problem. The dialogue should be self-contained: do not include footnotes or references, and do not attribute any proposed solution to a named author.

Set-up. An agent is shown two sealed envelopes and is told only that one contains exactly twice as much money as the other. She chooses one, opens it, and finds $100; she may now keep it or switch to the other envelope. One character, A, argues that she should switch: the other envelope is, as far as she knows, equally likely to contain $50 or $200, so its expected value is $125, which exceeds $100. Since the same arithmetic would apply no matter what amount she found, A adds that the observed amount is immaterial, and that she would have had the same reason to switch even before opening. The other character, B, argues that this cannot be right. The original setup is symmetric between the two envelopes, a policy of always switching produces exactly the same distribution of payoffs as a policy of never switching, and one cannot gain a reason to prefer the other envelope merely by opening whichever envelope one happened to pick. B insists that A's "equally likely" step is doing all the work and must be licensed by something the setup does not contain.

The dialogue must meet the following constraints. Both characters should be equally resourceful; neither is a straw man, and they should respond to each other's actual arguments rather than trade talking points. The exchange must weigh at least these moves. B should press A on what prior distribution over possible pairs of dollar amounts could make the other envelope equally likely to be half or double for every amount the agent might see, noting that no uniform distribution over all positive dollar amounts is normalizable. A should reply that agents must act under just this kind of ignorance all the time, and should challenge B to state a general rule of rational choice that tells the agent what to do without a prior, one that does not simply refuse to condition on the observed amount. B should then suggest that A is treating the observed $100 as if it were fixed independently of any process that generated the two amounts; A should answer by asking what the symmetry of the original setup actually licenses, if not the equal-probability assignment. The two characters may converge on a precise diagnosis, or they may instead isolate the exact step over which they disagree; either outcome is acceptable.

Write the dialogue itself, with clear speaker labels, and end your response with a final line beginning exactly with "Summary:" that states, in one sentence, the precise point at which the two views come apart.
