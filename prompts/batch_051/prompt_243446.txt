I am a journalist who writes about science and ideas. Recently I observed a disagreement between two clinical ethicists, and I have not been able to settle it to my own satisfaction. I would like a careful, point-by-point adjudication.

The case concerned a patient I will call Ruth. At sixty-two, shortly after a diagnosis of early-stage Alzheimer's disease, Ruth signed an advance directive. It stated that if she ever reached a state of severe dementia and then developed a life-threatening infection, she was not to be given antibiotics, even if the infection was easily treatable. In the document she explained that the person she would become in that state was not someone whose life she wanted prolonged, and that the wishes of her present, competent self should control.

Six years later, Ruth is in the late stage of the disease. She does not recognize her family and cannot communicate beyond single words. But she is not suffering: she hums to music, smiles when someone holds her hand, and plainly enjoys her food. She has now developed pneumonia. Without antibiotics she will almost certainly die; with them she will very likely return to her current, apparently contented state.

The two ethicists argued as follows.

(1) The first said the directive should be honored. It was the last clear, competent expression of Ruth's wishes, made for exactly this situation. To ignore it would be to treat the present, largely nonreflective preferences of a demented person as more authoritative than the settled, informed choice of a competent adult. Autonomy requires that a person's reflective decisions about her own body and death bind others.

(2) The second replied that autonomy is a capacity of persons, and the person now in the bed is not, in any morally relevant sense, the person who signed the directive. The features that made the decision hers — her memories, her projects, her values — are gone. Ordinarily a person may govern her own future because the future self is continuous enough to be governed: it can remember the decision, endorse it, or at least belong to the same psychological life. Here that continuity has broken down. A self that no longer exists should not rule a different person.

(3) The first answered that this overstates the discontinuity. We bind our future selves all the time, through contracts, promises, and wills. The person who signs a contract is not psychologically identical to the person who later performs it, yet we hold the later self to the agreement. An advance directive is stronger, not weaker, because it concerns Ruth's own body.

(4) The second said the analogy fails. Contracts can be renegotiated, and the person who performs them can still understand and accept the obligation; Ruth can do neither. More important, the point of honoring an advance directive is not fidelity to a document but concern for the person's good. If Ruth is content now, and a low-burden treatment would return her to that contented state, then the directive serves the interests of a person who no longer exists while harming the person who actually exists.

(5) The first replied that this was the deepest mistake in the other's position. It counts only experiential interests — pleasure, comfort, the texture of daily life. But people also have critical interests: interests in how their life as a whole goes, in dignity, in not surviving as someone they would have found unrecognizable. Ruth's earlier self had a critical interest in not lingering in this condition. That interest survives her loss of the capacity to appreciate it, much as the force of a promise survives the promiser's forgetting it.

(6) The second responded that critical interests are still interests of a subject, and a subject can change. Ruth may have abandoned her earlier critical interest — not by deliberating, but by becoming someone whose good is now almost entirely experiential. The first's position privileges the person Ruth was over the person Ruth is. Why should the version of her with the better cognitive resume count as the real one, and why should that earlier self rule the later one?

(7) The first said: consider a Jehovah's Witness who refuses a blood transfusion and then loses consciousness. We honor the refusal even though the unconscious patient cannot feel the wrongness of receiving blood. The structure here is the same.

(8) The second said the cases are not parallel. If the unconscious Witness could be woken for a moment, she would still refuse. If Ruth could be made competent for a moment, there is no reason to think she would endorse the directive; she might say that the soup is good and that she wants to stay. The analogy presupposes the continuity of the self whose values are being honored — the very thing in dispute.

They stopped there. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the eight moves from clearly right to clearly mistaken, with intermediate gradations as needed, and briefly justify each rating. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing, and state which overall position is stronger — or explain why the case is genuinely underdetermined.
