I've been teaching futarchy—the proposal that we open a prediction market for each candidate policy, with each market betting on the value of a democratically chosen welfare metric if that policy were adopted, and then enact whichever policy's market predicts the best score—and I keep tripping over a worry about the standard epistemic argument for it. The argument is that markets beat committees at aggregating dispersed information, because traders have to put their money where their beliefs are.

The worry is that the argument treats a market price as a pure signal: an estimate of what happens if the policy is adopted. But under futarchy the prices decide which policy gets adopted. Once traders know their bets will determine whether we get carbon tax A or carbon tax B, the market becomes part of the causal story of the outcome rather than a neutral forecast of it. A trader can then profit by nudging the price in the direction that selects a policy he likes—or that makes the welfare metric look good—rather than by being right about the world.

Does the epistemic argument for futarchy survive this reflexivity? If you think it does, say which premise does the work once it's made explicit. If you think it doesn't, give a repaired version if ones available—perhaps markets that inform but don't decide. I'm interested in whether the argument survives, not in whether futarchy is desirable overall. And could you answer in British English?
