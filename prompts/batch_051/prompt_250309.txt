Lately I've been editing Wikipedia a lot, mostly small history articles, and I keep tripping over the verifiability rule: every substantive claim is supposed to be backed by a reliable source, and if it isn't, someone tags it [citation needed]. The weird part is that the source is just another text making claims. If the sentence in the source had been inserted directly into the article, it would need its own citation too. So citing a source doesn't stop the regress; it just moves it. In practice people treat some things, like established reference works, government archives, or mainstream newspapers, as stopping points. But what makes them stopping points rather than just more claims that could themselves need support?

A friend said this is basically the ancient regress problem and pointed me to a passage from Aristotle's Posterior Analytics, Book I, Chapter 3, in E.S. Bouchier's translation, where Aristotle attacks people who think demonstration can go in a circle. Aristotle writes: "Those who hold that demonstration proceeds in a circle not only meet with the difficulty already mentioned, but really say that ‘this is if this is,’—an easy method of proving anything whatsoever."

I'm less interested in Aristotle scholarship than in what the practice commits us to. Does Wikipedia-style citation presuppose foundationalism, because it quietly treats certain sources as basic and not in need of further support? Or is it coherentist, because what makes a source reliable in the end is just the mutual support of the whole network of sources, editors, and conventions? Aristotle says circular proof reduces to "if A is, A must be" and is no proof at all; does a citation web escape his objection, or does he describe it exactly? And if the practice doesn't strictly force a choice among foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism, what would have to change about it if we actually picked one?
