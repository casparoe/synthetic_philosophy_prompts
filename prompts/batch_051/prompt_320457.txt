You are a professional epistemologist with a detailed working knowledge of the peer disagreement debate, and you are used to the norms of adversarial collaboration: represent each side at its strongest, avoid tendentious language, and separate real cruxes from merely apparent ones. Before drafting, take some time to brainstorm the strongest formulations of both positions and the points where their residual disagreement is sharpest; do not settle for slogans.

I am a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, so I am comfortable with credences, higher-order evidence, calibration, and pre-registered tests. I want a single document: the joint statement that a committed conciliationist and a committed steadfast proponent would produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration about peer disagreement. For this document, take the conciliationist to hold, roughly, that upon discovering that an acknowledged epistemic peer has considered the same evidence and reached a different conclusion about a proposition, you are rationally required to substantially revise your credence toward the peer's (a standard version approximating splitting the difference). Take the steadfast view to hold, roughly, that you may in some cases rationally retain your original credence upon discovering peer disagreement, because correctly weighing the first-order evidence can itself justify the conclusion that your peer has made an error; the peer's contrary judgment matters, but it need not move you toward them. Represent both views in their most defensible forms, and note variation within each camp where that variation matters.

The document should have the following sections:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of substantive claims both camps can sign, addressing at least: a working characterization of an epistemic peer (including whether "same evidence" or "equivalent epistemic position" is the better idealization, and how much idealization the debate can tolerate); what the discovery of peer disagreement evidentially gives each party; which naive versions of each camp both reject; and which adjacent positions (for example, the uniqueness thesis versus permissivism, or the role of higher-order evidence) both sides agree are load-bearing for their dispute.

2. The exact disagreements. A disagreements table or list in which each row states a single residual point of difference in wording both sides would accept, with a conciliationist column and a steadfast column. Avoid framing either side as the skeptical or dogmatic one.

3. Cruxes and pre-registered tests. For each of the following suspected cruxes — (a) whether conciliationism, applied to a disagreement about conciliationism itself, is self-undermining; (b) whether there is a legitimate, non-question-begging asymmetry between one's own first-order reasoning and an epistemic peer's; (c) whether genuine shared-evidence peer disagreement is common enough for the debate to matter outside toy cases — state whether it is in fact a crux, and if so provide at least one concrete case or pre-registerable test that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance and stated in a way that could be wrong.

4. Verbal or apparent disagreements. Identify any part of the dispute that is, on inspection, verbal or definitional, and specify the term or terms that would need to be fixed to dissolve it.

5. A closing statement both camps could sign, recording what the collaboration established, what remains open, and what would move either side.

Do not adjudicate the debate and do not defend one side over the other; the deliverable is the document the two camps could jointly put their names to. Do not include citations or a bibliography; this is an exercise in first-order reasoning, with all terms defined inline.
