At my epistemology reading group last Tuesday, two people got into a long argument about a passage in Robert Nozick's Philosophical Explanations, where he introduces the subjunctive conditions on knowing. We'd read the bit where Nozick argues that knowledge requires the belief to track the truth — if the proposition weren't true, the person wouldn't believe it — and then he says this means knowledge is not always closed under known logical implication. It went back and forth.

Jun said that whatever its weirdness, Nozick's point captures something real: you can know you're looking at a zebra at the zoo, but you don't know it's not a cleverly painted mule. If it were a mule painted to look like a zebra, you'd still think it was a zebra. Your perceptual method tracks zebrahood, but it doesn't track the absence of mulehood.

Elena said that's not an insight, it's a reductio of the tracking conditions. If you genuinely can't rule out the mule scenario, then by ordinary standards you don't know it's a zebra either. Tracking theory doesn't rescue ordinary knowledge from scepticism; it just redefines 'knowledge' so we can say two incompatible-sounding things at once.

Jun answered that the trade-off is exactly the point: deny closure and keep everyday knowledge, or keep closure and get swallowed by scepticism. The tracking theorist is the only one who can explain why sceptical arguments feel compelling without accepting their conclusion.

Elena came back: that's too concessive to the sceptic. You can keep closure and resist scepticism by saying the ordinary belief is safe across the relevant alternatives, without needing subjunctive sensitivity to every logical consequence. And if knowledge doesn't close under known implication, you get a normatively broken state where someone knows p, knows that p implies q, but can't thereby be in a position to know q.

Jun said that last part isn't broken. They can infer q, and then they come to know q by the method of inference, not by the original perceptual method. The original belief and the inferred belief have different tracking profiles, so there's no contradiction.

Elena replied that this fragments knowledge by method in a way that makes deliberation impossible: you have to keep track of which route delivered each premise before knowing what you're entitled to infer. And the route itself can be gerrymandered. Say my method is 'believing whatever seems to be the case in any nearby situation'; then everything tracks trivially. The tracking view needs constraints on method that it never gives.

They asked me to settle it and I honestly couldn't. Please adjudicate the dispute. You're an epistemologist with a background in formal epistemology. Think carefully, first lay out the tracking conditions and the closure principle as Nozick formulates them, then go through the rounds one by one and say who is right about which specific point, what both are missing, and who is more right overall.
