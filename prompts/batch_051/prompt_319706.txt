I have recently taken over as a teaching assistant for a philosophy of law course and am grading the first set of exam essays; because a close family member has recently gone through the sentencing process, the question of what can justify punishment has become personally significant to me, and I want to be careful not to grade hastily. Below are the exam question and one student's answer. Please assign the answer a numerical or letter grade and give me the point-by-point comments that justify that grade. I am particularly interested in subtle errors that a quick read might overlook, or places where the answer sounds right but does not quite follow.

Exam question: Compare retributivist and consequentialist justifications of punishment. The answer should state the central claim of each view, explain the standard objection that consequentialism could permit punishing the innocent, and briefly indicate how a mixed theory might combine the two approaches.

Student answer: Retributivism holds that punishment is justified because the offender deserves it. Its justification is backward-looking, appealing to the offense rather than to any future benefit, and its natural measure of severity is proportionality to the wrong done. Consequentialism, by contrast, holds that punishment is justified by its expected future benefits—deterrence, incapacitation, and rehabilitation—and that punishment is warranted when those benefits outweigh the costs.

The familiar problem for consequentialism is that it appears forced to permit punishing an innocent person whenever doing so would produce the best overall outcome, as in framing a scapegoat to prevent a riot. Consequentialists usually answer that such scapegoating would in practice backfire, since discovery would undermine public trust, but this reply is only an empirical point: if the facts favored scapegoating, the theory would have to license it.

A mixed theory might treat desert as a side-constraint on consequentialist aims, so that only the guilty may be punished and the sentence may not exceed what is deserved, while deterrence and rehabilitation decide whether and how much to punish below that ceiling; some writers call this negative retributivism. Finally, retributivism and consequentialism are less opposed than they first appear, since they answer different questions—retributivism governs who may be punished and within what limits, whereas consequentialism justifies the existence of the practice of punishment in the first place. It follows that once the general practice is justified on consequentialist grounds, the sentence in any particular case should be fixed solely by its expected consequences.
