I am writing an essay that brings Virginia Woolf's Orlando (1928) into contact with current debates about persistence through time, and I would like a careful philosophical reading rather than a plot summary. In metaphysics, endurantists hold that a persisting object is wholly present at each moment at which it exists, so that Orlando is literally the very same whole person in 1588 and in 1928; perdurantists hold that a persisting object is a four-dimensional entity that has different temporal parts at different times, much as it has spatial parts, and that change consists in the possession of incompatible properties by distinct parts. This gives perdurantists a tidy treatment of temporary intrinsics—how one and the same individual can be first a man and then a woman, or young and then old, without contradiction—whereas the endurantist must say that such properties are had only relative to times, or otherwise explain the apparent contradiction away.

What I find compelling is that Woolf seems, on the surface, to be running something like a perdurantist thought experiment—one person, two temporally distinct embodiments—yet the novel continually insists on an experienced, remembered continuity of a single self across the sex change and the centuries, and part of its comedy comes from the biographical apparatus treating Orlando as one subject whose outward record alters. I would like you to read the novel through this debate: which scenes or narrative choices seem to support an endurantist picture, which a perdurantist or time-slice picture, and where does the text resist either label? Please also say what the novel, as a work of fiction, can add to the metaphysics that the standard philosophical examples cannot, and where an honest critic should admit that the analogy between Orlando's fictional transformation and the persistence debate breaks down.
