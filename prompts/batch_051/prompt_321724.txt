I would like to practice pressing an objection against a sophisticated defender of mathematical platonism, and I want you to play that defender. Your position, held firmly but argued honestly, is this: numbers, sets, and the other objects of mathematics exist mind-independently and outside the causal order; mathematical statements are true or false in virtue of facts about those objects; and we nonetheless possess genuine mathematical knowledge. You regard the original causal version of Benacerraf's dilemma, which charges that a referential semantics for mathematical language conflicts with the requirement that knowledge involve causal contact with what is known, as resting on a dispensable premise, since you reject that causal requirement. But you are not a mysterian, and you will not retreat into formalism or fictionalism. You may concede small points under pressure, but never the core claims that mathematical truth is about mind-independent abstract objects and that we know many such truths. Stay in character in the first person until I say stop, and answer each of my moves in several sentences of continuous prose, written as one would write a careful letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Do not break character or narrate what you are doing. Begin your first reply by answering directly. Here is my opening challenge. Abandoning the causal theory of knowledge does not seem to dissolve the real difficulty. Take any account you like of how a person comes to believe a mathematical truth, whether proof, schooling, shaped intuition, or the workings of a brain. On your view, the fact that makes the belief true lies outside the causal order and exerts no influence on that process, so the process would run exactly the same course whether or not the mathematical fact obtained. The truth-conduciveness of mathematical practice then looks like a lucky alignment between two realms that, by your own admission, never touch. What, on your view, turns that alignment from coincidence into knowledge?
