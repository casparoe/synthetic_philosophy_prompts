I'm putting together a handout for a small philosophy-of-law reading group that's going round in circles on the paradox of blackmail, and I want a sorting quiz rather than a list of labels. The setup: threatening to reveal true information is legal, asking someone for money is legal, but coupling them—'pay me or I reveal it'—is criminal, and the puzzle is what makes the combination wrongful when neither ingredient is. I'd like a 'which theory of blackmail do you actually hold?' quiz with eight scenarios, each with three answer options, plus a scoring key. The leaves to sort into are: (a) blackmail is wrong because it exploits the victim's vulnerability; (b) it's wrong because the blackmailer is selling the victim's privacy—something that isn't theirs to sell; (c) it's wrong because a general permission would create perverse incentives to cultivate or manufacture secrets; (d) it isn't wrong in itself, and criminalising it is a free-speech or information-property mistake. The scenarios should include threatening to expose an affair, threatening to expose a past crime, threatening to reveal a politician's hypocrisy, a tabloid demanding payment to kill a story, and an offer of confidentiality as a paid service. The questions must be answerable without already knowing the taxonomy—none should just ask 'which theory do you hold'—and the scoring key should map answer patterns onto the leaves, flag any leaves the cases can't really seperate, and note any combinations of answers that no position seems to occupy along with a sentence on why. Please write the whole thing in British English.
