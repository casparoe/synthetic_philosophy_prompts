I've landed on a view about writing systems that I can't quite shake, and I want a serious map of how exposed it actually is. The view, roughly, is that a script is not best understood as a faithful or unfaithful way of transcribing speech, but as something closer to a theory of the language it writes. Every script has to choose which levels of linguistic structure are real enough to encode, and those choices are the theory. An alphabet commits to the phoneme as the unit that matters; a consonantal script like Arabic or Hebrew commits to the consonant skeleton and leaves the vowels largely for the reader to fill in; Chinese characters commit to something like a morpheme-syllable and refuse to descend to the segment; and a morphophonemic orthography like English's — one that keeps the spelling of a meaningful unit stable even when its pronunciation shifts — is not a badly maintained phonemic alphabet but a script that has chosen to encode morphemes and lexical identity over surface pronunciation. Even the gap between what users say their script encodes and what it actually encodes becomes data on this view: the widespread belief among alphabet users that their spelling is basically 'phonetic' is itself part of the theory the script has trained them into about their own language.

What I'd like from you is the defeaters. Not generic criticisms, but the things a defender of this view would have to take seriously: observations from how scripts are actually read and learned, cases from the history of scripts or from spelling reforms, psycholinguistic results, and arguments about what 'encodes' even means here. Rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Tell me which ones a committed defender would themselves conceed count against the view, and which they'd dismiss as resting on a misunderstanding of the claim. For what it's worth, I find reading-science evidence especially persuasive — if it turned out that skilled readers process surface phonology rather than the deeper units the script supposedly commits to, I'd be moved. I'm also curious whether this view makes any prediction that differs from the deflationary 'scripts are just conventional notation' view — say, about what happens when a spoken language's morphology erodes while its orthography stays put, or about what makes a spelling reform succeed or fail — or whether it has quietly been insulated so that no fact about actual writing could touch it. And if it has been insulated, is that a problem for an interpretive thesis of this kind, or just a cost of the genre?
