I'm a retired chemist, so I used to assume geological time is read off the rocks: the boundary is there or it isn't, and committees just ratify what nature already did. The Anthropocene vote has me doubting that. As I understand the story, the Anthropocene Working Group spent 15 years arguing that the Anthropocene should be a formal epoch starting in the mid-20th century, with the plutonium spike from hydrogen-bomb tests as the marker and Crawford Lake as the proposed golden spike. Then in 2024 the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy voted the proposal down by a wide margin, and the ICS and IUGS left it as an informal term. Analyze that episode through the lens of the realism-vs-conventionalism debate about scientific kinds: is a geological epoch something you discover or something you decide, and what should the stratigraphers have done under each view? Does the rejection show there just wasn't a good enough joint in nature, or that it was a decision dressed up as a discovery all along? And what does the episode leave of the "when did it start" question—does it have an answer now, and if so, who's answer is it? Map it carefully: who the players are, what the golden-spike requirement commits them to, and where the theory stops being useful.
