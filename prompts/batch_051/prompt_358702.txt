I'm not an academic; I came to this view on my own through several years of sitting in local-government technology meetings, and next month I have to defend it in front of people who, for the most part, think I'm making a category mistake. I'm presenting to a mid-sized city's smart-city steering committee: mostly engineers from the transportation and public-safety departments, two data scientists, and the lead product manager from the vendor building the city's new automatic license-plate reader network. The committee members work from a stable background assumption that the hardware and software are neutral tools, that politics enters only upstream when the council sets policy or downstream when a person misuses the system, and that their own job is to deliver something reliable, on budget, and within whatever legal and privacy constraints they are handed. What they care about in the room is meeting deployment targets, avoiding the next procurement embarrassment, and keeping the system off the front page; two of them have said in meetings that fairness is a data-quality metric, not a design question.

The thesis I'm presenting is that this specific system is already a political arrangement disguised as a technical one. My argument, in brief, is that the decisions about where cameras are placed, how long plate records are retained, which other databases they are joined to, and who may query the results are underdetermined by engineering constraints — they could be set many ways; that once the council approves the system as specified, those settings quietly distribute surveillance, deciding who is watched and who is exempt; and that describing all of this as the neutral implementation of a policy makes a political judgment without having to defend one.

I'd like a question-period prep sheet rather than a polished defense: the objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely they are and by how much damage they can do, each phrased the way these engineers and product people would phrase it — in terms of requirements, tradeoffs, scope creep, misuse, and policy — with a two-minute reply for each and a note on where that reply is softest. Include the question I should be most worried someone will ask, the one I need to prepare for rather than hope they miss.
