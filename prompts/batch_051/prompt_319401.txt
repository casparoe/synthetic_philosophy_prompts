I am a novelist drafting a character whose central flaw is meant to be weakness of will — akrasia — rather than mere impulsiveness or poor planning. In the scene I am working on, she has resolved after long reflection to refuse a certain offer, and she refuses it convincingly in the morning; by evening, with the offer in front of her, she takes it, knowing full well that she is undoing her own decision and that she will despise herself afterward. I want to render the inside of that reversal without reaching for the cliché of a soul at war with itself.

An economist friend has been urging me to abandon the "two selves" picture altogether. In the behavioral economics she reads, people discount delayed rewards, and the discount function declines steeply over short delays and more gently over long ones; as a result, an agent's ranking of two future options can reverse as time passes and the options draw near, the earlier and smaller reward overtaking the larger and later one once it becomes imminent. On this account, weakness of will just is hyperbolic discounting: there is a single self distributed across time, equipped with a discount function, and the familiar apparatus of a divided will, a self that struggles and loses, an impulse experienced as alien — these are folk-psychological decorations rather than machinery. Her practical conclusion is that I should write the character as a unified agent whose preferences are time-inconsistent, not as two selves at war; she says this will be truer to the actual structure of self-defeating choice.

I would like an audit of that analogy before I take her advice. Start with the correspondence table: what maps onto what — the present and future selves, the morning resolution, the reversal at the moment of temptation, the sincerity of the resolution, the self-reproach afterward. Then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular, does the discounting story preserve the claim that the agent acts against her own better judgment at the moment she acts, or does it quietly substitute a preference reversal in which she now genuinely wants the near thing? Does it preserve the difference between an impulse she identifies with and one she experiences as an intrusion? Which of the disanalogies matter for my purpose — rendering the phenomenon from the inside — and which are merely technical? Finally, tell me whether the friend's practical conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if the discounting analogy was doing more work than it can carry, what better analogy a novelist might use instead.
