I'm trying to drag two of the philosophy faculty at my university into an adversarial collaboration instead of the usual yearly slapfight, and I want the document they'd co-sign after a semester of back-and-forth. One is an epiphenomenalist about phenomenal consciousness: the felt what-it's-like character is real, it's caused by the physical goings-on, but it never causes anything physical, including the very reports and judgments we make about it. The other is a mental-causation realist about experience, who thinks the qualitative character of an experience is part of the causal explanation of at least some of our judgments and reports about that experience. Draft the joint statement, with this structure. First, a numbered agreed-claims section: what both can sign about the first-person data, the correlations between phenomenal states and reports, the role of neural precursors to reports, and whichever version of physical causal closure each will actually assert (and why they stop where they do). Second, a disagreements table: each row is one load-bearing claim, phrased in the wording each side would accept for its own view, plus a note on whether the other side flatly denies it or reads "cause" and "justify" in two different ways. Third, the cruxes: the subquestions such that a settled answer would make at least one side revise, written so both agree that's exactly what they are. Fourth, for each crux, the test, argument, or thought experiment both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction pre-registered. The epiphenomenalist keeps saying a zombie twin (a physical duplicate with no phenomenal character) would issue the same reports, and that only shows the reports aren't caused by the qualia, not that the reports are false or unjustified; the realist keeps saying that makes the reports evidence-free in a way that undercuts the very data epiphenomenalism was built on. If part of this quarrel is verbal—if "because" or "evidence for" is doing two jobs—say which part and what remains seperately at stake after it's set aside. Don't adjudicate; produce the document they'd both sign, with the leftover disagreement priced precisely.
