Most people believe it is a serious harm to have one's life cut short: someone who dies at forty has lost the decades they would otherwise have lived. But almost no one believes it is a harm to have one's life begin late: a child born forty years later than they might have been, who then lives a life of the same length, is not normally described as having lost the years before their birth. In both cases there are years in which a life containing goods, relationships, and projects could have been lived but was not; the only plain difference is that the unlived years fall after death in the first case and before birth in the second. Is this asymmetry in our ordinary judgments justified, or should we instead believe, with the Epicurean tradition, that it reveals a confusion in the way we think about death and time? Give me the strongest principle you can find that treats losing a possible future as a real harm but losing a possible past as no harm at all, test it against at least one nearby case (for example, a person kept unconscious during an interval that could have been part of their life, or a person whose birth was delayed by an accident), and then tell me honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. Please keep your answer to at most 500 words.
