I'm a novelist. I've been running a low-stakes Friday poker game out of my garage for a couple years now, mostly because I'm writing a book about a professional gambler and I want the table talk to ring true. Last night two of my regulars got into a long argument about stake sizing that I only half followed, and I'd like to use a version of it in the novel — but I need to know which parts actually hold up before I put them in a character's mouth. I kept out of it at the table and I'm not going to tip my hand here either. Can you go through it move by move?

(1) Marcus, to Elena: "You always leave chips on the table. Say you've got a real 60/40 edge on an even-money bet. Every dollar you stake earns twenty cents in expectation, so the more you bet, the more you expect to make. If your actually trying to maximize expected money, you don't stop at a fifth of your roll like you do — you bet as much as they'll take."

(2) Elena: "Because I'm not trying to maximize expected dollars over every possible history. I'm trying to grow a bankroll I have to keep playing from. If I bet it all, one loss ends me. What I want to maximize is the growth rate per bet — the expected log of the multiplier on my roll — and for a 60/40 even-money bet that peaks around twenty percent. A fifth of my roll isn't leaving money on the table; it is the maximum, in the only sense that matters for a long-run gambler."

(3) Marcus: "See, you just admitted it. You want to maximize expected log wealth. That's a utility function, and a weirdly cautious one. If I don't have logarithmic utility — and I don't — then Kelly is just a theorem about somebody else. You can't dress up a risk preference like its a law of nature."

(4) Elena: "It's not a preference, it's what compounding does. Over a long run your wealth gets multiplied by a string of factors, and the growth rate of the single history you actually live is the average of the logs of those factors — the log of the geometric mean. Kelly maximizes that rate. If you overbet, more than twice that twenty percent, the rate goes negative and you drift toward zero almost surely. Utility doesn't enter it."

(5) Marcus: "Almost surely in the limit, sure. We play maybe sixty hands a year. And that growth rate is still an expectation over possible histories; you've just done the averaging in log space. The geometric mean is a median-ish number, and you can't buy anything with a median. If a bet had huge expected value but a fifty-five percent chance of leaving you down half your roll, you'd pass. That's prudence, not mathematics."

(6) Elena: "That's exactly my point. In a multiplicative gamble the median ending wealth does converge on the geometric mean, and the arithmetic mean gets dragged up by the tiny set of histories where you hit an absurd run of winners. Those histories are real, but they're not the one you're going to live. Make decisions for the single history you actually get, not the average of a thousand parallel gamblers."

(7) Marcus: "Now you're just telling me to be prudent. Fine, be prudent — but call it prudence, not rationality."

Please adjudicate it: which moves are solid, which are off, where the real disagreement sits, what neither of them thought to say, and who's more right overall.
