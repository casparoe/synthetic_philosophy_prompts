I'm a PhD student working on higher-order theories of consciousness, and I keep getting stuck on the same image. Everyone in the area—and honestly Locke himself—talks as if being conscious of a mental state is basically like percieving it: an "inner eye" pointed at your own mind. Locke floats the analogy in An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (Book II, chapter 1, section 4), right after listing the operations of the mind (perception, thinking, doubting, believing, reasoning, knowing, willing):

"This Source of Ideas, every Man has wholly in himself: And though it be not Sense, as having nothing to do with external Objects; yet it is very like it, and might properly enough be call'd internal Sense."

The "very like it" is doing a lot of work, and I want to know how much work it can actually bear. The analogy gets leaned on from both directions: Lycan-style inner-sense theorists use it to say consciousness is a quasi-perceptual monitoring faculty, while Rosenthal-style HOT theorists use the disanalogies (no sense organ, no transducers, no way to be "perception-blind" to your own mind) to argue the higher-order state has to be a thought, not a perception. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the sense organ, the sensory medium, the external object, and error or illusion in the inner-sense picture? Then say where the mapping breaks, which of those breaks matter for the debate and which are just loose talk, and wether the inner-sense conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table—or whether Locke's calling it "Reflection" a few words later was the better call all along. I don't want a survey of the literature; I want an audit of the analogy.
