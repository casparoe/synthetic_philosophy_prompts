I've spent the last few weeks trying to understand Pearl's causal hierarchy for a piece on what machine-learning models can and can't infer about cause and effect, and a statistician I interviewed sent me an argument that seems to prove the hierarchy is mostly marketing. Here's my reconstruction, with the steps numbered because I keep loosing the thread. (1) For any effect that's identifiable, the do-calculus lets you derive an interventional distribution P(y | do(x)) from two ingredients: the observational joint distribution P(v) and a causal graph G. (2) But P(y | do(x)) is what people mean by the causal effect of x on y, so the causal effect follows from observational facts plus a graph. (3) And the graph itself can be estimated from observational data by standard causal-discovery algorithms, at least up to a class of observationally equivalent graphs. (4) So for a wide class of identifiable effects, rung 2 of Pearl's hierarchy is reachable entirely from rung 1 — observed correlations plus a graph you fit to those same correlations. (5) Yet the whole point of the hierarchy is that the rungs are strict: purely observational input, with no causal assumptions already built in, can't get you interventional results. (6) Therefore either the do-calculus is sneaking in causal information it pretends not to use, or the hierarchy isn't strict after all. Both of those feel wrong to me, but I can't locate the bad step. Where does the argument go wrong? If it doesn't, which premise is the least costly to give up, and what does giving it up cost? I'd like to keep the strict hierarchy, but not at the price of declaring causal discovery meaningless. And please, where I'm leaning on contested claims, seperate what's actually settled among people who work on causal inference from what's still disputed — don't present one research camp as the consensus.
