I'm reading Hume's *A Treatise of Human Nature*, Book 3, Part 2, Section 5 ("Of the obligation of promises"), and I think I've found an argument that proves too much. The key passage is:

"A promise creates a new obligation. A new obligation supposes new sentiments to arise. The will never creates new sentiments. There could not naturally, therefore, arise any obligation from a promise, even supposing the mind could fall into the absurdity of willing that obligation."

Here's the argument I reconstruct from it:

(1) In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, two self-interested people each do better by defecting than by cooperating, whatever the other does.

(2) A promise to cooperate can create a new obligation only if willing the promise creates a new sentiment or motive to cooperate.

(3) But willing cannot create a new sentiment; that's what Hume says in the passage.

(4) So a promise cannot give a self-interested person a reason to cooperate in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma.

(5) Therefore, promises cannot explain cooperation in one-shot prisoner's dilemmas.

(6) But in the iterated prisoner's dilemma, cooperation can emerge from reciprocity and repetition alone, without any promise.

(7) So promises are either useless (because repetition already handles cooperation) or impossible sources of obligation (because no one can will a new sentiment). Either way, the whole practice of promising seems to be a wheel that turns nothing.

Where does this go wrong? I'd like to keep the game-theoretic point that one-shot defection is rational, but I also want to keep something like Hume's view that promises are human inventions. So which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? Please go through the argument step by step, and write the answer in American English.
