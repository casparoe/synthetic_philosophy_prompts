I've been mulling over a weird feature of how the law handles vague predicates, and I can't work out what semantic view it commits us to. In England and Wales you're legally an adult for almost all purposes at the stroke of midnight on your 18th birthday—a minute earlier you can't vote, buy alcohol, or be held to most contracts; a minute later you can. The drink-drive limit is similar: it's set at some precise number of milligrams of alcohol per 100 millilitres of blood, and a reading one milligram either side of the line changes whether you've committed a criminal offence. Yet 'adult' and 'drunk' and 'safe to drive' are exactly the predicates that generate sorites puzzles: if you're not an adult one second before turning 18, how could that final second make you one? The law doesn't merely tolerate these sharp lines, though—it builds criminal and civil liability on them, as if there were a fact of the matter about where the boundary falls.

I'm not asking what the law should do instead; I get the pragmatic case for bright lines. What I want is the implicit semantics: what theory of vagueness does this practice presuppose? Does it quietly commit the legal system to epistemicism—the view that there is a sharp, unknowable boundary out there in the world—or is the cutoff just a stipulated line that's strictly compatible with supervaluationism, degree theory, contextualism and the rest? And what about the fact that the same system happily uses vague standards like beyond reasonable doubt or reasonable care when it suits it? Do different bits of legal practice presuppose different, possibly inconsistent theories of vagueness? If we actually endorsed a degree-theoretic picture, would a legally enforced cutoff become philosophically inconsistent, or would it just need a different justification—and what would that justification look like?
