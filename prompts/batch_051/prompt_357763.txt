I keep meeting a one-liner in arguments about science: "Every successful scientific theory of the past has eventually turned out to be wrong, so today's best theories will probably turn out to be wrong too." I've seen it used against people who say physics has discovered real things about the world, and I've seen it used to suggest that believing in atoms or electrons today is no more reasonable than believing in phlogiston or the ether was in their day. I'm sure the sentence is doing some real argumentative work, but I can't tell exactly what work, because each time I try to pin it down the words slide around.

Could you take that slogan apart for me? I want the distinct precise claims it could be making — about the historical record itself, about what it means to say a past theory was "wrong," about whether a theory can be replaced and still be partly right, about what the track record licenses us to infer about current theories, and about how much confidence current science deserves. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or probably false, and say which readings the slogan quietly slides between in ordinary use.

Then say which reading or readings would actually have to be true for the slogan to support the conclusion that current well-confirmed theories should be trusted much less — and whether any precise version of the slogan gets to that conclusion without overreaching.

Please structure the response exactly as follows:

- The slogan unpacked (about 500 words): give at least five distinct readings, each in one or two precise sentences, with a one-line verdict for each (trivial, defensible, false, or needs qualification). Try to make the readings genuinely different in what they claim, not just different wordings of the same claim.

- What the argument needs (about 300 words): identify which reading or readings would have to be true for the slogan to justify lowering confidence in current physics, and say whether the slogan as usually repeated establishes those readings or simply helps itself to them.

- Settled versus contested (about 250 words): separate the parts of this issue that are broadly agreed by people who work on it from the parts that remain live disputes. Where a view is contested, say so plainly and do not present it as the consensus.

- Bottom line (about 100 words): in plain language, what I should and should not take away from the slogan when I next hear it.
