I'm a high school student in a philosophy of biology reading group, and we've been working through the debate between etiological (selected-effects) accounts of biological function and causal-role accounts. I'm trying to build a complete map of the standard objections to the etiological account, and so far I've collected four:

(1) The Swampman case: a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person, assembled by chance with no evolutionary history, would have a heart that pumps blood; on the etiological account, its heart has no function, which many find counterintuitive.

(2) The brand-new beneficial mutation: a mutation that helps an organism right away has not yet been selected, so the etiological account seems to withhold a function from it even though it is doing useful biological work.

(3) Exaptation: feathers were selected for thermoregulation, not for flight; the etiological account therefore seems to say their function is thermoregulation, which understates their current role in flight.

(4) Vestigial structures: the wings of an ostrich were selected in its ancestors for flight, so the account assigns them the function of flying even though they cannot fly.

I'm looking for one more objection or case that is genuinely different from these four — not a restatement, a special case, or a combination of them. In particular, I'd like one that does not depend on selection history being absent (as in (1) and (2)) or on history and current role coming apart in the usual exaptation/vestigial way (as in (3) and (4)). It should pressure the etiological account from a new direction. Explain what the case is, why it is not already covered by my list, and whether you think it is a fair objection or one with a standard reply. Finally, be explicit about what would change your mind: which consideration would lead you to withdraw the proposed case as not genuinely distinct, or would show that it misdescribes the etiological account's actual commitments?
