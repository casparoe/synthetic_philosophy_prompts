I recently had to think about Aristotle’s defense of natural slavery after a family member’s school began sorting students into vocational and academic tracks on the basis of cognitive testing. That led me back to Book I of the *Politics*, where Aristotle distinguishes a kind of person who is not made a slave by mere law or capture but is naturally suited to be ruled: such a person, on his account, can share in reason enough to recognize and obey it, but not enough to deliberate about the ends of life, and is therefore better off under another’s authority.

I want to stress-test that argument through a concrete counterfactual. Suppose a wealthy liberal democracy develops a highly reliable early-childhood test that identifies people matching Aristotle’s description—they can understand and follow rules, but are poor at independent deliberation about what to do with their lives. At age ten, those identified are placed in a permanent legal status of wardship: they cannot vote, make contracts, hold office, or choose their occupation, but they are guaranteed material support and assigned to guardians with a fiduciary duty to act in their interests. The state defends this explicitly as the natural ordering of different human kinds, not as punishment or failure.

Give me your reasoned prediction of how this institution plays out over the next two generations. I want the chain of reasoning made visible: which consequences follow robustly from the wardship institution itself, which depend on contingent facts such as test reliability, guardian incentives, or the economic position of the classified group, and where the Aristotle-inspired justification is likely to diverge from the institution’s actual dynamics. In particular, address whether the system would remain stable, whether the class of wards would tend to expand or contract, and whether the idea of their being “better off ruled” would survive contact with the actual behavior and capacities of the people classified.
