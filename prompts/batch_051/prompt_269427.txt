I've been mulling over a principle I call 'earned simplicity' in ethical theory, and I want someone to take it apart honestly. The idea: parsimony is not a free-standing virtue the way fit with intuitions is; it only becomes a tie-breaker once a theory has already captured the intuitions in the immediate vicinity of the one it wants to outvote. So a two-principle theory that nails the deontic landscape accept for one weird clause can cite its small number of principles against that clause, because its earned the right to treat the clause as noise. But a ruthlessly minimal theory that gets its simplicity by refusing to even address whole regions of intuitive territory hasn't earned anything, so its parsimony counts for nothing. I suppose I'm claiming that simplicity can only dismiss an intuition from inside reflective equilibrium, never from the outside. Is this just a dressed-up version of something Ross or Rawls already said, and does the 'neighbourhood' condition actually do any work? And where does it break — for instance, when theories disagree about which intuitions count as 'nearby', or when the disputed intuition is isolated and central rather than marginal?
