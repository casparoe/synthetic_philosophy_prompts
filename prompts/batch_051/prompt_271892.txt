I'm a novelist, and for years I've been turning over a parallel between what I do for a living and a certain view of consciousness — the user-illusion view, where the self you experience is a simplified interface the brain generates, and the real work is happening elsewhere, in subpersonal processes that don't correspond to anything in the story.

Here's the parallel. When I write a character, I never actually put an inner life on the page. I put down a handful of details: a hesitation, a phrase the character repeats, the way she folds a receipt. The reader then supplies the rest — the continuity across scenes, the unspoken history, the sense that there's a person in there even when nobodys watching. The felt depth of the character is mostly reader-side; the text itself is astonishingly thin compared to what people report experiencing.

That looks a lot like the self on the user-illusion account: a tidy, serialized story of a person making choices and having moods, layered over a massively parallel mess that doesn't actually run the way the story says it does. In both cases you get a rich, first-person-seeming narrative produced over something much sparser and less story-shaped.

My question is whether this is a real structural analogy or just the word "illusion" doing all the work. If it's real, what would have to be true on both sides for anything to transfer? For instance, what does the character case predict about the self case, and does that prediction come out false? And if it breaks, where exactly? One spot where I suspect it might snap: in fiction, the cues are deliberately planted by an author and assembled by a separate reader, so the illusion can be caught from the outside by rereading, whereas in the self case the reader and the author are both the brain, and there isnt anything outside to catch it. I can't tell whether that's a fatal difference or me being sentimental about my job.
