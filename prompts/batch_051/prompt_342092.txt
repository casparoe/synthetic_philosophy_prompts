I sit on a grant panel. Last month I assessed two applications: one proposed to investigate a conjecture generated by a machine-learning model — no proof, but it held on every case the team had tested — and the other proposed to investigate a human mathematician's conjecture with the same unproved, case-consistent profile. I gave the human proposal a higher score, and my stated reason was that the machine was almost certainly interpolating within or near its training distribution, whereas the human, who has a record of good guesses, might be making a genuine intuitive leap beyond it. My broader policy is this: when a result has no derivation, I treat a machine's having produced it as adding almost no evidential weight over and above the cases that support it, but I treat a human's having produced it as adding weight through their track record. Is this policy defensible, or is it a guild prejudice dressed as an epistemic rule? Answer it as a question about the policy, not just the two applications: under what assumptions does the asymmetry track a real difference in how novelty beyond a training distribution can arise; under what conditions would the policy fail; can it be restated to keep whatever is defensible without making provenance a credential; and am I, as a grant panelist with limited time, entitled to keep relying on it?
