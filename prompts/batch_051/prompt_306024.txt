Epistemologists have spent nearly two decades on peer disagreement, and the dispute between conciliationists and steadfasters does not look any closer to resolution than when it began. Both sides can handle the stock cases; both have ready replies to the obvious counterexamples; the same moves — the independence principle, the right-reasons objection, the bootstrapping worry, the first-person/third-person asymmetry — keep being traded without moving anyone. My working hypothesis is that the two camps are not quite answering the same question: conciliationism is a third-personal norm about how a rational agent's confidence should respond to the bare fact that an apparent peer disagrees, whereas steadfastness is a first-personal account of whether a conscientious reasoner may retain her belief given how the evidence actually struck her. If that's right, the appearance of contradiction depends on an under-argued assumption that the other party really is an epistemic peer, and the notion of an epistemic peer may be doing most of the work. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a substantive disagreement underneath that I'm missing? And on your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: abandoning peer talk, formalizing the two questions separately, a different kind of case, or something else?
