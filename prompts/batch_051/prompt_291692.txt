Last month I ran a graduate seminar on degrees of consciousness, and after we discussed a recent paper on whether insects feel pain, the discussion outgrew the classroom. I am still not sure how to score it, and I would like a careful adjudication. The dispute went roughly as follows.

Dana defended a thoroughgoing gradualism. She said the paper's evidence — nociception, wound-directed grooming, behavior that trades off painlike states against other motivations — made it plausible that a bee is somewhat sentient: not sentient in the way a human is, but genuinely so, to a lesser degree. Once one admits that, she argued, the moral picture must be scalar too. Sentience is what underwrites moral status, so status comes in degrees, running down from us through mice and bees. And since the underlying capacities — sensing the environment, adjusting internal state, acting on it — are themselves continuous, there is no non-arbitrary point at which the scale stops. She was willing to say that a thermostat sits at an extreme lower end of the same gradient: it detects a variable and alters its state in response, and although its degree is vanishingly small, it is not nothing.

Marcus replied that this is a Sorites with a moral conclusion. A thermostat is not "a little bit" sentient; it has no point of view at all. He said Dana was conflating the degree of sentience, once it exists, with the fact of there being a subject in the first place. He compared her position to a dimmer switch: brightness is graded, but a lamp is either on or off. And he said it was a further slide, not an argument, to move from graded sentience to graded moral status: moral status might be threshold-structured even if the capacity underlying it is continuous, much as the law imposes an age of majority on a continuously developing maturity.

Dana answered that the dimmer analogy begs the question, because it assumes a binary circuit when the argument is exactly about whether nature contains anything analogous to one. She said it was now Marcus who needed the Sorites solution: he owes a principled line — a physical discontinuity in nervous systems between the last sentient creature and the first insentient one — and none is forthcoming. The moral cutoff, she granted, might be useful policy, but it is a concession rather than a metaphysics: the underlying reality remains graded, and any floor is a matter of practical caution, not a joint in nature.

Marcus came back that nature supplies sharp transitions born of continuous variation all the time; a liquid becomes a solid at a precise temperature. And he said the whole dispute was located in the wrong place: sentience indicators are graded, but what they indicate is not. Someone in dreamless sleep is a subject with currently no experiential contents; the subject is a standing capacity of a living brain, not a point on a brightness dial. A thermostat lacks even that standing capacity, and a creature that lacked it could not have even the smallest moral claim.

Dana ended by asking what non-question-begging feature the dreamless sleeper has that a thermostat lacks, if "standing capacity" is allowed to include response dispositions realized in physical systems. She added that phase transitions obey laws that specify where the jump occurs, while Marcus was invoking them as a promissory note. On the moral point, she said the scalar view does not force absurd bedside practice: a thermostat can rank so low that almost nothing turns on it, yet zero remains a stopping rule we impose, not something we discover.

I stayed quiet for most of this, partly because each correction seemed to regain lost ground. Please write the referee's report I could not give.
