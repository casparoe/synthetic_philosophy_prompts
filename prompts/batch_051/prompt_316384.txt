Bit of an odd one. I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I somehow got asked to give a short talk at a philosophy-of-mind workshop on whether the human mind could be a machine. I've decided to defend a modest, qualified version of the Lucas–Penrose line. Not the strong claim that humans provably outcompute every Turing machine, but the weaker claim that once you're in a position to endorse some specific formal system as the correct theory of your own arithmetic competence, you're already in a position to see its Gödel sentence as true—and that sentence lies outside what the system can prove. So there's an instability built into any attempt to fully identify the mind with one formal system from the inside. The room is mostly philosophers of mind with physicalist sympathies, plus two logicians who know Gödel's theorems cold and one ML researcher who thinks scale is basically all you need. Give me the Q&A prep sheet, in American English: the objections I'll actualy get from this crowd, ordered by how much damage they'd do, phrased in their vocabulary, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft, and ending with the question I'm praying nobody asks.
