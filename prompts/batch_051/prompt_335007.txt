For several years a discussion in my church small group has circled the same territory without anyone moving, and I'd like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a ruling on who is right. The question is what the crucifixion actually accomplished. The facts aren't in dispute among us: Jesus was executed, and the New Testament treats his death as somehow dealing with human sin. The disagreement is entirely about the mechanism. One side holds what I've gathered is close to the satisfaction view: sin has left an unpaid debt of honor or justice owed to God, and the cross repays that debt, restoring the order wrongdoing broke. A second side says any debt language misses the point and defends something like penal substitution: our sins deserved punishment, and Jesus bore that punishment in our place, so that the sentence justice required was actually served on him. A third side is uncomfortable with both pictures and argues that the cross changes us rather than God: it is the decisive display of divine love, meant to break our attachment to sin and draw us into a new way of living. They call this the moral account, and they say the first two portray God as needing payment before he can forgive. The first two reply that if the cross only influences us, then forgiveness costs nothing and sin is never really dealt with.

What frustrates me is that all three groups agree on nearly everything else. They accept the same texts, the same broad theology, and the same conclusions that the cross was necessary and effective. And yet, in five years, no one has budged. I've begun to suspect the real disagreement isn't about the evidence at all, but about what certain words have to mean: whether forgiveness by its nature requires someone to absorb a cost rather than collect one, whether justice requires that guilt land on somebody, whether God's being just is compatible with simply setting sin aside. But I don't trust my own sense of it, and I can't tell whether I'm seeing the structure clearly or just reshuffling my own assumptions.

What kind of disagreement is this, at bottom? Is it a hidden verbal dispute, a difference about which parts of the texts should count as central, a difference about what justice and forgiveness must consist in, or a contest between bedrock intuitions about whether love can deal with wrongdoing without any transfer of punishment? And given whatever diagnosis you reach, what would actually count as progress: a distinction that could unstick the group, a question each side should be expected to answer before pressing its case, a reframing of what is at stake, or a reason to expect the impasse to persist no matter what is said?
