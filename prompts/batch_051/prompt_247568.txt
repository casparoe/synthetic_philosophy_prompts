I've been trying to get a handle on eliminative materialism, and there's one part of the old case that keeps bugging me. The standard move is to say folk psychology is a stagnant, pre-scientific theory: it's been stuck for centuries, it can't explain mental illness or sleep or memory, and so belief and desire are like phlogiston, waiting for neuroscience to sweep them away. I get the force of that. But then I look at how people actually work with language models today. They debug, prompt, red-team, and fine-tune these systems by saying things like 'the model believes the user is asking for X,' 'the model wants to be helpful,' 'it's trying to game the reward signal.' This isnt just PR fluff—engineers lean on these attributions to predict what the system will do next and to decide how to intervene on it. And we have the weights; there's no mystery organ in there. So here's the question: take the classic eliminativist argument from the stagnation and predictive failures of folk psychology, and tell me whether it survives this development. Does the fact that belief-desire talk is genuinely useful for predicting and controlling LLMs blunt the argument, does it actually help the eliminativist (look, the system does all that without any real beliefs in there), or is it just a red herring because LLMs are the wrong kind of system? I want you to identify which premise of the argument is doing the work, say whether it still holds in this setting, and then either repair the argument or explain what weaker claim an eliminativist should retreat to. Don't end on 'it's complicated'—defend a position.
