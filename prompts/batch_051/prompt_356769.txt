I have been helping a small team decide whether to train an AI assistant with process-based or outcome-based supervision, and while reading the case for rewarding each intermediate reasoning step rather than only the final answer, I kept feeling that I had heard the argument before, in an old debate in political economy. The case for process supervision is that a scalar outcome signal—an answer is wrong—arrives too late and too compressed to tell the system which step in a long chain of reasoning went bad, or why; by the time the error surfaces, the information that would have localized the fault has been discarded, and the learning procedure must solve a nearly blind credit-assignment problem. That struck me as the same move Hayek makes against Lange. Lange proposed that a central planning board could set prices for capital goods and adjust them by trial and error, raising prices when a good showed excess demand and lowering them when it showed excess supply. Hayek replied that the board would be adjusting in the dark: a shortage observed at the final stage is itself a scalar signal that no longer contains the dispersed, local, often tacit knowledge about which of many upstream decisions produced it, so the board cannot know what to reprice or why.

The mapping I cannot decide about is this: Lange's board is an outcome-supervision scheme, since it observes only end-state disequilibria, while markets look like process supervision, since prices at each stage of production carry local information at the point where the relevant decision is made. If that mapping is right, then the difficulty with outcome-only reward in long-horizon AI reasoning and Hayek's difficulty with Lange-style planning are the same difficulty under two names: a global signal, whether a wrong final answer or a final-stage shortage, does not decompose into instructions about which local decision to change. But I am not sure the analogy is deep rather than a pun on the word signal. Deep learning does train from scalar losses, and it works because backpropagation decomposes the scalar error into a gradient for every layer, giving each local node its own error signal; would that count as outcome supervision or as something closer to process supervision? What would the economic analogue of a gradient be—a price, or something else? I would like to know whether the parallel is a genuine structural identity, a loose analogy, or a confusion; what would have to be true on both sides for lessons to transfer; and whether the analogy predicts something that turns out false in either the AI case or the economic case.
