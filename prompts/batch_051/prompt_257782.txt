I'm a cog sci PhD student and a friend in a policy-adjacent lab just sent around a proposal for a "Reverse Virality Fund," and my study group is split on whether its clever or just a machine for laundering spin. An antimeme, as she uses the term, is a true, decision-relevant idea that people don't retain or transmit — they nod at it and forget it, or they can recite it but never think of it in the actual situation where it matters; base rates, compounding, selection effects, that kind of thing. The Fund would work like this. Each month an expert panel picks a short list of candidate antimemes: empirically solid, important-on-paper claims that underperform in public recall. Creators — journalists, podcasters, textbook writers, whoever — opt in to carry one of the claims, and they pre-register the exact framing or example they'll use to get it across. Two months later, a stratified random sample of adults takes a surprise-ish quiz on the claims that were carried; the quiz has to test whether the concept is actually being retrieved in a shifted context, not just whether people memorized the words. Creators get payed per panel member who uses the claim more or less correctly, with a cap so no one can monopolize a claim. The funding comes from a levy on posts that cross a high-virality threshold on a major platform — tax virality to subsidize anti-virality, basically.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a mood. Walk me through the incentives it creates among creators, the expert panel, the quiz writers and graders, and the people being quizzed. Where does it get gamed first, and how does that reshape which antimemes get picked and which framings of them actually end up spreading? Does paying for retained belief in pre-approved true claims converge on those claims getting genuinely stickier, or does it mostly produce a new genre of clickbait, a market for teaching to the test, or a reason to choose only claims that are easy to quiz? And can any of that be fixed without gutting the scheme?
