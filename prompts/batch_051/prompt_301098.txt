In Plato’s Republic Book VII, shortly after the cave allegory, Socrates warns against thinking of education as a craft that puts sight into an organ that lacks it. He says the soul’s organ of understanding is already present; the difficulty is that it is turned in the wrong direction. Education, on his account, is therefore an art of turning the whole soul around, away from what comes to be and passes away, until it can endure the sight of real being and of the brightest part of real being, which he calls the Good (cf. 518c–d). I am a philosophy professor, and I would like you to write a short dialogue between two equally competent interpreters of this passage. One interprets it as committing Plato to a conversionist epistemology: the soul must undergo a total reorientation of desire and character before it can know the forms, so that dialectic by itself is insufficient. The other reads it as an intellectualist account: the turning is achieved by dialectic itself, and the passage uses the language of conversion only because the soul’s attachment to the visible is a cognitive error, not a condition that needs non-rational therapy. The two should argue over what the metaphor of turning actually does: whether it names a precondition for understanding the Good or a dramatic description of what understanding feels like. Require them to respond to each other’s specific exegetical moves, to raise the strongest objections rather than convenient caricatures, and to consider whether their disagreement is substantive or partly a disagreement about how literally to read Plato’s imagery.
