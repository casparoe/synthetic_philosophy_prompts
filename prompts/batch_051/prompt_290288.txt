I am working through David Hume's essay "Of Suicide" and have become stuck on a step in his argument that suicide cannot be a transgression of our duty to God. I would like help understanding the passage below, which occurs early in that section:

"All events, in one sense, may be pronounced the action of the almighty: They all proceed from those powers, with which he has endowed his creatures. A house, which falls by its own weight, is not brought to ruin by his providence more than one destroyed by the hands of men; nor are the human faculties less his workmanship than the laws of motion and gravitation."

My difficulty is not with Hume's conclusion but with the structure of the inference. He seems to be saying that because God governs through general laws, no event—whether natural or voluntary—is more or less an act of God than any other. But if that is right, why does this show that suicide is not *wrong* against God? It seems to show, at most, that God's relation to a suicide is the same as his relation to any other event. How does sameness of divine causation remove moral blameworthiness? And if the argument works for suicide, does it also imply that no human act can be a sin against God, since all events alike proceed from God-given powers? I would be grateful for a reconstruction of the argument and an assessment of whether this move depends on a particular early modern view of providence that I might be missing.
