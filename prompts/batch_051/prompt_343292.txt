I am a retired physicist whose career was spent in nonlinear dynamics and statistical mechanics, and I have recently been reading philosophy, especially work on longtermism and the hinge of history hypothesis. As I understand it, that hypothesis holds that the present period has unusually high leverage over the long-run future: rapid technological and economic growth appears to be a transient rather than a stable condition, catastrophic risks are plausibly concentrated in the coming centuries, and choices made now about technology, institutions, and risk could therefore have an outsized effect on whether the far future is large and flourishing, small and damaged, or absent altogether.

I would like you to do two things. First, restate the hinge of history hypothesis as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I know best, without weakening the claim or silently importing assumptions. The vocabulary is dynamical systems and statistical mechanics: deterministic and stochastic trajectories, sensitivity to initial conditions, Lyapunov exponents, bifurcations and control parameters, basins of attraction, critical slowing down, phase transitions, correlation lengths, coarse-graining, transient dynamics, absorbing states, and the distinction between ensemble averages and single realized histories. In the restatement, please separate the different claims that seem to be bundled into the hypothesis, including a dynamical claim about the sensitivity of the far future to present actions, a claim about the distribution and timing of catastrophic risks, and a normative claim about how much the far future should matter to us now, and translate each on its own terms.

Second, give me a residue report. Tell me which parts of the hypothesis survive the translation intact, which parts can be made to fit only by distortion or by adding machinery the physical vocabulary does not itself supply, and which parts have no counterpart at all. Finally, say what you think the residue tells us: whether the untranslatable elements are the parts doing the real work, or whether they are less precise than they looked in their original language. I want a translation, not an analogy, and I expect the failures of translation to be the most informative part of the answer.
