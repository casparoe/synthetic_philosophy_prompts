I retired a few years ago after a career in physical chemistry, and I've been using the time to read philosophy of science — mostly the realism debate, because I spent my whole working life assuming our theories are getting at something real and I want to know whether that assumption can survive the history of science. Last week a friend from my reading group, who is a bit further along than me on this stuff, summarized a position called structural realism over lunch. As I remember it, her summary went roughly like this: structural realism says you should be a realist about the mathematical structure of your theories but an antirealist about the entities those theories postulate. The argument is that when theories change, the entities get thrown out — the ether, phlogiston, caloric — but the equations often carry over. Fresnel's equations for reflection and refraction were derived from a picture of light as vibrations in an elastic ether, but the same equations stayed put when light was reconceived as an electromagnetic wave; same math, different story about what was really going on. So you should commit to the structure — the equations and the relations they express — and remain agnostic about the natures and existence of the unobservable things. She also said that in its strongest form, structural realism implies electrons don't exist, and that all there really is is the web of relations. I pushed back and said this sounds like dressed-up instrumentalism, and she got a bit annoyed and said no, it's a realism because it accepts the no-miracles argument: our theories are too successful to be a coincidence, so the structure must be true.

I can't shake the suspicion that this is partly right and partly a caricature, and its the "only the equations are true" part that bothers me most. The Fresnel story fits my own experience — formalisms did survive conceptual upheavals in chemistry too — but "electrons don't exist" sounds absurd, and I don't yet see why committing to the math is anything more than saying the math is useful for prediction. I've since come across the terms "epistemic structural realism" and "ontic structural realism" in an encyclopedia, and I have the feeling my friend ran them together, but I don't know enough to untangle that on my own.

So here's what I'd like. Please act as an expert philosopher of science who works on the realism/antirealism debate. Before writing your final answer, take a paragraph to brainstorm the main versions of structural realism and how a careful defender would typically respond to objections. Then go through my friend's summary claim by claim, and seperate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what a structural realist would reject or want to qualify. Finally, give me a verdict on whether the "electrons don't exist" reading and the "it's just instrumentalism" charge are fair. Please write your answer in American English.
