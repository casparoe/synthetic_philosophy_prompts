I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep running into a comparison that I suspect is doing a lot of work in arguments about law. In my world, dead code is code that remains checked into the codebase and could in principle be executed, but no current code path actually calls it. Engineers mostly treat dead code as a maintenance nuisance and a latent risk: it is not doing anything right now, but it can mislead someone reading the code, and if some old or unexpected path does call it, it may run on assumptions that no longer hold. The analogy I keep hearing is that a statute that is never enforced—desuetude, as I have learned to call it—is just dead code in the legal system. The conclusion people draw from this is that we should not worry much about unenforced laws: just as a codebase with some dead code can still be a perfectly sound system, a legal system with some never-enforced statutes can still be fully governed by the rule of law, and removing such laws is housekeeping rather than an urgent matter of principle.

I want to know how far this analogy actually holds. Please give me an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of the codebase, the call path, the dead function, the maintainer, the runtime, the end user, and the rest. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which of those breakdowns matter for the specific conclusion above rather than being merely cosmetic differences. In particular, I want to know whether the analogy quietly imports assumptions from software systems—for example, that unexecuted instructions are behaviorally inert, that components can be deleted without changing the behavior of the rest of the system, or that someone has privileged knowledge of what is really called—that do not hold for a legal system. Does the relaxed conclusion about desuetude survive once those disanalogies are on the table, or was the dead-code analogy carrying it the whole time? If a better analogy is available, say what it is and what it gets right.

One further request: if my question rests on a confused premise, or if the analogy is too broken for the comparison to be informative at all, please say so plainly rather than politely generating a mapping anyway. And where the answer depends on a contested view about law—for instance, about whether the point of a legal system is to guide conduct or to constrain officials—mark that explicitly and tell me how confident I should be in the parts of the answer that depend on it.
