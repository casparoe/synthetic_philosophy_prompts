Physicists and philosophers of physics have often defended a convergence argument for a final theory. Its core is that the history of physics is a sequence of reductions and unifications: thermodynamics was absorbed into statistical mechanics, chemistry into quantum mechanics, and so on, with each deeper theory treating its predecessor as a special or effective case. The argument takes this pattern to support the rational expectation of a final theory: a most fundamental set of laws from which everything else can in principle be derived and beyond which no further reduction is needed. A load-bearing assumption here is that the hierarchy of theories has a bottom, and that the laws at that bottom determine, at least in principle, what happens at every higher level.

The development of effective field theory and the renormalization group poses a serious challenge to that image. On this view, a theory is associated with an energy scale; low-energy physics can be insensitive, through decoupling, to the details of higher-energy physics; and a theory once regarded as fundamental may turn out to be merely the next effective theory in a tower that could in principle continue indefinitely. Moreover, even if the tower does have a bottom, the framework offers no obvious way to recognize that one has reached it rather than simply stopped at the next rung.

Does the classic convergence argument for a final theory survive this development, does it need repair, or is it dead?
