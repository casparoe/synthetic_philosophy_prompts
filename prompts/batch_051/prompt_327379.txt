A short-form video platform's Community Guidelines contain the following clause:

"4.3 Manipulative Content. You may not post Content designed to impair a user's ability to form, retain, or retrieve their own beliefs, memories, or preferences, including Content that produces this effect without the user's awareness."

The drafters of clause 4.3 had in mind subliminal flashes, hypnotic loops, and videos that "brainwash" viewers into beliefs or purchases. The following case was not among them.

A user posts a text card that is fully intelligible while on screen. The words are ordinary, the sentences are clear, and every reader who is asked at the time reports understanding it. But the card has a well-documented unusual property: within a few minutes of looking away, readers cannot recall what it said, cannot reconstruct it, and in most cases cannot recall that there was a card at all. The card contains no subliminal stimulus, no strobing, no seizure-like effect, and it affects memory only for its own content. The user, who knows about this property, labels it an antimeme — by his definition, a piece of content engineered to erase its own trace — and has pinned a comment: "Self-deleting thought, posted as pure demonstration. It persuades you of nothing and deletes only its own record." No complaints have been filed; the users exposed do not remember being exposed.

Assume for the purposes of this question that the property is real and robust. The question is interpretive: does clause 4.3 cover this post, such that the platform must remove it? Argue it from four angles: (a) the plain words of the clause; (b) the purpose or mischief the drafters were trying to prevent; (c) what the drafters would likely have said if this exact case had been put to them; and (d) what a reasonable user reading the clause would understand it to forbid. Then say which of these approaches should control in a case of this kind, and why. Finally, answer this: if clause 4.3 does not reach the post, is that a defect in the drafting or a sound limit on how far a rule against cognitive manipulation should extend? What does your answer imply about whether a rule can ever successfully govern content whose primary effect is to hide its own occurrence?
