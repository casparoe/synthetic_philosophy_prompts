I spent my career in stratigraphy and Quaternary geology, and now that I'm retired I've been reading more philosophy, so the Anthropocene debate has me going in circles. Lately I've been trying to write down what I actually think a unit of the geological time scale has to earn before it gets to be a formal epoch, then check the Anthropocene against my own criteria. So here are my three:

1. There has to be a globally synchronous marker that will show up in the actual rock record — the sort of thing you could point to as a 'golden spike' in a section a million years from now: an isotope excursion, a fallout layer, a sudden new fossil type.
2. It has to register an Earth-system change that is big and durable — a genuine state shift, not a regional or transient blip.
3. It has to get formalized by the people who maintain the timescale, the International Commission on Stratigraphy and its parent bodies, so it actually shows up in charts, maps, and textbooks.

The Anthropocene is a lovely borderline case for this. The Anthropocene Working Group pushed for a mid-20th-century boundary at the Great Acceleration, and the physical markers are real — plutonium fallout, plastics, concrete, the carbon isotope excursion. But the ICS recently voted against ratifying it as a formal epoch. And there's a deeper wrinkle: human modification of the Earth is diachronous. It begins with Neolithic land clearing in some places, colonial depopulation and reforestation in others, industrial coal in still others, so maybe the Anthropocene isn't a sharp epoch at all but a smear. Meanwhile the term has a huge life outside stratigraphy, in history and climate policy, where people use it as a historical and ethical category.

So my questions: does the Anthropocene actually pass my three tests? Which test is doing the real work, and is that the right test for a geological unit? And is the 'who decides' part a normal feature of how timescale units work — so the difference between a unit being discovered and being decided is smaller than I used to think — or is it just politics creeping in? I'd also like to hear wether the public use of the term should count as pressure on the formal decision, or whether stratigraphers should bracket all of that.
