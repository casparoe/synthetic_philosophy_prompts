I have been reading the rationalist corpus long enough to treat the Pascal's mugging problem as standard equipment: an expected utility maximizer with an unbounded utility function can be dominated by arbitrarily tiny probabilities of enormously good or bad outcomes, and the usual repairs are bounded utility or a prior that suppresses extreme hypotheses. What I have not seen done carefully is to go back and check whether Pascal's original wager in the Pensées actually survives this development. In the passage where Pascal poses the wager, he does not merely say that the reward for belief is infinite. He starts from the premise that we cannot know whether God exists, then insists that we must choose a way of life anyway, compares the situation to a game played at the far end of an infinite distance, and argues that the stake is finite while the possible gain is infinite, so that the expected outcome of wagering on God infinitely exceeds the alternative. Treat the development as the full modern apparatus of expected utility theory, including the St. Petersburg paradox, infinite and unbounded utility, and the Pascal's mugging objection. Does Pascal's original argument survive that development, or does it collapse into a mugging once it is stated precisely? I am asking for a premise-level verdict: which premise of the original wager is actually under pressure from the modern framework, whether the argument can be repaired by bounding utility or by limiting the hypotheses to those with finite expected value, and what remains of Pascal's reasoning after the repair. Also tell me the residue: are there parts of the original passage that the expected utility framework cannot represent at all, and are those parts doing any work or are they just seventeenth-century rhetorical framing?
