I am taking a course on computational models of mind, and for the past few weeks we have been working inside the letter-string analogy microworld associated with the Copycat project. The basic setup is this. If abc is changed into abd, and you are asked to change ijk in the same way, you can answer ijl by treating the change as replace the rightmost letter with its alphabetic successor, or you can answer ijd by treating the change as replace the letter c with the literal letter d. The course has leaned hard on this little ambiguity. The professor's view is that successor, same, group, and rightmost are not fixed tags selected from a menu, but fluid concepts that can be stretched, renegotiated, or partially applied when a problem exerts pressure on them.

The claim I keep getting stuck on is that there is a genuine and empirically detectable difference between two kinds of analogy-maker: a fixed-rule engine that works through a large but finite repertoire of stored analogy rules, and a system that genuinely possesses fluid concepts that bend under contextual pressure. My worry is that the difference may be invisible in practice, because any behavior produced by the second system could be mimicked by a sufficiently elaborate rule library. So I want to know how one could test this claim within, or close to, the letter-string microworld. What concrete experiment or battery of problems would provide the best evidence for fluid concepts rather than a very large rule library, and what pattern of answers, errors, response times, or transfer behavior would count for and against? Where would a finite-rule mimic still be able to keep up? And supposing the strongest such test came out one way or the other, what would it still leave unsettled about whether a system really has fluid concepts as opposed to merely behaving as if it did?
