I've noticed that I've turned Goodhart's law into a blanket rule about how I consume evidence. Any time I learn that a number is being used as a target or evaluation metric — test scores, citation counts, hospital readmission rates, ML benchmark accuracy, quarterly revenue, patient-satisfaction surveys — I immediately reclassify that number as evidence about the gaming process rather than about the underlying property everyone originally cared about. I don't even make myself produce a specific story about how the metric got gamed; the fact that there was an incentive is enough for me to discount it hard. So when a school's test scores jump after an accountability reform, my main reaction is 'someone optimized the test,' and I resist updating toward 'the school teaches better.' Same when a company's revenue spikes after bonuses get tied to it, or a paper beats a benchmark the authors obviously tuned against. I apply this even when the metric is new enough that nobody has had time to game it — if I can see that the designers wanted the number to go up, I treat that as enough to make the number suspect.

Is this defensible, or have I overlearned Goodhart into a bias where I throw away real information? i know the rule must fail sometimes: some targets are hard to game, some games are costly enough that beating the metric still signals real competence, and some metrics keep tracking the underlying thing for a while after they become targets. The problem is I can't tell, from the inside, when I'm looking at one of those cases versus when I'm just rationalizing my standing assumption that institutions are full of metric-gamers. Is there a version of the rule that keeps the bullshit-filtering benefits without making me impossible to update? What should I actually check, in practice, before I let a targeted metric move me?
