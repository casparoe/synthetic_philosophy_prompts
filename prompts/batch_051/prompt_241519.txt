I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar called Time, Rationality, and the Future -- about 20 students, 75-minute sessions. They've read the standard account of exponential versus hyperbolic discounting, but they can do the math without feeling it's force, and last year the conversation kept sliding back into 'well, it's rational to care less about the future because you might be dead.' I want one session that makes them actually feel the difference between pure time preference and the defensible reasons to discount the future (uncertainty, opportunity cost, the future being richer, and so on). Design a game, or a sequence of short games, for a single 75-minute session, with real choices and small real stakes but no actual cash changing hands -- candy, tokens, extra-credit fractions, whatever. Two or three rounds, ideally: one that quietly lures them into displaying present bias and a preference reversal themselves, one where they have to act as trustees or planners for future generations and pick an actual discount rate, and maybe a structured vote afterwards where they have to defend their rates against each other. The rules and incentives should be set up so the ideas show up in what they choose before anyone names anything -- no lecture up front, don't say pure time preference or hyperbolic discounting until the debrief, and don't assume I'll assign anything beforehand, it has to work cold. Make sure the later rewards are guaranteed (delivered by me, not some flaky promise) so nobody can hide behind uncertainty. Give me exact rules, materials, a timing plan, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the formal distinctions: constant versus declining discount rates, time consistency, the Ramsey equation and its pure time preference term, and the question of whether it's okay to discount future people's welfare just because of when they'll be born. By the end I want them to be able to say, in their own words, why discounting for uncertainty is one thing and discounting future people's welfare purely because they live later is a substantive ethical choice rather than a neutral technicality. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to fix it on the fly, and be honest about which part of this the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply in discussion. After the main write-up, give me a JSON summary with these fields: phases (array of objects with name, duration, facilitator_does, students_do, intended_reveal), debrief_map (array of objects with moment, formal_idea, ethical_point), failure_modes (array of objects with symptom, likely_cause, fix), and not_reproducible (string saying what the game can't get them to feel).
