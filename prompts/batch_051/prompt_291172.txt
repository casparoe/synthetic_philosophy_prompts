People in the free will debate have gotten a lot of mileage out of the idea that we've been hung up on the wrong kind of free will — that the interesting question is which varieties of free will are actually worth wanting, not whether some flatly impossible contra-causal power exists. But that framing has its own open problems: how you test what's worth wanting without just smuggling in whatever your account of moral responsibility already assumes, whether the main candidate varieties (alternative possibilities, sourcehood, reasons-responsiveness) actually cash out differently once you stop talking in slogans, and what the whole framing implies for blame and punishment. Suppose you were planning a two-year, three-person research project on this cluster and you had to pick the two or three questions that would move the debate the most. Which ones, in what order, and why? I want the ranking actually defended — for each one, why its upstream of the rest, how tractable it is in two years, and what would change downstream if it got settled — and I want you to flag at least one much-discussed question you'd deprioritize as mostly noise, with a reason someone who works on it would find at least a little annoying.
