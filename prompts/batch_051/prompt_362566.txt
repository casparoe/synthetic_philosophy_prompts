I've been working through Marx's contrast between the formal and the real subsumption of labour under capital, and I keep finding it more slippery than it first appears. As I understand it, formal subsumption names the situation in which capital takes over an existing labour process without fundamentally transforming it, and extracts surplus value mainly by lengthening or intensifying the working day within inherited ways of producing. Real subsumption, by contrast, describes what happens when capital rebuilds the labour process itself around machinery and a fine-grained division of tasks: skill migrates out of the worker and into the technical system, the worker becomes a living attachment to equipment owned by someone else, and relative surplus value comes increasingly from rising productivity rather than from the sheer extension of labour time. Marx developed this framework while trying to make sense of industrial machinery and the factory system of his own century.

I've been arguing with friends about whether that framework can handle contemporary forms of automation. One side says algorithmic management in warehouses and on gig platforms—proprietary software that decomposes work into micro-tasks, assigns each movement a sequence and a time budget, and uses the resulting data to tighten those budgets further—is best understood as a continuation and intensification of real subsumption, with the algorithm playing the role the machine played for Marx: the worker is directed, measured, and disciplined by a technical system that embodies decisions made elsewhere. The other side says this misses what is new, because in such firms the worker who matters for production is no longer the picker or the driver but the software engineer, data scientist, or machine-learning researcher who builds and maintains the system. On this view, the supposedly exploited warehouse worker is more like a residual appendage of an already-automated process, while the real productive labour is the technical labour of automating everyone else.

Taking Marx's own framework seriously—his distinction between formal and real subsumption, his labour theory of value, and his insistence that machinery is an instrument of capital rather than a neutral technical force—how do you think Marx would have responded to the claim that the software engineer, not the warehouse operative or driver, is now the real productive worker? In particular, would he have treated algorithmic management as the completion of real subsumption, or as something genuinely unlike it, and what would he have expected such systems to do over the long run to the composition of the working class and to the possibility of work becoming anything other than a means for the self-expansion of capital?
