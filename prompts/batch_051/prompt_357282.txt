I'm a student in a philosophy of punishment course, and I am applying for a one-semester term-paper fellowship, so I need a proposal of about two pages. I've been reading Camus's "Reflections on the Guillotine," and I want to build the paper around a passage that seems to be doing an important part of his argumentative work:

"When the imagination sleeps, words are emptied of their meaning: a deaf population absent-mindedly registers the condemnation of a man. But if people are shown the machine, made to touch the wood and steel and to hear the sound of a head falling, then public imagination, suddenly awakened, will repudiate both the vocabulary and the penalty."

My half-formed idea is that Camus is not merely offering an emotional description here; he is proposing a method. Represent the penalty concretely, wake up the public imagination, and both the euphemistic vocabulary and the penalty itself lose their legitimacy. But I am unsure whether that method can carry the weight he places on it. A retentionist could reply that she already knows exactly what the machine does and still believes the penalty is necessary, and that the revulsion Camus relies on is precisely the unruly emotion that a theory of punishment should keep in check. So my question is whether Camus's appeal to the awakened imagination is a genuine argument against the death penalty or only vivid rhetoric that presupposes abolitionism.

Could you turn this into a proper proposal? I would like: a precise statement of the question that has a determinate answer; a reconstruction of the position Camus is actually staking out in the passage — I suspect there are two readings, one on which the imagination supplies missing information about what the penalty really is, and another on which its revulsion functions as a kind of verdict that carries its own normative force; an account of why the standard abolitionist and retentionist positions do not settle what the passage claims; an approach that is conceptual rather than empirical, with a reason why that approach is better here than polling people about their reactions or revisiting deterrence data; a statement of what a successful result would look like — perhaps a criterion that separates vindicating from debunking uses of revulsion — and what would count as failure; and an honest assessment of what could make the project too large for a semester. Do not include a bibliography; I will add the citations.
