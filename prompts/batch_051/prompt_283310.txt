I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on political philosophy and law — 18 students, one 90-minute session. I want an in-class activity that makes students feel the force of a claim they currently hear as an abstract slogan: that the spread of mandatory arbitration amounts to a privatization of justice. The version I want them to encounter is not the claim that arbitrators are corrupt or that contracts are invalid; it is the structural claim that when dispute resolution moves from public, precedent-generating courts into private, contractually-imposed arbitration, adjudication changes from something like a public good into something like a private service, with predictable market-like consequences for who writes the rules, who can learn from outcomes, and whether third parties get the benefit of public decisions. My students keep saying some version of "both parties signed the clause, so what's the objection?" I want them to run into the objection themselves, before it is named.

Design a runnable activity for this setting: a structured negotiation or game with roles, rules, and point-based payoffs that don't use real money — not a debate in which students are assigned positions. The point is for the idea to show up in what happens, in the incentives and choices, rather than in a framing lecture. Arrange the rules so that, over several rounds, participants discover for themselves that:

- risk-averse or rationally apathetic individuals accept clauses that quietly transfer adjudication advantages to the repeat-player party;
- the fact of signing does little to legitimate the resulting system, because the live choice is only between near-identical clauses, not between genuinely rival proposals;
- the repeat player can learn from closed-door outcomes and shape future terms, while one-shot counterparties cannot;
- public, on-the-record adjudication generates precedents and norms that benefit even people who never litigate, yet individuals still have reason to take small private gains by opting out of it; and
- once enough parties sign such clauses, the public system erodes as a practical matter even if it remains formally available.

Please specify the materials, setup, roles, round-by-round rules, a scoring scheme, and a timed schedule. The debrief should map concrete moments in the activity onto the concepts usually invoked in this area — repeat-player advantage, pre-dispute waivers, class-action waivers, confidentiality and sealed outcomes, arbitration forum selection, the difference between one-off and aggregate concerns, and the public-goods character of precedent and public adjudication — but only after students have described those moments in their own words and given the experience its own vocabulary.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — participants gaming the scoring, colluding, disputes arising too rarely or too often, one side finding the task boring, the drafting stage eating the clock — and how to adjust the rules to fix it. Finally, be frank about which feature of the critique the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply explicitly. For example, an exercise like this cannot easily reproduce the coercive legal background in which courts make arbitration clauses effective against parties who never meaningfully negotiated them, or the pre-existing market power that puts the clause in front of the weaker party in the first place. Avoid depending on specific case law, statutory detail, or named authors; the activity should work for students who know no actual law.
