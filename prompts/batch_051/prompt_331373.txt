I sit on the board of a small community arts nonprofit, and a donor (eccentric but serious) has offered us a strange arrangement. She flips a fair coin until it comes up heads. If the first heads is on the nth flip, then when n is odd she gives us 2^n/n dollars, and when n is even we give her 2^n/n dollars. So: heads on the first flip, we get $2. Heads on the second flip, we pay $2. Third flip, we get about $2.67. Fourth flip, we pay $4. Fifth flip, we get $6.40, and from there the amounts keep growing in both directions.

Most of the board wants to take it. They've worked out the expected value as 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ... which comes to about 69 cents in our favor, and they figure we should take any deal with positive expected value. I've been thinking about this on my own, and I dont think that 69 cents is doing the work they think. If I add up the same outcomes in a different order, the running total heads toward a different number; I'm pretty sure that by ordering the flips cleverly you could push the "average" toward almost anything, or stop it from settling down at all. So I suspect there just isn't a well-defined expected value here, and the 69 cents is a artifact of one particular order of writing the list.

Draft the short memo I'd circulate before our meeting. It should recommend accept, reject, or accept with conditions, and it should take the 69-cent expected-value argument seriously rather than just waving it off as weird math.
