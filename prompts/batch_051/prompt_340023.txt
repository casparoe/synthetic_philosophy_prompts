A friend of mine who served in the army and I got into an argument about whether soldiers on both sides of a war really are "moral equals," the way the traditional just war folks say. I tried to reduce the revisionist critique to a clean argument of my own, and now I want to check whether it actually works. It goes like this:

(1) If I fight in an unjust war, the people I kill are people I had no right to kill, so my killings are unjustified.
(2) What I have a right to do doesn't depend on what I believe about it; a soldier's sincere conviction that his side is just cannot itself create a right to kill.
(3) So a soldier fighting for an unjust side has no right to kill the soldiers fighting for the just side.
(4) If you have no right to kill someone, then killing that person is wrong for you.
(5) Therefore, a soldier on the unjust side acts wrongly every time he or she fights — and the moral equality of combatants is false.

On the surface this looks like a knockdown, but I cant shake the feeling that step 4 is doing the real work, and that "wrong for you" might mean one thing in (4) and a different thing in the conclusion. Maybe the traditionalist only means that the unjust-side soldier is excused, because from inside his epistemic situation fighting looks like the reasonable thing to do, and that's all "moral equality" needs. Or maybe step 2 overreaches. I'd like a precise diagnosis: is this argument valid? If not, which step or steps fail, and what's the strongest repaired version of the whole argument? If it is valid, does the conclusion really force us to abandon the traditional position, or does the traditional position survive under a weaker reading of "equality"?
