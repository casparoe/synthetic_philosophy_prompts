Consider a pair of things otherwise careful people say about historical atrocities such as chattel slavery in the United States or the Holocaust. They will say, without wincing, that the nation was and is guilty: the United States is guilty of slavery, Germany is guilty of the Holocaust, and the debt does not expire — the state owes an apology, reparations, memorials, and school curricula that teach the crime as "ours." Yet those same people will insist, with equal confidence, that no particular citizen born decades later is guilty of those acts. They will say it would be unjust to punish or resent an eighteen-year-old for what her ancestors or predecessor-citizens did, and that she has at most inherited privileges or special obligations, never guilt. Thus a collective that is said to be guilty across generations turns out to have, at any given moment, almost no guilty members. That is the asymmetry I want you to examine.

In your answer, do three things. First, state and defend the strongest justification you can find for treating a nation as a continuing moral agent that can be guilty of, and owe reparations for, wrongs committed before any current citizen was born, while refusing to say that any current citizen is guilty. Second, test that justification against nearby cases: a corporation fined now for harms caused by employees long dead; an heir who must return a stolen painting she had no idea was stolen; a family whose wealth derives from an ancestor who enslaved people; a citizen whose family immigrated long after the atrocity but who now votes and pays taxes in the state; and two simultaneous claimants, one whose ancestors suffered the wrong and one whose ancestors inflicted it. Say where the justification holds, where it becomes strained, and why. Third, give a verdict: should we keep the asymmetry, revise it, or eliminate one of its two sides — and if you would eliminate one side, say what the surviving side commits you to in practice.

Please write your answer in American English.
