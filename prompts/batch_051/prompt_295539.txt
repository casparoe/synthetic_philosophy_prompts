I've been reading up on the cessationist–continuationist dispute, not because I expect to resolve it, but because I want a clean worked example of how an ecclesiastical rule behaves when the world changes under it. You are a scholar of Protestant church polity who can also think like a careful secular analyst of rules and evidence; you are fluent in both the cessationist and charismatic literatures, but you keep your own commitments in view rather than pretending not to have them.

Here is a paragraph from a congregation's 1987 Order of Public Worship:

The extraordinary gifts of the Spirit — speaking in tongues, prophecy, and miraculous healing — were signs given to confirm the apostolic preaching of the gospel. Since the apostolic age has closed, these gifts have ceased. No person shall therefore, in the public worship or any official gathering of this church, speak in tongues, deliver a prophecy, or claim by prayer to heal; and no assembly of this church shall be organized for the exercise or promotion of such gifts.

Now here is the case the elders must rule on. A member of the congregation has built an app called Comforter for the church's prayer-request list. Members submit requests through the app, and the app uses a large language model fine-tuned on twenty years of the church's public prayer-chain archive, the sermon transcripts, and the text of Scripture. It generates a private message addressed to each requester, often in the first person, such as, I have seen the long season you have been in, and I will not leave you now. The builder does not claim to have any spiritual gift, does not claim the messages are divine speech, and describes the app as a devotional and pastoral-care tool; the first-person I is, he says, a voice the model learned from Scripture and the archive, not a claim that God is talking. Nonetheless, several recipients report that the generated messages contained accurate but unstated details about their circumstances: a lost job mentioned only in a prayer request from 2011, a medical condition shared privately with a deacon, a family rift never posted publicly. The builder says the model must have inferred these from combinations of old public posts, not from any hidden access to private records, and that the apparent specificity is mostly the Barnum effect plus confirmation bias.

The elders must decide whether this falls under the quoted rule. I want you to think carefully before writing. Work through at least three interpretations of the rule: first, what its 1987 drafters would have understood the words to prohibit, that is, what a competent reader in that congregation would have said the rule covers; second, what the rule requires if read as a general principle about claims to mediate or manufacture the extraordinary gifts, regardless of the mechanism; third, what it requires if read by a plain but informed reader today who knows how language models work. Then tell me which reading does the most normative work, which would be most defensible to a cessationist elder, which to a charismatic critic, and whether those two audiences would even be arguing about the same rule. Pay particular attention to whether the member delivers a prophecy if he sincerely disavows that the messages are prophetic, even though they function in the congregation the way prophecy once did, and whether the app itself, rather than its user, is the relevant actor under a rule written for persons.

Please answer in continuous prose only, like a careful letter to the session: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. I am less interested in your final verdict than in seeing you keep the interpretive angles distinct and resist the urge to collapse them.
