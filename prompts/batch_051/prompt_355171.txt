I got into a long, basically unresolved argument with a friend last night about whether a certain recent crackdown should be called genocide. He kept falling back on the legal definition — he's not a lawyer, but he plays one on the internet — and saying you can't use the word unless you can prove a court-grade intent to destroy a group. I kept trying to say that Lemkin, when he coined the word in the 1940s, wasn't just handing lawyers a checklist; his whole idea was that a group can be destroyed as a way of life, through attacks on its institutions, language, religion, the material conditions of its existence, and that a pure body-count approach misses the thing he was pointing at. Then the argument got worse, because we started fighting about whether the politics of the word — who gains from saying it, who gains from refusing to say it — should count at all, or whether letting the politics in just corrupts the classification. We never landed anywhere, and now I want to turn that argument into something useful.

So here's the thing. I work as a standards editor at an international newsroom, the person who decides what language we're allowed to use in our reporting. We have to rule on whether to use the word genocide (or genocidal) maybe a few times a year, so it is rare, but the stakes are brutal: use it too loosely and we are accused of bias and of cheapening a term with legal weight; use it too narrowly and we are accused of denial, of setting the bar so high that nothing will ever count. The editors on shift aren't legal experts and have maybe a hour. I want you to act as an expert on Lemkin's concept and on the moral philosophy of classifying atrocities, not just a lawyer reciting the convention by heart, and to think carefully before you commit to a final procedure. Before you commit to anything, brainstorm the edge cases and the distinctions that actually do the work. Then write me a reusable step-by-step decision procedure a shift editor could actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure stops with something like do not use the word yet, use it, or hand this to a senior editor because it has become a values call rather than a legal or factual one. It should incorporate both the Genocide Convention's two-part test — the listed acts (killing members of the group, causing serious bodily or mental harm, deliberately subjecting them to conditions meant to destroy the group, preventing births within the group, forcibly transferring its children) plus the specific intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group as a group, whether wholly or partly — and Lemkin's broader idea that a people can be destroyed through their social and cultural foundations before the bodies pile up. I especially need you to flag where those two frameworks part ways, and to be honest that the word is not only a legal classification but a political act with real consequences: using it and withholding it both do something in the world, and a procedure that pretends otherwise is lying to itself. Build in the hardest practical distinction too: evidence that points toward an aim to destroy a group versus an aim to drive it out or assimilate it, because in real conflicts those get tangled and a lazy procedure will let people collapse them.

Then run the procedure on three hypotheticals (you can fill in surrounding details, but keep these facts): (1) a government demolishes a minority group's villages, schools, places of worship, and archives during a counterinsurgency, relocates the survivors to a desolate camp, and its officials openly say the minority will cease to exist as a people, though its public line is security, not extermination; (2) a state massacres and tortures members of a political opposition movement with every sign of an intent to eliminate that movement entirely, but there is no national, ethnic, racial, or religious dimension to the target; (3) a decades-long program separates indigenous children from their families into state institutions designed to erase their language and identity, and officials openly state that the goal is for the group to disappear as a distinct people, though few are directly killed. Apply the procedure even where it gives an answer I or another editor am going to resist, and say explicitly where that happens — and whether that means the editor should overrule the procedure, revise it, or just sit with the discomfort. Finally tell me what the procedure cannot settle. Please don't give me citations or a reading list; I need the instrument itself.
