One evening last autumn, on the porch of a writers' retreat in the Hudson Valley, I sat listening while two of the other residents argued about a poet whose work is famously resistant — syntax doubling back on itself, clauses nested inside clauses, lines that refuse to resolve. I am drafting a novel about a charismatic artist who is either a genius or a fraud, so the argument cut close to my work, and I reconstructed it afterward from my notes, nearly verbatim.

(1) Marcus said the poet's difficulty was not a failing but a signal, in the sense biologists give to a peacock's tail: a conspicuous, expensive, risky trait advertises quality precisely because it is costly. A writer without real command of language who attempts ornamented, recursive prose simply produces muddle and is rejected; a talented writer can hold a sentence at the edge of collapse and still bring it home. Readers are therefore reasonable to treat sustained difficulty as rough evidence of ability. (2) Elena replied that he had compressed the logic too far. A costly signal is honest only when the cost is differentially higher for low-quality signalers than for high-quality ones. Difficulty by itself is just effort, and effort tracks discipline or vanity as readily as it tracks literary merit. A mediocre writer can spend years making prose knotted and still be bad, while a great writer may find plainness nearly free; so difficulty alone signals something closer to ambition than ability. (3) Marcus answered that her objection assumes readers cannot tell hard-but-controlled writing from mere murk. But if experienced readers can tell the difference — can see that a sentence is being kept at the edge of collapse rather than allowed to fall apart — then difficulty is doing real evidential work: it supplies the conditions under which that discrimination can be exercised. Plain, clear prose is too cheap for a gifted writer, so it reveals little; complex prose that stays coherent across three hundred pages is affordable only to someone with the underlying ability. That, he said, is exactly the differential-cost structure she had granted as the test. (4) Elena said this made difficulty either redundant or worthless. If readers can already recognize controlled complexity when they encounter it, they do not need difficulty as a signal; they can judge the writing directly. If they cannot, then difficulty unbacked by independent judgment is noise, not evidence. His account, she said, secretly borrows the very capacity to detect quality that the signal was supposed to supply, and then gives the credit to the ornament. (5) Marcus said the capacity she was borrowing is not available to readers before the fact. Originality and depth of thought are not directly observable, but a writer's ability to sustain controlled complexity across hundreds of pages is observably more expensive for a shallow or undisciplined writer than for a genuinely original one. The signal is the sustained performance, not the local difficulty, and that is what readers are responding to when they call a book demanding. (6) Elena said that even granting sustained difficulty is costly, an honest-signal equilibrium requires the low-quality type to find it more costly than the high-quality type, and that is precisely what is in doubt. Stubbornness, institutional patronage, and readers who mistake agitation for depth can all cover the bill. And once a signal is selected for its cost rather than its honest correlation with quality, it can run away in the manner of a peacock's tail until the display no longer tracks the trait it was meant to advertise. On his account, she said, the poetry world might be selecting for the display rather than the ability.

I kept out of it, but I want the argument settled for reasons that are not only social. Please referee the exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say what is sound and what is overstated, flag any slide between different senses of "cost" or "quality," and then say what both of them are missing and who, if either, is more right overall.
