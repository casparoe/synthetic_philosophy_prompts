I keep a running list of the respects in which scientific revolutions can still be called rational even on a fairly Kuhnian picture — my standing answer to the charge that paradigm change is more like conversion than like inference. So far I have five moves, and I suspect they are the standard ones:

1. Kuhn's later values reply: competing paradigms are often judged by shared epistemic values (accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness), which guide theory choice without determining it algorithmically.
2. The piecemeal-replacement point: revolutions don't raze the entire paradigm; instruments, data, and large stretches of theory survive, and those continuities anchor the change.
3. The local-decision point: a scientist can rationally defect to the new paradigm because it cracks a long-standing anomaly the old one kept failing at, without needing a neutral global comparison of the two as wholes.
4. The Bayesian reconstruction: anomalies drive the old paradigm's likelihoods down far enough that switching is ordinary rational updating.
5. The Lakatosian variant: revolutions are rational at the level of research programmes, with the new programme progressive and the old one degenerating.

I want a sixth argument for the rationality of revolutionary change that is not in this list and not a rewording, special case, or combination of these. It should be something an actual defender of Kuhn, or a Kuhn-sympathetic critic of Popper, could run. Then say what it adds that the five don't, whether it is any good, and what would change your mind about that last judgment — what consideration would most likely lead you to drop the argument or to come around to it.
