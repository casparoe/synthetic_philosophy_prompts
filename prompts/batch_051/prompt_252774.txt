Consider the following legal arrangement, which is emerging in several jurisdictions. When an autonomous system—a self-driving car, a surgical robot, or a warehouse vehicle—causes harm, civil liability spreads widely: manufacturers, component suppliers, operators, and maintainers can all be made to pay damages under product-liability or negligence doctrines that look mainly at defect, foreseeable risk, and causal contribution rather than at anyone's private state of mind. Criminal liability, by contrast, remains anchored to a human individual and to a culpable mental state such as intention, knowledge, recklessness, or negligence. If an automated vehicle does something lethal that flows from its own training on billions of miles of driving, in a way no engineer anticipated and none could reasonably have been expected to anticipate, criminal law in most such systems finds no person to punish. Some road-safety laws even classify the automated driving system as the driver for traffic rules, while denying that it could be the guilty subject of a crime. Civil liability expands to cover the gap; criminal blame does not.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of moral responsibility and agency that this arrangement presupposes. In particular:

(a) Distinguish what the arrangement strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with. Is it committed to the view that criminal responsibility requires an individual human with a guilty mental state, or could someone coherently explain the asymmetry on other grounds, such as the different social functions of civil damages and criminal punishment?

(b) Assess whether the arrangement is internally stable. Can one coherently treat the same system as a driver for regulatory purposes and as mere machinery for purposes of criminal law, or must one of those classifications give way?

(c) Say what would have to change if the presupposed theory were replaced, in one direction by a strict-liability conception of criminal fault and in the other by a systems-safety conception that assigns responsibility to design, training, and oversight structures rather than to individual mental states.
