I have been working through the Chase–Sanborn challenge to qualia, and a friend has turned it into an argument that I find hard to escape but also unacceptable, because it seems to prove too much. I want to know which move in the argument should be given up.

Set-up. Two professional tasters, Chase and Sanborn, have spent decades grading the same house blend. Each reports that he now enjoys the coffee less than he once did. Their discrimination abilities, sensory vocabularies, and work histories are otherwise indistinguishable. Chase is certain that the taste itself is unchanged and that what has changed is his liking for it. Sanborn is equally certain that his liking is unchanged and that what has changed is the taste. Neither is insincere, and each has complete mastery of the taste–liking distinction.

The argument runs:

(1) Chase and Sanborn differ in no respect that could be checked independently. All the shared, public facts about their two histories are the same; the only observable divergence is the content of their retrospective similarity judgments.

(2) Each subject's judgment issues from the only method there is for such questions: comparing a present experience with a remembered one and sorting felt sameness and difference into the categories of taste and liking.

(3) If qualia are real, then for each subject there is a determinate fact about whether the taste-quality of his experience persisted unchanged through the relevant period, and the subject's sincere judgment about that fact is normally authoritative.

(4) But the Chase–Sanborn pair shows that the method in (2) does not track which of these determinate facts obtains. Two equally competent uses of the method arrive at opposite verdicts under the same public history, and there is no further check that could expose either as mistaken: more introspection merely produces another confident sorting, and the public record is identical for both.

(5) Therefore the sorting is not read off the experience at all; it is imposed retrospectively by each subject's conceptualization.

(6) If no method tracks which qualitative feature changed, then the presumed fact about the quale's persistence is idle: it explains none of the subjects' reports and is undetectable even in principle.

(7) An idle, undetectable posit is not a coherent posit. We should conclude that there is no fact of the matter about whether Chase's taste-quality changed, and that "the way the coffee tastes" cannot do the individuating work qualia were introduced to do.

I find (7) unacceptable, and my deeper worry is that the argument proves too much. The machinery in step (4) does not seem to depend on anything special about taste. Applied to a retrospective comparison of a friendship (did the friendship change, or did my feelings about it change?), a returning pain (is it the same intensity as before?), or a shift in political outlook (did the world change, or did I?), the same reasoning would dissolve all of them into fact-free zones. I want to keep the plain thought that there is a determinate, if sometimes hard-to-recover, fact about whether a present experience is the same in a given respect as a past one.

So: which premise or transition should I reject? For whichever step you reject, explain why it looks compulsory from inside the argument, and lay out what the main ways of rejecting it each commit one to about the authority of introspection, the possibility of retrospective comparison, and the metaphysics of qualitative states.
