After retiring from condensed-matter physics, I have been reading moral philosophy with the seriousness I used to give to phase diagrams, and I keep coming back to the situationist critique of virtue ethics. I would like an honest assessment of a way of reframing virtue that I have been calling the basin account. The thought is this: we go wrong when we treat a virtue such as honesty as a property an agent carries with her across all circumstances, just as we would go wrong saying that a material is strong without specifying temperature, pressure, and strain rate. On my account, a virtue is a basin of attraction in situation-space: the set of circumstances in which the agent's characteristic thoughts and actions settle into a stable, virtuous pattern. An ordinarily honest person who lies to protect a child is not, on this view, best described as partly honest; she is in a region of the space where honesty is simply not the attractor. The situationist experiments, as I read them, do not dissolve these basins; they map their boundaries, which is exactly what a dynamical picture would predict. What the tradition called the practically wise person is then, roughly, someone whose basins are unusually wide and steep-walled, and moral education is the work of widening basins rather than implanting traits.

I can see that this resembles talk of local traits, and perhaps it merely restates the situationists' point in the vocabulary of dynamical systems. My question is whether the basin account keeps enough of what virtue ethics needs — a person who is robustly reliable across a broad but not unlimited range of circumstances — while conceding only what the data actually show. Or does it purchase consistency with the experiments at the price of abandoning what made virtue admirable in the first place: that the virtuous person is good, not merely well-calibrated to her situations?
