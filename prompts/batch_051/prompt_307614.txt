I am a novelist by trade, fluent in the craft vocabulary of fiction — backstory, point of view, narration, canon, retcon, worldbuilding, origin story, unreliable narration, the implied reader — but only an interested amateur in the historical study of nationalism. I keep encountering a broadly modernist thesis, associated with Eric Hobsbawm and Benedict Anderson, that nations are far newer than their self-presentation suggests, that many traditions which pass as ancient are recent constructions, and that the nation is an imagined community made possible in part by the circulation of print. I find the comparison to narrative craft tempting, but I suspect my own vocabulary is doing part of the thinking for me, and I want to know how much of the resemblance survives honest translation.

Restate this modernist account of nations and invented traditions in the vocabulary of fiction-writing, as faithfully as you can. Specifically: in this picture, is a nation's relationship to its history best rendered as backstory, origin story, retcon, or some combination? What corresponds to canon, to worldbuilding, to point of view, to the unreliable narrator, and to the implied reader? Who or what narrates a nation, and who is the audience — and can those last questions even be posed in my vocabulary without distortion?

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the historians' account translate cleanly into fiction-craft terms; which translate only by fudging some feature of one side or the other, and exactly where the fudge occurs; and which have no analogue in my vocabulary at all? I would like your verdict on the residue: is it a defect in my vocabulary, a place where the historians' categories are themselves unclear, or a sign that the resemblance between nations and narratives is only partial, with the interesting work lying in the difference?

Impose a few constraints, because I want the exercise run honestly. Do not smuggle in a single author, a finished text, or a reader standing outside the story; I know enough to suspect those are the defaults my trade would quietly insert. Keep the historians' sense of the invented distinct from the merely fictional, and show me whether words like fiction, retcon, and backstory can carry that distinction without leaking. Finally, tell me which is doing the explanatory work at each step: the historians' actual claims, or the built-in assumptions of the craft vocabulary.
