In a modern European history reading group I'm in, two friends got into an argument that none of the rest of us could bring to a close, so I have written down what each of them actually said and want someone to referee it. The dispute concerned the claim, pressed by the clause of the Treaty of Versailles that assigned Germany and its allies responsibility for all the loss and damage of the war and revived in our seminar by the historian Fritz Fischer, whose work argued that Germany's leadership had willed a continental war, that Germany bore primary responsibility for the First World War. Their exchange, cleaned up only slightly:

(1) Daniel: The Fischer thesis is right in spirit. In July 1914 the German leadership gave Austria-Hungary an unconditional assurance of support and then pressed for a quick strike against France and Russia rather than allowing the crisis to stay a Balkan one. Other parties contributed to the background — the alliance system, the arms race, imperial rivalry — but Germany's decisions converted a local crisis into a general European war. Saying Germany was primarily responsible is not victors' justice; it is a causal and moral judgment with real evidence behind it.

(2) Priya: But the background is not just background. The alliance system was built for exactly this, and every great power had a hand in it: Russia ordered general mobilization, Austria delivered the ultimatum, France backed Russia. Picking Germany as the responsible party is like blaming the last domino in a line. You owe us an account of why one contribution outweighs all the others.

(3) Daniel: The domino image assumes all the moves are equivalent. They were not. Austria could at most start a war with Serbia; Germany's assurance, and then its invasion of Belgium and France, are what made it a world war. A causal account does not have to treat every necessary condition as equal.

(4) Priya: Even if I grant that Germany's leaders made the largest causal contribution, you have just moved from blaming Germany to blaming a small governing clique. Blaming a nation treats millions of Germans — Social Democrats, conscripts, civilians — as answerable for decisions they did not make. The war-guilt clause blames Germany as a whole, and that is collective punishment dressed up as history.

(5) Daniel: That is too quick. We hold states responsible all the time — for treaty violations, for wars of aggression, for reparations. The state is a corporate agent: it acts through officials, but the actions and their costs belong to the collectivity. There does not need to be a single person called "Germany" for it to make sense to say that Germany decided to risk war.

(6) Priya: But that illustrates my point. "Germany" as a corporate agent is a legal convenience, not a moral subject: a state cannot feel remorse, and it has no character. And once the moral verdict attaches to that legal entity, it gets used to justify concrete penalties — reparations, lost territory — so the supposedly moral judgment is doing political work. We can say that the German government of 1914 acted recklessly; we should not hand the Allies a moral weapon and call the result disinterested analysis.

I cannot tell where each of them has the better of it, and I also cannot tell whether they are really disagreeing about the history or about what kind of thing can bear responsibility for a war. Please go through the exchange move by move and rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification. Then say where, if anywhere, the two are talking past each other; whether Daniel's corporate-agent reply actually answers Priya's objection about blaming a whole population; and which of the two, if either, has the more defensible overall position. I am especially interested in whether the step from "Germany's leaders made the largest causal contribution" to "Germany, as a nation, is primarily responsible" is licensed, or whether the two could agree on the causal history and still part ways on the moral verdict.
