I'm giving an econ department seminar next month and I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a restatement of the talk. The audience: mostly micro theorists, two experimentalists who have actually run Allais-type choice experiments, and a bunch of grad students who learned the paradox three weeks ago and are smug about it. The senior people split roughly into (a) a guy who teaches the expected-utility chapter of MWG like it's scripture, (b) another who's fine with non-EU models but has no patience for hand-wavy "just redefine the consequence" moves, and (c) the experimentalists, who think Kahneman and Tversky settled this in 1979 and that anyone still defending expected utility as a normative theory is doing motivated reasoning.

My thesis, bluntly: the Allais paradox as it's normally taught doesn't refute the independence axiom; it refutes the assumption that consequences are just money amounts. The standard setup: 1A is a sure $1M; 1B is 0.89 $1M, 0.10 $5M, 0.01 $0; 2A is 0.11 $1M, 0.89 $0; 2B is 0.10 $5M, 0.90 $0. People take 1A and 2B, which contradicts independence only if the 1% zero branch in 1B is the same consequence as the 1% zero branch in 2B. I claim it isn't: in 1B the zero branch is the one where you gave up a sure million, and in 2B it's just a bad draw with no sure thing on the table. Individuate consequences so they include what the agent could have had, and the two choices satisfy independence. So I want to hold the line that expected utility is the right normative theory, and that the real lesson of Allais is that money amounts are not consequences.

Write me the prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way this audience would phrase them (not the way the LessWrong crowd would), ordered by how likely and how damaging they are. For each one give me a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. Also tell me what I should conceed up front, what I should refuse to concede, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Keep the whole thing in American English, and make the last line a one-sentence summary of my overall strategy.
