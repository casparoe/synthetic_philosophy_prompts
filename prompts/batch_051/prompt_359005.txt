I'm taking an upper-division course this term on public reason and religious arguments in politics, and a case that came up in discussion section has been nagging at me. We've been working with a broadly Rawlsian account of public reason, presented as a family of views rather than a single fixed doctrine. On the version we use, a contribution to public political debate counts as a public-reason argument only if it satisfies four conditions. First, a domain condition: the contribution concerns constitutional essentials or matters of basic justice, where the demand of public justification applies. Second, a content condition: the reasons offered are cast in terms of shared political values—equal basic liberties, equal opportunity, the common good—rather than in terms of the authority of a particular scripture or revelation. Third, a reciprocity condition: the arguer could reasonably expect other free and equal citizens to accept the reasons without having to adopt her particular comprehensive doctrine. Fourth, a sincerity condition: the public reasons offered are the arguer's actual reasons, not a post-hoc translation of a religious conviction.

Now the case that has me stuck. A state senator who practices a religious tradition teaching that life belongs to God and that capital punishment is always murder introduces a bill to repeal the death penalty. In committee and on the Senate floor she offers only secular arguments: racial and socioeconomic disparities in application, documented wrongful convictions, the higher fiscal cost of capital litigation, and the absence of a deterrent effect. The arguments are careful and factually serious. But at a gathering in her congregation the previous month, she reportedly said that her opposition to the death penalty comes from her faith, that she would not support repeal if her tradition did not require it, and that the empirical arguments had been assembled afterwards to persuade colleagues who do not share her faith. A staffer confirms that she regards the secular case as true but secondary: it is the religious conviction that actually moves her.

Does the senator's contribution qualify as a public-reason argument on the account above? I'd like the answer to work through the four criteria one by one, with the fourth treated as the genuinely contested one. Where the criteria pull in different directions, say so explicitly. Then tell me whether this case shows that the sincerity condition should be revised or dropped, or whether it shows instead why sincerity matters. Finally, give an all-things-considered verdict: should the ideal of public reason count this contribution as within its bounds or outside them, and does the case reveal a defect in the criteria themselves?
