I'm in a course on Augustine and the Pelagian controversy, and one thing keeps bugging me. In On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins and on the Baptism of Infants, Augustine seems to argue roughly like this (I'm paraphrasing from a translation, so don't quote me on the wording): the Church baptizes infants for the remission of sins, so infants must have some sin that needs remitting; but since an infant hasn't committed any personal sin yet, the sin must come from Adam. That looks like a claim that someone can be genuinely guilty for an act he didn't choose, before he even existed.

But we normally treat guilt as strictly personal. If my father robs a bank while my mother is in labor, nobody thinks I'm guilty of bank robbery, even if I might face practical consequences from his imprisonment. The law doesn't transfer guilt across generations like that. So what is Augustine actually saying? Is original sin literally inherited guilt—something like a forensic status passed down—or is he really only saying we inherit a damaged nature, with the guilt-language being loose or figurative? If it's literal, what conception of agency or personhood would make that coherent? If it's figurative, then what work is "remission of sins" doing in infant baptism?

I'd rather you push back on my setup if I'm conflating guilt, shame, and legal liability in a way that muddles the theology. But if the distinction runs through the issue, tell me which horn you think Augustine has to take and why.
