I am writing a novel in which a character is offered a version of Kavka's toxin puzzle, and I want to be sure that when I alter the setup I am not inadvertently changing what the puzzle was meant to show. As I understand the original: a billionaire will pay the character tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, she genuinely intends to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon; the toxin will make her ill for a day, but the money is paid solely for having the intention, not for drinking. The standard diagnosis is that she cannot form the intention, because she knows that by tomorrow afternoon the money will already be hers and she will have no reason to drink; wanting to intend is not the same as intending, and one cannot intend what one believes one will not do. That diagnosis tacitly assumes she has no way of binding her future behaviour.

My alteration is this: at midnight she may take a slow-acting, irreversible drug that will causally compel her to drink the toxin tomorrow afternoon whatever she then wants, with no antidote and no way of cancelling its effect. I am less interested in the plot outcome than in whether the standard diagnosis survives the alteration. Does it still go through, does it need repair, or does it now dissolve into a question of prediction plus self-binding? If she takes the drug, can she be said to intend to drink, or only to intend to make herself drink, and does that distinction carry the philosophical weight here? Which premise has the drug removed, and is there a repaired version of the puzzle that preserves whatever the original was meant to show?

I would be grateful if you would answer in British English and treat the question as one about intention, belief, and rational constraint rather than about what a plausible character would do.
