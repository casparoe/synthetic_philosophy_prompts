In a well-known passage of A Treatise of Human Nature, Book II, Part III, Section III, Hume writes:

“It can never in the least concern us to know, that such objects are causes, and such others effects, if both the causes and effects be indifferent to us. Where the objects themselves do not affect us, their connexion can never give them any influence; and ’tis plain, that as reason is nothing but the discovery of this connexion, it cannot be by its means that the objects are able to affect us.”

I teach this as a pure statement of the position Williams later sharpens into the thesis that all genuine reasons are internal — that an agent has a reason to act only if the action can be reached by a sound deliberative route from something already in the agent’s subjective motivational set, so that there are no external reasons floating free of that set. The objections to the Humean claim in this passage fall, as I present them, into four families. (1) The evaluative-cognition objection: to judge outright that a state of affairs is good or reason-giving can itself move an agent, without any prior aversion or propensity; reason is not confined to discovering causal connexions. (2) The formal-practical-reason objection: a train of reasoning that proceeds by universalization, consistency, or reflection on the shape of the will can generate a new motive rather than merely redirect one already present. (3) The agent-neutral-reason objection: the content of another person’s pain, or of some future good, can supply a reason whose force does not depend on what the agent currently cares about. (4) The cognitive-passion objection: emotions such as anger, fear, and grief are partly constituted by evaluative judgements, so the premise that reason and passion contribute separable ingredients to action is too coarse to support the conclusion that reason alone lacks motivational power.

I want one further objection not already on this list: not a rewording, special case, or conjunction of these four. It need not occur in the literature. State precisely which sentence or presupposition of the quoted passage it denies, and then say whether it would also tell against Williams’s internal reasons thesis or whether that thesis could survive it.
