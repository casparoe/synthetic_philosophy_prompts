The book isn't a philosophy lecture, but there's a long argument among the human characters about what they owe these pigs, and I don't want that argument to just be whichever half-remembered position I absorbed years ago. So before I write those scenes, give me the desiderata. What does any adequate theory of moral status — and of whether speciesism is the right way to frame what's going on — have to get right? I want the requirements a theory would have to meet, not the theory itself. The cases it has to handle: the marginal humans on one side, maybe insects on the other, the talking pig in the middle. The distinctions it can't collapse: having moral status versus how much that status weighs, sentience versus higher cognition, being a member of a species versus having the traits that usually go with it. And whatever from biology it can't wave away, like continuity and the absence of any clean line. For each requirement, say why it's a genuine requirement rather than one camp's preference, flag any that can't be jointly satisfied, and tell me which single requirement does the most to weed out the positions people usually reach for. Continuous prose only please — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, write it like an email — and in British English.
