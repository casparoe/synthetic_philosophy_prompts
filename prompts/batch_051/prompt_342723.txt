I need a thought experiment built to order, and I want it done carefully enough that the verdicts actually fall out of the two views rather than being stipulated. This is for a note I'm writing on whether the weak procreation asymmetry can do real work without sliding into Benatar's stronger conclusion that it's better never to have been born, so don't smuggle in the strong conclusion.

Here are the two views. Don't bother looking anything up; work from these definitions.

The weak procreation asymmetry: the fact that a possible child would be happy is not, by itself, a reason to create that child. If you don't create the child, the absence of that happiness isn't bad for anyone, because there's nobody to miss it. By contrast, the fact that a possible child would suffer is a reason not to create them, at least when the suffering would make the childs life on balance not worth starting. For a life clearly worth starting, the view is neutral on child welfare: there's no welfare-based reason in favor of creating, and no welfare-based reason against. For a life clearly not worth starting, the suffering counts as a reason against, and the happy parts don't offset it in the create-them direction.

The symmetric view: a possible child's expected happiness counts in favor of creating them, and their expected suffering counts against. Aggregate the two. All else equal, create when expected lifetime welfare comes out positive; don't create when it comes out negative.

Now the build. Give me two nearby cases, ordinary and realistic (a parents' decision, a known medical condition, no sci-fi, no weird metaphysics). In the first case, the two views should deliver the same verdict — don't have the child — but for different deep reasons: the symmetric view says the expected value is negative; the weak asymmetry says the life is not worth starting, and the good parts don't count on the positive side of the ledger at all. In the second case, change exactly one thing: the child's expected lifetime happiness goes up, enough to make the projected life clearly worth starting, while the expected suffering stays roughly fixed. After that single change, the views should come apart: the symmetric view says have the child, all else equal; the weak asymmetry says there is still no child-welfare reason to have the child, so if the couple is indifferent and only open to being moved by the child's own welfare, they don't have the child.
