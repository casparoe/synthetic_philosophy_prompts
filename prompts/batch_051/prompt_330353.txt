I am a PhD student in behavioral ecology, and I have been asked to give a single seventy-five-minute guest session in an undergraduate animal behavior course. The students have had a standard introduction to natural selection and kin selection, but no game theory at all. I want the session to explain, at a conceptual level, how cooperation between unrelated individuals can be evolutionarily stable without appealing to conscious intention, the good of the species, or group advantage. My plan is to build the whole session around the contrast between the one-shot and the iterated prisoner's dilemma, and to use the iterated version as the organizing image for reciprocity as a self-interested equilibrium. I do not want to teach formal payoff matrices or run through a large taxonomy of strategies; a whiteboard sketch and one worked numerical story is as formal as I want to get.

Please plan the session as a teacher rather than write the explanation itself. I want the plan to make the dependency structure explicit: which ideas have to be secured before which later ideas can make sense, where a digression into public goods games or general multilevel selection would derail rather than help, and which points need a slow worked example rather than a crisp summary. I also want you to specify the single animal or human example that should carry the session from start to finish. It should be a case in which a defector can do better in any single encounter, but where repetition, recognition of partners, and the expectation of future encounters make conditional cooperation plausible; say what properties the example must have, what distracting features it must lack, and justify the choice. Then map the misconceptions you expect these students to bring or form during the session and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. I am especially worried about the following traps: the thought that evolution favors the good of the species, the belief that repeated contact automatically produces cooperation, the idea that tit-for-tat is a theorem rather than a successful but limited strategy under noisy conditions, and the inference that the last round of a finite repeated game gives the key to the whole thing whereas the real logic runs from the shadow of the future backward. Finally, say how you would know at the end whether students have understood the difference between one-shot and repeated interactions rather than merely learned the vocabulary.

Please structure your answer in the following named sections, in this order, with approximately the indicated lengths:

1. Learning objective and prerequisites (120–180 words)
2. Order of ideas and dependency structure (350–450 words)
3. The carrying example (180–250 words)
4. Misconception map and defusing points (250–350 words)
5. End-of-session check for understanding and conscious omissions (150–250 words)
