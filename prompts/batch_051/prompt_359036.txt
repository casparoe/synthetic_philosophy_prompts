Working definition: a population counts as an invasive species if and only if (a) it is not native to the ecosystem where it occurs, (b) its presence is due to human agency, (c) it establishes and spreads on its own, and (d) it causes or is likely to cause substantial ecological or economic harm. Now consider a gene-drive eradication project. On an island whose only rodents are invasive house mice, conservationists release laboratory house mice engineered to carry a synthetic gene drive that biases its own inheritance; the drive is expected to spread through the wild population and crash it by disrupting reproduction, perhaps to local extinction. The species is already present and already invasive; only the engineered element is new, and the release is intended to be irreversible once begun. Is the released gene-drive mouse population itself an invasive species under (a)–(d)? Work through the criteria in order, identify the places where they underdetermine the verdict, and then say whether the case reveals hidden assumptions in the definition — for example about whether harm should be assessed per affected species or for the ecosystem as a whole, and whether an engineered genetic element can count as its own kind of invader.
