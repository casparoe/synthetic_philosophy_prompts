I'm helping put together a small workshop on self-regulation and performance, and two of the participants have been going at each other for months about whether thinking about thinking is actually good for you, on net. One of them—call her the reflection-first person—is convinced that explicit metacognition, things like monitoring your understanding, verbalizing your reasons, self-testing, and calibrating your confidence, pretty reliably improves learning, decisions, and skilled performance, and that the apparent failures (rumination, choking) are mostly cases of metacognition done badly, or a different thing altogether. The other—call him the automaticity person—thinks the failures are the real story: verbal overshadowing disrupts exactly the abilities that are best left nonverbal, step-by-step self-monitoring makes experts choke under pressure, rumination is what people get when they take the advice to examine their thoughts too seriously, and metacognitive training doesn't transfer much beyond the trained task. Both actually know the research well enough that neither can dismiss the other as ignorant, which is why the argument never ends.

The useful thing would be for them to have co-written an adversarial-collaboration statement instead of arguing, and I'd like you to draft it. Please make it include: (1) a numbered section of claims both sides can genuinely sign—not truisms, but precise enough to teach a third party something—about what metacognition is, what its costs and benefits are thought to operate on, and where the existing evidence is actually solid; (2) a table of the residual disagreements, stated first in neutral wording, then in the wording each side would accept for its own view, without caricature; (3) the real cruxes. I'd guess one crux is whether verbalizing your reasons deforms the underlying judgment or only your ability to report it afterwards, another is whether rumination should be grouped with metacognition at all or treated as a seperate maladaptive style, and another is whether metacognitive training improves transfer or just performance on the trained task—but pick the 3 to 5 cruxes you think actually do the work, and for each one give a concrete experiment or real-world case both sides would accept as bearing on it, described concretely enough to be clear without citing a paper, plus each side's prediction recorded in advance; (4) a short paragraph on which disagreements are mostly verbal and which are substantive.

Commit to it: give me the single wording you think each side would sign for each clause and each crux, not a menu of alternatives, and if you think the visible evidence already leans one way on a particular crux, say so flatly and say what result would flip you. Where the two camps are actually closer than they admit, say that too. They would both rather be disagreed with precisely than flattered vaguely. Aim for somewhere around 800 words, formatted as an actual statement they could put their names on.
