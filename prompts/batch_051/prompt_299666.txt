I've been trying to work out when society owes people compensation for bad outcomes, and my verdicts on particular cases won't sit still under any obvious rule. Here is where I land.

Anya was born with a painful but treatable genetic condition whose treatment costs will outstrip anything she can earn. I think all of it should be covered.

Ben was a financially literate adult who read the disclosures, ignored knowledgeable friends, and put his entire retirement savings into a speculative venture that collapsed. I don't think he's owed a bailout.

Carmen started smoking at thirteen in a household where every adult smoked and cigarettes were always within reach, and developed lung cancer at fifty. My gut says cover her treatment in full.

Dan is an accomplished alpinist who, against clear weather guidance, climbed into a storm and fell; he needs several surgeries and a year of rehabilitation. I think he should receive the emergency care and rehab, but I feel the costs should partly fall on him — and I can't say why I'd rescue Dan but not Ben.

Elena's house was destroyed by a tornado no one could have predicted. I think we should help her rebuild.

Farid, who knew the odds and bet his savings on sports, lost everything. I don't think he's owed a bailout.

Henry was raised in wealth and acquired tastes only that wealth could keep satisfying; he says he cannot get the well-being others get from modest housing and ordinary food. I don't think the rest of us owe him the extra money it would take for him to feel equally well off.

And then there's Grace, the case I keep going back and forth on: raised in poverty, she took a dangerous logging job at eighteen — injury rates were public and high — because it paid enough to support her younger siblings, and she is now disabled. Part of me says she chose the risk and must bear it; part of me says the choice was so constrained that the injury should count as bad luck instead.

Is there a principle that captures these verdicts, or nearly all of them? State the best one precisely, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it has to overrule, since I don't insist that all of them survive. Say what the principle implies about Grace. Two pairings worry me most: Carmen and Ben both made choices with known risks, yet I want to treat them differently; Anya and Henry both face unchosen deficits in what their circumstances do for them, yet I want to treat them differently too. Your principle should either explain these contrasts or tell me which of the paired verdicts to abandon.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the principle; (2) which of my verdicts it gets right and why; (3) which it overrules and why those are the verdicts my gut most likely has wrong; (4) the strongest rival principle you considered and why you didn't settle on it; (5) which consideration would most likely change your mind about the principle, or where its reasoning is most likely to fail.
