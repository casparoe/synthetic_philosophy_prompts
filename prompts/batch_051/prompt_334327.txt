I teach a university course in informal logic and argumentation theory, and my department has been circulating a proposal to replace part of the course with a competitive "argumentation league" modeled on varsity sport: bracketed oral debates, a codified rulebook, trained judges, time controls, regular practice drills, spectator attendance, and a ranking system. The proposal's central argument is that rigorous reasoning would be improved by borrowing the machinery of competitive athletics, because argumentation and sport are analogous enterprises: both are structured contests in which skilled performance is developed by repeated, scored, adversarial practice and improved by public accountability. I find the analogy suspicious, but the people making the proposal are smart, and nothing I have read treats the comparison systematically enough to settle the matter.

As a philosopher of argumentation who also thinks carefully about the design of competitive institutions, I would like you to audit this analogy for me before I respond to the proposal. Start by brainstorming the correspondences: what, if anything, plays the role of the sport, the athlete, the coach, the rules, the referee, the score, the training regime, the fans, and the ranking in argumentative practice. Then run the audit: which correspondences are genuine and load-bearing, which break under pressure, and what features the sports analogy quietly imports into argumentation that argumentative practice might lack or should refuse—for example, the priority of winning over truth, the incentive to exploit loopholes, the rewards for entertaining an audience rather than for soundness, and the effects of time pressure and partisanship on inquiry. Decide whether the conclusion that modeling argumentation on sport would make it more rigorous and more honest survives the disanalogies, or whether the analogy was doing the normative work all along. If it fails, say what a better source-domain would be (engineering, medicine, scientific collaboration, adjudication, or something else) and what that alternative would license instead. Be explicit about which of your objections are fatal to the proposal and which only show that the sports model needs to be heavily modified; a table of correspondences is fine, but the substance should be in the audit.
