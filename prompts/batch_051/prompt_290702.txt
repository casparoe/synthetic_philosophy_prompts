Take Truman's decision to drop the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki and analyze it through Anscombe's moral framework — the one behind her famous public protest over Oxford's plan to give Truman an honorary degree. I know the basic history: August 1945, two bombs, over a hundred thousand dead, overwhelmingly noncombatants, Japan surrendering within days, and the standard justification that the bombs ended the war and made an invasion unnecessary. Don't rehearse the history.

What I want is a careful mapping of the episode onto her actual commitments. Her view, roughly, is that deliberately killing the innocent as a means to your ends is murder, full stop, and no accumulation of good consequences — lives saved, war ended — can make it permissible. She also treats the intention/foresight distinction as easily abused in cases like this, so don't let a double-effect story do all the work. Using that aparatus: who exactly is the murderer on her account, and why — Truman, the aircrews, the scientists? What did they intend, and who counts as "the innocent" here? Then say what she thinks the decision makers should have done instead, and be honest about whether her framework actually yields an answer to how the war should have been ended, or whether it just says "not that."

Then turn it around. Where does her lens distort or go silent? The combatant/noncombatant line gets blurry in a total-war economy (factory workers, transport networks, cities already flattened by firebombing), and a large part of the actual decision was about the Soviet entry into the war and the deadlock inside the Japanese cabinet, which her framework doesn't seem built to model. Also think about the idea that the two targets were chosen partly for demonstration effect rather than purely as means-to-killing — does that change her verdict, or is it exactly the kind of move she predicted? In the end, say whether the episode is better read as a confirmation of her absolutism, an anomaly for it, or a case she was never trying to handle.
