In my Galois theory course this term, we have repeatedly encountered a basic point about naming mathematical objects: the roots of an irreducible polynomial over a field cannot be canonically ordered or labeled by the base field. For instance, if an irreducible cubic over Q has Galois group S₃, then no expression built from the coefficients and the field operations can single out one of the three roots as "the first one"; any construction that appears to depend on such a labeling is only legitimate if it is invariant under all permutations of the roots. Separately, in a course on the semantics of programming languages, we have been studying α-equivalence in the λ-calculus, where the name of a bound variable is similarly arbitrary: λx.x and λy.y denote the same term, and a definition that pattern-matches on bound-variable names fails to be well-defined unless it is invariant under renaming. I have started to suspect that these are two instances of one idea: define an object only up to a group of renamings, then require all properties to respect the quotient by that group. But I can also see reasons to worry that the analogy is superficial — in the algebraic case the roots are distinct objects that the base field happens not to be able to distinguish, whereas in the λ-calculus the variable names are not objects in the semantics at all. Is the parallel between "no canonical labeling of roots" and "α-equivalence of bound variables" a genuine structural similarity, or only a verbal one? Where exactly do the two cases match, and where do they come apart?
