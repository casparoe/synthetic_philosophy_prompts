I have been arguing with two friends about the relationship between Burkean conservatism and the principle people now call Chesterton's fence. The Burkean in the group defends something like this: long-standing institutions carry a presumption in their favour; they embody a fund of inherited wisdom that no present generation can fully articulate or replicate; change should therefore be slow, piecemeal, and respectful of the social fabric; and the burden of argument falls heavily on the reformer. The Chestertonian defends a more narrowly epistemic principle: to borrow the familiar image, if you cannot see the use of a fence, you have no business pulling it down until you have understood what it is for; the fact that its function is invisible to you is a fact about your ignorance, not about the fence. On the surface these two positions look like natural allies, but our argument kept returning to the same question: are they actually the same idea at different levels of abstraction, or does each commit you to things the other does not?

I would like a careful comparison of the two principles rather than a summary of either. In particular, please sort out the following. First, is Chesterton's fence best read as a mere heuristic for identifying hidden functions, while Burkean conservatism is a substantive thesis about where political legitimacy comes from? Second, does Chesterton's principle, once the function of an institution has been discovered, leave the reformer free to abolish it, whereas Burke adds an independent reason for preservation that survives that discovery—something like the value of continuity, prescription, or the social fabric itself? Third, could a broadly rationalist or utilitarian reformer accept Chesterton's fence while rejecting Burke, and could a Burkean consistently reject Chesterton's fence in at least some cases—for example, where an institution is too new, too local, or too clearly the product of rent-seeking to deserve the reformer's deference? Finally, what is the cheapest way of stating the genuine difference between the two: one suggests an objection to a particular kind of argument for change, while the other suggests a standing distribution of political authority across generations? Please give me a structured comparison, and where the two principles part company, say which of them carries the stronger claim in that particular respect. Please answer in British English.
