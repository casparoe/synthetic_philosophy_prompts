Question: Set out the standard one-urn Ellsberg problem. Explain the typical choice pattern, why it is commonly taken to violate Savage's sure-thing principle (P2) and hence subjective expected utility, and then describe one non-expected-utility model (Choquet expected utility or maxmin expected utility) that can represent an ambiguity-averse agent. In doing so, state exactly which feature of the standard SEU framework the model relaxes.

Student answer:
"In the one-urn version there are 90 balls: 30 red, and 60 black or yellow in unknown proportions. In the first pair, people typically prefer a bet paying $100 on red to a bet paying $100 on black. In the second pair, they typically prefer a bet paying $100 on black or yellow to a bet paying $100 on red or yellow. The first preference tells us p(red) > p(black). The second tells us p(black or yellow) > p(red or yellow); since both bets pay the same on yellow, this reduces to p(black) > p(red). These two inequalities contradict, so no single additive subjective probability can rationalize the pair. The choices thus violate Savage's sure-thing principle, which says preferences between two acts should not depend on states where the acts have identical consequences. On the yellow state the two bets in each pair agree, so the red-vs-black comparison should be the same across pairs, but it flips.

The usual explanation is ambiguity aversion: people dislike bets whose probabilities they don't know. But this is really just an extreme form of risk aversion. An urn with an unknown black/yellow split has a higher-variance payoff distribution than a known urn does, so a sufficiently concave utility function already explains the Ellsberg pattern; there's no need to abandon expected utility.

Still, if we want a model of it, Choquet expected utility replaces the additive probability with a non-additive capacity v and evaluates acts by the Choquet integral. For instance, v(red) = 1/3, v(black) = 0, v(black or yellow) = 2/3, and v(red or yellow) = 1/3 deliver both preferences at once. The capacity is allowed to be non-additive, and that is exactly what gets relaxed relative to Savage. In maxmin expected utility, the agent has a set of priors and evaluates each act by its worst-case prior; an agent of that kind will always prefer a bet with known probabilities to a bet with unknown probabilities, whatever the payoffs, which is just another way of building in ambiguity aversion."
