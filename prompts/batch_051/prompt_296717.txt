Here's an asymmetry I've never been able to get straight. Take two people who commit the exact same crime, say an embezzlement, same amount and same kind of employer, in a jurisdiction where that offense has a ten-year statute of limitations, and the evidence against each is airtight. One gets caught after a month and goes to prison. The other covers it up, moves across the country, and is caught 25 years later only because a deathbed confession finally names him. The law says too late: no prosecution, even though nobody doubts he did it. The standard explanation is that evidence goes stale, witnesses die, memories fade, papers get lost. But that cant be the real principle, because we would also refuse to prosecute when the evidence has not decayed at all, and we usually make murder an exception that never expires while spending public money on cold-case units chasing older crimes with shakier evidence. So the practice seems to treat the wrongness itself as having an expiration date, but only for some crimes and only through this weird administrative side channel.

What I want is the strongest possible justification for treating two identical acts differently when only the calendar changed, not just a list of the practical considerations. Then test it against the hard cases: the offender who caused the delay by hiding the crime, the victim who lived with the harm the whole time, the offender who spent 25 years becoming a genuinely different and decent person, the murder exception, and this thought experiment: a state quietly eliminates its statutes of limitations for five years, and nothing terrible happens — no uptick in wrongful convictions, no flood of shaky old prosecutions, just some guilty old people going to prison late. If the best justification survives all of that, tell me the practice should be kept as is. If it survives only some, tell me exactly how the rule should be restricted. And if it collapses under all of them, tell me we should get rid of the limits entirely. Write the whole thing in American English.
