Late last night a friend and I were arguing about performatives — specifically the way saying "I promise" doesn't describe an obligation but creates one. I built this argument about how a release from a promise could possibly work, and it felt airtight at 2 a.m. but has to be wrong. Can you walk me through it and say whether it's valid? If not, tell me exactly which step fails and what the correct version of that step would be.

(1) Necessarily, if a promise is in force, the promisor is obligated to do what was promised.
(2) A promise can be waived by the promisee; saying "I release you from your promise" does that — it just is what it is to release someone.
(3) To waive a promise, the promise has to be in force at the moment of the waiver — otherwise there'd be nothing to waive. But a moment later, once the waiver has happened, the promisor is no longer obligated.
(4) So if waiving a promise is possible, there is a possible situation in which a promise is in force and the promisor is not obligated: the promise is still standing, but the promisee has just let them off the hook.
(5) But (1) says such a situation is impossible. So being released from a promise is impossible.

The conclusion is obviously absurd, and my friend kept pointing at step 4, but I couldn't articulate exactly what the slide is from "in force at the moment of the waiver" to "still standing afterward." Did I equivocate on "in force"? Or is one of the earlier premises doing secret work?
