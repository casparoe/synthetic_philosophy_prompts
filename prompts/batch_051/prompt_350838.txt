I'm not a scholar, just someone who has been turning a particular theological puzzle over on my own for a few years, and I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher of religion while you work on it. Please think carefully, and before you settle on a verdict, take some time to brainstorm several possible diagnoses and only then defend the one you find most convincing.

The debate I have in mind is the Christian one about final punishment. Roughly, the three main positions are eternal conscious torment, annihilationism (sometimes called conditional immortality), and universal salvation. What strikes me is how much the three sides agree on: that God is perfectly loving and perfectly just, that wrongdoing calls for some kind of adverse response, and that the relevant texts and arguments are complicated enough to give everyone something to point to. Yet each side holds that the others are not merely wrong about a detail but wrong about what justice or love even consists in. The debate has gone on for centuries, and as far as I can tell it simply recycles the same moves: the eternal-torment side argues that a finite act can merit an endless punishment because the one wronged is infinite in dignity; the annihilationist side answers that a just punishment must fit the deed and that endless conscious suffering overshoots it; the universalist side answers that any punishment that never restores the punished person is not God's kind of justice at all.

I do not want a survey of the positions or their scriptural cases. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal, with the sides meaning different things by terms like punishment, justice, or love? Is there a hidden factual or interpretive question doing the real work? Do the sides differ about which considerations may count as decisive — for instance, about whether our human intuitions about proportionality are a reliable guide to divine justice? Or is this a clash of bedrock starting points that none of the sides can argue for without already assuming what they are trying to show?

My working hypothesis is that the three positions are not really answering the same question under one heading. One side is asking what a finite creature can deserve for wronging an infinitely perfect being; another is asking what punishment is for in the first place — retribution, deterrence, reform, restoration, or something else; and a third is asking what a perfectly loving God is permitted to allow. They then talk past one another because each side treats its own question as if it were the whole debate. Is that hypothesis right, or is it too neat? If it is wrong, what is the better diagnosis?

Finally, given whichever diagnosis you defend, tell me what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a reframing of the question, an argument, a way of testing the matters at stake — or, alternatively, make the case that no progress should be expected and explain why.
