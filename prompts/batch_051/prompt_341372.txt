A friend walked me through this and I still can't see where it goes wrong.

(1) How much a being matters morally is a function of how sentient it is.
(2) Sentience isn't binary; it's graded. A dog has more of it than a bee, a bee more than a hoverfly, and as you keep going down the ladder the features doing the work—reacting to stimuli, integrating signals, something loosely like valence—shade into the ordinary feedback loops of a thermostat.
(3) Adjacent systems on this continuum can't differ in whether they count morally: one more neuron, or one more feedback loop, can't be the place where a moral patient switches on.
(4) But adjacency chains. If no single tiny step matters for whether something counts, then chaining all the tiny steps says the thermostat counts morally too.
(5) That's absurd, and the obvious exits all look bad: draw a sharp line on a gradient (arbitrary), deny that sentience is graded (false), or deny that sentience grounds moral status (speciesism?).

So which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Or is there a move I'm missing?
