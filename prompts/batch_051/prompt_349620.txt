Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument runs roughly as follows: start from whatever distribution you consider just; then allow any sequence of voluntary transfers — a million people each paying twenty-five cents to watch Chamberlain play. Because each step preserves justice (the starting point was just, and the transfers were consensual), the resulting highly unequal distribution must also be just, and any patterned principle of justice would require continuous interference with people's free exchanges. Now change one feature of the case. The transfers are no longer cash paid at a ticket window. They are microtransactions inside a personalized, algorithmically optimized application: the choice architecture is continuously A/B-tested, purchase offers are timed to moments of demonstrated impulsivity, and the product uses variable-reward mechanics known to produce compulsive engagement that many users report wanting to escape but cannot. Assume the dollar amounts and the final inequality are otherwise the same as in Chamberlain's case. Does the Wilt Chamberlain argument survive this development? Identify the exact premise the changed setting puts under pressure — presumably the voluntariness of the transfers — and explain whether Nozick's argument can absorb the case, whether it needs a repaired account of voluntary transfer, or whether the notion of consent it relies on was doing more work than the argument acknowledged. Be specific about what, if anything, of the original argument remains intact.
