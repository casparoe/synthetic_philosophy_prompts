I serve on a fitness-to-practise panel for a healthcare regulator. We meet about once a month and usually have a single day to hear a case of serious professional misconduct — falsifying records, breaching patient confidentiality, covering up an error, failing to act when a colleague was harming a patient — and then we must issue written reasons that will survive an appeal. The panel is a mix of clinicians and lay members; none of us is a philosopher or a lawyer, though a legal adviser attends for questions of process rather than substance. A recurring defence is, in one form or another, that the person had no real alternative: they say they were threatened by a superior, or feared losing their job, or were placed in a structure where refusing would have ended their career. At present we treat that claim as a matter of gut feeling, and I suspect our findings are inconsistent from one panel to the next.

I have been reading about Frankfurt-style cases and the principle of alternative possibilities — roughly, the idea that a person is morally responsible for an action only if they could have done otherwise — and I take the point of the Frankfurt cases to be that a lack of alternatives need not undermine responsibility when the constraint never actually moved the person. Please turn that insight into a procedure my panel can actually run. I want an ordered set of questions we ask about each case, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a finding of no culpability or of mitigation, and where it must hand the residual judgment to the panel because the question is evaluative rather than factual. The procedure should separate, at minimum: constraints that were causally inert in the person's actual deliberation; constraints that entered their reasoning only as pressures any professional should be expected to withstand; and constraints that removed a fair opportunity to do otherwise and therefore properly excuse. Be honest about where the procedure misfires — for example, cases where the claim of having had no alternative is true in one sense but irrelevant in another, and cases where a mechanically applied Frankfurt test would be unfair to the accused.

Then run the procedure on three cases: first, a junior doctor altered a note after a consultant told her he would make her training a misery if she refused, and she believed, plausibly, that refusing would destroy her career; second, a data manager deleted adverse safety results, knowing it was wrong, after a manager threatened her with redundancy if she did not, but the evidence shows she had already decided to delete them before the threat was made, to protect the unit's funding; third, a nurse breached a patient's confidentiality while under a credible threat to her own safety, where there is no evidence she had any prior inclination to breach and the threat was the whole explanation of her act. At least one of these cases should produce an answer the panel will not like; say so plainly and explain why the procedure nevertheless gives that answer. Please write in British English throughout, and end by telling me how confident you are in the procedure and its applications, and identifying the single step or judgment in it that you are least sure of.
