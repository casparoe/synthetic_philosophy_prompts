I've been going around in circles on the absent-minded driver problem alot, and I've landed on a view. Now do the thing where you tell me what counts against it.

Quick setup, in case the version I have in my head is nonstandard. A driver is on a highway with two identical-looking exits in a row. Turning at the first gives 0; turning at the second gives 4; missing both lands him at a motel for 1. He's absent-minded: when he's at an exit he can't tell whether it's the first or second, so anything he actually does must treat both exits the same. If he turns with probability p at whichever exit he's at, the expected payoff is p·0 + (1 − p)(p·4 + (1 − p)·1) = (1 − p)(1 + 3p), which is maximized at p = 1/3 and pays 4/3.

The position: the fancy "continue at the first exit, turn at the second" plan was never really available to him, since it needs exactly the distinction the case denies him. So the paradox mostly dissolves, and p = 1/3 just is the right play.

What would have to be true for me to be wrong? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: wich decision-theoretic principles p = 1/3 secretly offends, which variants of the case (commitment before the drive, a co-driver, different payoffs, a long line of identical exits) would push the answer away from 1/3, and which rival answer (p = 1/2, or the view that there's no stable answer) would have to be correct. Say which of these a committed p = 1/3 person should themselves concede as counting against them, rather than brushing off as "not the same problem." And give me an honest read on whether my view has been padded into unfalsifiability, and whether, for a view like this, that's actually a problem. What would you personally find most persuasive as a defeater?
