I’m a high school student trying to get clear about proper names, especially the difference between given names and family names. I recently read part of John Stuart Mill’s A System of Logic, Ratiocinative and Inductive, Book I, Chapter II, Of Names. The passage I keep turning over is the one where Mill argues that proper names are not connotative. My rough paraphrase of his point is this: a proper name serves to mark an individual, but it does not imply or attribute any characteristics to that individual. I want to see whether my own reactions to naming cases support or complicate that claim.

Here are my verdicts on six cases.

1. A classmate legally changes her given name from Linda to Priya. I think both names refer to the same person, and neither says anything true or false about her. If someone said, There goes Linda, after the change, I would not think they had said something false; they just used an outdated label.

2. My neighbor is Ms. Baker. I know she is not a baker and no one in her household has ever baked. I do not think calling her Ms. Baker says, even implicitly, that she bakes or is from a baking family. It just refers to her. If I learned she had changed her surname from Miller to Baker for no reason at all, I still would not think her name was false, though I would think it was unusual.

3. But when I hear a surname like Patel or O’Brien, I do feel I have been given some information. It is not just a bare label; it points, however weakly, toward family origins. I do not think it says this person is from Ireland in the way a description would, but it seems to carry more than a pure marker. This feels hard to square with how I treat Baker.

4. People in my school call a straight-haired boy Curly. I think Curly, as his nickname, refers to him. Even though the common noun curly describes hair, his nickname does not claim his hair is curly. If his hair is perfectly straight, the nickname is ironic, not false.

5. For a name like Robert Downey Jr., I think the Jr. is different. It says something relational; if he were not the younger one in the family named after his father, I would not call him Jr. That suffix seems to carry descriptive content in a way that Robert and Downey, for me, do not.

6. A friend of mine chose the name Ash because it is gender-neutral. I think Ash refers to him. It does not say this person is nonbinary or this person chose this name, even if his choosing it gives listeners information about him in context.

Can one principle about proper names, denotation, and connotation capture all six verdicts? Or do I need to split names into types — given names, inherited family names, chosen names, nicknames, suffixes — and treat them differently? If a single account is possible, state it precisely and say what it predicts for a case I have not decided: parents name their baby Emerson, a family surname from earlier generations, but they just like the sound and have no connection to any Emersons. What does the name Emerson do there? Also tell me which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether your principle is closer to Mill’s view or a revision of it.
