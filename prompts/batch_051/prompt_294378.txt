I'm in a political philosophy seminar and one of my classmates keeps running this argument about voting restrictions, and every time I think I've pinned down where it goes wrong it turns out I just don't like the conclusion. He sets it up like this: (1) In every other domain where a decision is high-stakes and there's a body of relevant expertise, we require demonstrated competence before letting someone make it for others — surgery, aviation, structural engineering. (2) Voting is high-stakes and there is plainly relevant expertise about the things governments actually do: tax incidence, monetary policy, immigration, epidemiology, how a bill becomes law, what the office you're voting for even controls. (3) So unless there's some special feature of voting, we should treat it like those other domains and require demonstrated competence — say, a basic test of how the legislature works and who's responsible for what — before letting someone vote. (4) He says the obvious reply, that everyone has an equal right to have a say, can't be wheeled in independently because whether that right exists is exactly what's being questioned. (5) Meanwhile the fallback, that universal suffrage protects against elite capture, is itself an outcome-based argument and cuts both ways: if you're willing to let outcomes decide, then the empirical case for an uninformed public veto is at best shaky. His conclusion is that the only honest positions are epistocracy or abandoning the idea that competence matters for high-stakes decisions.

The thing that bothers me is that (1) looks right and (5) feels almost like a trap, but the argument seems to prove too much. By its lights we should also be asking why we let people make completely unvetted, high-stakes decisions in other parts of life — how to raise their kids, whether to decline a recommended treatment, how much debt to take on — and almost nobody, including my classmate, wants licenses for those. So either there's a principled difference between voting and those other decisions, or something in (1)–(5) is doing hidden work, or the argument is valid and we're all just being squeamish. I'd like to keep the idea that competence matters and keep universal suffrage, if I can. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
