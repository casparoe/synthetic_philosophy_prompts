I run a small renovation firm and spend much of my time attaching numbers to things I do not know: how long a subcontractor will take, what a material will cost next quarter, whether a supplier will deliver on schedule. Over the years I have settled on a rule for these judgments. When I know only that a quantity lies between a lower and an upper bound, I treat every value in that interval as equally likely. When I know a little more — say, the average of past values — I choose the probability assignment that stays as close to that even spread as possible while still matching the few facts I actually know. I like the rule because it feels as though it adds no opinion of my own; it refuses to favor any possibility except to the degree the evidence does.

Recently the rule has begun to bother me. The same state of ignorance, expressed in two equally natural ways, forces two different answers out of it. Suppose I know only that one amount stands between one-third and three times another. If I write the unknown as the ratio of A to B, my rule spreads probability evenly over the interval from one-third to three. If I write the identical information as the ratio of B to A, the even spread over the same interval implies something different about A to B. I have been patching this by quietly choosing whatever feels like the natural scale, but I now suspect that the choice of scale is doing hidden work, and that my policy of adding no opinion is actually adding a very particular opinion.

I want an honest assessment. Is a policy of always choosing the flattest, most even probability assignment the known facts allow defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as neutrality? Is there a principled way to decide which description to spread evenly over, or a restatement of the rule that keeps its benefits without the contradiction? Or should I give up on trying to be maximally unopinionated?
