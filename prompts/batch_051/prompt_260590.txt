I'm taking a Heidegger seminar and we've been working through his account of readiness-to-hand (Zuhandenheit) versus presence-at-hand (Vorhandenheit). The way I've internalized the criteria is something like this: a piece of equipment is ready-to-hand when (1) it withdraws into the task — you don't observe it, you use it; (2) it's understood in terms of its "in order to" and the whole network of assignments it sits in, not as a self-contained object; and (3) it only becomes conspicuous, obtrusive, or obstinate when it breaks, fails, or go missing. The hammer is the usual example: while hammering you're not looking at the hammer, you're looking at the wall; the hammer vanishes into the whole project. Pick up a hammer whose head is loose or find the hammer missing, and suddenly there's this thing with properties lying there, and then you can start theorizing about it.

Here's the case I keep going around on: a guide dog. My cousin is blind and uses a guide dog, and once she said she sometimes forgets the dog is there — not in a bad way, but the way you forget you're feet while walking. The dog stops at curbs, steers around obstacles, locates the door; she experiences the route and the coffee shop at the end, not the harness. So far that sounds exactly like a hammer disappearing into the work. But the dog is alive, gets tired, can be distracted, can refuse. When the dog got distracted by another dog behind a window and walked her into a sign, she didn't suddenly see the dog as a broken object the way I see a hammer with a loose head; she had to deal with it as a creature with its own attention. So my question: does the guide dog qualify as ready-to-hand equipment in Heidegger's sense? If yes, does that mean breakdown does not always flip things into presence-at-hand? If no, which criterion actually fails, and does that show the criteria were built around hammers from the start?

Please organize your answer into these sections: "The criteria" (a short restatement of what makes something ready-to-hand and what breakdown is supposed to do), "The case for" (around 150-200 words: the respects in which the dog is like a hammer), "The case against" (same length: the respects in which it's not, especially living agency and the odd kind of breakdown), "Where the criteria go quiet" (roughly 100-150 words on whether the inherited criteria just don't settle this), and "My verdict" (roughly 150 words, including whether the case should make us revise the thing about breakdown).
