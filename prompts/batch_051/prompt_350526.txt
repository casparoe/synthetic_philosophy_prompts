I work in trust and safety at a fairly large platform, and every few weeks I have to decide whether a bunch of accounts that are loosely coordinated should be treated as one actor for enforcement purposes. The obvious cases are easy — somebody with forty sockpuppets. But we keep hitting weirder ones: a hashtag mob nobody is running, a set of algorithmic accounts all reading the same queue and reacting to eachother, a wiki whose edits come from hundreds of anonymous IPs but keep producing something that 'wants' the same outcome. I want an actual procedure, not a vibe.

I've had this connected in my head to Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary and the Ant Fugue dialogue in Gödel, Escher, Bach, where the claim is that an ant colony can have a kind of mind even though no individual ant does, because the colony-level traffic of signals and roles is doing the thinking. Dennett's intentional stance is hovering nearby: treat something as having beliefs and desires when that's the useful way to predict it. Turn that into a checklist my team can run on a case in about twenty minutes, with a yes/no/flag-for-human outcome. The decision isn't 'is it conscious', its 'should we treat this swarm as one agent' — for example, can we attribute a coordinated campaign to one responsible actor, does it make sense to issue one notice, and does banning the supposed leader do anything when there isn't one.

I want the checklist to resist both failure modes at once: telling a cute story about every crowd and calling it an agent, and refusing to treat a genuinely headless swarm as one agent because there's no individual in charge. Ask the questions in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and don't smuggle in more than the ant-colony/intentional-stance line actually gives you — where it underdetermines the call, flag that. My worry is that any procedure we adopt will goodhart into people ticking boxes while the actual decision gets made on vibes anyway, so design it so that ticking the boxes is what does the conceptual work.

Then run it on three cases: a real ant colony as a sanity check; a meme-stock hivemind where thousands of separate accounts copy each other's trades and the collective apparently 'decides' to hold; and a botnet with a shared command queue but no hierarchy, where the behaviour keeps shifting as the bots update from the same feed. At least one of these should come out in a way that feels wrong from gut instinct, and you should say whether that means the procedure is wrong or the gut is.
