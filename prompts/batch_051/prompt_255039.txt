Below is a passage from David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature, Book 3, Part 2, Section 5 ("Of the obligation of promises"):

"The act of the mind, exprest by a promise, is not a resolution to perform any thing: For that alone never imposes any obligation. Nor is it a desire of such a performance: For we may bind ourselves without such a desire, or even with an aversion, declar'd and avow'd. Neither is it the willing of that action, which we promise to perform: For a promise always regards some future time, and the will has an influence only on present actions."

I'm a philosopher of action, and I want you to act as a careful, unsentimental referee for an idea I've been poking at for a paper. Start by brainstorming the strongest possible version of my view before you evaluate it, take your time, and then tell me where it actually lands.

The view, which I've been calling "promissory forfeiture," goes like this. Hume is right that promising can't be an act of willing a future action. But I think he and most people after him still miss what promising actually is. My proposal is that a promise is a present act of forfeiture: when I promise you that I'll do X, I don't reach into the future and bind my later self. I give you, now, my future self's entitlement to treat non-performance as a mere change of mind. Saturday-me can still decide not to help you move; what he can't do is frame that refusal as just having changed his mind, because Tuesday-me already spent the authority that would have made that framing available. The obligation, if that's even the right word, isn't placed on the future action at all. It's created now, as a transfer of standing from me to you.

I know this sounds like a Ulysses-and-the-mast story, but I think the interpersonal transfer is doing different work than self-binding, and I want to know wether that difference is real. I'm also aware it might just be Hume's convention view dressed up: maybe what I'm calling a "transfer of standing" is only intelligible because a convention of fidelity already exists. So here's what I'd like from you. Is my theory a version of Hume's, a competitor to it, or just a metaphor? What does it get right that Hume's account doesn't, if anything? Give me the three strongest objections, and tell me whether any of them is fatal. And don't be nice: if it's a picture with no argumentative payoff, say so.
