I’m a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I’ve been building an expected-value simulation for a pricing experiment. While testing how my code reasons about stochastic payoffs, I fell into the two-envelope problem and ended up reading David Chalmers’s paper *The Two-Envelope Paradox: A Complete Analysis*. The passage I keep coming back to is this one:

“Recognizing the existence of a distribution immediately shows us that the reasoning that leads to the paradox is not always valid, as Jackson et al note. For example, if the distribution is a uniform distribution over values between 0 and 1000, with amounts over 1000 being impossible, then if A > 500, it is always a bad idea to switch. It is therefore not true that for all distributions and all values of n, p(B>A|A=n) = 0.5.”

That passage makes sense to me as a point about needing a probability distribution, but I want to know whether it fully captures the principle behind my own reactions to variants of the puzzle. Here are my verdicts on a set of cases:

1. Symmetric unknown distribution, before opening: I have no reason to switch. The two envelopes are symmetric.
2. Same unknown-distribution setup, but after I open my envelope and see $100: I still won’t switch, because the bare observation “$100” doesn’t by itself justify the claim that the other envelope has a 50% chance of being $200 and a 50% chance of being $50.
3. Known bounded uniform distribution over the smaller amount between $0 and $1000, and I see $700: I stay, because doubling would be impossible and halving is the only live possibility.
4. Same bounded uniform distribution, and I see $100: I switch. Here both halving and doubling feel live, and the expected value of switching seems above $100.
5. A purported “uniform distribution over all positive real numbers” prior: I treat that as not a genuine probability distribution at all, so I don’t switch and I don’t think the paradox even gets off the ground.
6. A known proper but heavy-tailed distribution with infinite mean for the smaller amount: I see $1000 and I’m genuinely torn; my gut says refuse expected value and stay, but I don’t know if that’s consistent with the rest of my verdicts.

My tentative principle is something like: conditionally switch only when a well-defined probability distribution makes the conditional expected value of the other envelope greater than the amount observed; when there is no proper prior, the 50/50 half-versus-double argument has no force. But I’m not sure this principle handles the heavy-tailed case, and I’m also worried about the apparent tension between verdict 1 and verdict 4: why should seeing $100 under a bounded uniform prior give me a reason to switch, when the two envelopes are still symmetric before the draw? I’d like you to act as a professional decision theorist and philosopher of probability. First brainstorm a few candidate principles that could organize these verdicts. Then state the most defensible principle precisely, show how it handles each case, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and say what it predicts for two cases I haven’t listed: a known bounded but highly non-uniform distribution, and a case where switching costs $1. Finally, tell me whether Chalmers’s quoted point about needing a distribution is enough to justify your principle, or whether the principle needs extra commitments beyond that passage.
