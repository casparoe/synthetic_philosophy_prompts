I keep calling myself a deflationist about truth, but when I lay out my actual verdicts I'm not sure they cohere. Here they are.

Working principle: "p" is true iff p, and truth-talk adds nothing except a device for compressing endorsement or generalizing (so I can say "everything she said is true" without repeating all of it).

Now the cases:
- "Snow is white" is true just in case snow is white. Adding "corresponds to a fact" doesn't tell me anything more.
- Saying "everything the Pope says is true" is just a compact way of endorsing whatever he says, not a report that all his claims share some one glow-y property called truth.
- "There are infinitely many primes" is true, but I can't shake the sense that this reaches past the sentence to the whole subject matter in a way the bare sentence doesn't. If truth is nothing over and above that equivalence, why does the true sentence feel like a stronger claim?
- There are truths nobody will ever believe, like "the number of grains of sand on this beach at noon is odd." I want to say it's true anyway, in exactly the same sense as "snow is white." But if truth-talk is just a device we use to endorse sentences, what's going on with truths nobody endorses?
- I step back at the curb because I believe the bus is coming and my belief is true. But I'm tempted to say the bus and the belief do the explaining, and the truth is idle. I keep flip-flopping on this one.
- "Torture is wrong" — when I answer "that's true" I'm just agreeing with the moral claim, not reporting an extra moral fact.
- I also hate it when people slide from deflationism to "true is just a compliment we pay to sentences we like." Plenty of true sentences are hated, and truth doesn't bend to anyone's liking.

Is there one principle that captures all of this, and does my working principle survive? If some of these verdicts have to go, which ones, and is "truth is just a device for generalization" enough for the primes case and the unendorsed-truths case?
