I'm taking a course this term on games as art, and we've been going hard on this idea that what makes games a distinctive art form isn't the graphics or the story but the agency the game gives you — the choices it makes available, their stakes, the feel of committing to a branch, the way a designer sculpts your options before you even notice them. For my essay I want to bring this to The Stanley Parable. On first look it seems like a perfect fit: the whole game is about following or disobeying a narrator, and it actively comments on that. But the more I think about it, the less sure I am wether the game is art *of* agency or just a very long joke *about* agency. Every choice is pre-scripted; the narrator has a line ready no matter which door you take; the broom closet ending literally praises you for standing in a tiny room doing nothing; a bunch of endings mock you for believing you had any freedom at all. So I'm stuck between two readings: either the game is making agency its medium in a pretty sophisticated way (the texture of option space, complicity, being narrated), or it's actually a counterexample to the theory, because fake, puppet agency still feels like playing.

I'd like a serious reading of The Stanley Parable through the aesthetics-of-agency lens that's fair to both the game and the theory.
