In my research in philosophy of religion, I hold to a doctrine of biblical inerrancy: rightly interpreted, the text contains no errors. I am aware that this commitment is an interpretive hazard, because once error is ruled out in advance, every apparent conflict between passages becomes a problem that has to be solved, and the solving can outrun what the evidence will support. To guard against that, I impose on myself a policy I think of as the common-standard rule. When I meet an apparent contradiction — two accounts of the same episode that disagree about sequence, number, or timing, say — I ask whether the harmonizing move I am tempted to make would be accepted by a competent interpreter of an ordinary ancient text, with no doctrine of inerrancy at stake. If it would not be accepted, I refuse to treat that move as settling the difficulty, and I apply this test strictly. But I combine it with a second rule: when no harmonization passes the common-standard test, I do not record the passage as an error. I record it as unresolved and move on, while my global commitment to inerrancy remains unchanged.

My question is whether these two rules can be rationally held together. I see the force of the first rule: it is meant to keep my harmonizing honest. But I also see how the second rule may smuggle back in, at one remove, the very bias the first rule was designed to prevent — I am strict about individual solutions while ensuring that no accumulation of failures ever counts against the doctrine itself. Is this a defensible epistemic policy, or is it special pleading converted into a hermeneutical method? If it is the latter, can the policy be revised so as to keep the discipline of the first rule without making the doctrine unfalsifiable?
