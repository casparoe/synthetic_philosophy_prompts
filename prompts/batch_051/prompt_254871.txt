Question:
"Explain the Condorcet jury theorem. State its main assumptions and conclusion, and assess how far it supports the claim that larger groups make better decisions than smaller ones."

Student's answer:
"The Condorcet jury theorem concerns a group that must choose between two options, exactly one of which is correct, by majority vote. It states that if each voter independently has a probability p of voting for the correct option, and p > 0.5, then the probability that the majority selects the correct option is greater than p. Moreover, as the number of voters increases, this probability rises and tends to 1. The theorem is therefore often taken to show that large groups are more reliable than any single member, and that adding voters of even modest individual competence improves collective accuracy.

The result rests on two central assumptions. The first is voter competence: each voter must be more likely to be right than wrong. The second is voter independence: each voter's vote must not be influenced by the votes of the others. When these assumptions are met, larger electorates unambiguously outperform smaller ones in the model. When they are not met, the conclusion can fail. If p < 0.5, the majority is actually less likely to be correct than an individual voter, and as the group grows the probability of a correct majority tends to 0. Similarly, if voters simply copy one another, the group does not become more accurate as it grows.

In real collective decision-making, however, these conditions are not particularly demanding. Independence can be secured by preventing communication between voters before the vote, and competence can be secured by selecting people with some relevant expertise. Once those two conditions are in place, the theorem guarantees that a larger jury will outperform a smaller one in practice. This is why the result is so widely invoked in debates about democratic competence and the wisdom of crowds."
