I recently spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether human dignity is doing any real philosophical work. My friend is something of a Kantian, and whenever the discussion got difficult he returned to an architectural metaphor: dignity is the foundation on which the whole edifice of human rights is built. Remove the foundation, he argued, and the rights people actually care about — bodily integrity, freedom of expression, equal treatment, due process — are left hanging with nothing underneath them; they become policy preferences that a legislature could revise away when the political winds shift. I pressed the now-familiar deflationary objection: once you have a worked-out account of autonomy, and perhaps of equality, talk of dignity adds nothing. It is either a vague honorific we hand to conclusions we have already reached on other grounds, or a way of restating those conclusions with more solemnity. My friend replied that this gets the order of explanation backwards. Autonomy grounds a right, he said, only because autonomous beings have a dignity that other things lack; to explain why autonomy matters in the first place you need the foundation, not the upper floors. We went in circles, and I came away suspecting that neither of us could say exactly what the metaphor was licensed to prove.

I would like you to audit the analogy rather than settle the dispute directly in the abstract. Treat the foundation-and-edifice picture as the analogy under examination. What maps onto what — the ground, the foundation, the load-bearing structure, the rooms, the building materials? Where does the mapping genuinely illuminate the role dignity plays, and where does it begin to mislead? In particular, which features of a building's foundation does the metaphor quietly import that dignity may not have: being laid down before the structure exists, being causally necessary for it to stand, being made of a different material from the floors above, being invisible once construction is complete, supporting every part uniformly, and failing all at once if it fails at all? Then separate the disanalogies that matter for his intended use from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Most importantly, does the conclusion he wants — that dignity is a non-redundant ground of rights, one that a deflationary account of autonomy cannot do without — survive once the disanalogies are on the table? And is there a better analogy for what dignity contributes, or should the architectural picture be abandoned rather than repaired?
