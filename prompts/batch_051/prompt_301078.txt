I'm setting up a mock interview for my philosophy reading group. Next week we're putting Johannes de Silentio, the pseudonymous author of *Fear and Trembling*, in the hot seat — the book, not the man, since I want to stick to the claims made under that pseudonym. I don't want him to get away with just repeating that Abraham is the father of faith and that faith is a paradox. I want questions that push on the places where the book's own machinery seems to jam.

Here's the working picture of his position, so the questions can assume it without a long setup. The ethical is the universal, valid for everyone. The tragic hero — Agamemnon giving up Iphigenia, Brutus ordering his sons killed — stays inside the ethical because he gives up something lower for something higher and can therefore be understood. Abraham isn't a tragic hero, because what he's doing with Isaac is a purely private relation to God; that's the teleological suspension of the ethical. Faith is a double movement: first infinite resignation (the knight of infinite resignation gives up the princess and won't even hope for her in this life), and then the further movement of faith, where you trust that you'll receive the thing back in this life, the way Abraham trusts he'll get Isaac back. The knight of faith looks entirely ordinary from the outside — he eats, sleeps, goes to work, nothing outward marks him. And because the single individual stands above the universal in an absolute relation to God, none of this can be said in the public language of ethics, which is why Abraham's silence fits the structure. De Silentio keeps adding that he himself can't understand Abraham; he can only admire him.

Draft me eight to ten questions for this, and don't give him the easy exit of "faith is a paradox" and leaving it there. Make sure there's at least one question each on: (a) the lack of any outward distinguishing mark, and what that means for telling the knight of faith apart from a fanatic who's just heard voices; (b) whether the second movement of the double movement — expecting Isaac back in this life — changes the sacrifice into something other than a sacrifice; (c) whether the ethical is actually preserved in the teleological suspension or quietly canceled, and what the "teleological" part is doing; (d) whether the book's own attempt to write about faith conflicts with the claim that faith can't be spoken; and (e) whether his repeated "I don't understand Abraham, I'm just the poet" is a substantive retreat or a deflector. The rest can go wherever you think the pressure is strongest.

For each question, write the reply de Silentio is most likely to give, phrased the way he'd give it, and then a follow-up question that keys off that reply and tightens the screw.

Format it as a JSON array. Keep everything in American English.
