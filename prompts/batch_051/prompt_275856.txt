I am a novelist, not a philosopher. In my current project I am deliberately reworking the scenario of Ursula K. Le Guin's 1973 story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas, and I want the moral argument in the book to be sharp enough that a careful reader cannot dismiss it as the familiar debate with the names changed. To do that, I need to understand exactly what my one alteration does to the philosophical structure.

As I understand the original: a city's flourishing depends on one child kept in solitary, filthy misery; citizens learn of this when they come of age; most accept it, while some leave the city. In my version, every visible detail is the same, but the mechanism is different. The child's misery does not purchase the city's happiness. Instead, the arrangement that keeps the child in the room is what holds back a much larger misery: if the child were released, thousands of people beyond the walls would suffer in the same way and for the same span of time. The citizens' happiness is real but incidental to the mechanism, not the point of the bargain. The ones who walk away still walk away, but what they are walking away from is no longer a city that buys its joy with a child's suffering.

I am especially interested in how this change alters the analysis under negative utilitarianism and suffering-focused ethics. The response to the original that I have most often encountered from that direction is that Omelas is impermissible because no amount of positive happiness can outweigh the child's suffering. In my variant, the child's suffering is no longer weighed against happiness; it is weighed against the prevention of a far greater quantity of suffering. Does the negative utilitarian now have to accept the arrangement, or at least lose the clean rejection? Or are there commitments within suffering-focused ethics that still allow a condemnation? Which standard positions on the original survive this change intact, and which flip or become unstable? I am particularly interested in the distinction between weighing suffering against happiness, which negative utilitarians typically refuse to do, and weighing suffering against other suffering, which some but not all suffering-focused views allow.
