In debates about whether artificial systems could be candidates for moral concern, a recurring analogy does a great deal of work: the mind is to the brain as software is to hardware. The intended conclusion is that, because a program can be run on many different physical substrates without changing what it computes, the mental properties that ground moral status are likewise substrate-independent, and it is the computation itself—not neurons, not carbon chemistry, not biological history—that matters morally. Audit this analogy for exactly that inference. Lay out the correspondence table in full: what plays the role of the program, the hardware, the execution, and the inputs; distinguish the disanalogies that are harmless for the moral-status inference from the ones that undercut it; and state whether the conclusion survives, needs weakening (for example, to a claim about only certain kinds of computation, or about unavoidable physical constraints), or should be abandoned. If the software/hardware analogy cannot carry the conclusion, say what a better analogy would need to preserve and which of the argument's conclusions, if any, could still be rebuilt.
