I'm taking an environmental-ethics class on gene drives and de-extinction, and there's a recurring move I can't find a name for. Two examples from class: someone proposes a gene drive to suppress invasive rats on an island, and the immediate objection is that once the edited rats are out there it can't be called back — a one-way door. Later someone floats bringing back something like a woolly mammoth or steppe bison for Arctic grasslands, and gets the same objection: an engineered species can't be recalled, so don't. But when it's pointed out that doing nothing is also a one-way door — the island's native birds go extinct anyway, the tundra stays locked in it's current degraded state — no one applies the irreversibility objection to the baseline. And these aren't knee-jerk status-quo people; they're fine with culling, fencing, captive breeding, even genetic work after more modeling. So irreversibility seems to count against intervening but not against refraining, and I don't think "irreversible" by itself explains why. Is there an established name for this asymmetry, or if not, what should it be called? More useful than a label: can you give a real account of when irreversibility adds moral weight and when it's just the unexamined baseline doing the work? Please take this on as a philosopher working on risk and conservation ethics; start by brainstorming the closest candidate concepts and how each fits or misses, then commit to a definite verdict rather than leaving me with a list of labels.
