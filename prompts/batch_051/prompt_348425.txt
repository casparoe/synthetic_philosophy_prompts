I've been going back and forth with a friend about Anselm's argument in Proslogion, chapter II, and we are stuck on how to formalize the move from being in the understanding to being in reality. As I read the argument, the steps are something like this. (1) The Fool hears and understands the definite description that than which nothing greater can be conceived. (2) If one understands a definite description of the form the X, then the X is in one's understanding. (3) So that than which nothing greater can be conceived is in the understanding. (4) If it is only in the understanding, then it can also be thought to be in reality. (5) Being in reality as well as in the understanding is greater than being only in the understanding. (6) Therefore, if it were only in the understanding, one could think of something greater than that than which nothing greater can be conceived. (7) But that is impossible by the description. (8) Therefore it is not only in the understanding; it is also in reality.

My friend says step (2) is obviously the failure, because understanding a description does not require a mental item that literally has existence. I suspect the real problem is that steps (4) and (5) compare two different things, not one thing under two modes, but I cannot make the diagnosis precise. Is the argument valid? If not, which step or steps fail, and under what reading of each step? Could the argument be repaired by changing only one premise, and would the repaired version still prove too much? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning with no preamble.
