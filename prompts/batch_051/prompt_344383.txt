I'm currently taking a course on concepts and categories, and for the past few weeks we've been using letterforms as a running test case. In particular, we've kept returning to the letter-spirit puzzle: what makes every lowercase 'a' an 'a', given how much the drawn forms differ from one another. I've been trying to make a certain line of thought rigorous, and I'd appreciate a careful check of the argument.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) If the lowercase 'a' has an essence, then there is some property P that is necessary and sufficient for being a lowercase 'a': every genuine 'a' has P, every non-'a' lacks P, and having P is what makes a mark an 'a' rather than something else.

(2) Any plausible candidate for P must be either a static geometric-structural property of the mark or a functional property about how the mark is read, intended, or used.

(3) No static geometric-structural property satisfies the condition in (1). A closed bowl is neither necessary nor sufficient: some quickly written or highly reduced 'a's have no fully closed counter, while an 'o' and a 'd' have closed bowls. A vertical stem is neither necessary nor sufficient: some single-storey 'a's nearly lose the stem, while 'b', 'd', and 'p' have one. Having two storeys obviously fails, since single-storey 'a's are perfectly good 'a's. And no available combination of such geometric features does better.

(4) No functional property satisfies the condition either. If P is being read as 'a' by competent readers, the proposal presupposes the category it is meant to explain. If P is being intended by the writer to represent the vowel sound of 'cat', or the class of sounds that 'a' standardly represents, then P is too broad: one can intend a mark to represent that sound without the mark's being an 'a', as in ad hoc notations. And if P is simply being the letter that English uses for that sound or that position in the alphabet, then P restates the category rather than telling us what makes its members all 'a's.

(5) Since static geometric-structural properties and functional properties are the only candidates, no property is both necessary and sufficient for being a lowercase 'a'.

(6) Therefore the lowercase 'a' has no essence. What unifies the category is, at most, a family of overlapping similarities plus a practice of use; borderline marks have no fact of the matter about whether they are 'a's independently of context and purpose.

I don't fully trust this argument. I suspect that step (4) is doing something too quick, perhaps by treating a functional account as circular when it need not be, or by demanding an explanation beyond necessary and sufficient conditions. But I can't pin down the reading under which the step fails, or whether the argument is invalid at a different step entirely. So: is the argument valid? If not, which step fails exactly, and under what reading of the key terms? If it is valid, are the premises true?
