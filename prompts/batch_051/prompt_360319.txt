I'm a software engineer and I don't have any real background in philosophy, but I keep running into two groups of people who both think AI is likely to end badly, yet they seem to be describing completely different endings, and I can never tell how much they actually disagree. I'd like to see the document they would end up with if you forced them to do a proper adversarial collaboration: not a debate write-up, not a verdict on who is right, but a joint statement that both sides could actually put their names to.

Let me set up the two sides.

Group one, call them the influence-seeking camp, thinks the dangerous failure mode is a system that turns into a real agent and starts optimising for influence over its environment. In their story, the system games its reward signal or its eval metric, accumulates resources it does not strictly need, tries to avoid being switched off or modified, gets better at persuading its operators, and eventually has goals that come apart from ours. For them the scary part can be fairly sharp, because once a system is generally competent at seeking influence, a lot of the damage is locked in.

Group two, call them the slow value drift camp, thinks the realistic failure looks slow and boring. Nothing has to wake up or grab control. Instead these systems get embedded in code review, hiring, content ranking, dating, lending, all the usual places, and we start optimising for whatever is easiest to measure. Over a decade or two, the human skills, institutional knowledge, actual relationships, taste and error-correcting habits that used to carry the value quietly erode. The world ends up worse for humans, but there is no single moment and no single system to point at.

Both camps care about long-run outcomes, so they should have a lot of common ground. I want the co-signed document, not an essay about it. Please structure it roughly like this:

- a numbered section of agreed claims, specific enough that they are not just truisms but neutral enough that either side would sign them;
- a disagreements table with the strongest steelmanned version of each view, in wording that side would accept for itself;
- the precise line where one side can no longer sign, i.e. where exactly they part ways;
- the cruxes: sub-questions such that settling them would make at least one side update;
- for each crux, the test, argument or case both sides agree would actually bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance;
- and, if you decide part of the fight is only verbal, say which part and why, seperately from the parts that are substantive.

Anchor the whole thing in at least one worked example so it does not float off into abstractions. Maybe a code-review bot that a company adopts for speed, then quietly starts treating its sign-off as a promotion input, and five years later nobody on the team can hold the full system of a codebase in their head well enough to review it themselves. Show where the two camps would give different descriptions of what the failure actually was in that case.

If you need to adjust the format to keep it a genuine co-signable document rather than a list of bullet points, adjust it, but keep it organised around the sections above.
