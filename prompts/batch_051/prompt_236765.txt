A friend who works on AI interpretability has been trying to convince me that there's a basic reason "using AI to interpret AI" can't ever bottom out in real knowledge, and I can't find the flaw. I'll number his steps because I keep losing track of exactly where I want to push back:

(1) Suppose we train one model—call it the interpreter—to inspect another model (the target) and output explanations of what the target is computing, like which features a given layer responds to or what algorithm a circuit implements.

(2) For those explanations to count as knowledge, we need good reason to think the interpreter reliably tracks the target's actual computation, rather than just producing plausible-sounding stories.

(3) The only way to get that reason is to understand how the interpreter itself works—that is, to interpret the interpreter.

(4) But interpreting the interpreter will require another interpreter; trusting that one will require interpreting it; and so on.

(5) Since the regress never bottoms out, we never have adequate justification for trusting any layer of interpretation.

(6) So AI interpretability of this sort cannot produce knowledge—at best it produces reassuring narratives.

He admits (6) sounds drastic but thinks it's unavoidable once you're honest about the opacity. I find it way to strong: it seems like the same argument would prove we can never validate any measuring instrument, since you'd have to validate the device you use to validate the first device, and so on. He says thermometers are different because they're not doing the interpreting, and their output is just a number, not a claim about another system's computation. I'd like to keep the idea that mechanistic interpretability can work at least sometimes, and I find (2) pretty hard to deny too. Where does this argument go wrong, exactly? If it doesn't, what do I have to give up?
