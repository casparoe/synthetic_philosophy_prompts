I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, mostly risk and the numbers behind everyday dangers, and over the years I've ended up with a particular cluster of views about micromorts and risk that I now want to turn into a column. Before I hand it to an editor, I'd like someone to audit whether the views actually hang together, because I suspect I've borrowed arguments from different camps without noticing the seams.

Here's the set. Label them A through F and use those labels in your answer.

A (the currency view). For small, independent, everyday mortality risks, the micromort is the right unit, and 1 micromort is 1 micromort no matter where it comes from. Two activities that each carry a micromort are equally risky, full stop, and I make personal decisions by comparing that number against the benefit I get.

B (the consent view). But I also think a risk I've chosen for fun is worth less than the same risk imposed on me by a planner, employer, or factory. I'd accept a 1-in-a-million chance of dying to do something I love, but a 1-in-a-million chance of dying from a chemical plant I never agreed to have next door genuinely enrages me, and I think that difference is normative, not just a bias I should debias away.

C (the identifiable-victim view). I've come to think the identifiable-victim effect is not only a bias. A 1-in-a-million risk spread over a million people is not, morally, the same thing as one certain death, and policy shouldn't pretend it is. In pieces about screening or traffic safety I increasingly treat "expected deaths" as a lossy summary rather than the real object.

D (the VSL view). At the same time, when I'm discussing policy I'm perfectly comfortable using a value of a statistical life — single-digit millions — as if it's a meaningful, tradeable number, even though I'd never apply that number to a decision about my own car's brakes or my own health.

E (the dread-premium view). I also believe it's rational to overweight very small probabilities of unusually horrible deaths. I'd pay well over the linear price to eliminate a one-in-a-million chance of dying in a mass shooting, and I don't think that's irrational: the badness of the outcome isn't only the fact of death, so the probability shouldn't be multiplied straight through.

F (the independence view). Finally, I think micromorts work fine for roughly independent risks — car crashes, falls, drug reactions — but they break for correlated or systemic risks like a pandemic, a reactor accident, or a financial panic, where a population-level expected-death number hides the fact that the tail is doing all the work.

So: do these hang together? Rank the tensions by severity, and be just as explicit about which pairs only look like tensions but aren't. Don't tell me in advance which one I should give up — that's your job to work out.

Order the "tensions" array from most to least severe.
