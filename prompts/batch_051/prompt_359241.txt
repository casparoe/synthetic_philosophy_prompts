American courts routinely announce that when a statute's words are clear, a judge's duty is to apply them as written, whether or not the result seems wise. Yet the same courts also recognize the absurdity doctrine: an unambiguous text will not be applied literally when the literal result would be absurd, on the ground that the legislature could not have intended it. A statute making it an offense to obstruct a public road, for instance, would not be read to convict an officer who stops traffic to investigate a crash, and a statute commanding what is physically impossible would not be enforced as written. Courts disagree about how absurd the result must be—some say it must be one no reasonable legislator could have accepted, others say it is enough that the result defeats the statute's evident purpose—but the exception itself is almost never abandoned.

On its face, this looks like a standing contradiction: either the enacted words determine what the law is, or they are only evidence of something else that does. What does the absurdity exception presuppose about what a statute is and where its legal content comes from? Reconstruct the implicit theory of legal content, legislative authority, and interpretation that the practice strictly presupposes, and distinguish it from commitments it merely fits with or leaves open. In particular, address whether the doctrine commits a court to legislator intent, to treating the legislature as a rational agent, to a distinction between what is enacted and what is intended, or to some combination—and whether the doctrine could be run without any appeal to legislative purpose at all. Then work out the price of consistency: what would a legal system have to give up, and what would it have to add, if it abolished the absurdity exception and applied every clear text literally, whatever the result?
