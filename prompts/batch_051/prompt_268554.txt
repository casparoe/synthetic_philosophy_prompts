Last night I had a long, unresolved argument with a friend about whether rights are best understood as side constraints on what each agent may do or as instruments for protecting interests that may be traded off when the balance of interests shifts, and I would like to see the dispute reconstructed at a higher level than we managed. Please write a dialogue between two equally sophisticated interlocutors: one who defends the view that rights function as side constraints, and one who defends the view that rights are essentially instruments. Neither should be a strawman; each should be allowed to grant the other's strongest examples and then show why those examples do not force a concession. The characters should argue with one another, responding directly to the previous move rather than delivering rehearsed monologues.

Push the conversation past the familiar textbook cases and into the structural difficulties of each position. I would like the dialogue to cover at least the following three pressure points, though the characters may raise others if the argument naturally leads there. First, the paradox of deontology: if a right exists because violations are bad, why must an agent not violate it even to prevent several comparable violations of the same right, and what exactly is the side-constraint theorist claiming that rules this trade-off out? Second, whether side constraints are grounded in something about the agent (for instance, the integrity of the agent's own deliberative stance) or something about the patient (for instance, the patient's inviolability), and whether these foundations come apart when a third party must decide whether to infringe one person's right in order to stop a much graver violation by someone else. Third, the instrumentalist's burden of explaining the felt asymmetry between deliberately using a person as a mere means and allowing harm as a side effect, without simply reintroducing the very constraint-like notion the view was meant to replace. The dialogue should not end in a forced resolution; its purpose is to make plain where the disagreement ultimately bottoms out and what each side would have to give up to reach the other.
