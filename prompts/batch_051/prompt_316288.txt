I work on software for advance directives, and I keep hitting a conceptual edge case. The standard analogy is Ulysses at the mast: he makes his crew bind him, expecting that his future self will be temporarily tempted by the Sirens, and trusting that later he will recover his original values. Now consider a variation. Suppose Ulysses knows the Sirens' song does not just tempt him, but permanently replaces one of his core preferences—say, his desire to return to Ithaca. After hearing it, his later self will sincerely not care about Ithaca and will ask to be untied. Knowing this, he writes an advance directive before departure, instructing the crew to ignore any later request. Is the earlier self still the person who gets to decide? If the later self has genuinely different values, can the directive still be said to protect him, or does it simply coerce him? Then vary the same case again: the change is not total but affects only one central commitment, while the rest of his values stay intact. Does that make the earlier self's authority stronger or weaker? I am asking about the underlying principle: on what does the authority of an earlier self over a later self rest—on identity, on best-interest judgment, on narrative continuity, or on the conditions under which the earlier decision was made? Which of those grounds survives the permanent-value-change case, and does the case show that advance directives need a different justification than the standard Ulysses story suggests?
