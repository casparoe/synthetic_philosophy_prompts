I'm putting together a handout for a class on group rationality, and I keep running into the same tired example — the three-judge appellate court version of the discursive dilemma (doctrinal paradox). I want a thought experiment built to order that gets the same structure in a fresher setting.

Here's the spec. A five-member commitee in an ordinary non-legal setting (a hiring committee, a co-op board, a product-safety panel — you choose) votes yes/no on whether to approve something. Approval requires two conditions, call them P and Q, so the conclusion C ("approve") holds only if both P and Q hold. Each member's individual judgments are perfectly consistent: if a member accepts both P and Q they accept C, and if they reject at least one of P or Q they reject C. The vote uses plain majority rule on every question — no supermajorities, no weighted votes, no abstentions, nobody confused. Build the profile so that P passes 3–2, Q passes 3–2, but C fails 3–2. That way premise-based voting (vote on P and Q, then infer C) gives "approve," while conclusion-based voting (vote directly on C) gives "reject." The scenario itself should be one paragraph and realistic; don't let the divergence come from anyone being irrational or from a gerrymandered voting rule.

Then do the walk-through. Lay out the five members' positions on P, Q, and C (a small table is fine), confirm each member is individually consistent, and show how the two procedures give opposite verdicts. Then identify the feature of the profile that's doing the work: what exactly would you have to change to make the two procedures agree, and why does removing that feature collapse the dilemma? Finish with a short paragraph in which a defender of premise-based voting attacks the case (for example, by saying the group never actually took a position on C, or that real deliberation would have changed someone's vote), and then give the one- or two-sentence reply a conclusion-based voter would make. Hard cap: 500 words.
