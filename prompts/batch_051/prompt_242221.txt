I'm preparing a short piece for a film-and-philosophy group on how a self might survive the death of the person whose self it is, and I want to think through Pixar's Coco with Douglas Hofstadter's account of the self as a pattern that persists in other minds. Hofstadter's view, developed at length in I Am a Strange Loop, is that a self is not a soul-like substance but a pattern, and that partial, lower-resolution copies of a person's pattern keep running in the brains of those who knew them closely. On this view, when someone dies, something of them genuinely continues in the habits of attention, turns of phrase, remembered arguments, and felt presence that remain active in the people who loved them; the continuation is graded and partial rather than all-or-nothing, and it requires no threshold and no supernatural machinery.

Coco gives this a literal, supernatural staging. In the film, the dead persist in the Land of the Dead only so long as someone among the living remembers them, and their photograph on an ofrenda is what allows them to return to visit the living; when the last living person who remembers them forgets, they undergo a final death. Héctor, the protagonist's great-great-grandfather, is on the verge of that final death because his daughter Coco, now an old woman losing her memory, is the only one still holding him in mind.

I'd like a committed critical reading, not a survey. Please argue for a definite position on where the film genuinely dramatizes Hofstadter's thesis, where it departs from it or distorts it, and whether the film's supernatural frame nevertheless captures something the philosophical account misses. Use the sections below, in this order, with roughly the indicated lengths:

1. The reading (about 200 words): state your central claim about the relationship between Hofstadter's view and the film, and the exact sense in which you think Héctor persists or fails to persist.
2. Where the film bears the reading out (about 350 words): identify the specific scenes or mechanisms, such as the ofrenda, Miguel's borrowed memories, the lullaby, and Coco's revival, that dramatize the idea of a self carried in other minds, and say what they get right.
3. Where the film resists the reading (about 350 words): say where Coco's supernatural binary of remembered versus forgotten, photographed versus unphotographed, and its final death diverge from Hofstadter's graded, materialist account, and why that divergence matters.
4. What the film adds to the philosophy (about 200 words): argue for something the film shows or makes vivid that Hofstadter's account underplays, for instance about material tokens, intergenerational memory, or the ethics of being forgotten.
5. Verdict (about 150 words): give your bottom line on whether Coco is a faithful vehicle for this philosophy, a seductive distortion of it, or something in between, and state the single strongest reason for your answer.

Where you take liberties with Hofstadter's actual view or lean on a contested reading of a scene, flag it briefly rather than hedging throughout; I want the main line of argument kept sharp.
