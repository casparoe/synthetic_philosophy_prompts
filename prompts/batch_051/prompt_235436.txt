My friend has a bunch of settled verdicts about when you "really" have a reason for an attitude, and I'm trying to reverse-engineer her rule. A demon offers you a million dollars to admire a saucer of mud that has no admirable features — she says the money isn't a reason to admire the saucer, though it is a reason to want to admire it and maybe to take a pill that would make you admire it. A demon threatens to hurt your family unless you believe the butler did it, when all the evidence points to the gardener — she says the threat isn't a reason to believe the butler did it, but it is a reason to take a pill that would cause the belief. A nervous flyer's fear is making her miserable; she says the misery isn't a reason to stop being afraid, though it is a reason to want to stop being afraid and to see a therapist about it.

New case: a friend betrayed you once, and it would be good for both of you if you could trust him again. You can make yourself trust him by deliberately not dwelling on the betrayal. Do her rules say the benefit is a reason to trust him, or only a reason to want to trust him / take steps that bring it about? I'm less interested in the actual answer than in the rule she seems to be running, what other rules fit her earlier verdicts just as well, whether the trust case actually separates them, and what further case would settle which rule she holds.
