Something's been bugging me. The law can't seem to make up it's mind whether "what you said" is fixed by your words or by what your audience reasonably takes you to have communicated. In perjury, a witness who gives a literally true answer walks no matter how carefully the answer was designed to mislead; the doctrine treats the words, not the impression, as what counts. But in fraud, courts routinely punish people for engineering a false impression with statements that are each technically true, and in contract law we bind people to what a reasonable hearer would take their words and conduct to mean, not to some private literal sense. I'm trying to figure out what theory of meaning, or of the difference between what's said and what's merely implied, this patchwork presupposes. Is there a single picture of communication that makes the perjury rule and the fraud/contract rules coherent together, or do these doctrines presuppose incompatible pictures? And if you rebuilt perjury on the fraud/contract picture of meaning, what would have to change, both doctrinally and in how we think about lying under oath?
