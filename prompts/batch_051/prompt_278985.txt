I've been thinking about what happens to factual disputes as fake video and audio get cheaper to produce, and I keep coming back to an idea I want to pin down precisely. Before deepfakes were easy, photographs and recordings served a special social role: when two people's accounts conflicted, you could pull out the recording and settle the matter. I'll call this the epistemic backstop. There are two natural positions about what happens to it. The fix-it view says deepfakes are an engineering problem: as fakes get better, detectors, tamper-evident hardware, and provenance systems will get better too, and the backstop will hold. The erosion view says something in the social role of recordings is damaged simply by people knowing that convincing fakes are possible, and better detection can't fully repair it.

Construct a thought experiment that gives the erosion view a fair test: a case that comes out in favor of the erosion view even though the facts are stacked in favor of the fix-it view. The target result is this: a community loses the backstop function of recordings even though its detection and authentication tools are genuinely very good, and even though no particular deepfake in the story fools anyone. The collapse should come from the way the mere availability of deepfakes changes how people reason about recordings, not from a detector being defeated or a fake being believed.

The scenario must be realistic: a workplace dispute, a local government hearing, a civil lawsuit, a newsroom verification decision, or something similar, not a science-fiction parable. Write the scenario itself in one paragraph. Do not use shortcuts: no one in the story should be malicious beyond ordinary self-interested behavior in a dispute; the detection tool may be imperfect but its imperfections must not drive the result; and the result must fall out of the structure of the situation, not be stipulated.

After the scenario, walk through it step by step and show each of the following. First, that the backstop has actually collapsed in the case—that a recording that would previously have ended the dispute no longer does—rather than merely being slowed down or made more expensive to use. Second, why the collapse occurs without anyone believing a fake. Third, what the fix-it view would say about the case, and why its preferred remedies, even though they work well in this case, do not prevent the collapse. Fourth, what the erosion view would say about the case, and why the case supports it.

Then step back and identify the single feature of the case that is doing the real work, the feature such that removing it makes the thought experiment fail. Describe what happens when you remove that feature, and whether the changed case restores the backstop or collapses for a different reason. Finally, give the strongest objection that a defender of the fix-it view would make to the case—for example, an objection about where trust now sits, or about a technical fix the case has implicitly ruled out—and answer it honestly rather than by stipulating the objection away.
