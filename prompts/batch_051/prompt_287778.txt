I am working out the moral psychology of a character-driven novel in which several people must decide whether to do things they have no present desire to do, and I want one principle—or at most a small set of candidates—that sorts my own settled judgments. My judgments run like this. A chef, depleted after months of long shifts, no longer wants to plan the weekend away that would save his marriage, though he still loves his wife and wants the marriage to survive: he has a reason to plan the trip. A woman with no musical interest and no desire for self-improvement is told by a friend that learning piano would enrich her life: she does not thereby acquire a reason to learn. A depressed man in a clear interval wants his mood to lift and believes exercise would lift it, but feels no pull toward exercise: he has a reason to exercise. An employee who promised to finish a report has lost all interest in the project and in keeping the promise, and has nothing else to gain by finishing: he still has a reason to finish. A proud artist who publicly insulted a friend sees nothing to regret and does not care whether the friendship survives: he has no reason to apologize, yet I cannot say why the promise should bind the employee when the wronged friend does not bind the artist. Tell me which principle, or small set of candidates, comes closest to capturing these, which of my verdicts it cannot keep, and what it says about the case I cannot settle—a sheltered young man with no desire to travel and no sense that travel would serve anything he cares about, though in fact it would broaden his judgment. Reply in exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion; separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
