Last month the city fined me two hundred dollars for leaving a broken microwave in the regular trash, under an ordinance I had never heard of. It turned out the ordinance is posted only on a city webpage that is not linked from anywhere on the main site, was never mentioned in the move-in packet, and apparently has not been enforced in years. When I objected that I could not follow a rule I did not know existed, the hearing officer cut me off with "Ignorance of the law is no excuse." I am not after legal advice about my fine. I want to know what that slogan could actually mean, because it seems to stretch from something nearly tautological—a legal system could not function if every defendant could derail proceedings simply by claiming not to have known—to something very strong: that it is fair to penalize people under rules they had no realistic opportunity to discover.

Please tease apart exactly five distinct precise principles a person could be asserting when they say that ignorance of the law is no excuse. For each, specify what kind of ignorance it is about (ignorance of the rule's existence, ignorance that one's act falls under the rule, ignorance of how the rule is interpreted, or something else), what "no excuse" amounts to in that reading (no legal liability, no moral blame, no pardon, no procedural remedy, and so on), and whether the principle is a platitude, a contestable but defensible rule, or implausible as stated. Then say whether that reading would actually support penalizing someone under a rule they could not have known. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, in American English.
