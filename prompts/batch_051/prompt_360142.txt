I got into an argument with a friend about algorithmic collusion — the worry that pricing bots can learn to keep prices high without ever explicitly agreeing, just by responding to each other with punishments and rewards — and it made me want to think through a clean counterfactual. Suppose that starting next January, every airline is required to publish, in real time and machine-readable form, the exact pricing rule its algorithm is using. Not the price history, but the function that maps competitor prices, inventory, demand signals, and past history into the next price. Enforcement is perfect for the first few years: the rule on the feed is genuinely the rule being used. Airlines can change their rule whenever they want, but the change only takes effect one hour after it is posted, and during that hour everyone — rivals, consumers, regulators, and other pricing bots — can see it. Pricing bots are allowed to subscribe to the feed and condition on everything in it.

I want you to focus on domestic airfare and predict what happens over five years. Does forced transparency mostly make collusion harder, because a regulator or a fare-tracking site can now read the coordination, or easier, because every bot can now perfectly predict how its rivals will punish a price cut? Walk me through the mechanism rather then just give a verdict: trace what happens after one carrier cuts a price on a route, explain whether and how the system settles into an equilibrium, and say where you'd expect the biggest changes — average fares, price dispersion, the duration of fare wars, entry by low-cost carriers, the kinds of pricing functions airlines converge on. Which links in your prediction are robust, and which would flip if most airlines were actually running dumb rule-based repricers rather than anything you'd call an intelligent agent? What would have to be true for the policy to backfire completely?
