I run a philosophy of physics reading group of around sixteen advanced undergraduates and postgraduates, and I have ninety minutes for our next session. I want to run a hands-on activity — no lecture up front — on the thermodynamic arrow of time. The specific thing I want participants to come away seeing is this: a collection of local moves, each individually reversible under the same rule, can produce a macroscopic history that looks strongly directed in one direction, and the asymmetry is not in the dynamics but in the initial condition from which the history starts. I want them to discover this by running it themselves, and I do not want the phrases "thermodynamic arrow" or "entropy" used until the debrief.

Design the runnable activity for me. I need concrete rules, a list of low-tech materials I can assemble, and a timeline for the ninety minutes. The rules should be such that the participants can see, move by move, that the dynamics are time-symmetric, while the aggregate behaviour they observe drifts in a preferred direction. At some point they should be asked to replay the recorded history backwards and judge whether each move obeys the rules; they should discover that it does, and then have to say why the reverse history nonetheless does not occur from the states they actually reach. Where possible, build in a moment where they commit to a prediction before the run, so that their own expectation of what must happen becomes part of what gets examined.

Then give me the debrief. It should map specific moments in the activity onto the conceptual structure: the microstate/macrostate distinction, the statistical reading of entropy, the second law as a statistical regularity rather than a strict dynamical law, and the past hypothesis — or an analogue of a low-entropy initial condition — as the feature doing the real work. Be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce about the actual position and what I as facilitator must supply, and about what will predictably go wrong when I first run it — in particular, where participants will mistake the statistical drift for a bias in the rules, or misread fluctuations as violations of the second law — and how I should handle those errors in the debrief rather than by correcting them mid-activity. No readings or citations are needed; I want the runnable plan itself.
