I'm trying to draw a line between counting and measuring on one side and actual mathematics on the other, and I want you to pressure-test the checklist I've been using. The criteria I was taught are: (1) mathematics is general — it makes claims about every case of a kind, not a report about these particular goats or that particular field; (2) it gives derivations or at least some reasons, not just bare results; (3) it's done in a notation where the symbols do the work for you; (4) it deals in idealized objects — exact right angles, exact whole numbers, not 'roughly a right angle.' Tally marks on a bone fail basically all four; Euclid passes all four.

The case wrecking the line for me is Plimpton 322. It's a Babylonian clay tablet from around 1800 BCE, written in base sixty. What survives is mostly a table of numbers. The usual reading is that each row encodes a right triangle with whole-number sides: two of the columns give a short side and a diagonal, and the long side is left implied. One row, for instance, gives 119 and 169, with the implied side being 120 — so it's the triple (119, 120, 169). There are fifteen rows, and the numbers in the first column decrease steadily; people read this as the rows being ordered by the size of the angle (roughly 45 degrees down to about 31), which is a strangely abstract way to arrange what could have just been a list. The numbers aren't measurements of actual fields, they're exact, and they look deliberately chosen — mostly 'regular' numbers that make sexagesimal division come out clean.

So here's the problem. On my own checklist, does Plimpton 322 count as mathematics? It nails criteria (3) and (4). But there's no statement of a general fact, no proof, no 'here's the problem, here's the method' text — the tablet just sits there with its table, and for all we can tell it was a scribal exercise or a lookup table for making right angles. Do I let it in as mathematics, or do I call it very fancy counting? Or does the case show one of my criteria is misstated — maybe an ordered table of cases can be general even when nothing general is said, or maybe the derivation is baked into how the table was constructed even though its not written down? Work through it criterion by criterion, tell me where the checklist underdetermines the verdict, and then tell me which criteria, if any, I should revise and why. Be honest: if the price of keeping my criteria is saying this thing isn't mathematics, say so straight.
