I'm a novelist, so this comes out of a slightly mercenary place. I'm drafting a scene in which two characters argue about whether logic can actually force anyone to accept a conclusion, and I didn't want the scene to be a strawman. Last week I maneuvered two friends of mine into having exactly this argument at dinner and more or less transcribed it. Wether they'd have been half so neat if they'd known I was harvesting their dialogue is another matter.

Maya opened. (1) "No batch of premises ever forces a conclusion. A person can accept every premise and still simply decline to draw the inference. Being moved by logic is a choice, or a habit — not a compulsion."

(2) Jonas said that was wrong. "If you accept P, and you accept that if P then Q, then Q is forced on you in the only sense that matters: you're committed to it. Refuse Q and you've contradicted the very things you said you accept."

(3) Maya: "Why, though? Because of modus ponens? That's just another thing I might or might not accept. Write it down as a premise and nothing changes. I can accept P, P-implies-Q, and 'if P and P-implies-Q, then Q' — and still not accept Q, unless you add a further rule, and then another. That regress is exactly the point of Carroll's tortoise."

(4) Jonas: "But that's the whole joke of the tortoise. It treats a rule of inference as if it were an extra premise. A rule isn't a premise; it's a license to move from what you've accepted to what you haven't. No regress gets started, because the rule was never on the list of premises."

(5) Maya: "All right, but then what makes the license a good one? You can't justify the rule by adding premises, so you've just posited it. And if positing rules is allowed, why not posit a rule that takes you from P and P-implies-Q to not-Q? You'll say modus ponens preserves truth and mine doesn't. But any proof that modus ponens preserves truth will itself use modus ponens, so it's circular. Regress, circle, or arbitrary stop — which one is the justification?"

(6) Jonas: "Every justification stops somewhere, and stopping at deduction isn't arbitrary. The rules of inference are constitutive of what 'therefore' means; asking for a warrant for them is like asking why the knight moves in an L. And the truth-preservation proof isn't a vicious circle — it's a genuine explanation of why the rule is sound."

(7) Maya: "But a knight's move is a convention, and modus ponens is supposed to track how truth actually behaves. If deduction's rules are just constitutive conventions, logic stops being about what really follows and turns into a game we happen to play. That guts the very thing that was supposed to make refusing Q irrational."

After they left I looked up Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles." In it, Achilles has the Tortoise accept A and B but not Z, and offers him C — the conditional that if A and B are true, Z must be true — as an extra step. The part they kept circling around is this:

"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"

"You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."

I honestly can't tell which of them the passage vindicates. On one reading the Tortoise wins cleanly: no matter how many hypotheticals you add, accepting Z isn't forced until you accept one more. On another reading he only wins by insisting on treating a rule of inference as another premise, which is exactly what Jonas said was the confusion.

So can you adjudicate the whole thing? Go through moves (1) through (7), rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and give a short justification; then say who Lewis Carroll's passage actually supports overall, or whether it cuts both ways. I need the reasoning more than the verdict — I don't want my novel's scene smuggling in a bad account of what deduction is.
