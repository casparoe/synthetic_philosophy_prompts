As a novelist, I have come to think that my craft is itself a way of having a relation to the past, and I want to know which of your three historical relations it most resembles. In the second of your Untimely Meditations, On the Use and Disadvantage of History for Life, Friedrich Nietzsche distinguishes monumental history, antiquarian history, and critical history. Let me set critical history aside. I would like you to compare the monumental and the antiquarian modes in three respects: what each seeks from the past, what each suppresses or falsifies, and what kind of person each makes possible. Then I will offer a boundary case from my own practice. I have spent the last several years writing a novel about a family of silk weavers in Lyons in the 1830s, drawing on municipal archives, account books, and a run of letters. The book does not want to hold these people up as great figures or usable models; it wants to hold onto the smell and grain of their days, their feuds, their debts, their fear of bad harvests. At the same time, I have invented motives, compressed two generations into one, and organized the whole around questions that belong to my readers rather than to the weavers themselves. Is this antiquarian history in Nietzsche's sense? Is it monumental history in disguise? Or does fiction, because it must invent the very thing it preserves, reveal a fourth relation to the past that the essay does not name? Argue whether the novelist is, in Nietzsche's terms, an antiquarian who has lost his piety, a monumentalist who has lost his nerve, or some hybrid that neither mode can capture.
