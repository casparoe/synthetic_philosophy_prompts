I've been reading defenses of expected utility maximization that run through coherence arguments, and I keep having the same uncomfortable experience: each one is sold as establishing the same normative conclusion, but they're actually doing different jobs. Dutch book arguments for probabilism conclude that if your degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, there is a set of bets, each acceptable at your stated odds, on which you'd lose for sure. Money pump and dynamic consistency arguments get aimed at transitivity and independence. And Savage-style representation theorems conclude something much weaker and more conditional: that if your preferences over acts satisfy a set of axioms, then there exists a probability on states and a utility on outcomes such that you rank acts by expected utility. A representation theorem is not an argument that you ought to have representable preferences, and a Dutch book is not an argument that non-probabilistic belief states are incoherent until a pile of auxilliary assumptions are secured. That gap is exactly what I want the spec for.

Write me the desiderata. What must a coherence argument for expected utility maximization actually deliver before it counts as an argument for the normative claim, rather than a mathematical observation that happens to live nearby? For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it. I especially want the list to settle these: the existence-uniqueness gap between having some representation and being committed to the axioms; the assumption that losing money is losing value when utility is the very thing in question; the package principle, since the argument needs someone who accepts each bet individually to accept the book as a whole; why state-dependent utility or similarly gerrymandered representations don't let the violator off the hook; whether the argument needs to be diachronic or can be run entirely synchronically; and how much it may stipulate completeness and Archimedean continuity as idealizations without defending them. Then flag which requirements are non-negotiable for any coherence argument and which are just commitments of one camp, and tell me which single requirement, if enforced, does the most damage to the standard versions of the argument. I have a prior that it's the package principle, but I want an actual case for whichever one it is.
