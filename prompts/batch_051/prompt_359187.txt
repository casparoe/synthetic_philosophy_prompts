I want one essay question on Good's theorem: the result that, for an expected-utility maximizer who updates by conditioning, the expected value of cost-free information can never be negative. I want a question you can't answer by just reciting the proof. It should make students face the gap between the theorem and the looser slogan that a rational agent should never refuse free information. A good version would hand them a concrete case where someone plausibly *does* refuse free information — say, a free genetic test for an untreatable condition, or an agent who knows that whatever they learn will also be observed by a competitor — and make them identify which assumption of the theorem has to be relaxed for that refusal to come out rational.

The exam is three hours and this is one of three essays, so it should be doable in about 50 minutes and I'll mark it out of 40.

Also: in the model answer, keep seperate what's settled (the theorem under its stated assumptions) from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this (what the theorem actually shows about real agents once you allow strategic observers, non-Bayesian decision rules, or utility that depends on what you believe).
