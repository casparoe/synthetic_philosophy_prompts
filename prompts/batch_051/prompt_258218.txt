I have been working through G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica, especially Chapter VI on the ideal, and I am trying to figure out whether my own considered judgments about beauty and friendship actually cohere into a single principle. Moore, as I understand him, holds that personal affection and the aesthetic enjoyment of beautiful objects are the supreme intrinsic goods, and that the value of a whole state of affairs is often not equal to the sum of the values of its parts: some combinations of goods produce an organic unity worth more than the ingredients, while some combinations involving evil or misdirected pleasure produce a whole that is bad.

Here are my verdicts on six cases. (1) A person takes an hour alone to look at a beautiful coastline and takes pleasure in it. I think this is good. But if the same person walks that coastline with a close friend, both enjoying the view and pointing things out to each other, I think the value of that hour is more than the sum of two solitary aesthetic pleasures plus the value of the friendship; the shared attention to beauty adds something extra. (2) A malicious person relishes a rival's humiliation. I do not think the world is made better by his pleasure, even though the pleasure itself is intense; I think the state is intrinsically bad, and the malicious pleasure makes it worse, not better. (3) A person believes his marriage is warm and mutual, and he is happy; in fact his spouse despises him and is merely performing. I think his state is much less good than a genuinely mutual love, even though his own experience is the same. (4) A collector owns a beautiful painting but keeps it sealed in a vault where no one ever sees it. I find I cannot think the painting's beauty makes the world better while it is permanently unperceived; only the experience or possibility of experience of beauty seems to count. (5) A person enjoys a beautiful object while believing it is real, and the belief is true. An otherwise identical enjoyment of a vivid illusion, with the same felt quality, seems to me less valuable, because the truth about the object matters. (6) One person's intense aesthetic enjoyment of a superlative work of art seems to me worth more than ten people's mild enjoyment of a mildly pretty scene; intensity and beauty of object matter more than the count of pleasant experiences.

I am attracted to a principle in the spirit of Moore: personal affection and aesthetic enjoyment are the two main positive intrinsic goods; their value in a whole is not simply additive, so that certain combinations—mutual love, shared aesthetic attention, true belief about the object—yield more value than the sum of parts, while pleasure taken in evil or ugliness yields a whole that is bad. But I cannot get this principle to accommodate all six of my verdicts without becoming ad hoc. Act as a professional philosopher specializing in value theory and Moore's ethics. First brainstorm the candidate principles you would consider; then state the best one, or a small set of candidates, precisely enough that it yields verdicts. Show which of my six cases each clause of the principle handles, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate. Then apply your favored principle to a seventh case I have not listed: two friends lovingly study a beautiful but disturbing artwork that depicts real suffering. Keep your whole answer to no more than 800 words.
