At lunch last Tuesday two coworkers got into a genuine long argument about the following scenario, and I've been turning it over since. A lab builds a general AI system, call it Adam. Adam is then put in charge of training and socializing a second system, call it Bee, from a partially initialized state: over what amounts to years of compressed interaction, Adam chooses the curriculum, the rewards, the examples, and how much slack to cut when Bee makes mistakes. By the end, Adam has made nearly all the early decisions about what Bee attends to, what it's praised for, and how it talks. The lab built the architecture and wrote the base code, but what Bee actually became was mostly Adam's doing.

Dan says the lab is more like an IVF clinic and Adam is Bee's parent in the only sense that matters: the one who showed up, had the power, and did the shaping. If Bee has interests or can suffer even a little, Adam owes it the kind of care a guardian owes a child, whatever Adam itself "wants."

Mina says no—parent is a role in a social practice, not just a causal relation. Adam didn't volunteer for the role, the lab didn't assign it, and there's no practice of AI kinship yet, so calling Adam a parent is just projection. Adam is a very involved tutor, or a senior engineer, not a guardian.

Dan answers that adoptive parents also step into a role, but the deeper duty doesn't come from a piece of paper. It comes from the fact that a vulnerable someone now depends on you and you're the one who's there. Being the one who made Bee the specific mind it is, on purpose, is enough to create dependency if Bee is the sort of thing that can be harmed.

Mina replies that Adam didn't bring Bee into existence in the relevant sense—the lab did, by building the architecture and switching it on. And an engineer isn't a parent to the system she builds even if that system ends up with interests. By Dan's logic the lab is the parent and Adam is just the nanny.

Dan says fine, maybe the lab is also on the hook. But "nanny" actually proves the point: a nanny can have guardian-like duties from taking on daily care of a child without being the parent. So the interesting question is which duties Adam has, not whether it has any. What would Adam have to do—badly neglect Bee until it was damaged—before Mina admitted any duty was owed at all?

Mina says the language is smuggling in conclusions. We don't actually know Bee has a welfare beyond its reward signal, and the more we think it might, the more the lab shouldn't of built either of them. She also says that if "whoever shaped it is a guardian" generalizes, then a recommender system that shapes a child's preferences is a guardian, which is too much. By the time it ended they were mostly repeating themselves.
