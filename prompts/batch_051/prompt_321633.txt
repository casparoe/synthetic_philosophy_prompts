For a piece I've been reporting, I've spent time with the long-running scholarly dispute over how old nations are, and it seems stuck in a way that isn't plainly about the evidence. The modernist side, associated with Hobsbawm and Anderson, holds that nations are a modern formation: print capitalism, mass education, and newly invented traditions produced a popular national consciousness that earlier eras lacked, then projected that nation backward as though it were ancient. The rival side — call it the long-history or ethnosymbolist position — replies that modernists define "nation" in a way that presupposes the outcome: named peoples, shared myths of descent, common memories, and durable ethnic identities appear in medieval and earlier sources, and modern nation-builders reused those older cores rather than creating loyalty from nothing. Both sides seem to accept the same underlying facts about pre-modern life — limited literacy, layered identities, elites marketing continuity — and yet neither moves the other. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal, over the word "nation"; a hidden empirical question about what pre-modern identity was like from the inside; a quarrel about which continuities count as sameness over centuries; or a political dispute about legitimacy conducted through historiography? Diagnose its structure, and say what would count as progress given that diagnosis, or whether we should stop expecting convergence.
