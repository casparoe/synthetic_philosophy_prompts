I have been asked to deliver the opening address for a two-day workshop called After the Subject–Predicate Sentence, jointly hosted by a philosophy department and a computer science institute. The audience will be a deliberate mix: historians of logic, working logicians, formal semanticists, and graduate students from neighboring fields who have at most an introductory logic course behind them. I would like the address to do real argumentative work rather than merely welcome everyone. It should defend a specific and mildly contrarian thesis: that what Frege fundamentally changed was not simply the notation of logic or the ambition to reduce mathematics to logic, but the presumed anatomy of the simplest thought. The speech should argue that syllogistic and modern logic differ most deeply in what each takes a sentence to be made of — a subject and a predicate on the one hand, a function and its arguments on the other — and that the function-argument picture earns its keep because it can represent relations and nested quantification without distortion.

To make this concrete, the speech should be built around two worked examples. The first should be a sentence that syllogistic handles gracefully, so the audience sees what the old analysis was genuinely good at. The second should be an inference that defeats that analysis — for instance, the step from every horse is an animal to every head of a horse is a head of an animal, or another relational example in the same shape. I want the address to walk through what happens when someone tries to force that second example into subject-predicate form, showing why the standard workarounds (treating is a head of a horse as a single predicate, or quietly adding an extra premise) alter the content rather than merely the wording. The staging matters: the specialists should feel that the examples are doing real work, while the newcomers should leave understanding why the Fregean shift is treated as a discovery about logical form rather than a cosmetic rebranding. Please write the full remarks, roughly twelve minutes when spoken, in a tone that is precise but not pedantic, and let the examples, not the biography, carry the argument.
