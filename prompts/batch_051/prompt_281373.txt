In the Groundwork’s second section, in the example of the false promise, Kant considers someone who is in financial distress and borrows money on the strength of a promise to repay, while knowing that repayment will not happen. Kant’s stated view is that the maxim on which this person acts cannot even be thought as a universal law of nature, because universal false promising would undermine the very institution of promising and lending by which the person hopes to get the money. I have been turning that passage into the following argument, and I cannot decide whether it goes through or where it goes wrong.

(1) For Kant, an action is morally permissible only if one can will the maxim of the action as a universal law of nature.

(2) The maxim in the false-promise case, roughly: borrow money when in distress, promise to repay it, while not intending to repay—cannot be universalized, because if everyone in distress borrowed on false promises, the practice of lending on promises would collapse.

(3) The maxim in a routine honest-loan case, roughly: borrow money when in distress, promise to repay it, while intending to repay—can be universalized, because universal honest borrowing would leave the institution of lending intact.

(4) The only difference between the two maxims is the agent’s intention to repay, which is an inner state and does not show up in any outward act of borrowing and promising.

(5) Kant’s universal-law test, as applied in this passage, asks us to consider what the world would be like if everyone acted on the maxim: that is, it tracks the outward causal and institutional consequences of the behavior’s becoming a universal law.

(6) Inner states that make no difference to that universalized outward pattern cannot make a difference to the outcome of the test.

(7) Therefore, on Kant’s test, the false-promise maxim and the honest-loan maxim either both pass or both fail.

(8) But Kant plainly treats the false-promise maxim as failing and the honest-loan maxim as passing.

(9) So Kant’s universalizability test, at least as he applies it to this example, fails to track the distinction between honest borrowing and false promising; unless the test is supplemented with material the passage does not state, it yields a false classification.

I suspect the mistake is in step (4) or step (5), but I cannot make the diagnosis precise. Is the argument valid? If it is not, say exactly which step or steps fail, and give the corrected reading of Kant’s notion of a maxim or of the universal-law test that repairs the argument. If it is valid, is the conclusion one a Kantian must accept? And if so, which of Kant’s two classifications should be given up?
