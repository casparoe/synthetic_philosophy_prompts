I am taking a cognitive science seminar this term on concepts and categorization, and we have been working on the problem of what makes every lowercase a an a across typefaces, handwriting, cases, and reading contexts. In class I have been defending a working account of what it takes for some mark to be the letter a, with four criteria:

(1) Shape. It falls within the visual family that competent readers group together as a: a closed counter, a roughly circular or oval bowl, a right-hand stroke or stem, with the two-storey and single-storey forms as accepted variants; the uppercase A is linked to it less by visual resemblance than by conventional pairing.

(2) Alphabetic role. It is a member of an ordered alphabet, with a fixed position in that order (first, in the Latin alphabet), a paired uppercase or majuscule form where the script has case, and systematic contrasts with the other members of its alphabet.

(3) Sound and word function. It has a standard correspondence to a vowel in a language that uses the alphabet: in English, roughly the vowel of cat in words like cat, and a separate name-sound when the letter itself is named. Its occurrence in a written word is part of what tells a reader how the word sounds.

(4) Production and recognition. It is produced as that letter rather than as a merely decorative or accidental shape, and competent readers of the relevant writing system recover it as that letter in the ordinary course of reading.

Now consider this case. A typesetter is preparing an English-language poster for the word daily. In a copy-paste slip, the character placed in the position of the a is not the Latin lowercase a but the Cyrillic small а, which in the chosen roman face is strictly indistinguishable from the Latin one: same bowl, same counter, same stroke, same height. The two are nevertheless distinct characters, belonging to different alphabets. The printed word looks exactly as it would have looked with the Latin a, and a fluent reader of English reads daily without any hesitation or sense that something is wrong.

Is the character in that printed word the letter a — the same letter as the one in apple — or is it a different letter that merely looks exactly like the Latin a? Go through my four criteria one by one and say how each would rule. Then address whether the case shows that the criteria need revising or whether the borderline verdict exposes something about which criterion is doing the real work. If you conclude that the printed character is not the letter a, say what the shape criterion alone is missing; if you conclude that it is the letter a, say what, if anything, the distinction between the Latin and Cyrillic characters can then consist in.
