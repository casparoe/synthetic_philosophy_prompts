I'm taking a moral philosophy class and we just did deontology, and Ive been stuck on something my prof called the paradox of deontology. I came into the course pretty sympathetic to a simple agent-neutral picture of reasons: if killing is bad, then five killings is worse than one, and a rational person who can prevent five killings by performing one should do it. That picture has a lot going for it—it's unified, it gives a definite answer in hard cases, and it doesn't treat your own causal involvement as magic. But I also cant shake the intuition that a surgeon just may not carve up one healthy patient to save five transplant patients, even if that's the only way to save them. That's not a heuristic to me; it feels categorically wrong, like something about doing it yourself matters.

What's bugging me is that the tension feels more internal to deontology than the usual deontology vs consequentialism debate. If the constraint against killing is grounded in how terrible killing is, then the deontologist seems stuck saying that killing one to stop five killings is forbidden—but that's exactly the act that minimises the number of killings. So the constraint ends up forbidding you from making there be less of the very thing it says is forbidden. It makes me wonder whether the intuition is really just an obsession with keeping my own hands clean dressed up as principle—but then I look back at the surgeon case and I can't make myself believe it.

I want someone to walk me through the strongest version of both sides and force a negotiated verdict, not just pick a winner. What would it cost to debunk the deontic intuition—what's the best debunking story, and does it survive contact with the surgeon case? What would it cost to amend the agent-neutral picture so agent-relative constraints don't come out irrational, and does that make the theory ad hoc? And is there a stable reading where the constraint governs how I deliberate while agent-neutral values govern how the world goes, without one silently overriding the other? I'm not looking for "just trust the intuition" or "just bite the bullet"—I want to know what each move would force me to give up, and what would justify giving it up.
