Here is a passage from a footnote in David Hume's 1748 essay "Of National Characters":

"The vulgar are apt to carry all national characters to extremes; and having once established it as a principle, that any people are knavish, or cowardly, or ignorant, they will admit of no exception. Men of sense condemn these undistinguishing judgments: though at the same time, they allow, that each nation has a peculiar set of manners, and that some particular qualities are more frequently to be met with among one people than among their neighbours."

I want help understanding exactly what position Hume is staking out here. In particular:

1. Does the sentence about "some particular qualities are more frequently to be met with" commit Hume to the view that there are real, robust national character traits, or only something weaker, such as statistical tendencies produced by social institutions and government? What in the passage, or in the arguments surrounding it, settles this?

2. If Hume is right, does this mean it can be rational to treat an individual differently merely because of their nationality? If not, what would have to be added to Hume's view to license such treatment, and does Hume himself supply it?

3. How does this passage relate to his broader empiricist commitments about causation and general inference? Is there a problem for his view if national character judgments are both empirical generalizations and, as he admits, ridden with prejudice and bias?
