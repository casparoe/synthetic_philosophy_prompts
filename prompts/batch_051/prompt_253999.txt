I'm a novelist, two books into a series set in a near-future where major war has basically stoped and the old martial vocabulary is starting to sound like Latin. I keep tripping over the word 'courage,' so I want to nail it down before I write a key scene.

In the scene, one character calls another 'the bravest person I know' because she donated a kidney to a stranger through a paired-exchange program. Here's the case as drafted: she's healthy, nobody pressured her, she's never met the recipient and doesn't care to; she has a real phobia of hospitals and anesthesia, the surgery carries a small but genuine risk of death or serious complications, and she goes through with it because shes convinced the recipient will go on to do a lot of good if they live. She's terrified the entire time and does it anyway.

The working definition of courage I've been using in the book, put in the mouth of an old ex-soldier character, goes roughly: (1) the person is afraid, or has some strong aversion they have to master; (2) the danger is real, not imagined or exaggerated; (3) the end is worth the risk; (4) the act is voluntary, not coerced; and (5) there's something outside the person that the act stands up to — a threat, an enemy, an opposing force. I added (5) because without it I couldn't see why courage isn't just 'taking a risk for a good reason,' and then every base jumper or startup founder qualifies.

So: under that definition, is she courageous? Go through the criteria one by one and be honest about which ones she clearly meets, which are borderline, and which she fails. Then tell me whether criterion (5) is doing real work or whether it's just a leftover from thinking of courage as a battlefield virtue — and if it's the latter, whether I should revise the definition or let my characters fight about exactly that in the book. Last thing: is the deeper problem maybe that 'courage' in a peaceful society has to cover two or three different things that war used to fuse together?
