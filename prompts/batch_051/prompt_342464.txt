I got into an argument with a friend while packing dishes for a move several years ago, and it has since turned into a standing disagreement about what makes something fragile, soluble, or poisonous in the first place. She has very firm verdicts on particular cases, so I have kept a list of them in the hope of extracting the rule she is actually applying. On her account, an ordinary wine glass is fragile, and a steel ingot is not. A wine glass wired up for an afternoon in a laboratory so that the instant anything strikes it a machine injects quick-setting resin into the glass and keeps it from breaking is fragile, even though it never breaks when struck. A wine glass wrapped in foam and packed in a crate for a move is also fragile, even though you can hit the crate and the glass survives. But a steel plate fitted for an evening with small demolition charges so that the lightest tap shatters it is not fragile, even though a tap would shatter it. She gives similar rulings about solubility and poison, so this seems to be a general policy about whether a thing really has the property in question.

The case now puzzling me is this. An heirloom goblet has been kept, from the day it was made, inside a custom shock-absorbing mount that is never opened; the padded mount is the only environment the goblet has ever known. If you strike the mount with a hammer, the goblet remains intact. I would like a prediction of my friend's verdict—is the goblet fragile?—followed by the underlying analysis. Please lay out the rule she seems to be running and at least one other plausible rule that fits her track record equally well; say what each of those rules predicts about the heirloom goblet; give your best guess as to which rule she actually holds, with your reasons for preferring it; and name the further case, different from the goblet, that would most cleanly tell the competing rules apart, including what each side would predict about it.
