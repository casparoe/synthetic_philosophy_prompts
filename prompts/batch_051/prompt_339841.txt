I'm trying to figure out whether Keynes's argument in Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren about the good life still works once you give up the idea that wants are fixed and satiable. The essay starts from this passage:

" For I predict that both of the two opposed errors of pessimism which now make so much noise in the world will be proved wrong in our own time – the pessimism of the revolutionaries who think that things are so bad that nothing can save us but violent change, and the pessimism of the reactionaries who consider the balance of our economic and social life so precarious that we must risk no experiments."

His whole vision of the good life—the money-motive revalued, the art of life cultivated, work reduced to about fifteen hours a week—rests on the claim that absolute wants get saturated as per-capita output grows. But what if the growth process itself keeps generating new wants? Modern consumer economies now run on recommender systems and attention markets that are basically machines for manufacturing and reshaping desire; preferences aren't a fixed list that gets checked off, they're trained by the same systems that monetize them. Does Keynes's argument survive this development? Specifically, which of his premises is actually under pressure—the satiability of wants, the stability of preferences, or the assumption that the economic problem is a problem about absolute scarcity? And if the argument doesn't survive in its original form, is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still worth keeping? I want a committed answer here, not a survey: pick the premise that actually breaks and defend the repair or the burial.
