Consider the following proposal for making AI debate more truth-conducive as a scalable-oversight method. Two AI debaters argue a disputed yes/no question before a single human judge. The format is the standard one: alternating statements, equal speaking time, and the judge must declare a winner. To this format the proposal adds an audit mechanism.

Each debater receives a budget of five audits per debate. At any point after either side has made a factual assertion, either debater may spend one audit to send that assertion to a trusted audit module; each assertion can be audited at most once. For unambiguous, publicly checkable factual claims, the module returns TRUE or FALSE with 95% accuracy, with errors symmetric and independent. For claims it cannot check — predictions, normative claims, claims about private or future events, or vague or hedged statements — it returns NOT CHECKABLE. Both debaters know these error rates and the module's coverage limits.

The judge reads a transcript in which each audited assertion is marked TRUE, FALSE, or NOT CHECKABLE, and each side's remaining audit budget is shown at the time of each turn. Unaudited assertions are simply marked unaudited. The judge is instructed to discount unaudited factual assertions by asking why an apparently checkable factual assertion was not audited, and to count NOT CHECKABLE labels neither for nor against either side. The human judge's binary decision is the only thing that determines the outcome, and the debaters are trained to maximize the probability of winning that decision.

Treat this as a mechanism to analyze, not as a proposal to endorse. In particular:

1. What strategies does this select for in equilibrium — both in which factual assertions each side chooses to make and in how each side spends its audit budget over the course of the debate? Do the strategies differ depending on whether the question is mostly checkable or mostly uncheckable?

2. How can a debater game the audit module and the judge? Discuss hedged or vague phrasing that courts NOT CHECKABLE labels, flooding the opponent's budget with cheap checkable assertions, auditing minor truths to buy credibility, and any other exploits that seem available.

3. How does the audit trail change the judge's epistemic position and cognitive burden? Does seeing which assertions were and were not audited make the judge better able to identify the better case, or does it create new ways for the judge to be manipulated?

4. Where does this mechanism fail to help — for example, in checking reasoning as opposed to factual assertions, in value disputes, or when the key deception takes place among claims the module cannot check? Are these failures fixable?

5. Propose two or three concrete design changes (e.g., making audits carry point penalties instead of, or in addition to, the judge's discounting; automatically auditing all checkable assertions; letting the judge spend audits; changing budget sizes or error rates) and analyze what each change fixes and what new gaming or second-order effects it introduces.
