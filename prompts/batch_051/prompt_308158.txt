I keep noticing a mismatch in standard advice about risk and money, and I'm not sure which half is the real theory. Expected-value reasoning says: judge any gamble by its expectation, think of the long run as the average over many independent trials, and take any bet with positive expectation. But the same people warn about drawdowns, ruin, and sequence-of-returns risk, and say to size even favorable bets at a Kelly fraction or less because you only live out one path. What view of probability, time, and risk does the standard practice actually presuppose? Does the expected-value advice only make sense if time averages and ensemble averages coincide—if one long run will, with enough patience, come to look like the cross-section of all possible runs? And if that sameness doesn't hold generally, which half of the advice has to be rebuilt?
