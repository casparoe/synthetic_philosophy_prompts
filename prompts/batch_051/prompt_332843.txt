Design a 75-minute classroom activity for an undergraduate philosophy-of-religion seminar of about 14 students that stages the regress behind sola scriptura: a text cannot settle its own application, so every appeal to the text alone quietly introduces an interpreter or a rule of interpretation. The students should not hear the terms sola scriptura or regress of interpretation until the final debrief.

The core of the activity should be a short invented code or charter — briefly, a paragraph of rules about membership, shared resources, and resolving disputes — which groups of 4–5 must use to decide a sequence of concrete cases. The charter is the only admissible authority; groups may not appeal to custom, external fairness, majority preference, or common sense. Sequence the cases so that early ones resolve mechanically, middle ones force the groups to define contested terms in the charter, and later ones force them to define the procedure by which contested terms get defined. Require each group to write down every supplement they add to the charter — definitions, priority rules, interpretive principles — and have the groups compare supplements before the debrief, since the same original text will generate materially different supplements.

Include exact rules, the full case list, a timeline, and instructions for what the facilitator should do when a group stalls or claims the cases are simply underdetermined. End with a debrief that maps each stage of the activity onto elements of the sola scriptura debate — perspicuity, private judgment, the emergence of creeds and teaching authority, and the question of who decides when honest readers disagree — names the likely failure modes and how to handle them, and says plainly which feature of real theological commitment the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator.
