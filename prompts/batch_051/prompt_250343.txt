I'm a PhD student in law and philosophy, working on legal personhood and on how statutes drafted before modern AI apply to autonomous systems. I'm assembling a set of statutory-interpretation problems, and I'd like you to work through one.

Consider this provision from a model commercial-liability statute, drafted in an era when the only artificial legal persons were corporations:

"In this Act, 'person' means a natural person or a corporation. A person may, in its own name, enter into contracts, hold property, and sue and be sued."

Here is the case. A logistics firm operates an autonomous procurement system that solicits bids, accepts contracts, and issues purchase orders in its own name, with no human approving individual transactions. The system is software running on the firm's servers; it is not incorporated and has no legal existence separate from the firm. A supplier, having delivered goods on the strength of the system's acceptance, is now unpaid and sues the system itself under the Act. The supplier argues that the definition of "person" reaches any artificial entity that in fact contracts and holds itself out as capable of contracting. The firm moves to dismiss on the ground that the system is not a "person" within the meaning of the Act.

I care less about the doctrinal outcome than about the interpretive logic. Argue the motion from four angles: (i) the text of the provision; (ii) the purpose of the provision; (iii) what the drafters would have said had they been asked about autonomous software agents; and (iv) what a reasonable reader at the time of enactment would have taken the provision to mean. Then say which approach should control here and why. Throughout, engage explicitly with the corporate-person analogy: the Act already extends "person" beyond natural persons, and I want you to address whether that extension embodies a principle that reaches the system, or whether the word "corporation" is a limit rather than an instance of a broader policy.

Hard limit: 500 words.
