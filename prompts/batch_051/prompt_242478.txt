I've been trying to settle a question in the philosophy of law: when is it fair to bind someone to a rule they did not know? I have verdicts about a series of concrete cases, but I haven't yet worked out the principle behind them.

Here are my cases and verdicts.

(1) A city passes a food-truck parking ordinance effective immediately and publishes it only in a printed register kept at city hall. A food-truck owner working across town, with no reason to check, is fined during the first week. I think the fine is unfair.

(2) The same city prohibits right turns on red at one intersection, posts the sign at 7 a.m., and tickets a driver at 7:30 a.m. I think the ticket is unfair.

(3) A large online marketplace sells imported appliances that turn out to require a new federal safety certification. The rule appeared in the Federal Register and in trade newsletters, but the company's compliance team never flagged it. I think holding the company accountable is fair, even though no individual employee actually read the rule.

(4) A small landlord misses a rent-regulation filing requirement accessible only through an expensive legal database he cannot afford. I think penalizing him is unfair.

(5) A tourist violates a criminal statute in a country she is visiting. The statute is a genuine part of that country's code and a local lawyer could find it, but it isn't advertised to visitors, and the conduct is legal in her home country. I still think it would be fair for that country to punish her, on the ground that entering a place means accepting its laws.

(6) A taxpayer signs a return under a printed fraud warning but claims he never knew filing a false return was illegal. I think punishing him is fair.

(7) A legislature criminalizes conduct that occurred before the statute was enacted and prosecutes someone for that past conduct. I think this is plainly unfair.

My tentative principle is that a person may fairly be bound only by a rule they had a fair opportunity to learn. But I can already see it handles (3) and (5) badly.

Is there one coherent principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts? For each candidate, state it precisely, show how it handles my cases, say what it implies about cases I haven't decided (for example: a rule published only in English in a community where many residents read only Spanish; a secret national-security rule declassified years later; an illiterate person subject to a posted rule), and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. I'd rather get the principle right than preserve every verdict. Please write your answer in American English.
