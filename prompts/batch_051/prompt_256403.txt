I hold the position that a whole-brain emulation of me — by which I mean a computational reconstruction of my brain that reproduces whatever neural structure is causally responsible for my mental life at a sufficient level of fidelity — would be me: the same mind and the same self, and not merely a replica, a behavioural duplicate, or a successor. I accept that, after the emulation is made, the original and the emulation would go on to live distinct lives; the position concerns what takes place at the point of transition, not whether two later beings could both be identical with me. Tell me what would count against this position. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and for each I want to know the assumption without which it would fail, whether it is a live possibility or merely a logical one, and whether a committed defender of emulation-identity should themselves concede it as a cost rather than dismiss it as a talking point. In particular, address: the case of gradual replacement, in which one's neurons are exchanged one by one for functionally equivalent artificial devices whilst one remains conscious, and what that case implies, if anything, for a sudden destructive scan; the possibility that consciousness depends on biological features that no scan could capture; the branching objection, in which several emulations are produced; and the deflationary suggestion that there is no determinate fact of the matter as to which, if any, of the resulting beings is me. Finally, say whether the position can be qualified into being true by definition, and, if it can, whether that should count as a damaging insulation for a view of this kind.
