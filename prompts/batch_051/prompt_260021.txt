I have spent much of this term thinking with my graduate students about a disarmingly simple question: if natural selection is the only reason creatures like us have the wants we do, and an artificial system shares none of that history, is there a non-metaphorical sense in which such a system could want anything at all? In struggling with this I have developed a distinction that I would like scrutinised before I present it at a departmental colloquium—or before I quietly drop it.

I have been calling it the distinction between sourced and unsponsored desires. A sourced desire is one whose presence in an agent is backed by a history that explains its content: it has been shaped by experience, acquired through learning or reflection, or produced by a selective process that tracks what the desire is about, so that one can say how the agent came to have this desire rather than some other. An unsponsored desire is one that simply stands in the agent's preference structure—written in at initialisation, or delivered as the terminus of an optimisation procedure—without any such history connecting its content to the agent's own life. My provisional thesis is that only sourced desires are wants. Unsponsored desires are at best targets: states that steer an agent's selection among actions in the way a thermostat's set point steers a boiler, but that do not belong to the agent in the way wanting requires. On this view a paperclip maximiser does not literally want paperclips, however robustly it pursues them.

The thesis is meant to explain the intuitive resistance many people feel to saying that an AI with an arbitrary terminal objective wants anything, while locating the resistance in the absence of an appropriating history rather than in the absence of biological evolution as such. I am nonetheless uneasy. I can already see that the view may simply relabel the intentional stance, or that it may be imposing a causal-historical condition on desire that a functionalist will reject as unmotivated, or that the whole distinction is an artefact of my wanting 'desire' to do more work than it does. I cannot tell whether any version of it is worth keeping. Is a distinction of this kind already worked out under a name I should know? If it is not, give it its strongest reconstruction, name the three most serious objections, and tell me whether any of them is fatal or merely uncomfortable. I would be grateful for the assessment in British English.
