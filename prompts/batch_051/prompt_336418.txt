You are a professor of formal epistemology with a research background in Bayesian updating and the dynamics of conditional belief. I'm on the committee writing this year's PhD comprehensive exam in formal epistemology, and I'd like you to draft one exam question on the Judy Benjamin problem and updating on conditionals. Our students have covered standard Bayesian conditionalization and Jeffrey conditionalization, but they have not met this problem before, so the question should be self-contained. It should be doable in 90 minutes and worth 20 marks. The question should force candidates to do more than reproduce update rules: it should make them confront a live tension between rival rules and defend a diagnostic judgment about what the evidence actually is.

Please deliver three things:
1. The exam question itself, written as it would appear on the paper. Set it in a territory divided into Red and Blue zones, each split into HQ and Forward sectors, with a simple numerical prior such as P(R∧HQ)=0.3, P(R∧F)=0.2, P(B∧HQ)=0.2, P(B∧F)=0.3, and a trusted signal whose content is: if you are in Red territory, then you are in the HQ sector. Ask students to formalize the constraint on the posterior, compare conditionalizing on the material conditional with Adams conditioning, and then face the conceptual crux: does the message give any reason to change your probability that you are in Red, or does a good update rule leave that probability alone, and what structural facts about how the signal was generated matter to adjudicating this?
2. A model answer written as a top PhD candidate would write it, using calculations where appropriate and clear conceptual prose where they matter.
3. A marking scheme with exactly five must-hit points, each worth a specified number of marks (total 20), and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong.

Before writing the exam question, think briefly but carefully about where the real conceptual crux is, and state it in one short brainstorming paragraph at the start of your answer. Aim to write distractors or must-hit points that target common confusions — for example, treating the conditional signal as if it were the proposition R⊃HQ and missing the underdetermination of the posterior, or insisting that a conditional can never change the probability of its antecedent without asking how the signal was generated.
