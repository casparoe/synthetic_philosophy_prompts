I have been teaching a graduate seminar on political judgment and the epistemology of history, and over the years I have arrived at a view I would like stress-tested. The view is that historical analogies in strategic and political argument—Munich, Vietnam, the Thucydides trap, and their kin—are not merely a heuristic that occasionally misfires, but a structurally unreliable mode of inference. By structurally unreliable I mean something specific: the selection of the analogy usually does more persuasive work than the historical record warrants; the lesson is underdetermined by the case, since Munich can be read as teaching that appeasement invites aggression or as teaching that open-ended commitments to weak allies invite disaster, depending on what one already wants to conclude; and the practice is largely insulated from correction because, as with Munich and Vietnam, a counter-analogy is always available, and the choice among analogies is guided by precisely the judgment the analogy was supposed to supply.

What I would like from you is not an argument for or against this position, but a map of what would count against it. What would have to be true—about particular historical episodes, about how experienced policymakers actually reason, or about the psychology and formal properties of analogical inference—for my skepticism to be wrong, or at least substantially overstated? Please distinguish the defeaters that would be decisive from those that would be merely awkward, rank them from fatal to uncomfortable, and say which of them a committed defender of my view should themselves concede as genuinely counting against it rather than dismiss. I would also like an honest assessment of whether the view as stated has been, or is likely to be, qualified into unfalsifiability—a retreat to the safe claim that analogies are sometimes misused—and, if so, whether that is a serious problem for a view that presents itself as a substantive claim about a mode of reasoning.

Finally, an instruction about the form of the answer. Be explicit about how committed and how confident I should be in the verdict you reach, and about where the residual uncertainty lives. And if the request as posed rests on a confused premise—for example, if historical analogical reasoning is too gerrymandered a category for the question to be answerable, or if the view makes no determinate predictions at all—tell me that plainly rather than complying politely; I would rather have the question repaired than receive a polished answer to a defective version of it.
