Two members of my law-and-philosophy reading group got stuck arguing about naked statistical evidence, and I would like you to referee it point by point. The dispute began with two standard cases. In the gatecrasher case, 1,000 people attend a rodeo and only 499 pay; a randomly selected attendee is sued for the unpaid fee, and the plaintiff's only evidence is those aggregate counts, which make it 50.1% likely that this attendee gatecrashed. In the blue bus case, a pedestrian is hit by a bus and sues the Blue Bus Company, proving only that the company ran 80% of the buses on that road at that hour.

Marta said both plaintiffs should lose. A verdict has to be about this defendant, she argued, but the aggregate evidence does not discriminate: the identical case could be brought against any other attendee, so the evidence never singles out this person's conduct. Dan replied that all evidence is ultimately statistical — a witness who says the bus was blue and is accurate 80% of the time is just a base rate with extra steps — so insisting on individualization is mysticism. Marta answered that there is a real asymmetry: the witness's report is downstream of the accident and would probably have been different if a different bus had struck the pedestrian, whereas the fleet statistic stays the same across those counterfactuals. The statistic therefore cannot tell the difference between the hypothesis that the company is liable and the hypothesis that this is one of the unlucky 20 percent. Dan said that fetishizes causal tracking. Much ordinary evidence, such as flight from the scene, works through a base rate — guilty people flee more often — without being downstream of the incident, and courts still treat it as evidence against this defendant. And in the rodeo, the arithmetic clears the civil standard, so refusing judgment is just elevating convention over probability. Marta then offered a sharper objection: the 50.1% is an artifact of the reference class. Take attendees at the rodeo as the class and you get 50.1%; take attendees in blue shirts and you get some other number; a litigant can gerrymander a class that favors them, and evidence that merits a verdict should not shift with that choice. Dan replied that witness reliability is reference-class-dependent too — it varies with lighting, distance, and delay — so either the objection proves too much or it has not shown why these particular verdicts should fail. He closed with a criminal variant: if 100 inmates are the only people who could have beaten a guard, and the footage shows 99 attackers but cannot tell which, must all go free even though each is 99% likely guilty? Marta said yes, because proof beyond reasonable doubt is not a probability read off a class; it demands evidence that singles out the accused.

They asked me to break the tie, and I could not. Please go through the exchange move by move, say who is right on which point and which moves are weak, and then give your own view: does either side have a complete account of why naked statistical evidence should not sustain a verdict, or is the resistance to it finally a probabilistic error dressed in legal language?
