I'm taking a seminar on rational choice theory, and a classmate constructed an argument that has been stuck in my head because its conclusion seems absurdly strong. We were discussing the standard Allais-type pattern in its common-ratio form. In one choice, most people prefer A to B:

A: $3,000 for sure.
B: 80% chance of $4,000, otherwise $0.

In another choice, most people prefer D to C:

C: 25% chance of $3,000, otherwise $0.
D: 20% chance of $4,000, otherwise $0.

Those two pairings violate the independence axiom, but my classmate argued they violate something much more basic. His argument:

(1) Build a compound lottery C*: with 25% probability you receive lottery A, and with 75% probability you receive nothing. Build D*: with 25% probability you receive lottery B, and with 75% probability you receive nothing. The two compound lotteries share the same 75% branch.

(2) In the 25% branch, C* yields A while D* yields B. Since A is preferred to B, C* is strictly better in that branch, and neither is better in the shared branch.

(3) Therefore, by a principle no stronger than ordinary statewise dominance applied to the branches of these compound lotteries, C* is strictly preferred to D*.

(4) But reducing the compound lotteries to their final probability distributions, C* just is C and D* just is D: C* becomes a 25% chance of $3,000 (otherwise nothing), and D* becomes a 20% chance of $4,000 (otherwise nothing).

(5) So the Allais preferences A ≻ B and D ≻ C entail both C ≻ D, by steps (3) and (4), and D ≻ C, by the stated preference. That is a direct contradiction.

(6) Hence anyone with the Allais pattern violates the dominance principle used in (3), which is no stronger than ordinary statewise dominance. And since everyone in the debate accepts ordinary statewise dominance, the Allais pattern is not a serious counterexample to expected utility theory; it is just a mistake in reasoning.

I am not willing to accept (6). The Allais pattern is supposed to put pressure on independence, not on dominance; dominance is the one principle nobody in the area wants to give up. But I cannot figure out which of the earlier steps is doing the work or why it is so easy to read as an innocent dominance argument. Which step should be rejected, why does it look like a bare dominance claim when it is not, and what exactly does a theory pay for rejecting it? Please answer in American English.
