You are a professional philosopher working on the ethics of belief and the Clifford–James debate. Begin by briefly reconstructing each position in your own words, then apply the views with precision; do not merely rehearse the standard summaries.

Analyze the 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak as a case study in the ethics of belief. The standard account runs as follows. During a severe cholera outbreak in the Soho district of London, the physician John Snow, who had long suspected that cholera was transmitted by contaminated water rather than by bad air as the dominant miasma theory held, plotted the deaths on a map, found them clustering around the public water pump on Broad Street, and persuaded the local authorities to remove the pump handle. The outbreak soon subsided, and the episode is now standardly credited as a founding moment of epidemiology.

Now run this episode through the debate between W. K. Clifford's evidentialism and William James's will to believe. For the purposes of this exercise, take the relevant contrast to be this: Clifford held that it is wrong to believe any proposition on insufficient evidence, while James argued that when a choice between hypotheses is live, forced, and momentous, and the evidence cannot settle it, something other than evidence must legitimately decide it, and our hopes, fears, and commitments may properly do so.

Be explicit about the mapping. What exactly was the belief at stake, and for whom? What was Snow's evidence, and how should we characterize it given that his inference depended on a skill for reading spatial patterns that the official medical community did not yet share? Is the option best described as a private doxastic choice between waterborne and miasmatic theories, or as a public, practical decision to remove a pump handle, and do the two come apart? Which parts of James's account of a genuine option fit this case, and which parts do not? What would a strict Cliffordian have said Snow should do, and what would James's view have licensed?

Then run the lens in the other direction. What does the episode get right about each view, and what does it reveal as too crude? In particular, consider the relation between epistemic justification at the time and vindication in hindsight; the difference between believing a theory and acting under uncertainty; and whether deference to a trained eye can supply the function that James assigned to passion. Conclude by saying honestly what neither view can explain about the episode, rather than forcing the case to settle the debate.
