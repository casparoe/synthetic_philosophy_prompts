Two people in my public policy class got into it after a guest lecturer claimed, about a plan to subsidize insulin for low-income diabetics paid for by a payroll tax, that "the wellbeing gains to recipients more than make up for the wellbeing losses to the people who pay for it." I heard the whole argument and stayed out of it. It went about like this:

Priya: That sentence is doing a lot of work with "more than make up for." You'd need to put my wellbeing and a stranger's on some single scale and subtract. Utility functions don't give you that; they only order each person's own options. Saying my gain is bigger than your loss is like saying my temperature is higher than your altitude.

Jonah: That's a bad example. Temperature and altitude aren't the same kind of magnitude, but wellbeing is. A triage nurse compares how much pain two patients are in. You do it too: when you said the mask mandate helped the vulnerable more than it annoyed everyone else, that was an interpersonal comparison. So you're not really a skeptic about comparisons — you're a skeptic about precise comparisons, which is a different and weaker claim.

Priya: Fine, I'm not a global skeptic. The nurse's comparison is ordinal: patient A is in more pain than patient B, judged against each of their own baselines. But the lecturer needed cardinal comparison — the sum of the gains is larger than the sum of the losses. You can know who is worse off without knowing by how much, and "by how much" needs a common unit we don't have.

Jonah: The policy gives you a common unit, though: money. If beneficiaries would pay more for the insulin than taxpayers would demand as compensation, that's a revealed comparison of how strongly each side cares. You don't need any mysterious mental stuff; you can watch what people choose.

Priya: That conflates money and wellbeing. A dollar means more to a poor person than to a rich one, so willingness to pay is contaminated by ability to pay. The comparison just re-weights people by wealth, which is exactly the thing the lecturer was supposed to be measuring, not assuming.

Jonah: So you adjust for the diminishing marginal utility of money. But notice: the adjustment itself is an interpersonal comparison. You've stopped denying that comparisons can be made; now we're just haggling about how to calibrate them.

Priya: No, I haven't conceded that. The "adjustment" just changes the question to how much wellbeing a dollar produces for each person, which is the very thing in dispute. You're calibrating one unmarked scale against another unmarked scale and acting like there's a zero point somewhere. There isn't.

Jonah: There can be fixed points if we allow empathy — sometimes called extended sympathy. I judge your situation by imagining myself in it with your preferences, not mine. That's how actual humans do calibrate. It's noisy, but that's measurement error, not mystery.

Priya: Extended sympathy doesn't solve it; it just projects my psychology into your situation. Two empathic observers can calibrate the scales in opposite directions, and there's no independent fact that decides between them. Doesn't that show the comparison isn't a fact out there to be measured at all — it's a normative choice about who gets how much weight?

Jonah: Even if it's partly normative, the choice is unavoidable. Not making the comparison is also making one: it quietly weights the status quo's losers at zero. So the lecturer's only real mistake was presenting a half-normative judgment as if it were purely empirical. You can't escape the comparison by refusing to make it.

Priya: But you can escape presenting it as a cardinal sum. I can say "a dollar does more for them than for you" in a rough, ordinal way, or I can defend the subsidy on equality grounds, which doesn't require summing anyone's welfare. What I can't honestly do is say the gains "more than make up for" the losses, as if there were a scale with a common unit.

I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it. Can you walk through their exchange and adjudicate point by point? I'd like you to act as a philosopher who works on welfare economics and formal decision theory, and to take your time — maybe begin by distingushing the different things "interpersonal comparison" can mean before ruling on who is right about which specific claim and what both of them are missing.
