I recently joined my hospital's clinical ethics committee, and we spent our last several meetings revising the mass-casualty triage protocol. The discussion kept turning away from logistics and back to a puzzle I first met as an undergraduate: the anti-aggregation position usually associated with John Taurek. As it was presented to us, the view holds that in a forced choice between saving one person and saving five, the bare fact that five is the larger number should not settle the matter. There is no single person who suffers the loss of all five, so adding five separate losses together treats distinct people as if they were one combined claimant; a fair decision may therefore give each side an equal chance instead of automatically saving the larger group.

I want you to reason through a counterfactual rather than evaluate that argument in the abstract. Suppose that next year a wealthy country with a unified public health care system writes the anti-aggregation principle into binding law for all allocations of scarce, lifesaving resources. The rule reads, in effect: whenever a scarce resource can be deployed either to a smaller group of patients or to a larger group, and the two groups differ only in how many people they contain, the difference in size may not count as a reason, and the choice must be made by a lottery that gives the smaller and larger groups an equal chance. Predict in detail what this health care system looks like thirty years later. I am interested in mechanisms, not hunches: which domains—emergency triage, transplant listing, pandemic ventilator or dialysis allocation, mass-casualty response—absorb the rule most easily, and which generate workarounds; how clinicians, hospital administrators, lawyers, and patient groups adapt; how medical teaching and public expectations shift; and whether the rule survives intact, is hollowed out through reinterpretation, or is repealed outright. For each major prediction, say whether it is a robust consequence of the rule or a guess that depends on contingent features of that society, and state what would have to be different for the prediction to fail. Please write your answer in American English.
