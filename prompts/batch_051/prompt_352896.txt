I am a retired physical oceanographer who has lately turned to philosophy, and I serve as Honorary Secretary of the Meridian Society for Natural Inquiry, a small learned society whose Proceedings go back more than a century. A dispute on our Council has exposed a fault in one of our Regulations, and I would value an analysis framed in the philosophical terms I have been reading.

The Regulation reads:

"Regulation 14(b). No communication that asserts a fact shall be read before the Society unless the author cites in its support at least one prior publication in the Proceedings of this Society, or in the transactions of a body the Council judges of equivalent standing; failing such citation, the communication shall be returned to the author with a written explanation."

Last quarter a Fellow submitted a manuscript reporting that a certain alloy, when cooled at a specified rate in a weak alternating magnetic field, develops a regular lamellar pattern on its surface. He repeated the experiment many times, documents his procedure carefully, and states that the phenomenon has not, to his knowledge, been recorded anywhere. The central factual assertion — that the alloy forms the pattern under these conditions — is exactly the sort of assertion the Regulation exists to guard; but there is no prior publication to cite, because the work appears to be the first report of its kind. Read strictly, Regulation 14(b) would return the Society's first genuinely novel findings to their authors.

The rule was plainly drafted to keep out speculation, casual anecdote, and argument from authority; the drafters did not notice that, read strictly, it forbids the very communications on which the later literature depends. I am less interested in what the Council should do as a matter of prudence than in what the rule, correctly interpreted, actually requires in this case. Please argue it from the text, from the evident purpose, from what the drafters would most plausibly have said if asked, and from what a reasonable Fellow would understand the rule to mean; then say which of these approaches should govern and why. The dispute has reminded me of the epistemic regress problem, with its trio of foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism. I would be grateful if you would state explicitly whether the most defensible interpretation of Regulation 14(b) commits the Society to one of those positions, and what that reading implies about the standing of first reports in science.
