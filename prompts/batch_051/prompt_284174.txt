We've been doing hedonism in my value theory class, and I got kind of obsessed with the heterogeneity problem—the complaint that if pleasure is what makes a life go well, then all the different pleasures ought to have something in common, but they just... don't. I tried to write the worry up as a tidy argument so I can see where it actually breaks (if it breaks). It goes:

(1) If hedonism is true, then "pleasure" has to pick out one property that all and only pleasant experiences have.
(2) Pleasant experiences include things like the warmth of a hot bath, solving a hard crossword clue, the rush after a run, schadenfreude at a rival's failure, relief when a bad pain stops, and the satisfaction of a good cry.
(3) Those experiences share no felt quality in common; there's no single "what it's like" running through all of them.
(4) If a bunch of things share no felt quality in common, then there's no single property they all have.
(5) So there's no single property that all and only pleasant experiences have.
(6) Therefore hedonism is false.

I half-suspect the weak point is (4), becuase it seems to slide from "no common feeling" to "no common property at all"—like, maybe they all share the property of being wanted while they last, or of being taken up with some kind of pro-attitude. But then I worry that once we fix (4) like that, the hedonist just gets to redefine pleasure functionally and the whole heterogeneity problem becomes toothless. So: is the argument as written valid, and if not, where exactly does it fail? If it's valid but unsound, which premise is the culprit and what's the strongest replacement a hedonist could offer for it? And if I charitably fixed the argument up, does it still land the conclusion, or does it only refute a strawman?
