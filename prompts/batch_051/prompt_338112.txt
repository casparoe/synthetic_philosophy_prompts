A friend of mine is an illusionist about phenomenal consciousness, and she showed me an argument which seems to prove her own position is self-defeating. She can't find the hole and neither can I. Here it is, tidied up:

(1) Illusionism says phenomenal consciousness — the what-it-is-likeness, the raw qualitative feels — is not real; introspection systematically misrepresents us as having such things.
(2) An illusion is not the same as a gap: for a system to be under an illusion, some state of that system must represent things as being a way they are not.
(3) So if introspection misrepresents us as having phenomenal feels, there must be a subject with introspective states doing the misrepresenting.
(4) But for things to seem to a subject otherwise than they are — for the system to be genuinely misled, and not merely registering a falsehood — there must be something it is like to be that subject while the illusion is going on.
(5) So illusionism is committed to the existence of a subject with a what-it-is-likeness — the very thing it denies.
(6) Therefore illusionism is incoherent: it can't even state what the illusion is without presupposing phenomenal consciousness.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather see illusionism fail on the merits than get killed off by a word trick, but every premise looks solid on it's own, so I'm stuck.
