So I got into a long argument last night with a friend about peer disagreement, and by the end I cared less about who won than about why the whole debate feels stuck. My friend is basically a conciliationist: he says if we're epistemic peers—same evidence, same relevant abilities, no reason to think either of us is more reliable—then when we disagree we should both move, maybe even split the difference. I pushed back with the standard stuff: the equal-weight rule seems to undermine itself when you meet someone who disagrees with the equal-weight rule; it looks like it would force you to suspend judgment on nearly everything; and sometimes you can see exactly where the other person's reasoning went wrong, so why should you have to move? He had a comeback for each one, and around hour three I realized the published debate has the same shape—every argument has a well-known reply and nobody changes sides.

So here's what I want. The conciliationism-vs-steadfastness debate has been running since the early 2000s and, as far as I can tell, both camps have fully built-out positions and neither budges. Is it a substantive disagreement about what rationality requires, a verbal one about the word 'rational' or 'should', or are the two sides answering different questions—say, one about what's permitted and one about what's required? My hunch is that the real fight is hiding somewhere else: maybe its uniqueness vs permissivism doing the work, or whether higher-order evidence about your own reliability behaves like ordinary evidence, or which cases get to count as data (the restaurant case, the mental-math case, the politics case). Diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually says, and tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis.
