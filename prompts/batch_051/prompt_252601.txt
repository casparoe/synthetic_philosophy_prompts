I have been trying to reconcile a standard result in decision theory with a familiar puzzle, and I would like a careful worked analysis of a variant that sits between the two. Good's theorem on the value of information says, in its standard form, that for an expected-utility maximizer facing a fixed decision problem, costless information can never lower expected utility, since the information can simply be ignored. Now take Newcomb's problem in a modified form. An opaque box contains $1,000,000 if and only if a predictor predicted that you would take only the opaque box; a transparent box always contains $1,000. The predictor is right about your final box choice with probability a, whether that choice is one box or two. Before you make your choice, you may press a button that, at no cost, displays the predictor's prediction. The predictor knew in advance whether you would press the button, and made its prediction in light of that fact. In a second version of the variant, the button displays not the prediction itself but a noisy signal that matches the prediction with probability 0.8.

Work through both versions in detail. For causal decision theory, which evaluates acts by their causal consequences, and for evidential decision theory, which evaluates acts by the outcomes they are evidence for, what does each theory recommend, first about pressing the button and then about the boxes? Compute the expected value of pressing the button relative to not pressing it, under each theory and in each version. Then identify exactly which premise of Good's theorem fails in the perfect-signal version, and say whether the same failure occurs in the noisy-signal version. Finally, for the perfect-signal version, derive the value of looking for evidential decision theory as a function of a, and identify the threshold at which looking becomes harmful rather than merely useless; explain why the threshold sits just above one half.
