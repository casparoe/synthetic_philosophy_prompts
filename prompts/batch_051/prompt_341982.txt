Im working on a novel where characters from different countries keep trying to explain each others behavior in sweeping ways, and I picked up Humes essay Of National Characters to think it through. There's a passage I keep returning to:

The human mind is of a very imitative nature; nor is it possible for any set of men to converse often together, without acquiring a similitude of manners, and communicating to each other their vices as well as virtues.

My friend, who actually studied philosophy, gave me a summary of what Hume is up to in the essay. He said something like: Hume thinks national character is real but its entirely caused by institutions and social contact; he flat-out says climate and geography have absolutely no effect on temperament, because that would be physical causation and he's an empiricist. The spreading mechanism is basically unconscious contagion among people who live under the same government and speak the same language. But Hume is being inconsistent, my friend said, because he still says things like the common people in Switzerland are more honest than those in Ireland, and that's exactly the careless kind of generalization about whole peoples that his own argument should rule out.

I dont quite buy the last charge—I have a feeling Hume does allow some generalizations, he just distinguishes between careful ones and the vulgar carrying them to extremes—but I want to know how much of this is fair. Can you go sentence by sentence and tell me what Hume would accept, what a careful reader of the essay would push back on, and whether the inconsistency charge survives? And if Im right that the last part is unfair, is the rest of the summary good enough for my novel-writing purposes, or would it still mislead me about what he actually thinks?
