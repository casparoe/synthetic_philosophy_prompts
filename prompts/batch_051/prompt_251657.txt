I have recently found myself caught between two familiar epistemological puzzles in a practical way. While compiling a long assessment report, I checked each of several dozen entries individually and found that I was willing to stand behind each entry taken on its own; at the same time, I was willing to stand behind the claim that at least one of my entries was mistaken. When I tried to state what I believed about the report as a whole, my commitments did not fit together. The lottery paradox and the preface paradox have consequently become vivid for me in a way they had not been before, and I want a session that produces something of that same discomfort in students — not by explaining the puzzles, but by arranging a situation in which their own decisions exhibit the tension.

Design a runnable classroom activity for an upper-level undergraduate epistemology seminar (14 students, 80 minutes) on the lottery and preface paradoxes. The activity should make participants feel the pull of the following apparent conflict before it is named in the debrief. A threshold rule for rational acceptance — accept when the probability is sufficiently high — seems to license accepting, of each ticket in a fair lottery, that it will lose; yet it also seems to forbid accepting the conjunction that every ticket will lose, even though it is hard to see how one could accept each conjunct while refusing the conjunction. The preface case should then reverse the pressure: participants should find themselves wanting to assert both a long list of individually checked claims and the claim that the list contains at least one error, and they should feel why abandoning any particular item seems arbitrary even though the whole set cannot be consistently accepted.

Design the session as a structured game or series of small-stakes choices with real payoffs (trivial stakes are fine, such as course credit points or a modest prize). Use rules, roles, prices, or scoring incentives so that participants run into the problem through what they do — for instance, by setting the prices at which they would buy or sell claims, by certifying claims for a reward, or by having to defend a jointly inconsistent set of commitments against a rival. Be concrete: give the rules, the number of tickets or claims to use, a timeline for the 80 minutes, and the exact debrief questions.

The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the two paradoxes and onto the underlying ideas: acceptance versus graded confidence, conjunction closure, and the way stakes or context can shift what one is willing to assert. It should also say what predictably goes wrong when the activity is run — for example, participants treating acceptance as merely assigning high probability and thereby missing the clash — and how to handle it. Finally, be frank about which feature of the philosophical situation the activity cannot reproduce and that the facilitator will have to supply in discussion.
