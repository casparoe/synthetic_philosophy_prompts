I teach a course on the philosophy of punishment, and every year when we hit drug policy I end up in the same argument with a colleague who does criminal law theory. The dispute is whether decriminalization is a coherent middle position between prohibition and legalization, or whether it's an unstable halfway house that has to tip one way or the other. By decriminalization I mean the Portugal-style setup: personal possession and use are not criminally punished (maybe a civil fine or a referral to a health panel), but production and sale remain fully illegal. My credence that this is a coherent and stable middle position is about 0.65; my colleague's is about 0.25, and he thinks decrim is just prohibition that has lost its nerve.

I want your actual credence on the proposition that a state can coherently maintain Portugese-style decriminalization over, say, a thirty-year horizon as a matter of the logic of the position rather than just political inertia — where coherently means the regime doesn't either drift into de facto legalization or snap back to prohibition, and the state's message doesn't quietly become one of approval. Give me a number, but more importantly decompose it. I'm after the sub-questions: whether the expressive dimension of law can carry real condemnation without punishing the user; whether the user/seller distinction can bear the moral weight this position puts on it; whether supply-side enforcement keeps black-market harms alive in a way that undermines the attempted message; whether not punishing normalizes the conduct in practice regardless of what the law says; and whatever other terms you think belong. For each sub-question give a rough probability and one line of reason, then show how they combine into your headline number. Then tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and what development — philosophical or empirical — would move that term the most. Finally, tell me where my colleague and I most likely actually disagree.
