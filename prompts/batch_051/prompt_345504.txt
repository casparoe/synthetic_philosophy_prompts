I recently got into a hard disagreement with my brother about whether I should leave a stable job to train as a counselor. He ended the argument with what he clearly took to be a knockdown line: "If you were fully informed and fully rational, you would not want to quit." He seemed to think this showed that my actual desire—formed, he says, on the basis of incomplete information and a bit of wishful thinking—was not authoritative, and that I should not act on it. I recognize that this is the everyday cousin of the philosophical idea that a person's "real" or "idealized" preferences are what they would want under ideal conditions, but I cannot tell what precise claim the sentence is actually making. The words "fully informed" and "fully rational" seem to be doing an enormous amount of hidden work, and I am not sure whether my brother's inference is valid.

Act as a professional philosopher working on value theory, decision theory, and the nature of desire. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming several distinct candidate interpretations before settling on the sharpest set of distinctions. Then lay out a structured menu of the precise claims the one-liner could be asserting. I especially want you to distinguish interpretations along these dimensions, to the extent they make a difference:

- The informational base: all facts whatsoever, only the facts relevant to this choice, only the facts the speaker thinks matter, or specifically the facts about what the option would feel like from the inside.
- The temporal and metaphysical status of the claim: a prediction about what I would come to want after learning the information, a definition of what I already "really" want despite my avowals, or a normative claim about what it would be good for me to want.
- The kind of rationality being idealized: idealizing my reasoning while keeping my current cares fixed, versus idealizing my cares themselves according to some standard of correctness.
- The speech act being performed: a factual description of my psychology, an account of my well-being, or an instruction to defer to the speaker's sense of what I would want.

For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, with a brief explanation. Then say which reading or readings would actually support my brother's move from the counterfactual to the conclusion that I should not act on my present desire, and which readings would fail to support it. Be willing to be frank about where idealized-preference views run out—for example, where the idealization stops and whose values are being smuggled in. I am less interested in a single answer than in a map of the options, so I can go back and see exactly what he was claiming and whether I have to accept it.
