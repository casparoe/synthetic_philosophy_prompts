I spent thirty-five years in cell biology, so I'm naturally sympathetic to precision. But lately I've been reading Whitehead's The Aims of Education and he's got this recurring complaint that education goes wrong when it starts with precise definitions and formal statements before the learner has any sense of the problem or the phenomena. He thinks you need a stage of romance first — not in the love-story sense, but the stage where the material is vague, exciting, half-understood, and full of possibilities — then a stage of precision where you discipline that initial excitement, and then a stage of generalization where the precise knowledge gets applied to something new. I keep thinking about how much undergraduate biology gets taught in the opposite order.

I run a reading group at our local library, about 12 adults, mostly retired, one 90-minute session. We've been reading Whitehead for two weeks and people sort of nod at this rhythm idea but I don't think they've felt it. I want next week's session to be an activity, not a discussion of the text. Maybe something where half the group learns a small technical topic definitions-first and the other half gets a messy concrete puzzle or phenomenon first, and then both halves have to apply what they know to an unfamiliar case. I want the definitions-first group to feel the inertness that Whitehead keeps warning about — they can recite definitions but can't bend them to a new problem — and the puzzle-first group to feel the difference between an initial confused excitement and the precision they then actually want. It should use a non-philosophy example so the point isn't about the content, something like why soap works, or how lichen colonize bare rock, or a simple electrical circuit, anything with enough structure for real precision but enough surface appeal for romance.
