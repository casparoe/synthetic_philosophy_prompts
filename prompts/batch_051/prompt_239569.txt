Two members of my high school philosophy club got into an argument about whether statutes of limitations for serious crimes are morally defensible. I wrote down the exchange as best I could remember it:

(1) Arjun: A statute of limitations is morally indefensible in principle. A wrong doesn't become less wrong, and a wrongdoer doesn't become less deserving of punishment, just because a number of years has passed. The calendar is morally arbitrary.

(2) Maya: That fixes on the wrongdoer in isolation. The point of a limitation is that after enough time, evidence has decayed, witnesses are gone, and punishment would fall on someone whose life is now tied to other people in ways it wasn't then. Society can reasonably decline to pursue a claim whose costs have become disproportionate.

(3) Arjun: That isn't a defense of extinguishing the claim; it's a defense of choosing not to enforce it. You're really agreeing with me that the wrongdoer still deserves punishment, and we are just declining for practical reasons.

(4) Maya: No, I'm not only talking about evidence and cost. The moral relationship itself changes with time. The response that is fitting ten days after a wrong differs from the response that is fitting twenty years later. Resentment that would have been entirely fitting at first can become excessive simply because it has lasted so long, even if the victim's memory is perfect.

(5) Arjun: That confuses our feelings with what is deserved. The victim's cooling passions don't change what the wrongdoer deserves, and a community doesn't lose its standing to condemn just because the calendar has flipped.

(6) Maya: You're the one confusing things, confusing the permanence of the wrong with the timeliness of punishment. What a person deserves is a matter of how a whole life is to be assessed, not a single act pinned outside of time.
