I'm working on a longread about why people are so bad at saying where the line is—when a pile becomes a heap, when somebody counts as bald, that sort of thing. For it I've been interviewing a psycholinguist who runs judgment studies on borderline cases, and since she's unusually willing to give her own snap calls, I've kept a log of her verdicts to try to reverse-engineer the rule she's actually running.

On the sand-pile items in an ordinary driveway setting, her calls go: 100,000 grains, heap; 10,000, heap; 300, heap; 90, heap. Then 45, not a heap; 12, not a heap; two, obviously not a heap. At 65 grains, when I pressed for a yes or no, she refused and called it genuinely borderline, though she was happy to say it's more heap than 45 and less heap than 90. The wrinkle: a while back I showed her a photo of maybe 60 grains scattered across a lab balance and she didn't hesitate to call it a heap, which startled me, because 65 on a driveway had gotten a shrug. So it looks like the standard can move with the context.

Next week I'm going to show her 55 grains sitting in the groove of a camera lens and ask: heap or not? Before I run it, I want your prediction—what will she say, and which rule best explains the whole track record? I know the standard labels—epistemicism (a hidden sharp cutoff we can't know), supervaluationism (borderline cases are neither true nor false), degree theories (heap-ishness comes in degrees), and contextualism (the cutoff moves with the situation)—but I cant tell which one she's actually running, or if it's some mix. And if you could only ask her one more case to settle which rule she's really running, what should it be, and what would the different answers show?
