I recently joined a committee that is designing a small internal competition modeled, perhaps accidentally, on Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma. Twenty independent research groups are invited to decide simultaneously, without communicating, whether to submit a claim. Under the original Platonia rule, a one-billion-dollar grant is awarded only if exactly one group submits; if two or more submit, the funder awards nothing; if none submit, nothing is awarded. My understanding of the standard superrational argument is that, since the twenty players are symmetric and must all arrive at the same strategy, the payoff-maximizing mixed strategy is to submit with probability one in twenty, yielding each player an expected payoff of roughly nineteen million dollars. Before I bring this to the committee, I want to be sure that this is the correct baseline.

Our committee is now considering a slight change: if exactly two groups submit, rather than everyone receiving nothing, the two submitting groups would be required to collaborate and would split the grant equally. If three or more submit, no award is made. Please work through the symmetric superrational calculation for this modified rule. Specifically, assume all twenty players are superrational in Hofstadter's sense, so they each choose the same submission probability p and know that the others are doing the same, and find the value of p that maximizes each player's expected payoff. Is this optimal p higher, lower, or the same as one-twentieth, and what is the resulting expected payoff per player? I would also like to know whether the qualitative character of the dilemma changes: does the two-claimant floor undercut the "luring" pressure to send, or does it create a new coordination trap? Finally, if the committee instead generalizes the rule so that any number k of claimants receives an equal share of the grant, with no upper bound beyond the twenty, what happens to the superrational strategy and expected payoffs?
