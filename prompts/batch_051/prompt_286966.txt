I have been teaching the opening chapters of Harry Frankfurt's On Bullshit, and I find his central diagnosis hard to resist. He distinguishes the liar, who at least knows the truth and deliberately turns away from it, from the bullshitter, whose defining mark is something deeper: a lack of concern with whether what he says matches how things actually are. The liar pays truth the backhanded compliment of avoiding it; the bullshitter operates without any such constraint, shaping his words to whatever the occasion seems to require. This seems to me to capture something real about advertising, political talk, and certain kinds of academic prose, and it offers a clean criterion: what makes something bullshit is not falsity but the speaker's relationship to truth.

But I have a firm and recurring intuition about LLM hallucination that this criterion seems to classify in a way I cannot accept. Take a model asked to draft a legal memorandum that invents a plausible-sounding but nonexistent case. On Frankfurt's account, the model looks like a paradigm bullshitter: it produces fluent, confident, confident-sounding text with no concern for correspondence to fact. Yet I want to say the model is not bullshitting because it is not indifferent in the relevant sense. It is executing a procedure, trained to maximize likelihood over a corpus, that tracks truth well in many domains and fails in others. Calling its output bullshit seems to presuppose an agent that could care about truth and then withholds that care. The model neither cares nor withholds; its failures are errors of a different kind, closer to a broken barometer than to a spin doctor.

There is a further turn of the screw. Some LLM hallucinations are not simply random noise. Under induction, models reproduce the fluent, hedging, filler-saturated patterns of bullshit they have seen in training data. If a model explains a failed project as the result of a combination of factors when the prompt contains nothing of the sort, it is doing more than erring: it seems to be imitating the exact stance Frankfurt describes, because the corpus is full of people performing indifference to truth. Now my intuition splits. The imitation is not the model's own indifference, but the output functions socially as bullshit anyway.

I want to know what should yield. Should I give up the intuition that hallucination is not bullshit, because Frankfurt's criterion picks out a functional property of output rather than an inner act? Should I amend the theory by restricting it to agents with the capacity for truth-concern, so that a language model is neither liar nor bullshitter but a truth-apt mechanism that misfires? Or is there a reading of indifference on which both survive, with the model not indifferent yet its outputs bullshit in a derivative or social sense? Present the strongest version of each move, what it would cost, and tell me which you would accept rather than merely which side wins.
