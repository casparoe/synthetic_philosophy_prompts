I'm a journalist putting together a piece for the ideas section about whether we should replace career politicians—or at least rotate them out—and whether randomly selected citizens could do the job better. I want to run one of those branching "which position do you actually hold?" decision-tree graphics with the piece, and I'd like you to build it for me.

The tree should sort a reader from a starting hunch—something like "career politicians are the problem" or "this all sounds like tech-startup nonsense"—down to a named position on sortition, term limits, and the professional legislature. But I dont want it to be label-driven: none of the questions can be the equivalent of "do you support lottocracy?". They have to be concrete claims about politics that a reader can answer without already knowing where the branch leads.

The angle for my piece is that sortition and term limits get lumped together as the same anti-career impulse, but they may come apart: term limits keep elections and just cap how long winners serve, while sortition stops trusting elections altogether, at least for some body. So the tree should be able to seperate those two readers cleanly.

Here are the distinctions I suspect are doing the work—build the branch nodes out of these (you can add at most one or two more if you think my list is missing a load-bearing split):

1. Whether there's a real skill to legislating—drafting, procedural maneuvering, committee work—that ordinary citizens mostly lack and that takes years to learn.
2. Whether re-election is the main thing that keeps officials from slacking or getting captured, so removing that threat is dangerous.
3. Whether a legislature should mirror the population's demographics and life experiences, not just its policy preferences.
4. Whether the deeper problem with career politicians is that office gradually corrupts people (so rotation helps) or that elections select atypical, power-hungry people in the first place (so you'd want to change the selection mechanism, not just cap tenure).
5. Whether ordinary citizens who end up governing become more public-spirited and competent, or whether they get bored, parochial, or captured by staff and lobbyists.

The leaves I want at minimum: the professional-legislature defender; the term-limits reformer; the full lottocrat (some or all lawmaking by a random citizen body); the mixed-constitution person (an allotted chamber next to an elected one); and the advisory minipublics person (keep elections, use random panels as input or review). If the logical space spits out an extra leaf that seems honestly occupied—say, a "politics is a civic duty" person who wants both term limits and sortition—include it and name it as best you can.

Start from the logical space: lay out the questions and which combinations of answers are allowed, then locate the positions in it. For any cell in the space that's empty, say briefly why—incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any pair of leaves your tree can't pull apart, plus the question you'd need to add to split them.

Keep it readable for a general ideas-section audience, not philosophy grads—somewhere around five to eight leaves and four to six branch questions.
