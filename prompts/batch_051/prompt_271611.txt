I would rather not name the book; the author is a professional philosopher and the book is widely recommended, which is exactly why I want to know how much weight to put on its summary.

"The problem of moral luck, as formulated by Thomas Nagel and Bernard Williams, is the discovery that our practice of holding people responsible systematically conflicts with a principle we all accept: the control principle, that people can be morally assessed only for what is within their control. Nagel showed that this principle cannot be squared with the way we actually judge people. A driver who runs over a child after a moment's inattention is judged far more harshly than a driver guilty of the same inattention who happens to find the road empty, yet the only difference between their situations is a matter of chance. Since character, situation, and outcome are all ultimately matters of luck, Nagel concluded that moral responsibility is an illusion. Williams reached the same conclusion by a different route, arguing through the example of Gauguin, who abandoned his family to paint, that even our retrospective judgments of our own choices depend on the outcome of a gamble. The only ways to restore consistency would be to give up the control principle or to stop blaming people for the results of their actions, and most philosophers regard the first as unacceptable and the second as impossible."
