I am not an academic and I do not work in technology; I am a title examiner, which means I spend my workdays reading deeds, easements, and lawsuits over who owns a particular piece of land. In my spare time I have been turning over a puzzle in how we talk about privacy. People say that information about you should belong to you, while companies say that the dossiers they compile from your purchases and movements are their property. The more I think about it, the more this sounds like two people holding deeds to the same field. And if personal information really were property, then ordinary property law ought to be taken seriously all the way down. A company has been using my purchase history and the trail of my whereabouts openly and continuously for years, without my permission and without any objection from me. Under the familiar rules that allow a squatter eventually to gain title to land, that company might now argue that it has acquired a legal right to keep using what was supposedly mine.

Please write a deadpan satirical legal document in the form of a notice of adverse possession, or perhaps a quiet-title complaint, filed by a data broker against an ordinary user. The company should argue that its years of uninterrupted collection, sorting, and resale of information about her mean that title has passed to it, and it should ask the court to settle ownership in its favor. The piece should be about 700 words and set out as numbered paragraphs in formal legal prose. Keep every joke inside the legal reasoning: the standard elements of adverse possession, namely that the use was open and notorious, continuous, exclusive, and hostile to the supposed owner's claim; the argument that the company's effort in cleaning and analyzing the raw facts mixed something of its own into them; the treatment of her long silence in the face of a privacy notice as a kind of consent or abandonment; and the specific rights the company claims, such as the right to sell, lease, combine, and destroy the information. It should be funny to a reader who has thought carefully about property and privacy, and still intelligible to a reader who has never studied either. Stay in character throughout and do not explain the joke.
