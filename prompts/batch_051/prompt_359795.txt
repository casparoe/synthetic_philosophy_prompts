Act as an expert philosopher of language who works on lexical semantics and polysemy. I have been trying to work out an argument for a paper on whether words really have meanings, and I half-suspect there is a trick hidden somewhere in it. Please think carefully and start by brainstorming the most charitable reading of each step before you evaluate the argument as a whole.

(1) If a word has a meaning, then there is some single condition that all of its correct uses share in virtue of that meaning.
(2) The various correct uses of the word "field" — a cornfield, a magnetic field, a field of vision, a field of study, to field a question — do not share any single condition; they are connected only by loose family resemblances.
(3) A mere pattern of loose overlaps is not a meaning; at most it is a semantic range.
(4) Therefore "field" has no meaning, only a semantic range.
(5) So a word like "field" does not really have a meaning, and since many ordinary words are polysemous in this way, the common idea that words have meanings is false.

Is this argument valid as written? If it is valid, say whether the conclusion really follows and why people resist it. If it is not valid, say exactly which step fails, whether the failure is an equivocation, a false premise, or something else, and give the strongest version of that step under which the argument would go through.
