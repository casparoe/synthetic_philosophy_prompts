You are a metaphysician and philosopher of science. In his 1912 essay On the Notion of Cause, Bertrand Russell observes that the mature sciences, such as gravitational astronomy, have dropped causal vocabulary, and he suggests that the law of causality persists mainly as an outdated inheritance. A colleague has pressed Russell's observation into the following argument, whose conclusion I find excessive, but I cannot locate the mistake.

(1) The fundamental theories of physics state their laws without needing any causal vocabulary; they appeal to functional or probabilistic relations over physical magnitudes.
(2) If a theory's laws are complete with respect to some domain while using no causal vocabulary, then the phenomena in that domain are fully accounted for without causal relata.
(3) Fundamental physics is complete with respect to all physical phenomena.
(4) So all physical phenomena are fully accounted for without causal relata.
(5) If all physical phenomena are fully accounted for without causal relata, then there are no causal relata.
(6) If there are no causal relata, then higher-level causal claims, such as the claim that smoking caused her cancer, are not objectively true.
(7) Ordinary and special-science explanations require that at least some such causal claims be objectively true.
(8) Therefore, ordinary and special-science explanations are systematically non-objective — which seems to prove too much, since it would make explanation in biology and medicine a useful fiction and leave no principled line between genuine and illusory causal claims.

Where exactly does this argument go wrong, and what standard positions on the metaphysics of causation — eliminativism, reductionism, pluralism, or the view that causation is a real but non-fundamental relation — are priced into each possible response? In particular, which premise should be rejected if one wants to preserve the objective truth of at least some higher-level causal claims, and what does rejecting it cost? Take the argument step by step, explain why the tempting premises look plausible, and then give the diagnosis you find most defensible.
