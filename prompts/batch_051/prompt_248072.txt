OK so I spent most of last night going back and forth with my friend about whether heaven, even if it were real, would eventually just get boring, and I genuinely cant tell anymore whether either of us had a coherent position. He knows a bit of philosophy too. I opened with the old Williams/Makropulos line, and the arguement ended up going something like this (I'm cleaning up the moves a little, but this is the shape of it):

(1) Me: In heaven either we keep roughly the psychology we have now — finite, project-shaped, satiable — or we don't. If we do, infinite time means we eventually work through every project that engages us and repeat the good ones until they go stale. That's the point of the Makropulos case: the woman who gets centuries of life isn't crushed by tragedy, she's just terminally bored and indifferent. So eternal life is either boredom or requires changing us into something that isn't recognizably us.

(2) Him: That only works if you assume heaven is just ordinary life with a longer runtime. Heaven isn't supposed to be that; it's supposed to be union with God, or a perfected nature, where satiation doesn't set in the way it does now. You can't show the idea is incoherent by assuming a finite menu of desires, because the whole offer is that the menu gets expanded or replaced by an inexhaustible good.

(3) Me: But if the state is unchanging union with God, that's a single state — no narrative, no achievement, no variation — and a single state that never changes doesn't sound like something I'd actually want forever; it sounds like calling a pleasant sedation life. If instead heaven keeps giving new objects, the novelty problem comes back: after enough change, the person at the end isn't the same person who started, so no single individual lives the whole thing.

(4) Him: You're smuggling in scarcity. We re-read books and re-listen to pieces of music all the time, and the repetitions usually get better, not worse. If value doesn't get used up by repetition in a finite life, you don't have an argument that it must get used up in an infinite one. And if you say repetition only has weight against the background of death, then by that logic someone with a 200-year lifespan would care less about each hearing than a 20-year-old, which isn't obviously true.

(5) Me, a bit less sure: The difference is that in a finite life the repetitions are embedded in a life that is going somewhere — a self is being built — whereas unlimited time flattens the difference between going somewhere and going in circles. But honestly by this point I was reaching.
