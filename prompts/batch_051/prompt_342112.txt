There are two ways people seem to answer the question of who should get a say in a political decision. First: everyone whose life gets genuinely affected by the decision, even if they live outside the place making it. Second: only the people who are actually subject to that place's laws — they can be fined, forced to comply, arrested — because those are the ones on the hook. In normal cases these two groups mostly overlap, and thats exactly what I want to stress test.

Build me a single ordinary scenario — a real-feeling town or city, nothing sci fi, no invented countries — where one political decision gives us two people:

Person A: the decision hits their interests hard, but they are not subject to the laws of the body making it. So the first principle says they get a say and the second says no.

Person B: the decision barely touches their interests at all, but they are clearly subject to the laws of the body making it. So the second principle says they get a say and the first says no.

Person B should be someone a real city actually has power over — can stop them, fine them, make them comply — not a weird edge case like a spy or a castaway. Keep the scenario to one paragraph if you can.

Then walk me through it. State each principle in your own words and explain the verdict it gives for A and for B, and why. And be straight about the weak spots: how would someone who holds the second principle defend leaving Person A out without also kicking out Person B, and how would someone who holds the first principle handle Person B without saying every person who ever drives through town gets a vote. If the honest answer is that one of those defenses fails, say so and say what concession that forces.
