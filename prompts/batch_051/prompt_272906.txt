I've got a friend who has read way too much Locke on property and original acquisition, and she argues about it the way other people argue about sport. I've been keeping track of her verdicts because she swears she's running one consistent principle, but I can never quite pin the principle down. Her track record so far: she picks wild apples from a common tree and says they're hers once she's picked them; someone walks the boundary of an unimproved tract and posts signs, and she says he owns nothing; someone dumps a can of tomato juice into the ocean so as to mix his labour with the sea, and she says he owns no part of the ocean; someone clears and farms a small corner of a common meadow, and she says it's his, since there's plenty of just-as-good meadow left for everyone else; someone takes and farms the last arable valley in a district while leaving the other inhabitants without land and worse off, and she says he gets no title; and someone harvests grain and lets half of it rot in a barn while neighbours go hungry, and she says his title to the rotted half fails.

The new case is one a lecturer raised. A woman spends years draining the last common marsh in a district, after which there are no comparable commons left, and turns it into highly productive wheat fields. The villagers used to cut reeds in the marsh; now they can't, and they miss it, but she gives them enough free wheat that by any ordinary measure they're better off than before. Has she acquired the former marsh? Tell me what my friend would say, work out which principle or principles actually fit her track record, say which old case is the closest analog (I keep swinging between the small-corner-of-meadow case and the last-arable-valley case), and if her track record really underdetermines the answer, say what principle she'd be committing herself to with each verdict and what further case would cleanly tell those principles apart.
