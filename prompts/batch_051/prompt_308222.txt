Lange's idea was that socialism could keep the good parts of markets without private owners: the state owns the firms, but a central planning board sets prices by trial and error — raise a price when the good is scarce, lower it when there's a glut — and managers are told to produce where marginal cost equals price and given bonuses for doing that. I got into an argument with a friend about whether this would work. I'd put my credence at about 0.6 that the system gets roughly close to a normal market economy; he's at like 0.2, and we couldn't figure out where our disagreement actually lives.

So here's what I want. Take this thought experiment: in 2025 a country tries Lange's market socialism exactly as I described, and there are no actual markets for capital goods and no private owners earning profits. What is your credence that 20 years later this country is at least 90% as productive per worker as an otherwise-similar country that kept ordinary markets? I want the number, but mostly I want the reasoning decomposed into its parts. Give me rough probabilities and a sentence of justification for each of these: (a) the Hayek point, that too much of the knowledge needed to allocate resources is local, tacit, and dispersed for board-set prices to capture; (b) the incentive point, that state-employed managers will hide slack and won't actually follow the marginal-cost rule even if their bonuses depend on it; (c) the speed/accuracy point, that a board can adjust prices fast and accurate enough from observed shortages and surpluses; (d) the Mises point, that without real markets and profit-and-loss there is no meaningful capital accounting, and this is a separate problem from (a)–(c); and (e) the measurement point, that "90% as productive per worker" is too vague to be a real target.
