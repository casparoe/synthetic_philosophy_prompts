I've come around to thinking futarchy is basically the right answer to governance: vote on a welfare metric, then let prediction markets pick whichever policy maximizes that metric in expectation. The aggregation story feels obviously correct to me, as someone who works with systems that are good exactly when they compress lots of noisy inputs into a few continuously-updated outputs, and putting real money on it just fixes the incentive mess in voting and comment sections. But I want to know what would break it. What would have to be true for futarchy to be wrong, or at least importantly worse than ordinary democracy? Give me the case against sorted by how decisive it is. Include things that are arguably already observable (manipulation, actual prediction-market weirdness, metric gaming) and more formal worries — especialy the gap between a market being well calibrated about each candidate policy and a market being good at selecting the best policy, since argmax over noisy conditional expectations can fail. Which of these would a committed futarchy defender themselves grant as counting against the view, and which do they just dodge by qualifying the claim ('given a decent metric and liquid markets')? Is that qualification making the view unfalsifiable, and is that a bug or a feature? I want a committed verdict on how exposed futarchy actually is, not a balanced menu.
