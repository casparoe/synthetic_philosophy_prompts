I recently retired from a career in the natural sciences and have begun working through the foundations of decision theory. I keep meeting the Allais problem, and I should like to see it set out as a numbered argument, because I suspect the discussion usually moves too quickly over the step at which the reasoning does its controversial work.

Consider a person who is offered two choices on separate occasions.

Choice 1: between
A: £1,000,000 with certainty; and
B: £5,000,000 with probability 0.10, £1,000,000 with probability 0.89, and nothing with probability 0.01.

Choice 2: between
C: £1,000,000 with probability 0.11 and nothing with probability 0.89; and
D: £5,000,000 with probability 0.10 and nothing with probability 0.90.

The common empirical claim is that most people strictly prefer A to B in Choice 1 and, of those same people, most strictly prefer D to C in Choice 2. Take one person who exhibits this pattern, and consider the following argument.

(1) The person strictly prefers A to B, and strictly prefers D to C.
(2) Let L be the degenerate lottery that pays £1,000,000 for certain; M the lottery that pays £5,000,000 with probability 10/11 and nothing with probability 1/11; N the degenerate lottery that pays £1,000,000 for certain; and N' the degenerate lottery that pays nothing for certain.
(3) A is equivalent to the compound lottery 0.11L + 0.89N; B is equivalent to 0.11M + 0.89N; C is equivalent to 0.11L + 0.89N'; and D is equivalent to 0.11M + 0.89N'.
(4) Extensionality: the person ranks a gamble in the same way whichever mathematically equivalent form it is presented in.
(5) The strict independence axiom states: for any lotteries X and Y, any lottery Z, and any probability p with 0 < p < 1, X is strictly preferred to Y if and only if the compound lottery pX + (1 - p)Z is strictly preferred to pY + (1 - p)Z.
(6) From (1), (3), and (4), 0.11L + 0.89N is strictly preferred to 0.11M + 0.89N.
(7) From (6) and the right-to-left direction of (5), L is strictly preferred to M.
(8) From (7) and the left-to-right direction of (5), applied with Z = N', 0.11L + 0.89N' is strictly preferred to 0.11M + 0.89N'; hence, by (3) and (4), C is strictly preferred to D.
(9) But (1) says the same person strictly prefers D to C, which contradicts (8).
(10) Therefore the person's preferences violate the independence axiom.
(11) A set of preferences that violates the independence axiom is irrational.
(12) Therefore the person's pair of choices is irrational.

My question is this. Is the argument valid as it stands? If it is not valid, identify the exact step at which it fails and state the corrected step. If it is valid through step 10, identify the premise that a defender of the ordinary choice pattern should reject, and explain precisely what is at stake in rejecting it. I should particularly like to know whether there is any technical slip earlier than step 11, in the move from the two observed choices to the claim that the independence axiom has been violated.
