Analyse the Mytilenean episode of 427 BC—the Athenian assembly’s initial vote to put the adult male population of Mytilene to death and enslave the women and children, followed the next day by a reconsideration in which the assembly narrowly reversed itself and dispatched a second trireme that arrived just in time to stop the massacre—through the lens of the Condorcet jury theorem. Treat the theorem as a diagnostic model rather than a justification: it claims that a large group of decision-makers, each with a better-than-chance probability of choosing correctly, will tend to track the truth more reliably than any single member, provided their votes are sincere and sufficiently independent. Respond in British English as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. The five items should be: (1) what the relevant ‘correct outcome’ was in the first vote and then in the second, and whether the question before the assembly was identical on the two days; (2) how well the independence and competence conditions of the theorem held in each vote, distinguishing the information the assembly possessed from the information supplied by Cleon’s and Diodotus’ speeches; (3) whether the reversal itself supports or undercuts the theorem, given that the second vote was taken by substantially the same body of citizens; (4) what the episode reveals about the role of anger, overnight delay, and oratorical framing in weakening or restoring the theorem’s preconditions; and (5) whether the Mytilene case should make us more or less confident that large democratic assemblies have truth-tracking properties, and what that implies for the standard charge that Athenian democracy was mob-like rather than deliberative.
