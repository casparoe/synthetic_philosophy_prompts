At the start of its treatment of number, Euclid's Elements gives two foundational definitions (Book VII, Definitions 1 and 2, in Heath's translation):

"A unit is that by virtue of which each of the things that exist is called one."
"A number is a multitude composed of units."

I find the first definition illuminating but also suspect it may be circular: it tells me what a unit does by invoking the concept of being one, which sounds like exactly the thing I wanted analyzed. I would like you to do two things. First, interpret the definition carefully: what relation among unit, oneness, and existing things is Euclid marking off, and does his account of number presuppose a more basic concept of unity that it does not itself explain? Second, turn the definition into a working definition of a unit precise enough to classify the following cases. For each case, say whether it is a unit, a number of units, both, or neither, and by which sense of "unit" you are classifying it:

(a) a single apple;
(b) a carton of a dozen eggs, sold as one carton;
(c) a married couple;
(d) a corporation;
(e) a pile of sand;
(f) a line segment exactly one meter long;
(g) the number 1 itself;
(h) one half;
(i) a twelve-bar musical phrase, performed as one phrase.

Then say whether these cases force you to distinguish more than one concept answering to the word "unit," and whether Euclid's two definitions still do the work he needed them to do once those distinctions are drawn. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the definition you propose, and single out the case or point on which you are least sure.
