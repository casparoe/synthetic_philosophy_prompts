Having retired from a career in the physical sciences, I have lately been reading about games at the boundary between mathematics and philosophy, and one argument has been nagging at me. The strategy-stealing argument for the board game Hex is usually presented as an elegant proof that the first player has a winning strategy, although the proof never actually exhibits the strategy. I find the argument ingenious but slightly disconcerting, since it seems to deliver something for nothing, and I half-suspect that one of its steps is carrying more weight than is openly acknowledged. I would be grateful for a careful verdict.

The argument, as I reconstruct it, runs thus. (1) Hex is a finite, two-player, perfect-information game in which draws are impossible; hence, by the standard backward-induction theorem for such games, either the first or the second player must possess a winning strategy. (2) Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that the second player possesses one. (3) The first player may then make an arbitrary opening move and thereafter adopt the second player's supposed strategy: treating her opening stone as a harmless extra stone of her own colour, she plays on each later turn where that strategy would have the second player play—mentally interchanging the two colours and their respective sides—and if the indicated cell is already occupied, she plays in any vacant cell. (4) In Hex, an extra stone of one's own colour can never be a disadvantage, since stones are neither moved nor removed and a stone can only add to the possible connecting paths available to its owner. (5) Therefore, if the second player had a winning strategy, the first player could win as well, which is absurd; so the second player has no winning strategy, and by (1) the first player must have one.

What unsettles me is that the same general pattern appears to prove too much. If the reasoning worked simply in virtue of the features cited, one would expect it to show that the first player can force a win in any finite, drawless game, which is plainly not how games behave in general. I should like to know whether the argument is valid exactly as stated, and if not, exactly which step fails and how it ought to be revised. If the argument is valid, I should like the diagnosis stated with equal precision: which step or steps are doing the real work, why they hold in Hex, and what a game would have to be like for the argument to break down. I would also appreciate the answer itself being written in British English.
