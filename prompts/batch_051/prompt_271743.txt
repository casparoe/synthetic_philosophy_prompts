I'm taking a philosophy of language course, and we've been spending a while on family resemblance — the idea that a category like 'game' doesn't have one essence shared by every member, just a network of overlapping similarities, and the boundaries are partly up to us. At the same time I've been reading about the Letter Spirit project from AI/cognitive science, where the puzzle is what makes every 'a' an 'a'. The setup is that you're designing letters for a new typeface on a grid, and there are two constraints that can pull in opposite directions: letter-spirit, meaning each glyph has to stay recognizable as its particular letter no matter how much the style changes, and font-spirit, meaning each glyph has to look like it belongs with the other 25 letters in this font. What struck me is that this is basically the category problem from my philosophy class made visible. Font-spirit looks like family resemblance applied to a style, and letter-spirit looks like the claim that there's a fixed core underneath all the stylistic variation after all.

But family resemblance is supposed to show that there isn't a core below the similarities, whereas letter design seems to need one (or at least a functional anchor: the glyph still has to be readable as an 'a'). So I can't tell whether I've found a deep structural parallel or whether I'm just pattern-matching. If the parallel is real, what exactly would have to be true on both sides for anything to transfer, and what does it predict about edge cases that might fail — like a glyph most people can read as an 'a' but that any designer would say has lost the letter-spirit, or a style that drifts so far from its family that new letters stop feeling like they're in the same font? I honestly can't tell if its a real analogy or just a nice picture.
