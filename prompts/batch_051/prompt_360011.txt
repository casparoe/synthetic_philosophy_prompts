In recent reading in the philosophy of perception, I have repeatedly encountered a view whose name and exact commitments I cannot pin down. As I understand it, the view holds that an episode in which one genuinely sees a lemon and a hallucination with the same apparent content are states of fundamentally different kinds, sharing no common mental element; the genuine perception is partly constituted by the lemon itself, while the hallucination is not. It is usually contrasted with the claim that the two episodes have a common nature, but I do not know what the positive view is called. More importantly, I would like to know how the strongest version of the view explains the subject's inability to tell the two episodes apart without covertly reintroducing a common element. Does the view draw a clean boundary in cases of successful but inaccurate perception—for instance, genuinely seeing a straight stick partly immersed in water while it appears bent? If such cases fit awkwardly on either side, is that a reason to reintroduce a common element after all? I am interested less in the name than in how the boundary is drawn and where the position incurs its main costs.
