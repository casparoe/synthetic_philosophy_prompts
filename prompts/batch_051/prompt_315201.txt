I have been trying, on my own, to think through an old dispute between Mencius and Xunzi about whether human nature is good or bad, and I keep getting stuck on how a newer kind of evidence fits in. Mencius, as I understand him, holds that human nature already contains the beginnings of virtue: a person who suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well feels a jolt of alarm and compassion before any thought of reputation or reward, and that untaught reaction is the sprout from which full virtue can be cultivated. Xunzi holds the opposite. He thinks our raw material is self-seeking appetite and feeling, and that everything good in us — propriety, restraint, fairness — is an artifact, invented by sages and then imposed on us through teaching and practice, the way a piece of wood is steamed and bent into a wheel.

Now add a development neither thinker could have had in view. Developmental psychology has produced a body of results suggesting that preverbal infants, before they can have been taught very much, already tell helpful agents apart from hindering agents and prefer the helpful ones, and they show early forms of sympathetic response. Call this the early-moral-discrimination finding, without tying it to any particular study.

What I want to know is whether Xunzi's argument survives this development. Which premise in his argument does the finding actually put pressure on? Can he absorb the finding by saying that an infant's preference is just another untaught inclination and not yet the cultivated goodness he meant, or does the finding break his claim that goodness must be planted from outside? And does the finding strengthen Mencius, or does it only show something he was not really arguing about, since a looking-time preference is a long way from the virtue he thought takes a lifetime of cultivation? I want a verdict on the argument, not a summary of infant psychology.

- "strongest_xunzi_reply": the best way Xunzi could absorb or answer the finding, in one or two sentences.
