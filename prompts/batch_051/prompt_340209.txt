I clerk for a small confessional Presbyterian denomination, and we have spent the better part of two years deadlocked in a committee of the church court. Two study committees were charged with exactly the same task: to determine, from Scripture alone, the denomination's position on a disputed question. Both committees, working independently, used the same large language model to search the Bible and propose the other passages that speak more clearly, which all of us are committed to consulting before settling a controversy. The model surfaced largely the same verses to both committees; the two committees read those verses in opposite directions and returned incompatible reports, each insisting that the other has mistaken an obscure passage for the clear one.

The standard both committees say they are applying is the Westminster Confession of Faith, Chapter 1, sections 9 and 10, which is printed in our constitution as follows:

9. The infallible rule of interpretation of Scripture is the Scripture itself: and therefore, when there is a question about the true and full sense of any Scripture (which is not manifold, but one), it must be searched and known by other places that speak more clearly.

10. The supreme judge by which all controversies of religion are to be determined, and all decrees of councils, opinions of ancient writers, doctrines of men, and private spirits, are to be examined, and in whose sentence we are to rest, can be no other but the Holy Spirit speaking in the Scripture.

The assembly is now asking which committee, if either, has been resting in the sentence identified by section 10 rather than in a private spirit. The party that favors the model says it is only a fast concordance, doing in seconds what section 9 commands; the party that opposes it says the model is itself a human doctrine or private spirit of the kind section 10 lists, and that a ranking of parallels produced by statistical prediction over a corpus is not the Holy Spirit speaking in the Scripture. Each side accuses the other of having smuggled in a private judge, and each side invokes these same clauses to defend its accusation. The instrument we adopted to end interpretive regresses now seems to be the very thing the regress passes through.

I am not asking you to settle the immediate question about artificial intelligence, and I am not looking for a pastoral compromise. I want to know whether the Confession's rule can actually terminate a disagreement like this, or whether it merely moves the question up one level. On the words of sections 9 and 10; on their evident purpose; on what the Westminster divines would most plausibly have said if shown a machine that generates probable continuations of Scripture; and on what a reasonable present-day confessional reader would take the two clauses to require or permit: is there any reading on which the two committees are not symmetrically placed, and what would one side have to establish, using only the Confession's own principles, to show that the other has ceased to be ruled by Scripture at all?
