I have been reading about predictive processing, and in a well-reviewed popular neuroscience book I encountered a chapter that summarizes the free energy principle and active inference. I have tried to reconstruct the core of the passage from memory, and it goes roughly like this:

"The brain is a prediction machine sealed inside the skull. All it ever receives are noisy, ambiguous sensory signals, so it maintains a generative model: an internal simulation of the hidden causes in the world that produce those signals. The free energy principle says that any self-organizing system must minimize variational free energy, which is an upper bound on surprise, and for the brain this means minimizing the mismatch between the model's top-down predictions and the bottom-up sensory signal. Perception is the process of driving that prediction error down to zero, which is why it is more accurate to say that we experience our model rather than the world itself: perception is a kind of constrained hallucination, with sensory input merely nudging the model when errors grow too large. Action is just another route to the same end: rather than issuing commands to achieve goals, the brain predicts what its muscles should feel and moves until the proprioceptive signals match that prediction. An implication is that living systems will seek out calm, predictable environments where their internal models are most often confirmed."

From the more technical literature I have skimmed, this looks mostly right but also slightly too strong. In particular, I have seen people emphasize precision weighting — the idea that not every prediction error is treated as equally informative — and a distinction between accuracy and model complexity that seems missing from the summary. The phrase "driving prediction error down to zero" also sounds as if it would lead to hallucination or complete freezing rather than normal perception. But the chapter was written by a working scientist and is widely recommended, so I do not trust my amateur suspicions. Could you go through the passage claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful defender of predictive processing, active inference, or the free energy principle would accept, which parts they would qualify or reject, and whether the final implication about calm, predictable environments follows from the parts that survive correction? I would especially like to know where a formulation that is harmless as popularization becomes a substantive misstatement of the view.
