I teach a six-week graduate seminar for social scientists on the philosophy of scientific modeling, and next week's single fifty-minute session is on Wigner's puzzle: why does mathematics, developed for its own sake, turn out later to fit the empirical world so well? My students are fluent with statistical models but know little physics, and most arrive thinking the question is either trivial (we built the math to fit, so of course it fits) or already settled by survivorship bias (the failures just are not in the textbooks). Plan how I should teach that session, rather than the explanation itself. What has to be in place before the puzzle can strike them as real rather than as a category mistake? Choose one carrying example that does the work, and say what it must possess and what it must avoid for this audience; I had considered the conic sections later becoming planetary orbits, but I worry it invites exactly the hindsight reading I want them to resist, so either defend that choice or replace it. Map the misconceptions in the order they will surface—the tool analogy first, then selection effects, then the thought that anything can be formalized if one tries hard enough—and tell me the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. Conclude with what I should explicitly refuse to settle in fifty minutes, and what closing question would tell me whether they have grasped the puzzle itself rather than learned to repeat a diagnosis.
