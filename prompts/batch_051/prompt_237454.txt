You are a philosopher of machine learning who advises a private foundation, and the foundation is deciding how to allocate three years of funding to a small theory group. The group's mandate is conceptual work on legible AI, understood like this: a system is legible to an audience to the extent that the audience can understand the system's output, or the basis for it, well enough to predict, verify, correct, and responsibly delegate to the system. The foundation is not funding empirical machine learning; it wants concepts, frameworks, and research agendas that would tell empirical work what to aim at. The group can be three people, drawn from philosophy and theoretically minded ML, and it cannot run large experiments.

To make the terrain concrete, here are some live fault lines around which the group might organize. Some people mean mechanistic transparency by legibility: a human can trace the actual computation. Others mean narrative understandability: the model gives a faithful enough account of what it did and why, even when the account is not the literal computation. Still others mean verifiability: the model works inside a formal or procedural standard such that a cheap check can certify the answer. A second fault line is where legibility lives. If one trains a model to produce self-explanations, legibility looks like an output; if one constrains the model to reason in a formal language, legibility looks like a property of the training regime; if one optimizes the model to answer questions a human overseer can assess quickly, legibility looks like a fact about the overseer and the interface rather than about the model alone. A third fault line is the relation between legibility and competence. Suppose the most capable systems turn out to be systematically the least legible: is that a conflict to be managed, evidence that legibility is being measured in the wrong way, or a reason to prefer weaker but more legible systems for high-stakes work? A fourth fault line concerns whether legibility can be a training target at all. Can a model be rewarded for being understandable without merely being rewarded for sounding plausible, and would a model optimized for legibility thereby learn to be honest or learn to fake understanding?

Do not survey these fault lines. Please think carefully and deliver a defended agenda. Which two or three open conceptual questions should this three-person group spend its three years on, in what order, and why? For each question, explain what makes it upstream: which other questions, practical decisions, or technical research programs would an answer to it settle or reframe. Also explain how tractable it is for philosophers and theoretically minded ML researchers rather than engineers running large experiments, and how neglected it is relative to the attention current interpretability and safety work gives it. Identify at least one candidate question that looks appealing but that you would not prioritize, and say why: overrated, intractable for this group, or likely to be settled by empirical work regardless of what this group does. Finally, name two or three technical or empirical developments in machine learning over the next two years that would most change your ranking, and explain how they would change it.

Begin by brainstorming several candidate questions and the criteria you will use to weigh them; then present your final prioritized agenda with an explicit defense of the ordering.
