Consider two familiar patterns in price-control policy.

First, minimum-wage law. A statute sets a general wage floor, but it permits a lower cash wage for tipped workers on the assumption that their tips make up the difference; a lower wage for workers with disabilities in specified employment programs; and a reduced wage for young workers during their first months of employment.

Second, rent stabilization. An ordinance caps the annual increase in rent for sitting tenants, but exempts newly constructed buildings from the caps and allows the rent to reset to market level when a unit becomes vacant.

In each case the policy presents itself as protecting a vulnerable party from unequal bargaining power, yet in each case the protection is deliberately withheld from certain categories of persons or transactions. Work out what conception of the point of a price control each design presupposes. For the minimum wage, which candidate purpose — maintaining a baseline standard of living, ensuring a non-exploitative exchange, expressing that certain work is worth at least this much, or correcting monopsony — best explains the precise pattern of carve-outs, as opposed to merely fitting the general idea of a wage floor? For rent stabilization, do vacancy decontrol and the new-construction exemption presuppose the same conception of fair price and legitimate expectation, or a different one? Be explicit about which features of each policy strictly presuppose a particular conception and which are compatible with several rival conceptions, and say whether a single theory can make both patterns coherent at once or whether the two policies quietly embody different norms under a shared vocabulary. Finally, describe how each policy would have to change if it were rebuilt on a pure anti-poverty rationale, and how it would have to change if rebuilt on a pure anti-exploitation rationale; indicate which current carve-outs survive under each reconstruction and which become indefensible.
