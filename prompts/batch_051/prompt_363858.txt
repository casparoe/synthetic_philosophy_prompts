Act as a professional philosopher specializing in philosophy of mind and free will. Think this through carefully — maybe start by brainstorming two or three candidate definitions and stress-testing them before you commit.

I'm a software engineer, not trained in philosophy, and I got pulled into an argument about whether humans are any more free than the autonomous systems I work on. I keep trying to formalize voluntary action as if it's a spec for an agent, and I'm going in circles. People use voluntary, free, and chosen as if they're interchangeable, but I want one working definition of voluntary action — or free decision, if that's the better target — that actually sorts the following cases:

1. A reflex: your leg kicks when a doctor taps your kneecap.
2. A genuine choice: you spend a week weighing two job offers, lay out the tradeoffs, and finally pick one.
3. A chess engine that searches millions of positions and outputs a move. There's a decision procedure, but most people would say it didn't voluntarily do anything.
4. The Libet case: a subject in a lab watches a fast-moving clock and is told to flex their wrist whenever they feel like it, then reports where the clock was when they first noticed the urge. The EEG shows a readiness potential starting in the motor cortex a few hundred milliseconds before the reported urge, and around half a second before the muscle actually moves.
5. A post-hypnotic suggestion: you were told under hypnosis that when you hear someone say window, you'll stand up and open one; afterwards, when asked why, you give a plausible reason about the room being stuffy.
6. A smoker who has resolved to quit, spends a minute arguing with themselves, and then lights another cigarette, describing it afterwards as deciding to have one more.
7. Handing over your wallet at gunpoint: you really don't want to, and you choose to comply.
8. The Libet veto: in some versions of the experiment, subjects say they can abort a planned movement at the last moment, after they feel the urge starting.

My rough intuitions: 1 is clearly not voluntary, 2 clearly is, and 3 probably isn't an action in the relevant sense, but I want the definition to explain why rather than just assert it. The rest I slide around on. I can argue myself into either answer for 4, 5, and 8, and I'm not sure whether 6 and 7 are voluntary-but-compromised or something else entirely.

Propose the definition, run it through all eight seperately, and tell me where it surprises you or forces a revision. If your definition says the Libet case isn't voluntary, I want to know what that does to the job-offer case, since both presumably run on the same neural machinery. If it says it is voluntary, tell me why the readiness-potential timing shouldn't bother me. I care more about the definition doing honest work than about any particular verdict.
