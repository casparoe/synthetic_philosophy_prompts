Two people in my giving circle got into it over where to send a few thousand dollars we raised. (1) Noah argued for a malaria-net charity: the randomized evidence is strong, the cost per life saved is low, and at least you can tell you did something. (2) Priya said that's just bailing out a leaking boat. Kids die of malaria because the health system and the economy are broken; a dollar that shifts institutions or policy buys a small chance of fixing the leak, and that could swamp a mountain of individual nets. (3) Noah replied that 'small chance' is doing all the work there: the chance is unknown, and the sign of the effect is unknown too, so its hard to say a systemic bet helps at all. A verified marginal gain for one actual person beats an unmeasurable maybe for nobody in particular. (4) Priya called that measurability bias. We're grading interventions by how easy they are to measure, not how much good they do; on Noah's standard we'd never have backed abolitionist organizing or public health campaigns before their effects showed up in trials, and those plausibly did more good than any pile of bednets. (5) Noah answered that for every systemic win there are a hundred advocacy campaigns that flamed out or backfired, and without evidence you can't tell which bucket you're in. So the burden of proof should sit with the person claiming the huge unproven effect, not the person offering the verified small one. Then they both looked at me and asked me to settle it. Can you go through the five moves, rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and then say which position is more defensible overall and what the two of them are missing?
