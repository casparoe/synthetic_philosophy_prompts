I need someone to settle an argument I've been having with my housemate about omissions and causes. We agree on the obvious cases: if a parent doesn't feed their baby and it dies, the parent's not-feeding caused the death, and a hired lifeguard who just watches a swimmer drown also caused the drowning. We also agree on the opposite end: the fact that a stranger in another country didn't send money to that baby's family isnt usually treated as a cause of the death, and neither is every voter who didn't demand more foreign aid treated as a cause of a particular famine death. But all of those are omissions, so just pointing to inaction can't be what distinguishes them.

So what I want is a working definition of when an omission counts as a cause. It should count the parent, the hired lifeguard, and a trucking company that skips a mandatory brake inspection before a crash as causes. It should not count the faraway non-donor or the non-demanding taxpayer as causes. And it should give a definate answer either way for the cases my housemate and I keep splitting on: a random bystander who sees a child drowning and doesn't jump in; a doctor who withholds a ventilator from one patient because it's already keeping another patient alive; a homeowner who doesn't clear ice off their sidewalk and someone falls; and a maintenance report that says the absence of a cotter pin caused a plane crash, even though an absence isn't a thing that does anything. Bonus if the definition explains why some omissions feel like real causes while others just read as background conditions, and whether that cause/background distinction is doing genuine work or just tracking who we happen to blame.

And whatever definition you land on, tell me what would change your mind about it: which hard case or argument would make you revise it, or where you think the definition is most likely to break.
