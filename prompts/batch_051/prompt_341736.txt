I'm taking a course called "What is Life?" (cross-listed between philosophy and molecular bio), and we've spent most of the term on definitions and borderline cases rather than settling on one. This week my lab section ran into something that feels exactly like the readings.

Our biosafety manual has a one-paragraph policy, and I'm copying the relevant part exactly:

"No experiment involving the creation, modification, or use of a living organism may begin without prior review by the Biosafety Committee. For the purposes of this policy, a living organism is an entity capable of metabolism, growth, and reproduction."

The manual hasn't been revised since 2011, and it's obviously written with E. coli, yeast, tissue culture, and whole animals in mind.

A postdoc in the lab, Ana, has been building what she calls a protocell: a lipid vesicle containing a small set of molecules that catalyze each other's production. It takes up precursors from the surrounding medium and turns them into more of its own membrane and internal components, so the vesicle grows. Once it gets big enough, it becomes unstable and splits into two or three smaller vesicles. There is no nucleic acid in it, no genes, nothing like a sequence that gets copied — the only thing "inherited" is the overall composition of the vesicle. It's cycled for dozens of generations in a test tube as long as precursors keep being supplied. If they're not supplied, it just sits there.

Ana says it isn't a "living organism" under the policy. Her line: this isn't metabolism in the ordinary biological sense — it's not a cell doing controlled oxidation or harvesting usable energy, it's just a thermodynamically favorable coupled reaction network — and "reproduction" usually means copying heritable instructions, which doesn't happen here; it's a droplet getting big and fragmenting. She also says the authors plainly didn't have protocells in mind, and the purpose of the clause is to catch dangerous self-replicating biological agents, which this isn't.

Ben, another postdoc, says she's rules-lawyering. The thing grows, it reproduces (more vesicles arise from one), and the coupled reaction network is a crude metabolism even if it isn't oxidative phosphorylation. The policy's authors offered a three-part defintion, and definitions don't come with a hidden "plus whatever we're familar with" clause. He also says her move from "the authors didn't imagine it" to "the policy doesn't reach it" is just a guess about their surprise, and if the committee wanted the policy to track dangerousness or familiar model organisms, they should have written that.

I honestly can't sort out who's right.
