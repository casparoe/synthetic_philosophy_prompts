I retired from a career in the natural sciences not long ago and have since been working through philosophical questions about modality and probability. The following argument is one I wrote out for my own notes; it appears to prove far too much, but I have been unable to pin down where it fails. A precise diagnosis would help me more than a general characterization.

(1) Wherever an event has a well-defined objective probability, the event is genuinely possible just in case that probability is greater than zero. If the probability is zero, the event cannot happen; if it is positive, the event can happen.

(2) Let E be any genuinely possible repeatable event, with single-trial probability p greater than zero. If independent trials of the same kind are run indefinitely, the probability that E occurs at least once in the first n trials is 1 − (1 − p)^n.

(3) Since 0 < p ≤ 1, the quantity 1 − (1 − p)^n converges to 1 as n grows without bound. Thus, in an infinite sequence of such trials, the probability that E fails to occur even once is zero.

(4) By the zero-probability clause in (1), an event with probability zero cannot happen. Therefore E's failing to occur even once in the infinite sequence is impossible.

(5) Consequently, for every genuinely possible repeatable event, an infinite sequence of independent trials makes its nonoccurrence impossible. Every possible event, no matter how small its single-trial probability, must occur. What can happen must happen, and the numerical size of a probability becomes modally idle: a one-in-a-billion event and a one-in-two event end up equally necessary in the long run.

(6) I cannot accept this. Consider a fair coin tossed infinitely many times. The event that every toss lands heads has probability zero, so by the same principle it would be impossible. But nothing in the setup rules out a run of heads that never ends; each toss can come up heads, and no fixed rule forbids their all doing so. It seems wrong to call that sequence impossible merely because its probability is zero.

I would rather give up (5), and whatever in (1) generates it, than give up the limit calculation in (2)–(3). But I want to know exactly what that costs. Which step carries the error, and is one direction of the biconditional in (1) more at fault than the other? Once the argument is repaired, what is the cleanest account of how possibility and probability are related? And does probability retain any modal authority after the repair — that is, can I still treat a smaller probability as a weaker license for possibility and a zero probability as a merely fallible sign of impossibility, or must something like (1) be kept in a restricted form?
