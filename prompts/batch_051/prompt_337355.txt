I am a PhD student working on mechanistic interpretability for large language models, and I have been stuck on a methodological question that sits somewhere between engineering and philosophy of science. In our lab, some people claim that when an interpreter model (or a human using interpretability tooling) attributes a specific computation to a target model — for example, that a particular attention head encodes a feature, that a circuit implements a subgoal, or that the model's output was caused by a particular pathway — the interpreter can be genuinely right about the target's internal operation. The skeptic's reply is that such attributions are, at best, compressed summaries of input–output behavior: they may predict the target's outputs and even survive some interventions, while still being post hoc rationalizations that do not recover anything like the real computation.

I want to know whether that dispute is empirically decidable, and if so, what the strongest test would look like. Design the best experiment (or experimental program) you can for the claim that an interpreter's explanation of a target model is a faithful account of the target's internal causal structure, rather than merely a predictively adequate model of its input–output mapping. What result would count in favor, what result would count against, and how would a positive result fail to distinguish "the interpreter has recovered the target's actual computation" from "the interpreter has learned a useful causal model that passes all the same behavioral checks"? Be specific about what intervention-based evidence — for example, ablating or patching the components the interpreter identifies, and comparing the target's resulting behavior with what the explanation predicts — would establish, and where its force runs out. Finally, say whether anything changes when a second interpreter is asked to interpret the first interpreter: does recursive interpretability make the original problem easier to solve, or just relocate it?
