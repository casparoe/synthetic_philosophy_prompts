I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I am writing a scene that has to survive contact with readers who know the territory. In it, a character of mine—a philosopher who studies the ethics and sociology of work—is asked to speak for about forty minutes at a community meeting in a manufacturing town where the main plant is being automated. The room is a mix of soon-to-be-displaced workers, their families, a few managers, and a local journalist; they are wary, intelligent, and not academic. I do not want you to write the character's lecture, and I do not want the scene's dialogue. I want the pedagogical architecture beneath it, the thing a real teacher would have thought through before opening their mouth.

Act as a professional philosopher with substantial teaching experience and a feel for the ways lay audiences mishear arguments about work and technology. Think through the problem slowly before you answer, and begin by brainstorming two or three candidate examples you considered for carrying the explanation and telling me why you rejected them.

The idea the character is trying to land is roughly this: what automation threatens is not only income but a set of goods that jobs happen to deliver rather badly—obligations you did not choose, a legible place in a hierarchy, witnesses to your effort, a reason to get dressed and go somewhere—and the familiar reassurances about retraining and income support repair the first loss while leaving the second mostly untouched. The character wants the audience to leave distinguishing those two losses, not to be converted to either the cheerfully post-work or the doom-laden view.

Please give me, in continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write me a careful letter—the following: what must be established first and what depends on it, so each move in the explanation feels earned; the single example or concrete situation that should carry the whole thing, what features it must have, and which obvious near-miss examples I should keep the character away from and why; the misconceptions this particular room will walk in with and the further misconceptions the explanation itself will create, and the exact point in the sequence where the character should head each one off; the one question the character should ask near the end to find out whether the distinction has actually landed; and what the character should explicitly refuse to settle at that level, so the scene does not curdle into a manifesto.
