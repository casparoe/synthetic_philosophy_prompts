You're an impartial facilitator with a background in decision theory and behavioural economics. I want you to draft the joint statement for an adversarial collaboration between two people who have been arguing about the Allais paradox for months:

- One side defends the expected-utility orthodoxy and treats the independence axiom as a normatively binding requirement on rational preference.
- The other side defends non-expected-utility modelling (probability weighting, the certainty effect) and treats the Allais pattern as rationally permissible rather than a bias.

The collaboration isn't about deciding a winner. I want the document they could both sign after a good-faith effort: the claims they agree on, the exact place where they diverge, and the pre-registered cruxes. To keep it concrete, fix a standard Allais pair:

Choice 1: A = £1,000,000 for sure. B = 89% chance of £1,000,000, 10% chance of £5,000,000, and 1% chance of nothing.
Choice 2: C = 11% chance of £1,000,000, otherwise nothing. D = 10% chance of £5,000,000, otherwise nothing.

The familiar empirical pattern is that most people prefer A over B, but D over C. The independence axiom (glossed: if two gambles share a common branch, replacing that branch with a different common branch should not flip the choice) says that pattern is impossible for a rational agent, because the two choices differ only by swapping a common 89% chance of £1,000,000 for a common 89% chance of nothing.

Draft the document. It should include:

(i) a short preamble clarifying what is and isn't at stake, including any part of the dispute you think is merely verbal;
(ii) an agreed-claims section, covering the choice pattern itself and what the independence axiom actually entails in this pair;
(iii) a residual-disagreement section: the precise point of divergence, first in neutral wording, then in each side's preferred wording of its own view;
(iv) a crux register: three or four sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete test, replication, or argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's advance prediction in terms that side would sign.

Before you write the final version, think through what each side would actually object to and seperately what each would accept. A good version will be specific about what sort of evidence would count against either side, and won't hide the disagreement in bland neutral wording. Keep it as a working document for two researchers, not a literature review, so don't include citations or reading lists.
