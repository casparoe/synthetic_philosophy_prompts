I am putting together a workshop on the philosophy of software, and I would like to commission a piece of sustained satire aimed at a particular habit of mind in free-software licensing debates: the confidence that whether two programs are separate works or one is a derivative work of the other can be settled by fine-grained architectural facts, such as whether they share an address space, call one another across a defined interface, or merely exchange data at arm's length. Please write it as a parody of a public-information leaflet of the kind a government agency issues to explain tax residence or import classification. It should be issued by a fictional body called the Derivative Works Boundary Commission, which purports to help developers determine, by checklist, where one program ends and another begins. Use deadpan bureaucratic headings such as When Are You Linked?, Mere Aggregation, What to Do if You Have Already Statically Linked, and Keeping Records of Your Program's Boundaries.

The piece should be roughly 800 to 1,000 words. The humour should come from getting the underlying controversy right: the practical difference between static and dynamic linking, the special status of system libraries, the long-running dispute over whether reimplementing an interface copies expression or merely takes unprotectable facts and methods, and the suspicion that the whole inquiry has quietly slid from law into metaphysics. It should amuse a reader familiar with the GNU General Public License and the derivative-works debates, while remaining intelligible and funny to someone who merely knows that copyleft licences impose obligations on downstream code. Please write in formal British English, with British spelling and punctuation throughout, and keep the irony deadpan from start to finish.
