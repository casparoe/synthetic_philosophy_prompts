Act as a philosopher of mathematics who works on the epistemology of proof and who has served on a prize committee; take a moment to work through the strongest argument on each side before writing, then draft only the memo.

I am the student representative on my high school's mathematics prize committee, which awards a prize for the best student proof in our journal. This year's strongest submission proves a graph-coloring conjecture, but the argument depends on a computer program the student wrote that exhaustively enumerates and checks more than 30,000 cases. The student has released the code, and two outside readers have checked the program's logic, but no human has actually followed the enumeration, and no one realistically could. Half the committee wants to disqualify the entry: a proof, they say, should be an argument a competent mathematician can follow from beginning to end, and this submission asks us to take the computer's word for it. The other half replies that this is now normal mathematics — professional results like the four-color theorem rest on enumerations no single human has surveyed — and that we should not punish the strongest work. Draft the short decision memo the committee will circulate. It must recommend one concrete action (award the prize, disqualify the entry, or award with conditions) and must give the losing side's strongest consideration an honest, fair treatment rather than a straw man. Lead with the recommendation in the first sentence, and give no preamble and no restating of the question.
