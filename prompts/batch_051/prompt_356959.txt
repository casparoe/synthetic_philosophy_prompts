In my graduate seminar on Hobbes’s theory of the state, two students fell into a standoff over a sentence from the Dedication to Leviathan. Hobbes writes:

‘For in a way beset with those that contend on one side for too great Liberty, and on the other side for too much Authority, ’tis hard to passe between the points of both unwounded.’

Their exchange ran through five moves:

(1) Crane said the sentence complicates the standard reading of Hobbes on sovereign immunity: if the sovereign can do no wrong, then there is no ‘point’ of too much authority by which the sovereign could be wounded, and it would make no sense to place the sovereign between two dangers.

(2) Malik replied that Hobbes consistently distinguishes wrongdoing from imprudence. The sovereign cannot wrong subjects, because subjects covenant among themselves to authorize the sovereign rather than covenanting with him; but the sovereign can still be imprudent, and imprudence may wound the commonwealth without being an injustice.

(3) Crane answered that this only relocates the question: once Hobbes admits the sovereign can wound the commonwealth through too much authority, the doctrine that the state can do no wrong collapses into a merely legal immunity, while the moral question whether the state can do wrong remains open even on Hobbes’s own terms.

(4) Malik objected that Crane was importing a non-Hobbesian sense of ‘wrong.’ For Hobbes, the relevant interpersonal wrong is breach of covenant, and the sovereign has no covenant with the subject; apart from the laws of nature, which bind in conscience before God, there is no juridical standard against which the sovereign can be judged.

(5) Crane then said the dedication itself shows that Hobbes recognized a practical standard for rule independent of the sovereign’s will: the sovereign can choose a point too far toward authority, and Hobbes can say this without speaking from outside the commonwealth. Malik replied that the dedication is rhetorical self-positioning addressed to a patron, and in any case it says only that the middle is prudent, not that the sovereign can be unjust.

Adjudicate their exchange move by move. In your answer, give exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion; each item should evaluate one of the five moves above and state briefly who is right about that move and why.
