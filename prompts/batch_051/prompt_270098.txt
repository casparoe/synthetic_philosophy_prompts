I keep coming back to an impasse in normative decision theory, and I want a sharper diagnosis of what the impasse actually is rather than another survey of the paradoxes. On one side are partisans of expected utility theory, who treat the independence axiom as a constraint on coherent preference and regard the standard Allais patterns — the common-consequence and common-ratio versions — as mistakes to be debunked, artifacts of presentation or of untutored intuition rather than data about rational preference. On the other side are defenders of risk-weighted expected utility, who take those same patterns as revealing something real about how rational agents may weight probabilities, and who weaken or replace independence so that differences in probability near certainty and impossibility can carry disproportionate weight. Consider the common-consequence case: many people prefer a sure one million dollars to a lottery yielding one million with probability .89, five million with probability .10, and nothing with probability .01, yet prefer a .10 chance at five million to a .11 chance at one million once the common .89 chance at one million is stripped out. Both camps have representation theorems and both can accommodate the observed choices; they differ over whether choices like that one are allowed to count as normative evidence at all.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really an empirical one about what people choose under incentives, and not a formal one about what can be represented, but a meta-normative dispute about what an axiom of rationality is supposed to do: whether an axiom like independence is a constitutive requirement on agency, an idealization we approximate, or a modeling convention that may be traded against other virtues. But I am not confident. There may be a hidden empirical dispute — for instance, about whether Allais preferences survive transparent presentation, repeated choice, or reflection on exactly what the common component does — or a hidden formal dispute about which weakening of independence preserves the most minimal notion of coherence still worth calling rational. It may also be that the two sides are answering different questions under one word, "rational."

Give me a diagnosis of the structure of the debate, not a verdict on who is right. Lay out the strongest version of what each side is committed to, then identify what kind of disagreement this is: factual, formal, about standards of evidence, about the authority of pretheoretic intuitions, or about different problems traveling under the name "rationality." Then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a new experiment, a theorem, a conceptual distinction, or a reformulated question — and be honest about whether the two literatures are actually in contact with each other, or whether the question as usually posed is ill-posed and would need to be changed before any resolution could be expected.
