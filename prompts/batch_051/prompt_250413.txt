My practical reason seminar just did a unit on incommensurability, and I came out of it with a much more skeptical conclusion than the professor seemed to end with. Here's the argument as I've reconstructed it:

(1) A choice is rational only if you choose an option because it is at least as good as every alternative.
(2) You can judge that one option is at least as good as another only if there's some common scale of value on which both can be weighed.
(3) In hard choices — I keep thinking of a quiet academic career near my aging father versus a demanding but meaningful career as a doctor across the country — the relevant values (family closeness, intellectual fit, money, making a difference) have no common scale.
(4) So in those choices, no option is at least as good as every alternative.
(5) Therefore rational choice between them is impossible, and any decision is just a brute preference — no less arbitrary than flipping a coin or liking blue more than green.

A classmate keeps saying the options can be "on a par" and that this dissolves the problem, but that just sounds like relabeling the fact that reason fails. I want to know whether my argument is valid and, if not, exactly which step is doing the damage and how you'd repair it. If it is valid, tell me whether I should just bite the bullet about big life choices.
