William James famously distinguishes two possible epistemic policies: one aimed primarily at avoiding error, and another aimed primarily at acquiring truth. He suggests that W. K. Clifford’s ethics of belief—represented by the ship-owner who sets sail while suppressing doubts about his vessel’s seaworthiness—overvalues the first policy at the expense of the second. Yet James’s own criterion for when we may believe on less than decisive intellectual grounds seems, on its face, to describe the ship-owner’s situation as well. The ship-owner’s option is live, forced, and momentous: the hypothesis that the ship is sound is live for him, he must either trust it or suspend operational confidence in it, and the stakes are momentous. By James’s own lights, what prevents the ship-owner from being justified in believing as he does, rather than being exactly the kind of cautionary failure Clifford describes?
