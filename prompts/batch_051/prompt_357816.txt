I treat the personal cost a speaker has paid for making a claim as a major signal of that claim's credibility. In practice, when judging reports out of a repressive state, I give substantially more weight to a dissident who has been jailed, fined, or blacklisted for her account than to the government's denial or to a comfortable commentator saying something similar, even holding the bare content of the reports fixed. My reasoning is that cheap talk is easy and self-interested spin is abundant, whereas a cost the speaker plainly would not have chosen to pay acts like a hard-to-fake signal: she has put something real on the line. A colleague says I am misapplying the handicap principle. In animal signaling, cost can make a display honest because poor-quality signalers cannot afford it, but a human paying a price shows only that she is committed, not that she is accurate; such a rule may simply select for fanatics. Am I making a defensible epistemic move? If so, under what conditions, and how far should the weighting go? If not, what is the salvageable principle?
