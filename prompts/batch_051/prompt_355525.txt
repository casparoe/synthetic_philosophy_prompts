In the philosophy and science of consciousness, a dispute has persisted for decades without either side visibly moving, and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself rather than a verdict on it. The material case is the color phi phenomenon: a subject is shown two brief flashes of different colors at two separated locations in rapid succession, and reports seeing a single dot travel across the gap and change color around the midpoint of its path, even though the second flash is not physically presented until the end of that path. The reported color change therefore sits, in the represented scene, at a time before the second flash. One camp holds a Stalinesque view: the conscious percept is delayed until after the second flash has been registered, so the brain constructs a unified moving-and-color-changing experience in the first place, and there is no earlier, unrevised experience that the subject ever consciously had. The other camp holds an Orwellian view: the subject is conscious of something before the second flash arrives, but the later information triggers a retrospective rewriting of the conscious record, so the earlier experience is lost and replaced by the revised one. Within broad limits, both accounts can be adjusted to fit the same introspective reports and the same neural timing data, which is precisely why the argument has not moved. My working theory is that the disagreement may not be empirical at all: I suspect the two sides are making claims at different levels, one about the content of an experience (what the experience represents as happening, and when) and one about the vehicle of experience (the physical process that realizes it, and how long that process takes), so that they may never actually contradict each other. But I am not confident in that diagnosis, and I want it tested rather than assumed. Diagnose this disagreement: is it verbal, substantive, or ill-posed; do the two camps differ over facts, over standards of evidence, over what it means to say that an experience happens at a time, or over starting intuitions about consciousness that neither side can argue for; and what, if anything, would count as progress — a conceptual distinction, a new experimental paradigm, a formal result about temporal representation, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Engage with what each side actually commits itself to, and make clear which moves would show the dispute to be merely apparent and which would show it to be a real one.
