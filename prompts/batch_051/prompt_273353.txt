As a journalist who writes about science and ideas, I have spent years describing ant colonies as superorganisms and writing sentences such as "the colony decides where to move" while privately treating the verb as a metaphor. Recently I have been reporting on researchers who drop that hedge: they claim a colony is not merely like a mind but in some literal sense is one — that the colony, rather than any individual ant, is the subject of representational states that guide action. The clearest case is nest-site selection. A small ant colony scouts several candidate homes, evaluates their properties, and eventually commits to a single choice in a way that looks unsettlingly like deliberation. But appearances of agency are cheap; we know how easily many small units following local rules can produce them.

I want your help turning this from a metaphor debate into something testable. First, state the claim precisely: what must "the colony has a mind" mean if it is to be more than a figure of speech? Then design the strongest test you can of that claim. Be concrete about what would be measured, what would count as a positive result for the literal reading, and what result would favor the deflationary reading that talk of colony cognition is only a convenient summary of many ants interacting locally. Then be honest about what the test cannot settle. If it can tell us that a colony integrates information and makes decisions as a unified system, can it also tell us whether there is something it is like to be an ant colony? If not, is that a gap we should expect better methods eventually to close, or does it show that the question was never well posed? Finally, give me the journalistic upshot: under what conditions would a careful writer be licensed to say, without scare quotes, that the colony decided?
