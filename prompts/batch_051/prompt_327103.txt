i'm a TA for an upper-level undergrad course on 19th century moral and political philosophy, and i'm going through exam scripts. one question i need a second pair of eyes on went like this:

"Mill argues in *The Subjection of Women* that the subordination of women cannot be justified by appealing to women's 'nature.' Explain the core of his argument. Does his argument depend on the premise that there are no innate psychological differences between men and women?"

a student wrote the following:

"Mill's core move is to expose a circularity: the only evidence we have about women's 'nature' comes from how women behave in a society that has already subordinated, trained, and constrained them for centuries. So appealing to that nature to justify the subordination is like citing the twisted shape of a bonsai as evidence that the species naturally grows into that shape. Because every observation we have is contaminated in this way, Mill draws the strong conclusion that there are no natural differences between the sexes; all the differences we see are just the product of custom and education. This conclusion is actually essential to his argument, because if he allowed that even one difference were innate, a defender of traditional roles could point to that as a fact of nature and the whole argument would collapse. He backs this up with the analogy of slavery and other old hierarchies: people said it was in the nature of slaves or subjects to be subordinate, and later everyone could see those arrangements were just power dressed up as nature. So for Mill, any appeal to nature in a social argument is custom in disguise, and once you see that, there's nothing left for the defender of subordination to appeal to."
