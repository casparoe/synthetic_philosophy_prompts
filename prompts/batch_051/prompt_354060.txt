I'm in a seminar this term on aging and the good life, and we've been bouncing between the longevity crowd (aging is just a disease, cure it) and the camp our instructor calls deathism. Last week a guest lecturer defended the deathist side mostly through an analogy, and it landed way better in the room than I expected, so I want to stress-test it before I write my response paper. His claim was that a human life is like a novel or a piece of music: a good one has a form, with development and resolution, and the ending is not just the last thing that happens but the thing that gives all the earlier parts their point. An open-ended life, he said, would be like a novelist adding chapters after the resolution or a composer refusing to stop after the final movement; you wouldn't be making the work bigger, you'd be dissolving its shape. From that he concluded that radical life extension is at best a way of stringing together disconnected episodes, and that it may make the whole life less meaningful rather than more. He also offered a psychological diagnosis of the longevity side, that they're just terrified of dying and are reaching for a technological fix instead of doing the work of accepting finitude — which did feel a bit like armchair-psychoanalyzing, but maybe it's doing independent work.

I want an actual audit of the analogy, not just vibes. What maps onto what — is death the final cadence or the resolution, who or what is the author or composer in the life case, and is there anything like a reader or listener? Where does it actualy break? My guesses are that a life is lived from the inside while a novel is designed from the outside, that people revise what their life is about midstream in a way a finished piece can't, and that a lot of what makes a life good (relationships, enjoyment, being absorbed in something) seems not to be narrative at all. But I can't tell which of those are load-bearing disanalogies and which are just surface. So: which disanalogies actually threaten the argument, which don't, and does the conclusion that a good life needs to end survive if the analogy gives out? And if it doesn't, is there a better analogy that does the same work, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument?
