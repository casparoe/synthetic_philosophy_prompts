In the *Ethics*, Part IV, Proposition 54 and its scholium, Spinoza argues that repentance is not a virtue and does not arise from reason, because the person who repents is, in his terms, twice over miserable: first overcome by a bad desire, and then by the sadness of repentance. He supports this with his necessitarianism — since no action could have been otherwise, a rational person would understand the causes of what he did rather than regret it. I find this argument structurally elegant, and I accept that it follows from Spinoza’s broader metaphysics. But I also have a firm intuition that a person who has seriously wronged someone and feels no remorse is missing something of moral importance, even if the wrong action was causally inevitable. Does the theory genuinely contradict that intuition, or does it only conflict with a confused conception of remorse as requiring alternative possibilities? Should I debunk the intuition as an instance of the free-will confusion Spinoza diagnoses, or should I amend the theory so that certain painful passions count as appropriate even without counterfactual freedom? What would justify each move rather than the other?
