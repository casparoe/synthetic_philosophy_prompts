I'm a software engineer at a game studio, and I've been asked to write a one-page proposal for a three-month internal research project to run before preproduction on a military tactics game. The leadership team is split: some want the game to be serious and anti-war, while others say that is impossible for a game whose core loop is combat, and that the best we can do is make it tasteful. I want the proposal to turn this standoff into a determinate design question rather than a studio argument.

Here is the half-formed idea I want you to turn into a proposal. I suspect that when people say a war game glorifies war, they are mostly pointing at the mechanics — what the game rewards, punishes, scores, and makes efficient — rather than only at the presentation layer of visuals, writing, and cutscenes. If that is right, then the standard correctives (somber framing, muted color grading, loading-screen reminders about the cost of war) are aimed at the wrong layer, and the real question is whether a satisfying core combat loop can avoid implicitly endorsing what it rewards. I would also like the proposal to take seriously the opposite conclusion: that any loop built around skill-expressive killing is doing a kind of glorification that no narrative framing can undo, and that seriousness would then require changing the loop or the subject matter, not the wrapper.

Write the one-page proposal as follows: state the question precisely, so that it has a determinate answer and could in principle be settled by a mix of conceptual analysis and playtesting rather than taste; explain why the existing positions inside and outside the industry leave it open; lay out a method — what three people would actually do week by week over three months, including what small prototypes we would build, what we would observe in playtests, and how we would separate design insight from player preference — and explain why that method beats the obvious alternatives (hiring an ethics consultant, copying games already praised as serious, adding content warnings, or dropping war settings entirely). Then say what a useful deliverable looks like. I would like something like a rubric or decision procedure for evaluating a planned mechanic, backed by two or three tiny playable prototypes that test the boundary. Be explicit about what would count as a negative result or a failed project, and keep the scope honest for three months and three people.

Do not include a bibliography or citations; I will fill those in. Refer to positions and arguments by name only where they genuinely help. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who have worked seriously on war, games, and the ethics of play, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
