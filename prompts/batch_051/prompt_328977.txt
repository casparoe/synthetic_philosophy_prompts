I'm a novelist, a hundred pages into a book about a professional gambler — less poker, more someone who makes a living on the edges of markets and private prop bets — and I've been using a friend of mine, Marcus, as the model for how the character thinks about risk. Marcus used to trade options and now he doesn't, and he has strong, wierdly consistent opinions about bets, so I've been writing them down like little case files.

So far he's said: a single coin flip at +$110 against –$100, he'd take; the same flip scaled up to +$11,000 against –$10,000, he'd take; a whole weekend of small-stakes blackjack where he has a real 1% edge per hand, he'd take. He'd also put money into a fund that reprices every quarter as either +10% or –5%, equally likely, and leave it there — he likes that one. He turned down a one-off flip at +$95 against –$100, but nobody finds that interesting. Now I've invented a scheme for the plot and I need to know what Marcus would actually do with it, because the climax turns on whether the protagonist sees it as an opportunity or a slow bleed. The scheme: every quarter a coin flips, and whatever is in the pot is multiplied by 1.6 on heads and 0.5 on tails, and you can keep playing as many quarters as you want. What would Marcus say? Which rule is he implicitly running — expected-value thinking, time-average or ergodicity reasoning, ordinary risk-aversion, loss-aversion, something else? And what further case should I take to him to seperate whichever rules still fit his record? Honestly I want the diagnosis more than the verdict.
