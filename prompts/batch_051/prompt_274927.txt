I'm a novelist, about halfway through a near-future courtroom book, and there's a deposition scene I keep rewriteing because I can't tell whether the character's argument actually holds up or whether he's pulling a fast one. The character is an engineer who has spent a decade having his brain replaced in small functional units—a few thousand neurons at a time—each unit swapped for a prosthetic that reproduces its input-output behavior exactly. After each individual operation he wakes up, recognizes his family, keeps his memories and tics, and nobody claims the surgery killed him that afternoon. In the scene he lays out the following:

(1) Replacing a single small functional unit of a person's brain with a behaviorally identical prosthetic does not make them a different person; after that one operation, they are plainly still the same individual.
(2) So each step of the gradual replacement, taken on its own, preserves personal identity.
(3) The whole replacement is nothing over and above the sequence of those individual steps carried out one at a time.
(4) If each step in a sequence preserves personal identity, then the entire sequence preserves personal identity.
(5) Therefore the person at the end of the whole-brain replacement is the very same person who began it—not a copy, not merely a psychological continuation, but numerically the same individual.

I want the opposing lawyer to attack exactly one step, rather than just saying he 'died somewhere along the way.' Her best line so far is to ask, 'What about the final operation, when the last original unit is swapped out?' but that feels like special pleading, since that step is no different from any of the others. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and under which reading of 'same person'? I have a nagging suspicion step 4 is cheating with transitivity, but identity is supposed to be transitive, so I can't say why. Or maybe step 1 is carrying more weight than it looks. Whats actually going on?
