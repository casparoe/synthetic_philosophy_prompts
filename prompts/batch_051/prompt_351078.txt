I'm a software engineer who works on language runtimes, and I think most naturally in terms of specifications, parsers, compilers, reference implementations, undefined behavior, canonical sources of truth, and maintainership. A friend who studies Christian theology has been trying to explain a debate over sola scriptura and the regress of interpretation to me, and I keep reaching for engineering analogues. I would like you to perform that translation systematically rather than loosely. Restate the following positions in the vocabulary of my field: that a text is the sole sufficient authority and is clear enough on essential matters that an ordinary competent reader can judge it; that a text cannot adjudicate between conflicting readings of itself, so some interpreter outside or over the text must be authoritative; that any such interpreter in practice replaces the text as the real authority; and that the text is clear in its core while secondary matters may need interpretation. In my terms, the debate looks like a dispute over whether a spec is in any sense self-interpreting, whether one needs a canonical reference implementation, whether that implementation is the spec's servant or its master, and who gets to decide that some behavior is undefined.

Then give me the residue report. Tell me which claims map cleanly onto specification-and-implementation concepts, which you had to distort to force the mapping, and which parts of the theological debate have no counterpart at all in how engineers resolve textual authority. In particular, I want to know whether my instinct that the entire dispute is a special case of "who is the reference implementation, and does it get to determine what the spec means" is essentially correct, or whether the original debate carries commitments — about the text's origin, its authority over any interpreter, or what a community owes it — that engineering vocabulary cannot state. Do not settle the theology; I want an honest report on what the translation carries and what it drops.
