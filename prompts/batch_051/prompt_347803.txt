Retired chemist here — spent thirty years running reactions where if the mechanism didn't fit the data you changed the mechanism, no hard feelings. I've gotten into philosophy lately, mostly on Sunday mornings, and I keep picking at Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (Mind, 1895). The spot where I get stuck is this exchange:

"And why must I?"
"Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"
"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"

My position, stated plain: there is no non-circular justification of deduction. Try to justify modus ponens and you have to already use it (or some sibling inference) to move from your justification to the conclusion that the rule is good; try to add the rule as an explicit premise the way Achilles does, and you get the Tortoise's regress of A, B, C, D... with Z never forced. So deduction bottoms out in something we simply do, or a convention, or trust — not in anything that could be shown correct from outside. I cant shake the thought that Carroll nailed it, and that the regress is a real feature of reasoning rather than a puzzle to dissolve.

Act as a professional philosopher of logic who has thought hard about this paper. Before you answer, think carefully and sketch the two or three strongest ways to resist my reading of the passage. Then give me a committed verdict, not a menu of options: what would actually have to be true for my position to be false? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a defender of my view ought to concede really count against it and which are just the Tortoise changing the subject. I'd personally be most moved by a crisp formal result or a clean distinction between rules and premises, and least moved by appeals to "we just do this." And be honest: has my view been qualified into unfalsifiability, and should I worry about that, or is that just what you'd expect from a claim about the structure of reasoning? I'd rather you be wrong in an interesting way than hedged perfectly.
