Next month I am presenting a position paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on the future of public argument, and the discussion period is likely to be the real examination. The audience I will face is unusually well-defined: two deliberative democrats who take the point of argument to be cooperative convergence on reasons that all can share; an epistemologist who works on the evolutionary function of reasoning and is sympathetic to adversarial formats but suspicious of any formal scoring system; a virtue argumentation theorist who holds that good reasoning is a trait of intellectual character rather than a judgeable performance; the director of a national schools debating programme who has watched a decade of competitive formats reward speed, jargon and glibness at the expense of care; and a civic educator who doubts that anything learned inside a game transfers to ordinary disagreement. My thesis is that argumentation can be organised as a genuine sport without degenerating into mere theatre, and that a competition designed correctly would improve the quality of public reasoning rather than corrupt it. The design I am defending is a team league in which sides are assigned by lot and alternated across a season, with trained judges scoring each round against a rubric that rewards, in order: accurate steelmanning of the opposing case, assessed in part by asking the opposing team how fairly they were represented; early and precise concession of what the evidence does and does not establish; absence of evasion techniques such as the Gish gallop, equivocation and question-dodging; economy of expression; and the joint construction of the strongest version of both positions before any rebuttal begins. The paper's central claim is that competition supplies exactly what makes any practice improve — repetition under pressure, calibrated feedback, transparent adjudication, spectator interest and careers for the best — while the rubric aligns the incentive to win with the incentive to get things right, so that the two pressures pull in the same direction rather than against one another.
