I recently had to think hard about whether forgiveness can be something one owes, and whether it is compatible with still feeling the wound. I keep coming back to Joseph Butler’s sermon Upon Forgiveness of Injuries, Sermon IX of his Fifteen Sermons Preached at the Rolls Chapel. In the passage I have in mind, Butler maintains that resentment is a natural and useful passion—its proper office is to protect us against injury and injustice—but that forgiveness is not the elimination of that passion; it is the moral habit of refusing to let resentment run on into revenge, cruelty, or hatred. He goes on to argue, as I read him, that this disposition is not merely a heroic option but a Christian duty. I am trying to read the 1986 film The Mission through this passage. The film seems to dramatize something close to Butler’s view in the character of Rodrigo Mendoza: he carries crushing guilt for killing his brother, performs a punishing penance by dragging his armor up to the Guarani settlement, and is then received by the very people he formerly enslaved. But the film also shows forgiveness as an unearned gift from the victims, not as a duty the offender can claim, and it complicates the idea that resentment has a useful office when the wrongdoing is structural and ongoing. I am writing an essay on this, and I want the response to lead with its bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. What does The Mission, read through Butler’s account, actually show about whether forgiveness can be obligatory and whether it can be unconditional? Where does the film confirm Butler, where does it resist him, and what should the honest critic conclude the film adds to the philosophical debate that Butler’s sermon alone does not?
