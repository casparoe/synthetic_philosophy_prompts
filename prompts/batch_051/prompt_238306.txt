Ok so in my philosophy of mind class we've been doing the causal exclusion argument and I tried to write it out in my own terms, but I'm pretty sure one of the steps is off and I can't figure out wich. Here's my version:

(1) Physics is causally complete: for every physical event, there is some physical cause that is sufficient for the event to happen.
(2) A cause has to make a difference: if C causes E, then if C hadn't happened, E wouldn't have happened.
(3) My hand-raising is a physical event, and its sufficient physical cause would still have happened even if my decision to raise my hand hadn't.
(4) So, by (1) and (3), my hand-raising would still have happened without my decision; by (2), that means my decision didn't make a difference to the hand-raising.
(5) So my decision doesn't cause my hand-raising.

That can't be right, because it would make all my decisions epiphenomenal, and I definitely don't think my decision to raise my hand is irrelevant to my hand going up. But I can't tell whether the sneaky bit is (2) (maybe causation isn't literally difference-making) or (3) (maybe I'm just assuming the decision and its physical cause are two separate things I can pull apart). Is the argument valid? If it is valid but unsound, which premise fails exactly, and what would the most defensible version of that premise be?
