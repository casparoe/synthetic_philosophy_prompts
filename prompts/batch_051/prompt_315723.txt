I am a graduate student currently enrolled in a seminar on the conceptual foundations of quantum mechanics, and I am also grading for the undergraduate course in the same area. I would like a second opinion on the following take-home exam answer. The question and the student's answer are given below. Please assign a grade and provide the specific, point-by-point comments that justify it—what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong, and why each mistake matters for the overall assessment.

Question. In the many-worlds (Everettian) interpretation of quantum mechanics, the universal wave function evolves deterministically, and no single measurement outcome is selected out. Explain what the Born rule problem is: why it is hard to make sense of quantum-mechanical probabilities, or of why a branch with amplitude a should be expected with weight |a|² rather than some other weight. Then evaluate whether a decision-theoretic argument can justify assigning degrees of belief equal to the Born weights. If the argument succeeds, does it establish objective chances, rational constraints on a deterministic theory, or something else? Mention at least one serious objection.

Student's answer. In the many-worlds interpretation, measurement does not collapse the wave function; the universal state branches, and each branch contains an observer who sees a definite result. The Born rule problem is that, if every possible outcome is realized on some branch, it is hard to say what quantum-mechanical probabilities are probabilities of: they cannot be about ignorance of which branch is the actual one, since no branch is singled out, and the unitary dynamics itself contains no probabilistic element. The decision-theoretic approach reframes the problem by asking what a rational agent who accepts the Everettian picture should care about when choosing among acts whose payoffs depend on measurement outcomes. The agent should evaluate a present choice in terms of what happens to her future successors in the branches. The key move is that, because all branches are equally real and the agent will have one successor in each branch, rationality requires her to weight every branch at a given level of branching equally. She should therefore assign an act the unweighted average of the utilities obtained by her successors. When quantum mechanics assigns different amplitudes to different outcomes, that only means the larger-amplitude outcome separates into proportionally more fine-grained branches, so the fraction of branches realizing an outcome reproduces the squared amplitude. The Born rule therefore emerges from branch counting rather than being assumed. If this is right, the argument supplies a rational constraint on degrees of belief within a deterministic theory; it does not show that the universe contains irreducible stochastic chances of the kind a collapse theory would posit, since the underlying dynamics remain deterministic. A serious objection is that the argument may be circular: the notion of a fine-grained branch and the agent's utilities may already have to be amplitude-dependent, in which case Born-like weights are being smuggled in. I am not fully convinced that this problem has been solved, though I think the decision-theoretic approach does a good job of clarifying what probability must mean if the many-worlds picture is assumed.
