I have been trying to get clear about an asymmetry in the ethics of randomized trials that everyone seems to notice and no one seems to state. In a trial comparing a standard treatment with an experimental one, a data monitoring committee reviews the accumulating results at fixed intervals. Sometimes the interim results show a moderate advantage for the experimental arm: the effect is not significant by the stringent standard the trial set for early stopping, but it is large enough that many thoughtful clinicians, looking at the same numbers, would begin to favour the experimental treatment for their own patients. The committee will typically let the trial continue; the standard ethical gloss is that the trial remains in clinical equipoise, because the professional community as a whole has not yet been convinced. Randomization goes on, and some patients continue to be assigned to the arm that now looks worse. The people on the committee are usually clinicians who, in their own practices, act on far weaker and messier evidence every day. The trial, as an institution, thus holds comparative evidence to a much higher standard before that evidence is allowed to shape the care of the patients inside it. I want a reconstruction of what this practice presupposes. Does it presuppose a distinction between two kinds of warrant, one suited to a clinician's private decision and another suited to a community's certified conclusion? Does it presuppose that the patient in a trial is owed a different kind of protection, or the same protection differently specified, than the patient in ordinary practice? Or is the deeper presupposition a view about the ethics of accumulation, namely that continuing to randomize is permissible because the field has not yet reached the kind of settled belief that would make the trial's question closed? I want you to distinguish what the stopping practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, to say where it is philosophically underdetermined, and then to tell me what would have to change in trial monitoring if we adopted the opposite principle that randomization should stop as soon as the individual clinician-investigator would no longer be willing to take the apparently inferior treatment. Please write in continuous prose, as you would write a letter or an email, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
