I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about a concept that keeps appearing in debates over autonomous weapons systems: meaningful human control. His position was that the phrase is close to empty. He said that as long as a human operator has some technical ability to abort an engagement, the system is under human control, and anyone who asks for more is smuggling in an arbitrary standard that no military has ever met, even with conventional weapons. I don't think that is right, but I need a sharp case to show why the difference between "can abort" and "meaningfully deliberates" is not merely rhetorical.

Construct a thought experiment in which two interpretations of meaningful human control come apart in a specific direction: the system satisfies a human-override interpretation — a human operator possesses the physical ability to intervene and abort the system's intended action at every stage before lethal force is used — but fails a deliberative authorization interpretation — the human who is supposed to be responsible for the attack has adequate time and relevant information to assess, for this particular target, the legal and moral requirements of distinction, proportionality, and necessity, and can therefore meaningfully accept or refuse responsibility for the specific decision. Constraints: keep it near-future and militarily plausible, not science-fictional; no perfect AI, no magic targeting, and no malicious or negligent operator; the system should be one a reasonable professional might genuinely defend. After presenting the scenario, do the checklist: state exactly why the override criterion is satisfied, why the deliberative criterion is not, and which design feature of the case is doing the work. Then say how a defender of the override interpretation would try to block the case — for instance, by arguing that the relevant locus of control is earlier in the process, or that deliberation can be distributed across the chain of command — and whether removing the time-pressure feature would collapse the case back into one where both interpretations agree.
