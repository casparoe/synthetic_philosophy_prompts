I keep returning to a disagreement I had in a seminar on cultural evolution, and I want a careful treatment of the conceptual claim rather than a survey of the field. A colleague holds that cultural variants — a tune, a turn of phrase, a design motif, a folk remedy, an online meme — exist as more or less stable lineages that replicate and are selected, so that calling them 'memes' is a genuine explanatory move rather than a loose metaphor. I am drawn to the deflationary view: the thesis is either trivially true, because everything transmitted changes and differential spread is just a fancy name for popularity, or untestable, because any boundary between one cultural unit and the next is imposed by the observer. I would like to be wrong if the evidence supports that.

What would the strongest empirical test of the non-trivial version of the claim look like? Design it concretely: choose the cultural variants to track, the population and timescale, the quantities to measure (for example, fidelity of transmission, mutation rate, branching of lineages, or differential propagation), and the pattern in the data that would count as passing the test and the pattern that would fail it. You may use transmission-chain experiments, longitudinal digital trace data, or any other design you think is well suited, but specify the test in enough detail that it stands on its own rather than pointing me to a literature. Then say exactly what a positive result would and would not establish — in particular, what it would leave open about whether cultural variants have real unit boundaries, whether replication is faithful enough to matter, and whether selection is doing explanatory work beyond human intention and recombination.
