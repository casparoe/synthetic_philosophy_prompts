Im moderating the Q&A at an author-meets-critics for a new book defending a version of the causal exclusion argument. The author's view, roughly, is: mental states are realized by physical states, the physical domain is causally closed, and so the non-reductive physicalist is stuck — either every episode of mental causation is massively overdetermined, or the mental is epiphenomenal. She bites the epiphenomenalist bullet, but tries to soften it by saying mental causation is a loose way of talking, not a scientific claim. The room will be split between people who think that's the only honest conclusion and people who think she's given away the farm.

Draft me ten questions for the session. Dont ask her to restate the argument; everyone knows it. I want questions that force her to either give up one of the argument's premises or own a really uncomfortable consequence. At least one question each on: (i) whether the overdetermination she rejects is actually so bad — firing squads and two rocks breaking a window are overdetermined and we don't lose sleep over it, so what exactly is the extra problem in the mind-body case, and does the fact that the mental cause depends on the physical make the overdetermination harmless or just make the mental a free rider; (ii) the generalization problem — the same argument seems to make temperature, chemical bonding, and natural selection epiphenomenal too, so if she's happy to say that, fine, but then what makes the mental case special, and if she isn't, which principled exemption does she propose; (iii) interventionist accounts of causation, where X causes Y if intervening on X makes a difference to Y while holding other variables fixed — can a mental variable survive as a cause in that framework, and if it can, does it survive only when the physical realizer is held fixed in a way that makes the mental redundant; (iv) proportionality — if the mental cause is too coarse or too fine relative to the effect, is that an argument for epiphenomenalism or just for treating causal talk as interest-relative. For each one, include a follow-up that anticipates her most likely reply and doesnt let her move on to the next slide.
