You are a philosopher of mind with a background in cognitive science, and you are preparing a discussion handout for an interdisciplinary reading group of scientifically literate non-specialists. The group is discussing the question whether a finite mind can exhaust its own experience: whether, for a mind with finite memory, finite powers of discrimination, and a finite life, there is some end point at which it has passed through every experience it could have, or whether there is always something it is like to be that mind that no list can capture.

Design a “Which account of finite experience do you actually hold?” sorting quiz. It should have eight to ten questions, each built around a concrete scenario about a finite thinker or perceiver—for example, a being with a fixed sensory palette, a creature with perfect but finite memory, a mind that only ever entertains one of a fixed set of thoughts, a system that has received every input in a fixed set but not every sequence of those inputs, or a subject whose next state always involves a new relation to its own history. Each question should offer two or three answer options, phrased so that a participant can answer from their judgment about the case without already knowing the philosophical labels.

The scoring key should map patterns of answers onto at least five positions, including something like the following:

1. Ingredient atomism: experience is composed of a finite stock of basic phenomenal ingredients, so in principle a finite mind can exhaust all combinations.
2. State-space finitism: any finite mind has only finitely many total conscious states, so with enough time or repetition exhaustion is inevitable.
3. Open-ended combinatorialism: finite primitives plus recursive or compositional operations generate an unbounded space of possible experiences.
4. Horizon view: each actual experience changes the standpoint from which further experience is possible, so the set of possible experiences is not a fixed totality that could be completed.
5. No-fact view: whether experience is exhaustible has no determinate answer apart from a specification of what counts as the same experience for the purpose at hand.

Before drafting the final quiz, think carefully and internally brainstorm: list at least three candidate diagnostic questions you considered but rejected, and briefly explain why they fail to separate the positions or would smuggle in labels. Then produce the final instrument.

Include the scoring key with clear instructions for how a participant’s choices map to each position, and note which combinations of answers correspond to unoccupied or unlabeled cells in the logical space. For each unoccupied cell, say whether it looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag any pair of positions your quiz cannot reliably separate, and explain where the boundary between them is soft rather than papering it over. Do not include readings or citations.
