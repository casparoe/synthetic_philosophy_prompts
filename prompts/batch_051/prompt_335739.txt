In a philosophy of law seminar I have been trying to pin down what a legal system is actually committed to when it refuses to hear a defendant claim that they did not know their conduct was illegal. The received principle is that ignorance of the law is no excuse, and the courts generally enforce it as though everyone were conclusively presumed to know the law, so that a person who violates a valid statute may be punished even if they could not reasonably have learned of it. At the same time, the system treats the knowability of law as essential in several conspicuous places. A statute can be struck down for vagueness when it fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what is forbidden. A defendant who acted in good-faith reliance on an official's incorrect statement of the law will in some circumstances escape conviction. And for a few highly technical financial and regulatory offenses, actual knowledge of the specific legal duty is an element of the crime, so that unknowing violators are not convicted. The practice thus seems to assert both that a rule can bind people who cannot know it and that a rule must be knowable in order to bind at all.

What theory of legal obligation and culpability makes this pattern coherent — and is it coherent? Reconstruct the view the practice implicitly presupposes by working outward from the exceptions. Does the stern default rule presuppose a substantive claim, such as that members of a political community are under a duty to know the law under which they live or are conclusively accountable for it, or is it best read as a pragmatic device for avoiding unverifiable claims about private ignorance and preserving the law's power to guide conduct? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to be compatible with among rival views, including the principle that legitimate obligation requires a fair opportunity to comply. Identify any point at which different doctrines rest on inconsistent assumptions about whether law's binding force depends on its knowability, and say what would have to change in the practice if a legal system sincerely adopted the principle that it is unfair to bind people to rules they cannot know.
