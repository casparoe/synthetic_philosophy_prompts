I'm a PhD student working on decision theory and philosophy of action, and I've been building an argument for a seminar on whether a deliberating agent can coherently assign probabilities to her own choices. The argument now proves something that seems too strong to me, but I can't find the step that's gone wrong.

Here it is, cleaned up a bit.

(1) A credence in "I will φ" needs a ground. If it isn't answerable to some fact about the way I am — a disposition, a pattern in my behaviour, a chance — then it's just a feeling or an expression of wanting, not a real belief.
(2) While I'm actually deliberating whether to φ, the proposition "I will φ" doesn't pick out an independent fact. It picks out the very thing my deliberation is supposed to settle, so I can't simultaneously treat it as already settled to any degree.
(3) So the only candidate ground for a credence in "I will φ" would be something on the causal side of the choice — my habits, my weakness of will, my incentives. But a deliberating agent can't coherently use such a ground as evidence. If I take "I am disposed to choose φ" as raising the probability that I'll choose φ, I'm treating my own will as a mechanism I'm observing, not as a capacity I'm exercising. The credence starts to look like a half-formed decision rather than a measurement of something.
(4) There's no other ground available. So a rational agent who is genuinely deliberating must assign no nontrivial credence to the proposition that she will φ.
(5) And since the same goes for every open action, a rational agent must be totally agnostic about her own future choices whenever they are still up to her.
(6) But that seems manifestly too much. I can say, and act on, "I'll probably check my phone in the next hour" or "I'm more likely than not to skip the run tomorrow"; these look like ordinary, useful probabilistic beliefs about my own decisions, not illusions. Betting against them would be a mug's game, and a theory that says they are irrational seems to be losing touch with the phenomena.
(7) So either ordinary self-prediction isn't really probabilistic belief, or one of (1)-(4) has to go.

I'd rather keep (2) and (6) than give either of them up. Which premise do I reject, and what do I have to swallow afterwards? I'm least sure about (3), but I can't yet say what alternative ground a credence could have that wouldn't smuggle the familar third-person standpoint back in.

One more thing: please say how confident you are in the diagnosis and flag the point you're least sure about.
