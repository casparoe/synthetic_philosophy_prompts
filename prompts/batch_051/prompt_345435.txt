I'm a journalist working on a piece about whether the Church–Turing thesis is a deep empirical claim or a dressed-up definition, and an interviewee left me with an argument I cant get out of my head:

(1) The usual evidence for the thesis — that the informal notion of an "effectively computable" function lines up exactly with what Turing machines compute — is that many independent formalisms (Turing machines, lambda calculus, general recursive functions, register machines, and so on) all define the same class of functions.

(2) But that's just a finite list of formalisms that happened to agree. A gerrymandered rival can piggyback on it: "effectively computable = Turing computable plus the halting function" (the yes/no function telling whether a given program halts on a given input). This rival contains everything the standard formalisms capture, then tacks on one extra, non-Turing-computable function, so it fits all the convergence evidence exactly as well.

(3) So the convergence evidence supports the real thesis and the silly rival to exactly the same degree.

(4) Yet we treat the Church–Turing thesis as a pillar of the foundations of computation and dismiss the rival without a second thought.

(5) If that's not just bias, our confidence must come from somewhere other than the evidence. The only place left is that we've simply stipulated "effective computability" to mean Turing computability.

(6) And if that's all the thesis is, then it isn't a discovery about computation — it's a convention, like deciding what counts as a "planet."

That feels wrong: if this argument worked, it would seem to turn a lot of well-supported science into stipulation too. Where does it go wrong, if it does? If I have to keep one premise, it's (4) — I think our preference for the real thesis over the tacked-on rival is doing real work, and I want to know exactly what that work is.
