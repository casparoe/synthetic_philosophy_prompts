I have recently had to leave the safety of being a Bayesian who says that priors are just something you choose, because I now have to defend a prior to people who will act on it. In trying to find a principled way to choose one, I went back to E. T. Jaynes's paper "Prior Probabilities" (1968). If I have understood him correctly, Jaynes's claim is that a rational prior is not a matter of taste but of symmetry: if the problem you are considering is invariant under a group of transformations, then the prior you assign should also be invariant under that group, and he takes this to be the only non-arbitrary way to encode ignorance. The transformation group picks out a unique invariant measure in many cases, and that measure is the objective prior.

But I keep running into a difficulty that I do not think Jaynes addresses head-on. The invariant measure is frequently improper. For example, if I set up a problem with only translation invariance, the invariant prior is Lebesgue measure, which cannot be normalized. In practice I must truncate, damp, or otherwise regulate it, and the resulting proper prior depends on the choice of truncation bounds, the limiting parameterization, or the order of limits. These choices are not themselves dictated by the symmetry; indeed, they are invisible to the symmetry. I have started calling whatever is left over after a symmetry argument has done its work the "prior shadow": the part of a prior that survives when you have extracted all the content that invariance can supply, and that must still be filled in by something else. My suspicion is that the real action is in the shadow, and that the maximum entropy program works in practice by squeezing the shadow down to a small number of parameters and then choosing those parameters informally.

I want to know whether this is a real distinction or a confusion, whether it already exists in the literature under some other name, and what it gets right and where it breaks. Specifically: is there a defensible sense in which the shadow is smaller than the original subjective choice, or does the need to regulate an improper prior simply reintroduce all the arbitrariness under a different label? Are there classes of problems where the invariant measure is proper, or where the regularization is canonical, and if so, are they the cases that actually matter for applied work? And where does my picture fail—what would a successful Jaynesian reply to the objection look like?
