I am an emergency physician at a busy urban hospital. Several times a shift I make high-stakes diagnostic and disposition calls — admit or discharge, treat now or observe — and whatever I decide has to be defensible to the colleague who takes over. I have been reading around in formal epistemology, and I want the distinction between first-order and higher-order evidence turned into something I can actually run in the department.

As I understand it, first-order evidence bears directly on the patient: symptoms, exam findings, labs, imaging. Higher-order evidence bears on whether my first-order evidence supports what I take it to support, or on whether I am in a position to assess it well. I have a working four-way split of the higher-order evidence I actually face: (a) state evidence, about my current condition — fatigue, being rushed, emotional state; (b) track-record evidence, about my past calibration on similar cases; (c) disagreement evidence, about what a trusted colleague concludes from the same chart; and (d) source-reliability evidence, about whether the test or decision rule I am leaning on was generated or applied reliably, such as a miscalibrated machine or a rule validated on a different population. I also know that epistemologists dispute whether it can ever be rational to be akratic — to believe P while also believing that my total evidence does not support P. In the department that dispute shows up as: can I rationally think that the evidence says pulmonary embolism while also thinking that I should not trust my reading of the evidence because I am at hour 22 of a shift?

Please write me a reusable decision procedure — a checklist or ordered set of questions — for handling higher-order evidence about my own judgment in real time. It should be runnable in five minutes without formal models or numbers, and it should tell me: which questions to ask and in what order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; when to act on my first-order judgment; when to revise or defer; and when to stop and get a second opinion or hand the decision off. It should say which of my four categories must be treated differently and which can be collapsed. It should be explicit about which steps are genuine judgment calls dressed up as mechanical rules, and it should say what the procedure cannot do — the cases where it will silently misfire. Where the procedure tells me to do something I will resist, such as setting aside a diagnosis that feels obvious because of a general fact about fatigue, say so plainly.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) I am 85 percent confident of a pulmonary embolism from the presentation, and then I remember I have been awake for 22 hours and have missed two similar cases in the past month; (2) a colleague I trust clinically disagrees with my read of an ECG, though neither of us can point to a specific feature the other missed; (3) the only objective finding points against my strong clinical impression, but I know the machine that produced it has been miscalibrated all week. For each, say what the procedure outputs, and mark any step where two reasonable people running the procedure could land in different places.

End with a single line beginning "Summary:" followed by a one-sentence statement of the procedure's central rule.
