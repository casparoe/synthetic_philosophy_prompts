I am a novelist, and I am constructing a political order for a book in which a small republic fills its entire legislative assembly by lottery. In researching the real institutions that have done something of the sort — the extensive use of sortition in classical Athens, and the citizens' assemblies chosen by lot in countries such as Ireland and France in recent decades — I have noticed that advocates write about the lottery as if the selection mechanism itself expresses a philosophical position: a claim about what self-government is and what ordinary citizens are fit to do. I want to know precisely what that position is.

What does a standing practice of filling legislative office by lot presuppose? Does it presuppose that political judgment is a general capacity distributed evenly, or evenly enough, across the adult population, so that a random sample will be about as capable as any elected body? Does it presuppose a particular view of what a legislature is for — that it should mirror the citizenry, or embody equality of eligibility, rather than gather knowledge and experience — and that its legitimacy comes from that resemblance rather than from an electoral mandate? And what of the staff, experts, and civil servants who inevitably brief and support the lot-selected members: does their role quietly concede the very claim the lottery appears to deny, that governing is a specialized competence?

I am also struck by what looks like an inconsistency in the practice. Sortition is often defended on the ground that ordinary citizens are fully capable of governing, yet every serious lottocratic design surrounds them with facilitators, briefings, and expert testimony; and most of its defenders still favour elected offices, professional judiciaries, and career officials elsewhere in the system. If the lottery is supposed to express confidence in the ordinary citizen, why do its own advocates think ordinary citizens require so much scaffolding? Perhaps the lottery is not a claim about competence at all, but a claim about civic standing — that eligibility for office is a status no adult should be denied — or a claim about the corrupting effects of campaigning, or about excluding the self-selecting and the personally ambitious. I would like to know which of these commitments the practice strictly presupposes, which it merely fits well with, and where the institution is philosophically underdetermined, so that several different theories of political competence, representation, or legitimacy would each license it.
