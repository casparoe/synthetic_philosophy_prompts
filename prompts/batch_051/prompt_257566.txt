I'm in a decision-theory reading group, and the centipede game argument has stalled in a way that's starting to feel less like a disagreement about a solution and more like two groups reading different problems. One side treats backward induction as basically a proof: at the last node the second player takes, so at the second-to-last node the first player should take, and so on back to the first move; if common knowledge of rationality holds, taking immediately is forced, whatever the players actually do in experiments. The other side says that's a lovely piece of equilibrium analysis but it's answering the wrong question. Real players cooperate for several rounds in the lab, and even normatively the argument seems to assume the players can run a whole chain of counterfactual inferences that may not survive their own early moves. They also find it strange to call a move rational when it forecloses a long stretch of play that would leave both players better off than the immediate take.

I dont want another defense of either side; I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Are they disagreeing about a theorem, about the empirical plausibility of common knowledge of rationality, about what "rational" means here, or about what game is actually being played — for instance, whether a player's first move sends a signal that changes what the other should believe, so the "same" centipede game becomes a different strategic situation once play starts?
