I sit on the board of a mid-sized foundation that funds high-risk interventions in neglected areas of global health, and we are considering replacing our usual peer-review process with a decision rule borrowed from risk-weighted expected utility theory. I want to know how the rule would actually behave once applicants and reviewers adapt to it, not whether the theory is elegant. Under the proposal, every project is scored as follows. A reviewer specifies a finite list of mutually exclusive outcomes for the project, assigns each outcome a probability p_i, and assigns each a utility u_i relative to a status quo reference point. The project's score is S = Σ w(p_i)u_i, where w is a fixed inverse-S-shaped probability-weighting function: w(0)=0, w(1)=1, w(p)>p when p is small, and w(p)<p when p is large. The board ranks proposals by that score and funds down the list until the budget is exhausted. In addition, each reviewer's year-end bonus for a recommended project is a fixed multiple of w(p_j)u_j, where j is the outcome that actually occurred and p_j and u_j are the probabilities and utility the reviewer themselves assigned.

I would like an analysis of the incentives this creates for the reviewers, for applicant organizations, and for the foundation's program staff. What kinds of projects would get funded and what kinds would drop out? How would people game the probability estimates, the utility assignments, the reference point, or the outcome list? What second-order effects would show up in the applications the foundation starts receiving, and does the scheme reward genuine judgment or skill at massaging distributions? If the failure modes can be fixed by changing the bonus rule or the scoring rule, say how; if they cannot, say why. If I have misused or misdescribed risk-weighted expected utility, or if the mechanism as stated is internally inconsistent, please say so plainly rather than answering the question you think I meant to ask. Please write the response in American English.
