I've been trying to get a handle on why some fields seem to compound and others just cycle through fashions, and I keep running into the same explanation: fields with clear feedback advance faster, end of story. I wrote a short essay pushing back on that, and I'd like your comments on it, not just a summary. Here's the draft:

"Everyone says fields with clear feedback advance faster, and then points at physics or chemistry and contrasts them with 'unverifiable' fields. I think that's roughly right but the actual mechanism is being missed. What matters isn't whether a claim could in principle be checked, but how long the loop takes and whether the loop judges the thing you care about or a proxy for it. Software moves fast because you can run the test in seconds, not because programs are more falsifiable than economic models — macroeconomists get false predictions all the time, but the feedback shows up a decade later, once. And when the loop is fast but aimed at a proxy, a field can get very good at the proxy without improving on the target; I think this is what happened with some of the metrics-driven corners of academia, and maybe with ML benchmarks. So the usual takeaway, 'make your field more verifiable,' seems to me like it solves the wrong problem. The practical advice should be: shorten the loop, and check whether the loop is scoring the real thing."

I'm sure I've bundled together a couple of distinct ideas here — speed of iteration vs. quality of accumulated knowledge, and Goodhart's law as a special case of the proxy problem. Could you give me a determinate verdict on whether the main claim is right or not? Don't lay out the options — tell me where you'd stand: is verifiability doing more work than I credit it for, and does my central argument survive the conflation? If it's basically wrong, I'd rather hear that flatly, with the place it falls apart.
