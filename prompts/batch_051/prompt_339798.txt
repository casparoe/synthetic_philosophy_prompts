I have been turning over the problem of old evidence in Bayesian confirmation theory, and I want to know how much of it depends on an idealisation that many real reasoners no longer satisfy. The familiar argument runs as follows. A Bayesian agent only learns by conditionalising on evidence it has just acquired. If a datum is old, the agent assigned it probability one before the hypothesis was introduced, and Bayes's theorem then leaves the hypothesis's probability unchanged; so old data cannot confirm anything. On this argument, a Bayesian cannot say that the long-known anomaly in the orbit of Mercury confirmed general relativity, even though almost everyone accepts that it did.

Now suppose we consider a different sort of reasoner – perhaps a learned artificial system, perhaps a more realistic model of a human inquirer – that never assigns probability exactly one to any empirical proposition. Its memory and instruments are treated as fallible: every stored observation carries a high but non-extreme probability, and a sufficiently powerful theory can lower its confidence in a record or lead it to reinterpret what the record shows. It can also represent, and learn for the first time, propositions such as "this theory makes that old record probable." Does the old-evidence argument survive this changed assumption? I am trying to isolate which premise does the work: is the problem really about probability one, about the principle that confirmation requires newly acquired observations, or about treating stored records as fixed? If the argument fails for this reasoner, give me the strongest repaired Bayesian position that handles old evidence without licensing ad hoc accommodation; if it still goes through, say exactly what feature of this reasoner leaves the original conclusion intact.
