Two people in my reading group got into an argument after one of them read aloud this passage from Chapter V ("Of Property") of John Locke's *Second Treatise of Government*:

> "And the taking of this or that part, does not depend on the express consent of all the commoners. Thus the grass my horse has bit; the turfs my servant has cut; and the ore I have digged in any place, where I have a right to them in common with others, become my property, without the assignation or consent of any body."

Ana opened with the claim that the passage bears directly on whether a private company that extracted metal from a near-Earth asteroid would justly own that metal. Her first move was that the passage's central thought is portable: just as Locke says digging ore creates property, labor on an unowned body is what generates title, and on that principle asteroid mining fits the theory at least as well as anything on Earth.

Rob replied that the passage cannot be lifted off Earth so cleanly. He pointed to the clause "where I have a right to them in common with others" and argued that for Locke the whole acquisition story operates only within an already existing common stock in which human beings share. Asteroids, he said, are not yet part of any human commons at all, so the labor theory does not even get a foothold there.

Ana answered that Rob was mistaking an incidental feature of Locke's state of nature for an essential premise. Locke's controlling thought, she argued, is that labor on what is unowned creates a right; the "in common with others" clause is simply an artifact of the Earthly starting point where everything begins held in common. If anything, she said, the absence of a pre-existing commons off-world removes a hurdle to acquisition rather than creating one.

Rob then moved to the proviso. Even granting that labor on the unowned can create title, he reminded her that Locke builds the justification on leaving a comparably good share for others, and for many space resources that requirement is not met: some asteroidal orbits are uniquely reachable with present propulsion, and some lunar polar craters are unique as sources of water, so a first mover who takes one of them meaningfully forecloses others. On Locke's own terms, he argued, the proviso would forbid, not license, most first-come appropriation in space.

Ana countered that Rob had the economics of the proviso backwards. Locke's underlying point, she said, is that labor-appropriation enlarges the total stock available to human beings rather than depleting it — cultivated land supports more people than wild land — and asteroid mining is the purest case of that: metal that benefitted no one becomes part of humanity's usable stock, while the launch, propulsion, and extraction infrastructure become new common goods. The proviso, she concluded, is satisfied in the most Lockean way possible.

They never resolved it, and I left genuinely unsure who had the better of each step. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the five moves above, say who is right about what and to what degree, and note anything that both of them are missing. Write your answer as exactly five numbered items, one per move, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
