Over the past few months I have found myself unable to let go of the problem of external-world scepticism, largely because a close friend has started describing episodes in which the ordinary world suddenly feels like a staged presentation rather than something solid. Our conversations keep circling back to the question of what could ever settle the worry that one might be dreaming, or that everything appearing before the mind might be the work of some deceiving power. Somewhere in the middle of these conversations I came back to G. E. Moore's almost embarrassingly simple move: he displays one hand, then the other, and takes that display to prove that external things exist. I can see that the inference is hard to fault on its own terms — the premises imply the conclusion, and the premises are among the most certain things one could offer — and yet the sceptic's reply, that the demonstration merely assumes what it is meant to establish, strikes me as the true philosophical difficulty. I would like that tension made into a poem.

Please write a Petrarchan sonnet in the voice of Moore, or of someone who has just performed the proof. The octave should give the strongest plausible statement of the sceptical challenge — dreams, veils of appearance, the possibility that all this is only present to the mind. The volta at line nine should be the proof itself: the plain, bodily gesture of raising one hand and then the other, followed by the inference to an external world stated in the simplest possible words. Then the sestet should let the sceptic return, insisting that the proof is circular, or that it can rationally compel no one who genuinely doubts; the turn of the poem should be the exact point at which a valid proof and actual persuasion come apart. Let the rhyme do some of this work as well — perhaps the octave lingers on rhymes of seeming and dreaming, while the sestet's rhymes drop into blunt, concrete words such as hand, stand, or matter. Someone who already knows Moore's proof should recognise precisely where the argument is offered and where the objection bites; someone who does not know it should still find it a good poem, and should leave it understanding why the proof feels both devastating and somehow unsatisfying.
