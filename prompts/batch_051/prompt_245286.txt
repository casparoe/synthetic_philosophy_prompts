I run a small reading group for people without any academic background, and for the past few weeks we have been stuck on a single disagreement about the word "if." One member keeps claiming that there is no real difference between saying, in the indicative mood, that if Oswald did not shoot Kennedy then someone else did, and saying, in the counterfactual mood, that if Oswald had not shot Kennedy then someone else would have. Others in the group feel that the two forms are doing entirely different jobs, though none of us has a clear way to state what that difference amounts to. It has become obvious that everyone is applying a private theory of conditionals without knowing it, so I would like to turn this into a sorting instrument for the group: a "which account of conditionals do you actually hold?" decision tree with eight to ten concrete case-judgment questions and a scoring key. The questions should be organized mostly around pairs of mini-cases that differ only in whether the conditional is in ordinary/indicative form or in counterfactual/subjunctive form, plus a few one-off probes such as how the person handles "even if" or a conditional embedded under another conditional. Each question has to be answerable from ordinary linguistic hunches, without any terminology, so that the instrument sorts people by their case judgments rather than by which label they prefer.

The instrument must use at least these five cases, and may add more if they help separate the leaves: the famous pair about whether someone other than Oswald would have shot Kennedy, in its ordinary past-tense and subjunctive versions; a pair about a struck match lighting; a diagnostic pair about a patient's rash and Lyme disease; an "even if" sentence about an outdoor event and rain; and a complex sentence in which one conditional sits inside the consequent of a counterfactual about having attended a party. Please construct the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of schools: first identify the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they leave open, then locate the actual positions within that space, and mark any unoccupied cells with a one-line guess as to whether they are empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. I expect the leaves to include at least a single-underlying-meaning view on which the differences come from context, a two-kinds-of-meaning view on which indicatives concern evidence or inference while counterfactuals concern alternate histories, a view on which an ordinary conditional simply means not-A or B, and a pragmatic view on which context does most of the work. For each leaf, provide a plain-language name or mark it as "unnamed," and give a single sentence stating the most awkward commitment its defenders must accept. Finally, flag any two positions that your chosen questions cannot separate, and say whether a well-built additional question would separate them or whether any short quiz is likely to blur them.
