In a short course on Zen philosophy, our tutor introduced Dōgen's being-time (uji) with the old image of a river. On her picture, a life is a boat being carried downstream: the river is time, the current is the passage from past to future, the source is birth, the sea is death, and the present is wherever the boat happens to be. She then drew the moral that we are carried along by time whether we like it or not, and that Dōgen's point is just to get us to accept that we're in the boat and can't climb onto the bank. It's a lovely image, and I can see it doing real work in making impermanence feel true.

But then we read Dōgen himself, or at least a translation, and he seems to be saying something much stranger. As I understand it, he isn't saying that things sit inside time like a boat on a river; he's saying that each thing is itself time, that time is not a container or a road we travel down, and that mountains and oceans are themselves time rather than things placed in it. On this reading, the present moment is not a stretch of water under the hull but the whole of being-time in that moment.

So now I'm not sure the river image was ever doing the work she claimed. Could you audit it for me? Lay out the correspondence table (boat, river, current, source, sea), then say where the mapping breaks, and what the image quietly imports that Dōgen's own view seems to deny. Then tell me whether the moral she drew — that we are carried along and should accept it — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole time. And if there's a better image for uji, what is it?
