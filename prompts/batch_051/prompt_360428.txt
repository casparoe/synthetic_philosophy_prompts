I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about discounting the future, and I've gathered up my gut reactions to a bunch of cases to see if there's one principle behind them, or if some of them just have to go.

Verdict one. If I have to choose between preventing a certain death this year and a certain death in 2250, same cost, and I'm stipulating that both deaths are equally certain and equally preventable, I don't think the person in 2250 matters less because they're farther away. I'd be hard pressed to give a reason to save the nearer one.

Verdict two. But if the choice is between a program we're confident will prevent a hundred deaths next year and another that's expected to prevent a hundred deaths in twenty years, I'd fund the first without a second thought. Not because the future lives are worth less, but because the twenty-year program could easilly fail, or the world could have changed enough that the benefit doesn't land.

Verdict three. For my own life I'm a mess. I'd take a hundred dollars today over a hundred ten next year, but I'd wait for eleven hundred on a thousand. And I'll put off a painful dental procedure even when I know it'll be slightly more painful later. I feel like that's present bias, and I know it's irrational, but I still endorse taking the money now.

Verdict four. For climate damage I'd discount future harms at maybe one or two percent a year, but almost none of that is pure time preference — it's uncertainty about adaptation, economic growth, what the world will even care about, and the chance of a technical fix.

Verdict five. Nuclear waste, ten thousand years out. I refuse to treat a harm that far off as worth zero, but I also don't actually want to spend more than a sliver of current income guarding against a small leak risk. If I discount at even one percent, the far-future harm is worth basically nothing, which I reject. But if I don't, it seems to dominate current spending, which I also reject.

My tentative rule is: in impartial decisions pure time preference should be zero, and anything that looks like discounting the future should be redescribed as uncertainty, opportunity cost, or a probability of no one being there. Test that rule against these. Does it capture all five? If not, which of my verdicts do I have to overturn, and does a simpler or slightly revised rule fit better? And settle the case I keep going back and forth on: should I discount a cost or benefit to my own future self fifty years out — say, a disability I could prevent next year or start preventing now — at the same rate as a benefit to a stranger fifty years out, or is it legitimate to discount my own future at one rate and everyone else's at another?
