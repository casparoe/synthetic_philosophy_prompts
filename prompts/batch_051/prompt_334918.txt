A friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about Molinism, and we deadlocked on a single question that I now want help settling. The case against the view, as we were considering it, was the grounding objection. Middle knowledge is supposed to be God's knowledge of a vast set of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom: contingent claims about what some possible free creature would freely choose in some possible circumstance, known by God logically prior to the decree to actualize any world. The objection asks what makes such a counterfactual true at that stage, since no created world, and no creature or choice within one, is yet there to make it true. My friend proposed that this objection assumes that exactly one world is actualized. If one adopts a no-collapse many-worlds picture, in which every decoherent branch with nonzero amplitude is equally actual, then at the level of branch outcomes nothing is left merely possible: the alternatives are all realized in the branching structure, and the image of a single actual world chosen from among the rest no longer applies. I replied that the two uses of 'world' may be doing different work, and that a branching physical universe does not automatically supply a truthmaker for a counterfactual about freedom that is supposed to be true logically prior to creation.

I do not want a survey; I want an adjudication. Does the standard grounding objection to Molinism survive the move to a no-collapse many-worlds ontology? Identify the premise of the objection, or of standard Molinism, on which this development genuinely bears. If the objection can be repaired, state the repaired version precisely and explain what it still rules out; if it cannot, say exactly why it fails and what, if anything, replaces it. Take care not to conflate possible worlds with quantum branches: either show that they can be identified for these purposes, or show why they cannot.
