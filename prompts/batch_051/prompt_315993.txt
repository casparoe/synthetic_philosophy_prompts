I cover the science of mind and the philosophical debates it raises, and I want a feature-style essay for a general-interest ideas section on the controversy over what an emotion most fundamentally is: a judgment (or appraisal), a perception of bodily changes, or an irreducible felt quality. The essay should weigh the central arguments for each position, but it must not end on a non-committal note; reach and defend a definite verdict.

Make the analysis turn on the following passage from William James's 1884 article "What is an Emotion?" In the passage, James is describing what would remain of an emotional episode if the bodily responses were stripped away:

"Without the bodily states following on the perception, the latter would be purely cognitive in form, pale, colourless, destitute of emotional warmth. We might then see the bear, and judge it best to run, receive the insult and deem it right to strike, but we could not actually feel afraid or angry."

First, unpack exactly what James is arguing here and what work the passage does in his case for the view that emotion is the perception of bodily changes. Then use the passage to pressure-test the judgment or appraisal view: if removing bodily feeling leaves only a pale, affectless cognitive state, can a judgment or appraisal by itself amount to an emotion? Give the appraisal theorist a serious reply—for instance, that appraisals need not be detached calculations but are themselves urgent, evaluative ways of taking in danger, insult, loss, or opportunity—and assess whether James's challenge, updated to meet that reply, still stands. The verdict may side with one family of views or may be a hybrid proposed on principled grounds, but it must be clear, explicit, and defended. Aim for roughly 1,000 words, not counting the quoted passage.
