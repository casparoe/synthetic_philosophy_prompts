I maintain a small GPLv3-licensed tool that parses a proprietary settings format used by some lab instruments. A colleague is building a closed-source desktop app for analyzing the same instrument data, and instead of linking against or copying my parser, he took our source, fine-tuned a small code-generation model on it, and then had the model write a parser for his app in a different language. The generated code shares no literal lines with ours, but it has the same state machine, the same odd split between public and private helper functions, and even reproduces a specific workaround for a vendor bug that I'm pretty sure came from our implementation and not from the format spec.

He says its fine because his app contains none of our code and training a model on source is just learning from it, the way a person might read the code and then write something similar. The license, though, defines its terms very broadly. GPLv3, Section 0, says:

To “modify” a work means to copy from or adapt all or part of the work in a fashion requiring copyright permission, other than the making of an exact copy. The resulting work is called a “modified version” of the earlier work or a work “based on” the earlier work.

So the question is whether his generated parser counts as a “modified version” of my tool or a work “based on” it, and therefore has to be licensed under the GPL if he distributes it. I want you to work through what the plain words reach, what the purpose of the license would say, what the FSF would likely have said in 2007 if you had described this exact setup to them, and what a reasonable developer reading that sentence today would expect it to mean. Which of these should win when they pull apart? And tell me how confident you are in each part of your answer, including which step you're least sure of.
