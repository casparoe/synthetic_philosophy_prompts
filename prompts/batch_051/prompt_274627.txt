I'm a novelist by trade, so forgive me if I mangle the technical vocabulary — I'm writing a book where one of the viewpoint characters is a con man who doesnt actually think of himself as a crook. He thinks of himself as someone who finds structural flaws in the way otherwise rational people make decisions and then, as he likes to put it, charges them for the lesson. The flaw I want him to work is the money pump, but I don't want the standard version where the mark just has intransitive preferences — A over B, B over C, C over A — and the con cycles them around a triangle of trades. My guy would call that crude, and anyway he isnt interested in marks who are that blatantly inconsistent. He wants marks whose preferences, at any single moment, are perfectly coherent: complete, transitive, expected-utility-compliant in a snapshot. The incoherence is only visible across time, because the mark's preferences shift in a predictable way, and the con man is patient enough to wait out the shift.

So build me that con. The setting should be mundane, something that could happen in a neighborhood bar or a small office, no science fiction and no lying from the con man — every offer is fair, fully disclosed, and looks genuinely good to the mark at the instant they accept it. The mark just has to be the kind of person whose preferences predictably change between when a reward is far off and when it's imminent, or something of that sort; I'll leave you to make the psychology precise. At the end the mark should be exactly where they started in terms of the rewards, minus a stack of cash.

Then walk me through it in sections, in this order, with rough lengths: (1) The setup — the mark's underlying preference structure and the two or more dated rewards in play, 100–150 words. (2) The trades — the sequence of offers and acceptances, 150–200 words. (3) The static check — show why each acceptance is rational by the mark's own lights at the moment it happens and why a snapshot analysis of any single moment would see nothing wrong, 150–200 words. (4) The load-bearing feature — name the one property of the psychology that does the work, and say what happens to the con if you remove it, 100–150 words. (5) The escape hatch — whether a sophisticated version of the same mark who knew their own future preferences could refuse, and what that refusal would cost them, 100–150 words.
