The population axiology literature has spent four decades circling the repugnant conclusion. Total utilitarianism must accept that an astronomically large population of lives barely worth living could be better than a smaller population of flourishing lives; average, critical-level, lexical, and person-affecting views each escape it only by embracing a different counterintuitive verdict; and formal impossibility results in the style of Arrhenius suggest that any axiology satisfying a few innocuous-looking conditions will generate an objectionable conclusion somewhere. I have come to suspect this impasse is partly a mischaracterization: the rival theories are not all answering the same question, and the word 'better' is being asked to do several jobs at once — an axiological ranking of worlds, a verdict about what we are obligated to create, and a rule for aggregating across separate choices. Diagnose the structure of this stalemate. Is it a substantive disagreement about which lives are worth creating, a verbal or methodological disagreement about what a population axiology has to do, or evidence that the original question is underdetermined by our evaluative concepts? Provide an honest account of what, if anything, would count as progress — a theorem, a distinction, a reformulated question, or an argument that progress should not be expected.
