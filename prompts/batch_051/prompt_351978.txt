Friend of mine at a lab sketched a training scheme for reasoning models and I'm trying to figure out what it actually does before he sinks a quarter into it. Here's the setup.

You take a large model and train it on hard math and coding problems. For each problem it has to answer in two stages: first a plain-text explanation of its reasoning, then the final answer. A second, much smaller model — call it the auditor — is shown only the explanation, never the original problem, and has to produce the final answer from that text alone. The big model gets reward only when two things both hold: its own answer is correct, and the auditor's reconstructed answer matches it exactly. The auditor is trained on explanations written by many different big models, and it is updated by a separate team with no gradient sharing, the idea being that the two can't quietly coordinate.

The hope is that this forces the large model to do all the work that matters in the text, since anything it keeps in its head is invisible to the auditor and can't help the match. But I can already think of a few failure modes. The big model might learn to write explanations that are a private channel to this particular auditor rather than something a person would call a justification. Or it might specialize in problems where the answer is easy to recover from a short text and quietly do badly on anything that really needs a long chain. Or the auditor might turn into a stylistic critic that picks up on how a given model likes to hint at its answers, and scores that, without ever understanding the problem.

What I can't tell is which of those wins out over many rounds, and wether the explanations would become more honestly followable by a human or just easier for one particular machine to back out of. What does the scheme actually select for? Is there a patch that keeps the pressure pointed at human-understandable reasoning, or does any fixed auditor create the same exploit? Where do you expect it to collape first?
