my prof in a formal epistemology class keeps deflecting the problem of logical omniscience by saying it's just the frictionless plane of epistemic logic. the standard possible-worlds models assume that if you know p and p entails q, you know q, and that you know every tautology — which, once you throw in your mathematical axioms, starts to look like knowing all of mathematics. everyone knows real agents aren't like that. but his line is that physics got somewhere by solving the frictionless case first and then adding friction as a small correction term, and epistemic logic is doing the same thing. his conclusion is that the problem is overblown: it's a harmless idealization, and the right response isnt to throw out the models but to relax the assumption later.

i want to audit that analogy properly, because ive got a feeling the frictionless-plane picture is doing a lot of quiet work. can you lay out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the plane, the friction, the object sliding, the correction term, the engineer who ignores friction on purpose — and then say where the mapping breaks? in particular, which features of the frictionless-plane idealization get imported into the logical omniscience case without anybody arguing for them? and then the actual question: does the conclusion that logical omniscience is a harmless idealization survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying that conclusion? if there's a better analogy, i want that too.
