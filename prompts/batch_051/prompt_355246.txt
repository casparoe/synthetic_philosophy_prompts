I cover decision theory and its implications for ordinary life at a science-and-ideas publication, and I find myself committed to a view many of my colleagues write off as a clever paradox. The view is that it is sometimes rational to make yourself irrational. The standard illustration is Parfit's hitchhiker: a driver will not pick up a hitchhiker unless the hitchhiker can credibly promise to pay at the destination, but a narrowly self-interested hitchhiker, once he has arrived, would have no reason to hand over the money, and both parties know this. On the view I hold, the hitchhiker's best move is not to feign trustworthiness, but genuinely to become the sort of person who keeps such promises even when doing so is not, at the moment of action, supported by the balance of his reasons. The disposition is locally insensitive to his reasons, and he acquires it precisely because having that disposition serves his ends overall.

I want to know what would count against this position, in the strongest sense. I am not asking for the observation that the view sounds paradoxical, nor for the point that people cannot simply choose their dispositions on command. I want a map of the position's actual exposure: which arguments, cases, or results from neighboring discussions would most decisively weaken the claim that strategic rationality can require end-aimed irrationality, and which would merely force me to qualify it? Press on the joints. Does the view depend on a sharp distinction between the rationality of an act and the rationality of a disposition, and does it collapse if that distinction softens? If the promise-keeping act turns out to be rationally defensible at the moment of action too — because the hitchhiker has a reason to sustain the disposition that serves him, or a reason grounded in the value of trust or cooperation — would that refute the position or merely rename it? Which objections would a committed defender have to concede carry real force, and which are dismissible as attacks on a caricature? Finally, give an honest verdict on whether the view has been insulated against defeat, and whether such insulation is a defect in a philosophical claim of this kind.
