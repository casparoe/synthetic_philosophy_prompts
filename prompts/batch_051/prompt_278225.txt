I'm on the grant committee of a small foundation that funds what we like to call "foundational physics nobody else will touch," and I need to circulate a short written opinion before our meeting next week. The proposal under discussion is from a philosopher of physics, not an experimentalist, and asks for two years to work on the Born-rule problem in Everettian quantum mechanics. As I read it, the project is to push the decision-theoretic route: show that a rational agent who knows the wavefunction and accepts that all branches are equally real should still bet as if the squared amplitudes where probabilities, and to patch the places where existing versions of that argument look circular or quietly assume the conclusion. The applicant is upfront that the project won't produce anything empirically distinguishable from textbook quantum mechanics. Half the committee thinks this is exactly our mandate — the unresolved status of probability is maybe the biggest standing objection to many-worlds, and settling it would be real progress even without a new prediction. The other half thinks we'd be paying for philosophy-department work out of a physics grant: if the project makes no new predictions, it's unfalsifiable decoration, and a "derivation" of the Born rule that just relocates the squared amplitudes into axioms of rationality isn't physics. I'm honestly torn and could see myself voting either way depending on the criterion.

Draft the memo I'll circulate: it should recommend fund, don't fund, or fund with conditions; it should state a criterion, usable on future proposals, for where foundational physics ends and unfalsifiable interpretation begins; and it should take seriously the strongest version of the losing side. One committee member keeps saying the whole thing is moot because naive branch counting gives every branch equal weight, so the Born rule is just an extra posit stuck on top — I'd like the memo to say whether that's right, or whether it's one of those arguments that feels rigorous until you have to say what a branch is and how to count them.
