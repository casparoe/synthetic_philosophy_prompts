I've been trying to get clear on a distinction I can state formally but can't yet make intuitive sense of: the difference between common knowledge and merely mutual knowledge of a fact. For a group of people, mutual knowledge can climb as high as one likes—everyone knows that p, everyone knows that everyone knows that p, and so on through some very large finite number of levels—while common knowledge adds the entire unending hierarchy. In ordinary interpersonal terms it is hard to see what the last, infinite step could possibly add: people who share the first hundred or the first thousand levels of knowledge about a fact would, in any everyday sense, treat it as completely out in the open. Yet in epistemic game theory this distinction is supposed to bear a great deal of weight. The standard arguments that rational players avoid strictly dominated strategies, or that backward induction is justified in finite games of perfect information, are said to require common knowledge of rationality, not merely mutual knowledge of rationality up to some high finite level. And in the coordinated attack scenario—two commanders can communicate only by messengers who may be captured, and each will attack only if certain the other will attack—no finite chain of sent and acknowledged messages ever licenses the attack, even though both commanders can be rational and can know each other to be rational to an enormous number of levels. A formal cousin of this story, the electronic mail game, turns the point into a full equilibrium analysis: a small probability that a coordinating message is lost yields, in the unique rational outcome, the same stay-put behavior even when the message has in fact arrived.

I would like a careful but as non-formal-as-possible explanation of what the infinite hierarchy of common knowledge contributes over and above arbitrarily high finite mutual knowledge. In particular: why iterated reasoning about what rational players will do needs the full hierarchy rather than some large but finite number of levels; how the coordinated attack and electronic mail examples make the point vivid without technical machinery; and what the right lesson is for game-theoretic solution concepts—whether common knowledge of rationality should be read as a harmless idealization that almost always holds, or as showing that everyday attributions of rationality and knowledge are far more brittle than they look.
