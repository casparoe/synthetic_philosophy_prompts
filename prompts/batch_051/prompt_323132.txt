I have been thinking on my own about a view I associate with the Stoics, and I have reached a point where I no longer know whether a familiar modern fact breaks it. Let me lay the view out plainly.

The view starts from the observation that much of human misery has a single shape. Anger, fear, grief and craving are states in which a person is pulled about by things he does not control. The Stoic-style answer is that the good life consists in freedom from this disturbance. On this view the passions are not merely unpleasant; they are a kind of error. They involve treating externals — wealth, reputation, health, the opinions of others — as though they mattered more than they do. The cure is to correct one's judgements about what is worth caring about. Once the judgements are corrected, the disturbance fades, and what remains is equanimity.

The feature I want to press on is that this line of thought ties the value of equanimity to its cause. Equanimity is commended because it is what you get when you stop believing false things about what matters. The calm state and the corrected judgement are supposed to arrive together.

Now consider a development that seems to pull them apart. Some widely used psychiatric medications can produce a state patients describe as emotional blunting: a person becomes much less easily angered, frightened or grief-stricken, and may feel serenely detached, while his underlying beliefs about what matters remain exactly as they were. There is no new understanding, no change in what he judges to be important; there is simply a dampened response. Suppose someone takes such a medication precisely in order to be free from disturbance, and it works. He reports feeling calmer and more even-tempered, and he also reports that he still believes all the same things about what matters.

Does the Stoic-style argument survive this case? It cannot simply be obvious that the drug has given him the good life; yet the original view seemed to value the calm state itself. One could reply that the drug-induced state is not really equanimity, or that only equanimity achieved through corrected judgement has value, or that what was valuable all along was the understanding rather than the calm feeling. Each of these repairs, however, gives up something the original view cared about. Which premise is actually under pressure here? Can the view be repaired without turning into a different view, or does the case show that the original argument was silently depending on a coincidence — that in the ordinary course of things calm and correctness tend to travel together?
