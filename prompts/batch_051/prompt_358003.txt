Ive been trying to sort out what I actually think about whether a whole can have a mind even when none of its parts do, and I've ended up with a list of gut calls about particular cases. Tell me what principle, if any, captures them — and which of my calls it forces me to abandon.

The cases:

- A human being: has a mind.
- A single ant: no.
- An ant colony as a whole — mound, queens, workers, brood, trails, the way it keeps its temperature steady and reroutes foragers when one side of the nest is attacked: I want to say yes, or at least that it has a point of view. It responds as a whole to facts no individual ant knows.
- A corporation, with departments, emails and quarterly reports: I'll happily say it has beliefs and makes decisions, but I resist saying it has a mind — even though I just gave the ant colony one. That reversal bothers me.
- A nation: no. But I can't say how it differs from the corporation except by being bigger and leakier.
- A crowd doing the wave in a stadium: no, obviously. But then I don't know how the colony escapes the same verdict.
- A philosophical zombie — behaves exactly like a person, says it has thoughts and feelings, but there's nothing it's like to be it: no mind by stipulation, though this makes my 'responds sensibly as a whole' criterion look shaky.
- A large language model that writes careful, thoughtful-sounding replies: no, but there's a pull to say yes and I don't trust it.
- An ant colony I can actually converse with — call it Aunt Hillary — where each ant just wiggles its legs and the whole thing composes coherent answers: I'd say yes, but I worry that's only because it talks.

So: is there one account of what gives a whole a mind that gets all or most of these? And what does it say about the two cases I keep going back and forth on? First, an ant colony that has run for decades and has clearly 'learned' to avoid a pesticide, though no individual ant learnt anything. Second, a pile of cheap little robots, each running the same dumb rule, that collectively builds and repairs a shelter none of them could plan or even perceive. State the principle precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it has to overturn, and apply it to one further case of your own.

Also, I'd like the answer written in British English.
