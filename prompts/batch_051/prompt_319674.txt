Draft the document that would come out of an adversarial collaboration between two parties in the philosophy of physics who have been arguing past each other about whether symmetries explain.

The two parties are:

Symmetry-first: Symmetries do genuine explanatory work. If one asks why the energy of an isolated system is conserved, the right answer is that the laws are invariant under time translation; Noether's theorem is what turns that invariance into an explanation rather than a correlation. More generally, the symmetries one imposes on a theory — Lorentz invariance, local gauge invariance — explain and constrain the form the laws can take.

Law-first: Symmetries are properties of laws, not independent explainers. Noether's theorem, for systems with a Lagrangian or Hamiltonian formulation, gives a correspondence between continuous symmetries and conserved quantities, but the correspondence runs both ways. To say "the symmetry explains the conservation law" is therefore to redescribe one formal feature of the laws in terms of another. The explanatory work, if any, is done by the full law together with the formalism; symmetries are indispensable instruments for finding conserved charges and stating constraints compactly, but citing them does not explain.

Write the joint statement the two parties could both sign. Use this structure:

1. Numbered agreed claims. These should cover the mathematics of the Noether connection, the heuristic usefulness of postulating symmetries, and what the dispute is not about. Each claim must be neither vacuous nor slanted toward either party.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should give: the precise issue; the statement of the symmetry-first position that its advocates would accept as fair; the statement of the law-first position that its advocates would accept as fair; and a judgment whether that disagreement is substantive or merely verbal, with a reason.

3. Cruxes. These are the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. For each crux, say what would have to be established for each side to revise, and what each side currently predicts. Include at least these candidate cruxes: whether a symmetry can be specified without already specifying the law whose invariance it is; whether the two-way character of the Noether mapping defeats the claimed explanatory asymmetry; and whether gauge symmetries, often treated as redundancies of description, force the symmetry-first party to carve out an exception, and what that exception would cost.

4. Tests and cases. Where possible, give the concrete cases both parties agree would bear on the cruxes, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Consider at least: a system whose Hamiltonian is explicitly time-dependent (for example, a pendulum whose support is being raised or a gas compressed by a moving piston) so that time-translation symmetry fails and mechanical energy is not conserved; and the special case of local gauge invariance. For each, say whether it discriminates between the two parties or merely restates their initial split.

Finally, append a short note stating how confident the draft is in its formulation of the two positions and in its classification of which disagreements are verbal rather than substantive, and identify the single point the draft is least sure of.
