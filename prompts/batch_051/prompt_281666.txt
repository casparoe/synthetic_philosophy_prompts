I am a high school student in a semester-long elective on philosophy and the mind, and my class has been going in circles on Molyneux's problem for weeks now. The question, as it was first presented to us, is deceptively simple: if a person born blind could distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch alone, and then gained sight, would they be able to tell which was which by vision before touching them? We read that the question was first put to Locke in the late seventeenth century, and that early reports of patients after cataract surgery suggested newly sighted people could not immediately identify shapes they had previously known only by touch. We also read about a modern study that followed children in India who were born with dense congenital cataracts and received surgery years later; right after surgery they could not reliably match the shapes they saw to the shapes they had felt, though some improved after practice. Despite all of us agreeing on what these reports say, the class has split into three camps that have not moved. One camp treats the experimental results as proof that Molyneux's answer is simply no. A second camp argues that the experiments cannot answer the question as originally posed, because a newly sighted person is overwhelmed by an unfamiliar flood of visual input and has not yet learned to use their eyes, so the failures tell us about recovery from deprivation rather than about the basic relation between touch and sight. A third camp insists that the original question is conceptual—about whether shape concepts are tied to individual senses or held in some shared, amodal understanding—so the empirical results are beside the point.

My hypothesis, which my teacher finds plausible but unproven, is that the debate has persisted for over three centuries because it is not one disagreement but several sharing a single name: an empirical question about how actual patients perform after surgery, a developmental question about plasticity and visual learning, and a conceptual question about what it means to recognize a shape or possess a concept of it. I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this really is rather than a survey of the three camps. Are the camps genuinely answering different questions under one heading, or do they in fact disagree about a factual or conceptual premise that could decide the matter? And if the debate is, as I suspect, a bundle of questions that have been fused together, what would count as genuine progress on each strand—if progress is even possible for all of them?
