So I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether the phrase 'the will of the people' names something real, or whether its just a rhetorical wrapper for whatever a particular poll or election happens to spit out. My friend is on team real: he thinks there's a fact of the matter about what a public actually wants, and that surveys are like a thermometer that's slightly miscalibrated — noisy, but still measuring an actual temperature. I've gotten less sure, mostly because of a little poll I ran in my co-op building. We have 30 units. I asked whether people wanted us to build a bike room, and 21 said yes. I asked whether they wanted a roof deck, and 20 said yes. Then I asked whether they wanted us to do both projects in the same year, and 19 said no — the objection wasn't about money, since dues weren't part of the question; people just didn't want a full year of construction in the courtyard. Same people, same week. Depending on how I slice the questions, I get a building that wants each of two projects and a building that rejects doing those same two projects.

The claim I actually want to put to the test is the real-will view: that underneath the contradictory answers there's a single coherent collective will — something like an individual's preferences, maybe with priorities and intensity, but held by the group as a group — and that ordinary one-question-at-a-time polling just mismeasures it. One thing I considered: ask everyone to rank all the possible outcomes (no project, bike only, deck only, both) and check whether some consistent group ranking emerges that makes sense of the yes/no votes. But I can't tell whether getting a tidy ranking would be evidence that I'd found the real will, or just evidence that I chose a method that produces a ranking by force.

So design the best concrete test you can of the claim that a voting public has a stable, coherent will underneath contradictory answers. It should be something a grad student could actually run with ordinary people and survey software, not a constitutional convention. Tell me exactly what pattern of results would noticeably strengthen the real-will view, what pattern would noticeably weaken it, and what part of the claim no empirical test could settle even in principle, no matter how well designed.
