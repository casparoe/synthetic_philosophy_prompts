You are a moral philosopher who works on gratitude, interpersonal norms, and the ethics of reciprocity. Please think through the following carefully and be candid rather than encouraging.

I've been using a principle I call the "forked-cost criterion" of gratitude, and I want an honest assessment of it. The principle says: an action generates gratitude only if its cost to the benefactor is (a) real, meaning the benefactor could have retained the good without moral failure, and (b) unforced, meaning chosen rather than exacted by duty, convention, or social pressure. My central claim is that this criterion, not the size of the benefit to the recipient, fixes both whether gratitude is owed and how much. For example, a wealthy person who gives a small gift that genuinely costs them something deserves more gratitude than one who gives a large gift that costs them nothing; and we owe no gratitude to someone who "benefits" us only by doing what they were required to do anyway. I also think it explains why gratitude toward luck, or toward dead ancestors one never chose one's relation to, is strictly speaking misplaced.

I know about Seneca's view that the intention of the giver matters more than the magnitude of the gift, and about the standard philosophical view that gratitude responds to benevolence rather than bare benefit — but mine emphasizes cost, not benevolence. Is this already in the literature under another name? Name the nearest existing positions. Then give me the three hardest objections, including at least one case where the criterion yields the wrong verdict, and say whether any objection is fatal or merely shows the criterion needs patching.
