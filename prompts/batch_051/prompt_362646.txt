I have recently been seconded from my department to a cross-government working group reviewing how terrorism is defined in a piece of security legislation, and in three weeks I am to present a short paper arguing that the standard restriction of the term to non-state actors is a drafting convenience rather than a principled conceptual boundary. My thesis is that the best definition of terrorism is functional and victim-based rather than actor-based: an act is terrorist if and only if it is politically motivated violence directed at non-combatants, carried out in order to coerce a third party by means of the fear it produces, and the identity of the perpetrator — state, proxy, or non-state group — is irrelevant to whether the act satisfies the concept. I defend this with three main moves. First, the canonical cases used to teach the concept (bombings, hijackings, hostage-taking) are unified not by their non-state character but by a coercive structure in which immediate victims are used as a message to a wider audience. Second, the state/non-state line is porous in practice: states arm, fund, direct, and sometimes effectively merge with the very groups that official definitions classify as non-state. Third, excluding states is politically convenient for those who draft the definitions, because it ensures that a state's own military operations can never fall under the term regardless of what is done to civilians.

The audience is a monthly seminar of security-studies scholars, international lawyers, and a few retired military officers who advise the government; most of them work with operational definitions in which terrorism is by definition a tactic of sub-state groups, and several have written the very threat assessments my paper would complicate. I would like a preparation sheet for the question period: the objections in roughly the order they will come, weighted by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased as these people would actually phrase them; which objections have real force and what I should concede; what I must hold and how to say it in the time available; where each of my replies is weakest; and the one question I should be most frightened of, the one I must not hope they simply fail to ask. Please write the answer in British English.
