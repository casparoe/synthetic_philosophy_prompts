Imagine a mid-sized common-law country amends its constitution so that, from next year, no criminal statute may be enforced if it contains a vague predicate. Every gradable or context-sensitive term has to be replaced by a fixed numerical threshold before a charge can be brought. 'Reckless' becomes speeds and following distances; 'obscene' becomes a pixel-and-decibel formula; 'reasonable force' becomes newtons per square centimetre; 'a dwelling' gets floor-area and occupancy cut-offs; 'torture' gets pain-duration and decibel thresholds. Nothing else about the legal system changes, and a constitutional court can strike down or rewrite any provision it still deems vague. Give me your best prediction of how this legal system looks thirty years later, and show the causal chain. In particular: does vagueness disappear, or does it just migrate down into the thresholds themselves — what counts as exactly 17 newtons, what counts as a pixel, where the boundary of the boundary is? How do police, prosecuters, and defense lawyers adapt to rules that are exact but arbitrary? Which terms turn out to be impossible to precisify without gutting the underlying law, and what do legislatures do with those? Do juries gain power or lose it? And what would count as evidence in this world that the reform made the system fairer or less fair? Tell me which predictions you're most confident in and which rest on assumptions about how quickly people learn to game numerical rules, or about how the court itself understands 'vague'.
