A friend of mine moderates disputes for a small online gaming community, and for the past year she has kept a log of her rulings about whether damage done inside a virtual world counts as a real loss to the player. She is consistent, but I cannot tell exactly which principle she is tracking. I want you to reconstruct her rule, predict her next several rulings, and tell me where the remaining uncertainty is.

Her cases so far:

(1) A player spent about 250 hours hand-building a large ship in a game with an official marketplace where such ships can be sold for real money. Another player used a hacking program to delete the ship from the server. She ruled that the owner had suffered a real loss and that the company ought to restore the ship.

(2) A player completed a difficult week-long quest to obtain a rare pet, had dressed it and traveled with it for months, and had received an offer of about 300 dollars for it through the marketplace. A scammer tricked her into trading it away for nothing. She ruled that this was a real loss, not merely a violation of game etiquette.

(3) In a competitive demolition game, another player destroyed a watchtower that the first player had put together in three minutes as part of the fight. She ruled that this was not a real loss; the whole point of that game is to destroy other players' structures, and a temporary tower is exactly the kind of thing you consent to lose.

Now think about these new cases, which she has not yet decided.

(A) A player spends sixty hours mindlessly quarrying a generic building material in a game with no marketplace and no cash trading. He calls the work a boring chore and says he has no particular attachment to the materials themselves. A hacker deletes the entire stockpile.

(B) In a building game without real-money trading whose rules allow players to demolish one another's structures, someone destroys a large house on which the owner spent several weekends. The demolition violates no rule. The owner had built the house as a memorial and is deeply upset.

(C) A player spends two hours building a small in-game grave marker for a friend's dog that died, in a game with no marketplace. The marker has no real-money value and the build itself was simple. Another player destroys it using ordinary, rule-permitted game mechanics.

I am less interested in the bare yes/no predictions than in the underlying rule. Please lay out the main principles that would fit her three past rulings — an effort principle, an attachment principle, a market-value principle, and a consent-to-the-game's rules principle are my guesses, but add any others that fit just as well. Then, for each new case, say which principles would classify it as a real loss and which would not, and give your best prediction of her verdict with the reasoning behind it. Finally, identify which of these new cases would do the most to force the candidate principles apart, and describe one further case you would want to see decided in order to settle what her rule really is.

Please write your answer in American English, and tell me how confident you are in the overall reconstruction and which single point you are least sure of.
