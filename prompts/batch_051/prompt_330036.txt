I keep coming back to an argument I came up with about hard choices, and I can't tell if I've actually proved something or just tricked myself. It runs like this.

(1) Suppose A and B are options in a genuinely hard choice—say, two very different careers—and suppose they're incommensurable: neither is better than the other, but they're not equally good either.
(2) If two options are incommensurable in that sense, then there's no fact of the matter about which one is better, even for someone with all the relevant information.
(3) So a fully informed, perfectly rational agent choosing between A and B couldn't judge that A is better and couldn't judge that B is better.
(4) But such an agent still has to pick one. Say she picks A.
(5) If she picks A without judging that A is better—and while there's no fact of the matter that A is better—then her picking A isn't based on any reason that speaks in favor of A over B. Its just an arational event, like a twitch or a coin flip.
(6) But the choice of a perfectly rational agent can't be an arational event.
(7) So the original description can't be right: a rational agent choosing between A and B must, after all, judge one of them better, which means there is a fact of the matter about which is better.
(8) Therefore what looks like incommensurability is really just uncertainty about which option is better.

The conclusion feels too strong, but I can't find the slip. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and what's the mistaken reading? And if you give that step its strongest defensible interpretation, does the conclusion still follow?
