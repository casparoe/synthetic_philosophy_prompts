A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about two questions that kept entangling each other: whether a name like Sherlock Holmes refers to anything at all, and what makes a sentence true in a story whose objects do not exist. Every account of truth in fiction seemed to presuppose an answer to the reference question, and every account of the name seemed to presuppose a story-relative notion of truth. I have been turning the argument over since, and I want to test a proposal against your analysis rather than settle the metaphysics directly.

Suppose a collaborative fiction platform — hundreds of authors, one shared universe, no editor-in-chief — adopts the following canon rule.

- A proper name becomes canonical once it has appeared in at least ten independently submitted stories by at least four distinct authors. Before that point, uses of the name are non-binding: any author may contradict any earlier use without penalty.
- A sentence is true in the fiction if and only if it has not been repealed and it is stated verbatim, or is an unavoidable logical entailment of a sentence stated verbatim, in the earliest submitted story that both contains it and was submitted after every name occurring in it had already become canonical.
- A canonical sentence may be repealed only by a two-thirds majority of active contributors (authors with at least one submission in the preceding year), and only by publishing a story that states its negation; the negating sentence is then itself subject to the preceding rule. A repeal may not conflict with any canonical sentence it does not explicitly repeal.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a piece of literary theory. Walk through how it would actually behave. What does it incentivise among authors — around the ten-mention threshold, around strategic vagueness, around sock-puppet authorship, around who can vote, and around the timing of repeals? Where is it gamed first, and how does the gaming distort what ends up true in the fiction relative to what a competent reader would have said before the rule existed? Does the rule succeed in giving the names referents, or does it merely regularise their use — and what is the difference, once the threshold has been passed? What are the main failure modes, and can any be fixed without gutting the mechanism? Finally, take a definite view on whether any divergence between the rule's output and ordinary reader judgement shows that the rule is badly designed, or that truth in fiction cannot be settled by any rule of this kind.

I do not want a balanced survey of possible readings. Commit to the most likely dynamics, give reasons, and say plainly where you are speculating. Please write in British English.
