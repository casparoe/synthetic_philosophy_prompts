For an article I am writing, I have been talking to two researchers who disagree about whether a designed system — an artificial one, with no evolutionary history — could ever be said to want something in a literal rather than a metaphorical sense. One maintains that wanting is a biological category: organisms can want because natural selection installed goal states in them, whereas a designed system is at most executing objectives stipulated by its designers. The other maintains that the origin of a goal is irrelevant to whether it is genuine: if a system treats an outcome as a terminal goal — pursued for its own sake rather than as a means — and is disposed to bring it about, then it wants that outcome. Please commit to a number and show the reasoning behind it. What is your credence that a designed artificial system with no evolutionary history could in principle satisfy a literal criterion of wanting? If you believe that literal wanting first needs a working definition, state the criterion you are adopting and treat rival criteria as part of the decomposition. Decompose the question at least into these terms: whether literal wanting requires phenomenal consciousness, and whether a designed system could have the relevant phenomenology; whether functionally possessing a terminal goal is sufficient for wanting; and whether an evolutionary history plays an ineliminable role in installing genuine goals rather than merely being how ours happened to arise. Give a probability and a sentence of justification for each term, show how they combine, and identify the term you are least confident in and what argument or evidence would shift it most. Then say where the two positions above most plausibly diverge. Please write the answer in British English.
