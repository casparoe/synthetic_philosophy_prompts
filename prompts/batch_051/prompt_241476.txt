I’ve been rereading the Nicomachean Ethics and the Politics side by side for a seminar, and I keep getting stuck on how Aristotle uses “self-sufficient.” In NE I.7 he writes:

“Now by self-sufficient we do not mean that which is sufficient for a man by himself, for one who lives a solitary life, but also for parents, children, wife, and in general for his friends and fellow citizens, since man is born for citizenship.”

So self-sufficiency, which he’s just made a mark of the final good/happiness, explicitly is not the self-sufficiency of a hermit — it includes family, friends, fellow citizens. But then in the Politics, early in Book I, he says the polis is the complete community that exists for the sake of a full and self-sufficing life; before that, households and villages are not really self-sufficient, and he even goes so far as to say that someone who can live without a polis must be either a beast or a god. My confusion is this: is the self-sufficiency in NE I.7 the same thing as the polis’s self-sufficiency in the Politics? If it is, then happiness looks like a property of a whole community rather than an individual. If it isn’t, how does he avoid using autarkeia in two incompatible ways — one for the individual’s good, one for the state’s? Walk me through the candidate reconciliations (different senses, parts vs. wholes, a distinction he never draws explicitly, etc.), say which one is honest and which is special pleading, and then tell me whether there’s a coherent single account or whether Aristotle is just running together two different ideals.
