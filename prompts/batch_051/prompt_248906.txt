Peter Abelard, in his Ethics (Scito te ipsum), argues that the external act is in itself morally indifferent, that the inclination of lust and even the pleasure accompanying it are not sin, and that sin enters only when the will consents to what it takes to be contempt of God. I have been compiling genuine objections to this thesis, the sort that go beyond mere commonsense unease about a mental-state account of sin. So far my list runs as follows.

1. The completion objection: whatever Abelard says, a completed killing seems worse than an internally identical attempt that fails, and a theory that treats the deed as adding nothing to guilt cannot explain that residual moral difference.

2. The negligence objection: if sin requires explicit consent to what one believes is contempt of God, then culpable ignorance — never bothering to form correct beliefs about what God forbids — seems to fall outside sin altogether, yet we rightly blame such negligence.

3. The boundary objection: Abelard distinguishes consent from the antecedent inclination and its attendant pleasure, but the line between taking pleasure in a thought, entertaining it, and consenting is too indeterminate to bear the weight of an entire moral theory.

4. The self-deception objection: because consent is an inner event, agents often cannot reliably tell afterwards whether they consented, and a theory built on this distinction encourages ex post rationalisation rather than accurate self-knowledge.

5. The moral-formation objection: if external acts add nothing to guilt, it is obscure how performing them builds vice or virtue, yet moral education and habituation seem to operate precisely through repeated deeds.

I would like one more objection to Abelard's thesis that is genuinely different from these five — not a restatement, not a special case, and not a combination of items already on the list. In particular, I want something that does not depend on the empirical harm of the act and does not simply point to the epistemic difficulty of identifying the moment of consent. Then tell me honestly whether it is any good: would it genuinely trouble Abelard's distinction, or can it be absorbed within it? Please answer in British English.
