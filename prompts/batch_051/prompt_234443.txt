I've been going down a rabbit hole about subsidiarity lately and I keep hitting the same asymmetry. In the US, a person can talk about states' rights as if they're sacred when the topic is school curriculum or zoning, then five minutes later defend Washington overriding a state on voting rights or the drinking age. In the EU, the same people—often literally the same governments—denounce Brussels for harmonizing something like data protection or cucumber marketing standards as a subsidiarity violation, and then demand an EU-wide response to energy prices or migration when that suits them. Germany is strange too: it gets described as a federal system built around subsidiarity, but in many areas most law is made at the federal level and the Länder are left to administer it, so the principle is honored in the abstract even after the decision has already been centralized. And Switzerland lets its cantons set their own income tax rates while VAT is federal, which nobody seems to have a clean theory for either. So I'm wondering wether there is any actual principle that separates what belongs at the center from what belongs in the subunits, or whether subsidiarity and states' rights are just vocabulary people reach for after they've already decided what outcome they want.

Give me the strongest single principle you can extract from how these four systems actually operate—externalities, information, preference heterogeneity, fiscal competition, whatever—and then be honest about whether it can justify the selective, outcome-first way the principle gets invoked. If it can't justify that, is the right conclusion that the asymmetry is just politics, or that one of these systems has gotten it right and the others are confused?
