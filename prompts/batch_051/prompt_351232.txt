I work in the legal review office of my country's defence ministry. Our job is to issue an opinion on whether a proposed weapons system complies with our international law obligations before the procurement people are allowed to buy it. Recently the government added a policy commitment: every weapons system, however automated, must be subject to meaningful human control. The problem is that this phrase got dropped on us without a definition, and my office has to turn it into something we can actually run. Reviews come in batches, we typically have two or three weeks per system, the people on the pannel are lawyers and ex-officers rather than philosophers or engineers, and the final opinon gets signed off by someone who will face a public inquiry if we get it wrong. Often the description we receive is partial too: a vendor brochure plus some technical annexes, maybe simulation results, never source code.

So I'd like a reusable decision procedure we can apply to a system description: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a satisfactory answer to each, where the procedure outputs a no, and where it should stop and hand the question to a person because the remainder is a value judgment rather than a fact about the system. Ground it in the actual debate over meaningful human control, but write it for my panel, who haven't read that literature. Feel free to distinguish things like control over targeting from control over whether force is used at all, or control over a system's parameters from control over individual engagements, only where that distinction actually does work in the procedure. Then run it on three systems so we can see it in action: a loitering munition that finds and tracks targets itself but sends back an image and waits for a human yes/no before releasing; a ship's close-in weapon system that tracks and fires at incoming anti-ship missiles with no human in the loop because there are seconds to react; and a counter-battery radar-to-shooter system that queues targets for a human operator who has a ten-second window to veto before it fires. Be honest about the results: include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer we won't like, and say clearly where the checklist stops being a checklist and becomes a person making a moral call.
