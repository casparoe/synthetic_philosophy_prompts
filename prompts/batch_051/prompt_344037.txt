I am a journalist who covers philosophy, psychology, and behavioral economics for a general-interest publication, and I am planning an extended reported essay on weakness of will and hyperbolic discounting: the way people reverse their own preferences as rewards draw near, and what that does to the old idea that we sometimes knowingly choose the worse. I have roughly three months of reporting time, enough to pursue two or three threads in real depth through interviews, case studies, and the formal and experimental literature. Please respond as a professional philosopher of action who also works comfortably with the models and evidence of behavioral economics. Start by brainstorming candidate open questions in this area, then produce a defended ranking of the two or three that most deserve those months, and explain your reasoning. I am explicitly not asking for a survey, a bibliography, or a reading list; I am asking for your judgment about where the live intellectual action is and why.

To keep the ranking from being merely a matter of taste, defend it along several dimensions at once: importance, tractability for a journalist, neglectedness, and how much downstream debate would shift if the question were settled. I would also like a dependency-aware picture: which questions are upstream of which, where an answer would be comparatively inert downstream, and what new evidence or argument would cause you to reorder the list. You may engage with, reject, or combine any of the following threads I have been circling: whether the synchronic puzzle of akrasia, acting against one's own all-things-considered judgment at a single time, and the diachronic phenomenon of preference reversal are genuinely one problem or two; whether present-bias is best understood as a failure of rationality or as an intelligible strategy under uncertainty about one's future valuations; what self-binding strategies such as advance commitments and Ulysses contracts presuppose about the unity of the agent over time; how hyperbolic discounting relates to neighboring mechanisms such as the cold-to-hot empathy gap and failures of affective forecasting; and whether policy uses of commitment devices and default rules blur the line between helping people and overriding their current selves. Treat these as raw material rather than constraints: if the most important open question lies elsewhere, say so and justify its place.
