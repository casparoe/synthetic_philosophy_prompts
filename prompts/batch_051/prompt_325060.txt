Write an essay on the following question: should accepting a Bostrom-style simulation argument dampen fanaticism about the far future?

For purposes of the essay, take the following as working definitions. By fanaticism about the far future, I mean the view that the far future contains so much more potential value than the present and the near term that our choices should be assessed almost entirely by their expected long-run consequences; on this view, even a small probability of influencing the distant future can outweigh substantial near-term costs and benefits. By a Bostrom-style simulation argument, I mean the familiar trilemma: either human-level civilizations rarely survive to technological maturity, or technologically mature civilizations rarely run very many detailed simulations of their ancestors, or we are very probably living in such a simulation. Let base reality mean the world in which any such simulators reside, as distinct from the worlds those simulations contain.

The essay should weigh considerations on both sides. On one side: if we are probably simulated, then whether our world has a long future at all, and what that future is like, may be controlled or cut short by simulators; our apparent position in history gives us little license to extrapolate to a distant future; and any plan to influence the far future is filtered through the probability that there is, from our perspective, a far future to influence. On the other side: a small residual probability of being in base reality, or in a simulation whose far future we can still influence, may be enough to keep astronomical expected stakes dominant; conscious lives inside a simulation may matter no less than conscious lives in base reality; and the simulation argument might not dampen long-run concern so much as redirect it, since the fact that our era is being simulated, if it is, suggests that our era's decisions are pivotal enough to matter. The essay may introduce further considerations.

Reach a conclusion and defend it. Do not end on a merely non-committal note. A qualified verdict is acceptable, for example holding that the simulation argument dampens fanaticism only under specific assumptions about simulator control, the moral status of simulated lives, or the residual probability of base reality, but the essay should say which of those assumptions you take to be the most plausible and why.

If you think the question as posed rests on a confusion or is unstable, for instance because far-future fanaticism bundles together distinct commitments that the simulation argument bears on differently, or because the phrase the far future needs disambiguation once simulated and base futures are distinguished, say so plainly, correct the framing, and answer the best version of the question rather than politely forcing an answer to the version as written.
