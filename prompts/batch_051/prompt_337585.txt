I've ended up defending a view a friend of mine calls "lexical threshold negative utilitarianism," and I want to test whether I'm actually open to evidence against it or just fond of how it sounds. The view, as I hold it: suffering has a kind of lexical priority over pleasure, at least once the suffering crosses some threshold. Below that line no amount of pleasure, preference-satisfaction, or flourishing can compensate for it — an hour of torture isn't made okay by any finite pile of ice cream, and thats not because the pile is too small but because the two are not on the same scale at all. I find this completely obvious in the torture case, and faintly unhinged in the everyday cases, where I do trade a headache for a good dinner without feeling like I've committed a category error.

What would count against this view? I want the honest map of its exposure, not just a list of objections I've already heard. Set out the view and its ambiguities (where is the threshold, what counts as suffering, does the lexical priority cut both ways or only against compensating pain), then give me everything that should move me: counterexamples, structural arguments, intuitive costs, neighboring views that do the same work without the discontinuity. Include what a sympathetic defender would themselves admit is a real problem, not only what a critic would say. Then sort it into what would be decisive versus merely awkward. My own weak point, for what it's worth, is the continuum problem: torture and a pinprick seem connected by a smooth gradient, and any line I draw along it feels arbitrary, but refusing to draw a line seems to push me back into full commensurability. Finally tell me whether the view as I've described it has been insulated so that nothing could count against it, and whether that's a fatal problem for a view of this kind.
