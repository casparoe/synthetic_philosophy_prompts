I run a small fencing club, and I've been thinking about how we teach people to think under pressure. We do drills, we do scenario work, we have people freeze in tournaments anyway. Our coach likes to say "you fence the way you train," but honestly that feels like a slogan, not a procedure. There's a passage from William James in the Habit chapter of *The Principles of Psychology* that a friend showed me, where he says:

"The great thing, then, in all education, is to make our nervous system our ally instead of our enemy. It is to fund and capitalize our acquisitions, and live at ease upon the interest of the fund. For this we must make automatic and habitual, as early as possible, as many useful actions as we can, and guard against the growing into ways that are likely to be disadvantageous to us, as we should guard against the plague."

I keep coming back to the "capitalize our acquisitions" part, because it sounds like he's saying training works by accumulating a kind of interest-bearing stock of automatic actions. But in fencing, that picture seems incomplete: a fencer can have perfect automatic parries in drills and still make catastrophically stupid decisions in a real bout. I'd like you to write me a decision procedure for how to train fencers — from beginners through competitive ones — so that the automatic habits actually transfer to high-pressure situations. The procedure should be something our coaches can actually run each week: what to do in what order, what counts as a good enough execution of each step, where the procedure stops and says "go back and fix this first," and where it hands off to human judgment because the science or the philosophy can't settle it.

Then apply it to two cases: a sixteen-year-old who has beautiful bladework in lesson but falls apart in pools, and a veteran fencer who overtrains a single favorite parry-riposte and gets read like a book by anyone who's fenced her twice. Flag explicitly where the procedure gives an answer a coach won't like, and where it just can't say what's wrong.
