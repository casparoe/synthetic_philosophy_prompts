Lately I have been unable to settle whether a structural analogy I noticed between possible-worlds semantics for modality and the measure-theoretic foundations of probability is a genuine correspondence or only a family resemblance in the use of words like "space" and "event"—a colleague who works in probability theory dismissed it as a pun on the word "event," but I am not satisfied by that. The mapping that suggests itself is this: possible worlds correspond to outcomes, the points of a sample space; propositions correspond to events, the measurable sets of outcomes; necessity corresponds to holding with probability one, that is, everywhere except on a set of measure zero; and possibility corresponds to being a set of positive measure. On both sides the objects of study are subsets of a single underlying space, and the operators look like different ways of saying how much of that space a proposition covers. The likeness survives a first test: the intersection of two probability-one events has probability one, just as the conjunction of two necessary propositions is necessary, while two positive-probability events may have empty or merely null intersection, just as two possible propositions need not be jointly possible.

Yet I can see at least three grounds for suspicion. First, modal possibility is an existential notion—one accessible world suffices—whereas a single point in a continuous probability space can have probability zero, so a point can be a way things might be in the modal sense while counting as impossible under the positive-measure reading. Second, the modal framework is qualitative and, in versions that include an accessibility relation, relative to a world, which allows modalities to nest; a probability space fixes one measure and supplies degrees, but has no obvious counterpart of iterated constructions like □◇P. Third, the two sides handle duality uneasily. If necessity is probability one, then the modal dual of necessity—"not necessary that not P"—comes out exactly as positive probability, since the complement of P fails to have probability one just when P has positive measure. That dual relation is algebraically tidy, but it forces "possible" to mean positive measure rather than the semantic reading of "there exists a world." In spaces with null but nonempty events, those two readings diverge: a singleton may be nonempty yet measure zero, so it is possible on the existential reading and impossible on the dual reading.

I would like a judgment as to whether this analogy is deep enough that distinctions or results on one side transfer to the other, or whether it is a loose analogy that collapses under pressure. If the correspondence can be made precise—for instance by treating probability as a measure layered on top of a modal frame, or by deliberately weakening modal necessity to an "almost surely" operator—explain what has to be stipulated on each side for results to transfer legitimately, and identify a familiar result on one side that the naive mapping falsely predicts on the other. If it cannot be repaired, say where the two structures diverge in a way no reasonable stipulation fixes.
