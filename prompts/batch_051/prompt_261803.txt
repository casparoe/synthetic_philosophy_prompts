I am trying to extract a principle from my own judgements about when a disputed recording should settle a factual question, now that convincing synthetic media is cheap and ubiquitous. I used to think of a photograph or video as a kind of epistemic backstop: if the recording showed something, that was roughly what happened. Deepfakes seem to me to have destroyed that default, and I now find my trust varying case by case. Here are my verdicts.

1. A grainy, anonymously leaked CCTV clip appears to show a local councillor accepting an envelope of cash. No one can account for the camera's provenance or maintenance, and the metadata are missing. I would treat this as a reason to investigate, but not as settling that the councillor took a bribe.

2. The same visual content is supplied by the council's own surveillance system, with an unbroken chain-of-custody log and two independent technicians testifying that the file has not been altered. I would treat this as settling the factual question, absent strong contrary evidence.

3. A high-quality leaked audio recording circulates of a national minister using a racial slur. The minister denies it. Audio analysts say the recording is consistent with verified samples of the minister's voice, but there is no event, witness, or second source. I would not treat it as settled: consistency with the voice is not enough on its own.

4. The minister's own party then releases a recording of the same remark from an internal meeting, and several attendees confirm that the remark was made. I would now treat the matter as settled.

5. In a criminal trial, the prosecution introduces a bystander's mobile-phone video of a robbery. The defence argues only that the video could be a deepfake and offers no specific evidence of fabrication. I would not exclude the video on that ground alone, since that standard would make all digital evidence unusable; but I would equally not let a bare assertion of authenticity suffice if there were a specific reason for doubt.

6. A video shows a police officer striking a handcuffed person. The file arrives with no metadata through an anonymous account, but dozens of independent onlookers describe the same incident, and the officer's body-worn camera later surfaces showing the same event from another angle. I would accept the video as settling the factual question.

7. A flawless video of a celebrity endorsing a cryptocurrency is posted. The celebrity denies making it, there is no record of an event at which the endorsement could have been filmed, and no witness or second angle exists. I would treat the video as almost certainly synthetic and would not let it affect my judgement of the celebrity.

8. A state security agency releases a video, complete with a seemingly impeccable chain-of-custody certification, that appears to show an opposition leader handing documents to a foreign agent. The agency has repeatedly fabricated evidence against opponents, and no independent witness or source corroborates the incident. I would not treat the video as settling the question, despite the chain of custody, because the certifying institution is not trustworthy; I would treat it as a reason to investigate the agency's conduct as much as the leader's.

9. Days before a scheduled execution, a video surfaces that would exonerate the prisoner. It was found on an unencrypted phone belonging to someone now dead, and it cannot be fully authenticated in the time available. I would not treat it as conclusively exculpatory, but I would want it admitted long enough to investigate and would be inclined to pause the execution while that happens.

I cannot yet state the principle behind these verdicts. A natural first guess is that a recording should be taken to settle a question just in case it has either a trustworthy chain of custody or multiple independent corroborating sources, and otherwise should be treated only as a reason to investigate. But I can already see that this may be too crude: it may not explain the difference between the celebrity case and the exoneration case, or between merely possible fakery and specific reason to suspect fakery, or how much scrutiny the certifying institution itself must withstand.

Please do the following. State the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures my verdicts, and identify which feature of the principle each case motivates. Say precisely which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate without strain, and whether you think the principle or the verdict should give way, and why. Then apply the principle to two further cases I have not already listed, one of them being a live-streamed video of an atrocity that has continuous geolocation metadata but no second recording. Please write your answer in British English.
