I'm supposed to give a talk to a group of state legislators about what the philosophical work on political obligation and civil disobedience actually commits them to when they're deciding how to respond to a coordinated campaign of unlawful but nonviolent protest. They want to know which considerations should actually bear on charging people, negotiating with organizers, treating the lawbreaking as public speech rather than crime, and punishing holdouts afterwards. The debate gets invoked for all sorts of conclusions: that breaking a law for moral reasons can be justified, that the duty to obey the law is content-independent, that genuine civil disobedience has to be public and accept punishment, that punishment communicates community condemnation and should be proportioned to wrongness. I suspect some of those conclusions do no real work once you're in the legislator's seat, and some would flip if the facts of the campaign were different. Could you rank the implications by how much they should actually move a legislator, say what criteria you're ranking by, and tell me what feature of a protest campaign would most change your orderng? No headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables please; continuous prose only, like a letter or email.
