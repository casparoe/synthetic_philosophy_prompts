This has been eating at me since a seminar last week. Someone put the following argument on the board and I cant find the exit. (1) To deceive someone is to intentionally bring it about that they believe p while you yourself believe p is false. (2) In self-deception, the deceiver and the person deceived are one and the same. (3) So self-deception would require a single person to intentionally bring it about that they believe p while that same person believes p is false. (4) But no one can believe p and believe p is false at the same time and in the same respect — that's just a contradiction. (5) And even setting that aside, you can't simply decide to believe p: belief isn't under direct voluntary control, like how you can't decide to believe the room is empty while you're looking straight at someone standing in it. (6) Therefore self-deception is impossible, and what we call self-deception must be ordinary error, a change of mind, or wishful thinking with the lights on.

Every premise looks right when I read it on its own, but I can't accept the conclusion. My uncle has spent two years insisting his daughter's marriage is fine. He isn't performing; he seems to really believe it, yet he also dodges asking her anything in a way that looks an awful lot like someone who knows better. That doesn't feel like ordinary error, and calling it 'wishful thinking' seems to just rename the puzzle rather than solve it. Which step of the argument would you reject, and what does the replacement view have to look like? If the argument is sound, tell me what exactly in the uncle case I'm forced to redescribe and what that costs. I'd like to keep the ban on contradictory belief if I can, but I honestly dont know where the pressure should land.
