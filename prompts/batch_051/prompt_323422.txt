I am a PhD student in philosophy of medicine, working on the naturalist–normativist dispute about the concept of disease — roughly, whether a condition counts as a disease because of a value-free biological dysfunction or because of the harm or disvalue it involves. The abstract arguments strike me as under-tested against a fixed set of awkward cases, so I would like a working definition of the term 'disease' (or, if you conclude that no single definition of that term can do the work, of 'disorder') that a clinician and a philosopher could each apply without special pleading.

Here are my cases.

(1) Pulmonary tuberculosis with active cavitation, fever, and weight loss — I take this to be unambiguously a disease.
(2) A closed fracture of the radius — I take this not to be a disease in ordinary speech, though it is plainly pathological in some broader sense; I include it to force the boundary between disease and injury.
(3) An uncomplicated pregnancy — clearly not a disease.
(4) Idiopathic short stature in an otherwise healthy ten-year-old at the third centile, with no endocrine or genetic finding — I lean towards not a disease, though I am unsure how to defend that without excluding other biologically normal but disvalued traits.
(5) A person who meets the usual diagnostic criteria for a major depressive episode two months after being widowed — undecided; the case turns partly on how we distinguish severe but ordinary grief from pathological depression.
(6) An asymptomatic thirty-five-year-old with a pathogenic variant for Huntington's disease, who will develop the disease with near-certainty if nothing else intervenes first — undecided; is the person already diseased in some sense?
(7) Stage 1 hypertension, with clinic readings of 130–139/80–89 mmHg, in a sedentary but otherwise asymptomatic adult — undecided; this looks more like a risk factor than a disease state, yet it is treated.
(8) A lactase non-persistent adult living in a society where most adults digest lactose, who experiences bloating and diarrhoea after milk but is otherwise healthy — undecided; the underlying state is the ancestral and globally most common condition for human adults, which complicates any value-free account.
(9) Moderate obesity (BMI around 33) with no current metabolic complications — undecided, and I suspect this case matters for clinical practice more than most.
(10) An autistic adult without intellectual disability, in stable employment, whose distress arises mostly from sensory overload and from an unaccommodating built environment — undecided.
(11) A symptomless chronic hepatitis B infection without liver inflammation or fibrosis, in a person who can transmit the virus — undecided; the principal harm is to others rather than to the host.
(12) Male pattern baldness in a forty-five-year-old, with no other skin or endocrine abnormality — I lean towards not a disease, but it is disvalued and treated.

Please propose a definition, run it through all twelve cases, and flag any case where your definition's verdict seems independently doubtful to you. Then either revise the definition or defend the doubtful verdict. I would also like your view on whether the ordinary term is doing one job or two, and which case most cleanly separates a naturalist from a normativist.
