So I'm putting together a list for my seminar on antidiscrimination law and moral equality: what makes a ground of discrimination 'suspect', so that the state needs something stronger than rational-basis review to use it. So far I have: (1) immutability — the trait isn't something people can change or shouldn't have to suppress; (2) a history of discrimination against the group; (3) political powerlessness — the group can't reliably protect itself through ordinary democratic politics; (4) lack of fit, i.e., the trait is almost never rationally connected to a legitimate state purpose; and (5) visibility or stigmatization — the trait publicly marks the group in a way that invites prejudice. The old worry about minorities who are easy to single out and shut out of ordinary politics is in the background, but I'm inclined to treat that as a version of (3) unless you think it deserves its own slot.

I want one more serious candidate criterion — not a rewording, special case, or combination of these five. It should be able to chage at least one verdict: pick a group or classification where your criterion does real work and say why that's the right result. Then tell me wether the criterion is any good — does it over- or under-include relative to the five I have? Finally, say how confident you are in the details and single out the point you're least sure of.
