I've been arguing with a friend about a finite, opened-envelope version of the two-envelope paradox, and we can't agree on whether the paradox survives or just turns into an ordinary expected-value problem once the probabilities are actually well defined. The version we're looking at is this: a neutral third party draws the smaller amount S uniformly from {1, 2, ..., 1000} dollars, puts S into one envelope and 2S into the other, shuffles them, and hands you one at random. You open yours and see A dollars, and then you get to choose whether to keep it or switch. Before opening, switching is symmetric and shouldn't matter. But the usual reasoning that the other envelope is equally likely to contain A/2 or 2A, so it has expected value 1.25A, seems to apply only to some values of A and not others. Could you work through exactly for which values of A that calculation is valid, where it breaks down, and how the values where switching looks bad are precisely the ones that balance the values where it looks good, so that a blanket always-switch policy has no advantage over never switching?

Seperately (and this is where I actually got confused): suppose A = 400, so the posterior probability that the other envelope contains 800 is supposed to be 1/2. What does that 1/2 amount to on a frequentist story, a subjective Bayesian story, and a propensity story? A strict frequentist might say there is no well-defined probability for a one-shot event like this unopened envelope having 800, or that it has to be cashed out as a relative frequency over repeated draws that happen to land at A = 400. A subjective Bayesian says it's just your credence after conditioning on the stated procedure and the observed amount. A propensity theorist says the physical setup—the range of S and the shuffling—gives the unopened envelope a tendency or disposition of a certain strength. Do these three interpretations give the same numbers in this variant, and where, if anywhere, do they stop being able to make sense of it? Please also tell me how confident you are in your answer and which point you're least sure about.
