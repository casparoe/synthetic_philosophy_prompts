Last night I had a long argument with a friend about secession and self-determination, and I came away unsure what her commitments actually imply. She defended three positions, one for each of three familiar cases:

- On Scotland, she said the United Kingdom has no obligation at present to permit another independence referendum. The 2014 referendum was a lawful, jointly agreed process, it produced a clear vote to remain, and since then nothing has demonstrated a durable pro-independence majority; so the UK is entitled to refuse to rerun the question.

- On Catalonia, she said the Spanish state was within its rights to block the 2017 referendum and is under no obligation to negotiate secession. The Catalan government acted outside the constitutional order, and a functioning democracy need not treat an unlawful, partly boycotted, legally irregular vote as a trigger that obliges it to negotiate secession.

- On Quebec, she said Canada would be obligated in principle to negotiate secession in good faith if a clear majority of Quebecers voted Yes on a clear question, even though Quebec would have no right to declare independence unilaterally.

I then put a new case to her, and we argued about whether her own position decides it. Consider a region within a stable, rights-respecting democracy. The region has a distinct language and long-standing autonomy, and there is no serious oppression or systematic denial of rights. The national constitution, however, describes the state as indivisible and provides no legal route to secession, and the central government refuses to authorize any independence process. After an election in which independence is the dominant issue and pro-independence parties win a majority in the regional legislature, the regional government holds a free and fair referendum overseen by an independent electoral commission and monitored by international observers; 57 percent of the region's eligible voters vote in favor of independence. The central government declares the referendum unlawful and refuses to negotiate.

What should my friend say about this new case, if she wants to be consistent with her verdicts on Scotland, Catalonia, and Quebec? Which of her earlier cases is the closest analog, and why? If her three verdicts are jointly consistent with more than one candidate rule, spell out the competing rules, say what each predicts about the new case, and give your best judgment about which rule she would and should adopt. Finally, please say what would change your verdict: which argument or consideration would lead you to answer differently, and where in your reasoning this is most likely to fail.
