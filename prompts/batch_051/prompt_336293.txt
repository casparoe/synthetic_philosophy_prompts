Write a dialogue between a political theorist and a legal historian who disagree about whether early modern chartered companies (the East India Company, the Dutch VOC) are best understood as corporations that became states, or as instruments of states that were never anything more than a delegated bundle of powers. The political theorist thinks the company-state shows sovereignty is divisible, delegable, and basically corporate, and that the public/private split is a later invention. The legal historian thinks the company-state label overstates things: companies made treaties and waged war only because a real state chartered them, and when the charter was revoked the supposedly state-like powers evaporated, which shows where the soverignty actually sat. Make them argue about one concrete crux rather than swapping generalities—pick either the power to make treaties or the power to punish—and have each side say exactly what the other has to explain away about it. Neither should be a strawman; let the company-state person land real blows about divided sovereignty and the other land real blows about derivative authority. End with each saying, in a sentence or two, what would change their mind.
