My ethics professor has a running list of verdicts about moral worth — whether an action is praiseworthy, not just right. In all these cases the action is the right thing to do; the only question is whether the person gets moral credit for doing it. So far:
- a shopkeeper who charges fair prices only because it's good for repeat business: no worth;
- a guy who returns a lost wallet because he saw a reward poster: no worth;
- an employee who helps a coworker move boxes because the boss is watching: no worth;
- a nurse who volunteers for an extra shift only so her colleagues will think well of her: no worth;
- a hiker who keeps a deathbed promise to scatter his friend's ashes, and says he did it both because he had promised and because he thought it was his duty: worth.

Next week she's going to rule on this one. Lena was raised to believe that hiding an undocumented person from the authorities is wrong and against her religion. She finds her injured undocumented neighbor bleeding in the stairwell; she hides her, drives her to a clinic, and stays with her, because she can see the neighbor is in pain and terrified. The whole time she's convinced she's doing something wrong. You can take it as given in the case that hiding the neighbor and getting her care is the right thing to do.

Predict the professor's verdict, then spell out the two main rules that fit her record so far and say what each predicts for this case. Give your best guess and why, and say what one further case would most cleanly settle wich rule she's running.
