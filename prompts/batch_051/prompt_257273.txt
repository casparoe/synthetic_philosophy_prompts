Our housing co-op's bylaws contain a clause that has never actually been used: "In any matter touching the common good, the board shall decide as the members would decide were they fully informed of the facts and free of partiality." A telecom wants to put a small-cell antenna on the roof. The fee would cover ten years of repairs, but the health-effects research is genuinely unsettled, one board member's spouse works for the company, and the rest of us don't know how to wiegh the studies. The clause was written in the 1970s, when the hardest question on the agenda was whether to allow a shared washing machine; nobody then imagined anything like this.

I want two things. First, the boring lawyer's answer: does this clause tell us anything about whether we can approve the lease? Argue it from the words, from the purpose of the clause, and from what the original members would have said if they'd been asked, and say which approach should win here and why. Second, the philosophical answer: what does it even mean to "decide as the fully informed and impartial members would decide" when no such members exist and we can't become them? Is the clause an instruction to approximate, a standard to aim at, or empty words? Use the antenna case to argue whether telling people to decide by appeal to an idealized version of themselves is a real decision procedure or just a fancy way of dressing up a guess.
