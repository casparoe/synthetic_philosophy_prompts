I keep going in circles on wu-wei, the whole trying-not-to-try thing: if you set out deliberately to be spontaneous or effortless youve already sort of failed, because the deciding and straining is exactly whats not wanted. But heres the specific argument I keep having with a friend. He thinks the paradox is real and deep — that theres a small class of mental states (flow, spontaneity, falling asleep, being genuinely unpreoccupied) that are constitutively incompatible with being aimed at directly, and that indirect tricks dont really dissolve it, they just move the aiming one step to the side. I think its overblown: sure, you cant brute-force yourself into flow, but you can practice a thing until it becomes automatic, or aim at the task instead of at yourself, or set up conditions where the state tends to show up, and all that is still a kind of trying that works. Im at maybe 0.3 that at least some of these states are genuinely self-undermining in a way no intentional strategy can target; hes at 0.7. I want your own number, but more than that I want the decomposition. How much of your credence rides on what trying means (direct volition vs any intentional cultivation), how much on whether indirect strategies count as aiming at the state, how much on whether theres a real difference between trainable flow-like states and a purer spontaneity that noticing ruins, and any other term you think belongs. For each sub-question give a rough probability and one line of justification, then show how you combine them. Then tell me which sub-question the 0.3 person and the 0.7 person are probably actually disagreeing about, and what evidence would move that one the most.
