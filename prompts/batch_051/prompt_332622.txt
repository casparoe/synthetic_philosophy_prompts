A friend and I keep arguing about anti-realism — roughly, the idea that there are no truths that can't in principle be known — and he presented the following chain of reasoning. Every step looks fine to me, but the conclusion is nuts, and I can't find the bad step. Somethings off.

He starts with the knowability principle (KP): if a proposition is true, then it's possible for someone to know it. Not us, necessarily, and maybe not at our time, but some agent in some possible situation. Truth never permanently outruns knowledge.

Now suppose, just as a test, that there is an unknown truth — a true proposition that nobody knows. Call it P.

(1) P is true and nobody knows P.
(2) So the conjunction "P and nobody knows P" is true.
(3) By KP, it's possible for someone to know that conjunction: someone could know that (P and nobody knows P).
(4) Take a possible situation in which someone does know that. Knowledge distributes over conjunction, so they know P, and they also know that nobody knows P.
(5) Knowledge is factive — you can only know what's true — so if they know that nobody knows P, then it's true that nobody knows P.
(6) But in that very situation, they know P, so somebody knows P after all. That contradicts (5).
(7) So it's impossible for anyone to know that (P and nobody knows P).
(8) But that contradicts (3), which followed from KP plus (2).
(9) Therefore the assumption (1) is false: there are no unknown truths. Every truth is known.

He grins at this point and says: either KP goes, or you're stuck with the claim that every truth is known — including, like, the exact number of grains of sand in some random desert, which I'm pretty sure nobody knows. I'd like to keep KP, since I don't know what anti-realism looks like without it, but factivity and distribution also seem non-negotiable. Where does this go wrong, if it does? If it doesn't, which premise is the least costly one to give up, and what do I actually pay for it?
