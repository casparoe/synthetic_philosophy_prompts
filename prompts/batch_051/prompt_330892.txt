Software engineer here, so feel free to treat my cases like unit tests for a principle that might have bugs. I've been reading Benjamin Freedman's article 'Equipoise and the Ethics of Clinical Research' in the New England Journal of Medicine, and there's a bit near the start where he distinguishes two kinds of equipoise. As I remember it, he says something like: the old requirement was that each individual investigator had to have no preference between trial arms, but that's psychologically unworkable, and the real requirement should be that the relevant community of clinicians is honestly split. I don't have the exact wording, but that's the idea that stuck.

I'm trying to work out whether my own verdicts on a bunch of cases line up with that, or whether they need a different principle. Here they are:

1. A stroke trial compares drug A and drug B. The trial literature is genuinely mixed and guideline bodies disagree. The neurologist enrolling me privately thinks A is better and would prescribe it outside the trial. I still think randomising me is fine.

2. Same trial, but the community split mostly traces to two old, badly designed studies. The more recent, higher-quality studies all favour A. Many clinicians still believe B because they haven't read the meta-analysis. I think this trial is not fine, even though the community is split.

3. A trial in a low-income country compares a cheap drug against placebo, even though the drug is already standard in wealthy countries. The local clinicians are genuinely split, partly because they can't afford the drug. I think this is not fine, because the patients are being used to answer a question that mainly benefits richer healthcare systems.

4. A surgical trial comparing two techniques. No surgeon is personally uncertain: each one strongly prefers their own technique, and the profession is split. I think the trial is fine.

5. A placebo-controlled trial where the only reason the community is "split" is that several key experts are paid by the drug's manufacturer to argue that the evidence isn't settled. They genuinely believe what they argue. I think this is not fine.

6. A small single-centre trial run by a researcher who is genuinely uncertain about two treatments, but the rest of the field is not. I don't think the trial is automatically unethical, but I also don't think the researcher's personal uncertainty is what makes it ethical. Verdict: maybe fine, but not for that reason.

My question is whether there is one principle that captures these six verdicts, or whether I'm smuggling in extra moral ideas that Freedman's clinical equipoise doesn't cover. If you have to drop or modify one of my verdicts, say so. Also, whatever principle you propose, tell me what it predicts for a seventh case: the community is honestly split about a drug's effect, but the trial's primary endpoint is a lab surrogate that doesn't track anything patients actually care about. I'd rather you not just give me a label; I want the carving. Please separate what is settled in the literature from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. Could you write your answer in British English?
