Bentham famously held that natural rights are nonsense on stilts: the only real rights are ones some legal system actually creates and enforces, and talk of rights that pre-exist law is just empty moralizing. Now here's a development he didn't live to see. Over the last couple of decades, a handful of legal systems have started giving rivers, forests, and ecosystems the status of legal persons, with rights that can be enforced in court. Much of this is justified by the claim that nature has standing and value on it's own, before any statute says so—which is basically the natural-rights move Bentham said was empty. Does Bentham's argument survive this development, need repair, or get undercut? There seem to be two readings: vindication, since the river's rights only became real once the law made them; or trouble, since the law is doing little more than codifying a pre-legal moral claim and letting that claim do the real work. Which reading is stronger, and what does that tell us about whether Bentham's attack on natural rights still has teeth?
