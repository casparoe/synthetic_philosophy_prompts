I'm a retired materials scientist who's recently gotten into philosophy, and a friend who teaches aesthetics has asked me to help mark her midterm. One of the questions was on imaginative resistance, which I'd not heard of six months ago, so I could use a second pair of eyes on it—its not my area yet. Here's the question the students got, followed by one student's answer. Give it a letter grade, and write the specific comments that would justify that grade: where the student is right, where they're wrong or muddy, and anything a quick grader might miss.

Question: Explain the puzzle of imaginative resistance with a concrete example. Then lay out the 'can't' and 'won't' explanations of the phenomenon, and say which you find more convincing, giving at least one reason.

Student answer: Imaginative resistance is when a reader balks at a fictional claim that asks them to accept a morally deviant proposition. The classic example is a story that says a character did the right thing in killing her baby, because the baby was a girl. The reader doesn't just think the act would be wrong in the real world; they won't even allow it to be true in the story, whereas they happily allow the same story to say that there are dragons or that snow is purple. That's the puzzle: normally the author's say-so makes things fictionally true, but in moral cases it stops working.

The 'can't' view says this is a hard limit on imaginative ability: readers literally cannot imagine the morally deviant content, because their moral concepts are too bound up with their actual view of the world for the imaginative machinery to represent the deviant claim. The 'won't' view says the reader could imagine it but declines, because entering into that imagining would be wrong, or would feel like endorsing it. I lean toward the 'won't' view, because fiction is full of morally terrible things—murderers, torturers—that readers imagine without trouble, so there can't be a blanket inability to represent immorality.

Still, I think the whole dispute is mostly verbal. Both sides agree that in the problematic cases the target proposition doesn't end up imagined. No observation could settle whether that's a case of 'can't' or 'won't,' since the two descriptions apply to the very same failure. The only difference is one of emphasis: 'can't' sounds like a limitation and 'won't' like a choice. So I'd pick the won't view as more accurate-sounding, but I don't think anything substantive hangs on it.
