I'd like a candid assessment of a distinction I keep using. It is supposed to be a small theory of the doctrine of double effect, and I don't trust my own satisfaction with it.

Call it the dataflow account of double effect. The standard doctrine says the moral difference between the switch and footbridge cases — or between terror bombing and precision bombing — is whether the harm is intended or merely foreseen. Intention is usually treated as a private mental state: what the agent aims at, what she has in mind, how the plan represents the bad event. I suspect the real work is being done by a structural property of the plan, the same property you use when reading code.

Here is the proposal. Take any harmful event H in an agent's plan. Ask whether the plan's success condition requires H to be produced and then consumed by a downstream step that contributes to the good. If H occupies an input position — the plan reads H, feeds it through the rest of the machinery, and the machinery would fail to produce the good without that binding — then the harm is intended. If H occupies only an output position — the plan emits H as a byproduct, and nothing downstream consumes H to produce the good — then the harm is merely foreseen. In programming terms: intended harms are values whose bindings are read; merely foreseen harms are values that get produced but never read.

The proposal explains the standard contrast cleanly. In the footbridge case, the plan needs the large man's body to be in the trolley's path; the harm is consumed as the brake, so it is intended. In the standard switch case, the plan routes the trolley onto a side track, and the harm to the one is emitted by the fact that the track is occupied, but the plan does not read the harm to make the saving of the five work. The saving would work equally well, other harms aside, if the side track were empty. The loop case is commonly thought to be a stress test for double effect, and the dataflow account gives the standard verdict: the one's body is consumed as the brake, so the harm counts as intended, which tracks the intuition that the loop feels more like the footbridge.

I'm bringing this to you as a question rather than a thesis. Is this already in the literature under a better name, such as a means/end, causal-role, or plan-based theory of intention? Does the input/output distinction add anything those accounts don't already have? Where does it break? Give me the strongest cases you can think of where a harm is produced and consumed downstream but is intuitively not intended, and where a harm is produced but not consumed and is intuitively intended. And say whether, after steelmanning, the account still deserves to be called double effect or has collapsed into a consequentialist constraint against using people as means. I also want to know whether the phrase 'success condition' is smuggling intention back in; if it is, does the whole account merely relabel the distinction it was supposed to explain?

Please respond as a professional philosopher working on action theory who is also comfortable with programming-language semantics, and think the question through carefully rather than reaching for a stock verdict. Before committing to an overall assessment, brainstorm at least three possible interpretations of the input/output distinction and settle on the most defensible one. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument, counterexample, or repair would flip your verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
