I spent my career in the natural sciences, where reasoning from samples to populations and adding up small effects across many individuals was second nature, so my recent reading in moral philosophy has been unsettling in a productive way. The main source of friction is the argument associated with John Taurek, which I take to run as follows: in a forced choice between saving one identified person and saving five strangers, the bare fact that five lives are threatened on one side and one on the other is not itself a reason to save the five. There is no single subject who suffers the sum of five deaths; each of the five would lose only his or her own life, just as the one would. Losses, on Taurek's view, are not the sort of thing one may add across persons to produce a larger loss, so the relative numbers cannot settle the choice by themselves.

What puzzles me is not the one-against-five case itself but whether the argument survives a specific change in the setting in which many decisions are now framed. Much recent practical ethics weighs interventions aimed at very large, anonymous, or merely possible future populations, where the benefit is a small probability of affecting an enormous number of lives rather than the certain rescue of an identified individual. Suppose one must choose between funding treatment for a present, identifiable person and funding an intervention that slightly reduces the probability of a catastrophe affecting a very large number of future people, with no determinate future victim identifiable at the time of choice. That is admittedly no longer the rescue case Taurek described, and part of what I want settled is whether that change of setting is decisive. Here aggregation across persons seems unavoidable, and the thought that each of the many loses only his or her own life loses its grip because the members of the larger group have no fixed identity and no determinate loss that is theirs. Which precise premise does the one-versus-five conclusion actually depend on — the denial that there is any subject who bears the sum of losses, the denial that losses can be added across persons, or the move between those two ideas — and does that premise retain its force once the many are anonymous, distant, or possible? If it does not carry over, is there a principled repair that preserves Taurek's conclusion in the small identified case while permitting aggregation in the large anonymous case, and what would such a repair cost?
