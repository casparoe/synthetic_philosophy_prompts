I'm taking a course on causation this term, and we've been going round in circles on causation by omission — whether the gardener's failure to water the plant causes it to die, whether a parent who doesn't feed their child causes the death, that sort of case. On the one hand the standard counterfactual test seems to say yes: if the gardener had watered it, it wouldn't have died. On the other hand there's no event there, just an absence, and plenty of people want to say omissions only 'cause' in some derivative or norm-relative way, or don't cause at all. What's interesting to me is that the debate seems to turn on whether you're allowed to make an absence a first-class citizen in your causal story, with its own kind of behaviour, or whether you should treat it as a gap and deny it full status.

At the same time I'm doing a databases and functional programming module, and I keep noticing the same structure in how languages treat missing values. SQL has NULL, which is meant to be an absence, but it leaks: any comparison with it yields UNKNOWN, NULL doesn't equal NULL, and it silently propagates through expressions unless you handle it specially. Functional languages do the opposite with types like Maybe/Option: the absence is made explicit and the type system forces you to say what to do in the Nothing case before you can use the result. It feels like the philosophy debate is running the same split — some accounts treat omissions like NULL (a gap that only enters reasoning under special normative pressure), others treat them like the Nothing constructor (a genuine relatum with its own logic).

Is this a deep parallel or am I just pattern-matching on 'absence'? I'd like someone to spell out the mapping properly: what would have to be true of causation for the Maybe vs NULL distinction to tell us anyting about whether omissions cause, and where it breaks — e.g., is the disanalogy that NULL is about our representation of data whereas causation is about what's out there in the world? Also, if the parallel is real, does anything from the programming debate transfer back — for instance, is 'omissions only cause relative to a norm' the analogue of 'NULL only causes errors relative to a query'? And if you think it's superficial, name something the parallel predicts that turns out false.
