I am a journalist working on a magazine piece about the several different things researchers mean when, in the context of dynamical systems and chaos theory, they describe a system as unpredictable. I want to end the piece with a diagnostic sidebar rather than a prose summary: a decision tree that takes a scientifically literate reader from a few facts about a concrete system to a named type of unpredictability. The tree should be usable by someone who does not already know the technical labels. Every branch node should therefore be a question about the system's behavior, or about an idealized measurement or prediction task; no node may simply present the names of the leaves and ask which one fits.

Please construct the tree from the logical space of a small number of yes-or-no distinctions first, and only then locate the named types in that space. Keep it to at most ten questions. Unoccupied combinations of answers should be marked as such and briefly diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The following leaves must appear: stable equilibrium; periodic motion or a limit cycle; quasiperiodic motion; strange-attractor chaos; Hamiltonian chaos; stochastic noise; quantum indeterminacy; and unpredictability whose main source is computational irreducibility. Where the tree cannot genuinely separate two of these leaves, flag that explicitly and say what additional commitment or observation would separate them; no artificial node should be invented just to force them apart.

For my purposes, the four distinctions that matter most are these, and I want each of them present either as a node or as an explicitly flagged orthogonal dimension: (1) whether trajectories that begin almost identically stay close over the horizon the reader cares about or diverge; (2) whether the unpredictability would survive if external noise were removed and the initial state were measured with arbitrary precision; (3) whether the long-term motion is confined to a bounded region; and (4) whether a shortcut rule exists for predicting a future state, or whether one must effectively trace the system through its intermediate states. The tree should treat stable equilibrium, periodic motion, and quasiperiodic motion as the contrast outcomes in which the system turns out largely predictable despite appearances. I plan to illustrate the leaves with a driven pendulum, a double pendulum, a dripping tap, Brownian motion, the solar system, and the weather, so the nodes and examples should remain consistent with those cases. The language should be precise for a scientifically literate general reader but must not presuppose that the reader already knows what an attractor or a Lyapunov exponent is.
