Harry Frankfurt’s 1969 paper, Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility, opens with an observation that the principle of alternative possibilities — that an agent is morally responsible for what she has done only if she could have done otherwise — has generally been treated less as a defended thesis than as a piece of moral common sense, something taken for granted before the serious theorising starts. Frankfurt then offers the case of Black and Jones. The case, in my own words, runs as follows: Jones has already resolved to perform a certain action. Black wants Jones to perform that very action, and Black has a device that will force Jones to perform it if Jones shows any sign of deciding not to. Jones does not waver; he acts on his own initiative, and Black never intervenes. Frankfurt maintains that Jones is morally responsible even though he could not have done otherwise.
