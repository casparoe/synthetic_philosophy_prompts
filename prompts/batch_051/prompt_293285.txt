In my philosophy of mind course we have been comparing early modern criteria for mentality with the Turing test, and I am having trouble understanding a passage from Part Five of Descartes's Discourse on the Method. The passage is the one where Descartes asks the reader to imagine machines that resemble the bodies of animals. He claims that if we saw such machines, we would have no way of knowing they were not real animals, because their outward behavior would not give them away. But he says the case is different with machines that resembled human bodies. He gives two ways to distinguish them from real humans. The first is that a machine could not use language in the way humans do: it could be built to utter words in response to bodily movements, and perhaps even to say something in response to certain questions, but it could not arrange words in indefinitely many new combinations to answer the sense of whatever is said to it. Descartes says this ability is present even in people born deaf and dumb, and that parrots and magpies can utter words but cannot speak in this sense. The second is that a machine might perform some particular tasks as well as or better than a human, but it would fail in other tasks, which would reveal that it was acting not from knowledge but from the disposition of its organs. I want to understand the structure of the argument. Is Descartes claiming that flexible language use is something a material machine could never do, or only that the machines imagined in his time could not do it? Is the argument meant to establish that the human body must be united to a rational soul, or only that outward behavior gives us a practical test? How does the first test relate to the second—is the first just a special case of the second? And if Descartes is offering a criterion based on observable behavior, why does he think it can rule out the possibility that a machine might one day pass both tests while remaining entirely mechanical?
