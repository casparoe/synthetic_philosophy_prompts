I have a friend, Elena, a year ahead of me, who treats questions about reasons the way other people treat logic puzzles. For most of the semester we have been arguing about whether there are reasons for people to do things even when the people involved do not want to do them, and I have been keeping a running list of her verdicts. She said there is a reason for me to return a phone I found on the bus, even though the owner is a stranger, I did not want the hassle, and nobody saw me pick it up. She said there is a reason for a bystander to wade into a pond to save a drowning toddler, even if the bystander is a stranger with no desire to help and will ruin an expensive coat. She said there is a reason for someone to keep a promise they made to a friend who has since died, even though no living person would be affected either way and the promisor no longer wants to keep it. But she said there is no reason for me to count the blades of grass on our lawn, even though I suddenly want to do it and doing so would harm no one.

Next week she plans to give her verdict on a new case, and I want to be able to predict it rather than guess. The case is this: an island research station has been completely evacuated, no human being will ever return, and the last handwritten memoir of a language that went extinct when its final speakers died is about to be destroyed in a fire. The question she has set herself is whether there is a reason for someone to save it. What rule is she implicitly running? I can see at least two rules that fit all four of her earlier verdicts, and I want to know what those rules are, what each predicts about the research station case, which interpretation of her track record you think is best supported and why, and what further case would best settle which rule she is actually running.
