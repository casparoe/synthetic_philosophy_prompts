I'm a PhD student in social epistemology, and I've somehow ended up as the mediator of a two-person working group on the epistemology of testimony that has been argueing for a whole semester. One of them is a fairly strict reductionist about testimonial justification: she thinks believing what someone tells you is justified only if you have positive background reasons to think the speaker, or the practice of accepting testimony in situations like this one, is reliable — track records, corroboration, cues of competence and sincerity, and so on. The other is an anti-reductionist: he thinks there's a default, defeasible entitlement to believe what you're told unless you have a specific reason not to, in roughly the way perception gives you default justification for how things look. We've agreed they've talked past each other enough, and I want the document a good-faith adversarial collaboration between them would have yielded — something both could actually sign, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than made polite.

Please write that document. Structure it like this: first, a numbered list of agreed claims (about what defeats testimonial justification, about how adult reliance on testimony differs from a child's, about cases they classify the same way); then a disagreements table with columns for the topic, the reductionist's preferred wording, and the anti-reductionist's preferred wording; then a section on cruxes, where for each crux you give one concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree would bear on it and record each side's prediction in advance. At the end, say whether any part of their dispute looks merely verbal. To keep it from just being the standard historical restatement, make them engage directly with three cases we actually fought about in the group: a tourist taking a stranger's directions in a city where about a third of direction-givers are genuinely confused; a juror hearing an eyewitness who has a clear incentive to lie but stays consistent under cross-examination; and a six-year-old believing a parent's claim that the neighbor's dog bites, when the kid could in principle check by sticking a hand through the fence. Also have them address the standing fight about burden-shifting: the reductionist keeps saying the absence of contrary evidence is not itself a reason to believe, while the anti-reductionist keeps saying the real issue is that the other side has misplaced the default. Keep the whole thing under 1,000 words; the table can be compact, but the crux section should be specific enough that either of them could read it and say, yes, that's my view and that's what I'd bet on.
