I keep seeing debates about infinity settled by a single maneuver: someone cites Hilbert's hotel to show that actual infinities are coherent, someone cites Cantor's diagonal argument to show that some infinities are larger than others, someone retorts that formal consistency is not existence, and someone else says we only ever need potential infinity anyway. I want to stop arguing one example at a time and write the full specification first. What would an adequate theory of the actual infinite have to get right, if its job is to settle whether actual infinities are legitimate objects or only useful ways of speaking within mathematics? Give me the requirements list: the cases and phenomena it must handle (Hilbert's hotel, the diagonal argument on the reals, the natural numbers as a completed totality, the use of infinite limits in calculus); the distinctions it cannot collapse (potential versus actual infinity, cardinal versus ordinal size, infinity as a mathematical posit versus infinity in a physical description); the established mathematical results and successful applications it must respect; and any constraints from mathematical practice or physics. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and say which requirements are negotiable and which are not. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to separate those who accept actual infinities from finitists, and briefly score the main positions against the list. A table is fine.
