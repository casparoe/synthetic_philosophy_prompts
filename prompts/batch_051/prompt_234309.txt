Spent an hour arguing with a friend about advance directives and whether your earlier self should get to bind the person you turn into, and we just went in circles. I run a workshop for about a dozen humanities grad students, 90 minutes, and I want them to come away seeing why neither answer feels clean, not just hear about it.

Design a structured role-play around a case like this: a woman wrote an advance directive years ago refusing life-sustaining treatment if she developed severe dementia; now she has severe dementia, doesn't recognize the document or her family, but seems content and wants to live. Split the group into her earlier self's advocate, her present self's advocate, a family member, a clinician, and maybe a neutral decision-maker, with a deadline to actually decide whether to honor the directive. The setup should make them feel the pull of both sides—honoring it feels like respecting her, ignoring it feels like protecting someone who seems like a different person—and neither side should be a strawman. No lecture up front, and don't use the philosophical labels until afterwards.

Then give me a debrief that maps what happened onto the tension between the authority of the earlier self's settled wishes (sometimes called precedent autonomy) and the interests of the present self, says where each side has to bite a bullet, says what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run and how to handle it (e.g. they reduce it to what the family would prefer, or to whether the original decision was well-informed), and is honest about what the role-play can't reproduce, like the actual phenomenolgy of dementia or how law and institutional pressure would shape the real decision.
