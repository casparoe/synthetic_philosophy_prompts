I'm a philosophy professor, and I sit on the executive committee of a small academic society that has run into a governance mess I'd like a proper philosophical take on. Our bylaws, which were written in the 1970s by people who apparently never imagined a three-way race, contain the following rule:

"The Committee shall resolve every contested election by a single procedure satisfying three conditions: (a) any member may cast a ballot ranking the candidates in any order; (b) no one member's ballot may determine the outcome in every possible set of ballots, that is, the procedure shall not be dictatorial; and (c) no voter shall ever have an incentive to submit a ranking other than their sincere one, whatever the other ballots say."

We always vaguely assumed something like majority rule with a sensible tie-break would do. But now we have three candidates and a genuinely split membership, and a colleague who does game theory tells us that, by the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, no procedure satisfies (a) through (c) once there are at least three candidates. The drafters clearly never considered that their conditions might be mutually inconsistent.

The comittee has asked me to advise them, and I don't just want a fix; I want the interpretive reasoning. What does the clause actually require or permit once we learn it cannot be satisfied as written? Walk me through the answers you get from (i) the plain words, (ii) the purpose the drafters most likely had, and (iii) what the drafters would probably have said if someone had shown them the theorem. Then say what you think we should actually do: abandon one condition, allow a dictator, tolerate some strategic voting, restrict which preference profiles can arise, or replace voting with something else altogether—and whether that amounts to obeying the rule or amending it. And, if you don't mind, could you write the response in British English? I'll be circulating it to the committee.
