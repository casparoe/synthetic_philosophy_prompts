The interpretability slogan I keep seeing in these threads is that we should want models that are understandable rather than merely correct. But the same people pushing it never seem to apply it to the human experts we already deploy. A radiologist can flag a tumor on "pattern recognition" and a long track record, with no ability to say exactly which pixels did the work, and everyone calls that medicine. Swap in a CNN with the same sensitivity and a cleaner, better-audited error rate, and suddenly correctness isn't enough: now we need feature attribution and a causal story before it can go anywhere near a patient. The human gets licensed and trusted on outcomes; the model is treated as guilty untill it can narrate itself. Nothing changed except the substrate.

Is there an actual principle that generates both judgments, or is it just that we're comfortable extending opacity-privilege to other humans? Give me the strongest steelman of the asymmetry you can build — something about our shared architecture and the possibility of cross-examination, something about human pattern recognition being less brittle out-of-distribution, something about only a human being sanctionable — and then tell me whether it survives the pressure cases. Put both behind a screen so you don't know which is which. Let the human's explanation be a post-hoc confabulation. Let the model be a conservative ensemble that abstains when unsure. And watch for the move where "explanation" quietly turns out to mean "a person I can blame." Does the asymmetry hold, should we reverse it, or should we give it up?
