I’m a junior at a public high school and the student representative on our district’s extracurricular eligibility committee. The committee is revising its policy on which activities count as “sports” for PE credit, varsity letter awards, and the academic eligibility rules that come with representing the school. A student group has petitioned for the varsity esports club to be recognized as a sport. Some committee members argue that esports lacks physical exertion and direct bodily competition; others reply that it has teams, coaches, regular practices, leagues, rankings, and intense mental discipline. In my philosophy elective we have been reading Ludwig Wittgenstein’s Philosophical Investigations, especially §§66–67, where he develops the idea of family resemblance by looking at the many different activities we call games. He refuses to assume that there must be one common essence shared by all of them, and instead says that if you actually look at board-games, card-games, ball-games, and the rest, you find a complicated network of overlapping similarities rather than a single common thread.

I have been asked to draft a short memo recommending whether the committee should classify competitive esports as a sport under our policy. Please write the memo from my perspective, addressed to the committee. It should use Wittgenstein’s family-resemblance thinking to examine the assumption that “sport” must have an essence, but it should also test whether esports shares enough of the relevant overlaps with traditional sports—organized competition, skill hierarchies, rule-governed play, institutional recognition, and whatever else seems relevant—to justify the classification, or whether the differences should make us treat it as a separate category. The memo should be exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. The final point should state a clear recommendation, and the memo should seriously engage the strongest argument on the losing side.
