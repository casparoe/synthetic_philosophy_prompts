I keep noticing a structural resemblance between two ideas from different fields, and I want to know whether it is deep or merely verbal. In Bayesian game theory, the common prior assumption says that all agents share a single probability distribution over the relevant states of the world, so that every difference in their posterior beliefs must be explained by differences in their private information. Rawls's original position seems designed to secure unanimity by an analogous move: the veil of ignorance strips away whatever would let a party identify their own place in society, their natural endowments, or their conception of the good, leaving parties who all reason from the same general facts through the same shared standpoint. Both devices, it seems, trace disagreement to something removable rather than to a brute divergence of fundamental perspectives. Is that a genuine identity of structure, or does the analogy come apart because the common prior assumption is a constraint on rational belief, whereas the original position is a device for reaching agreement about practical principles among people whose interests will still conflict once the veil is lifted? Lay out the mapping, say what would have to be true of both sides for a result to transfer, and identify one result on either side that the parallel would predict but that does not hold.
