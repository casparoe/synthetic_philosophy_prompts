A lecturer I heard recently summed up the Church-Turing thesis and its epistemic status in a way that struck me as confident, but I do not know how much of it to trust. His account ran roughly as follows. The thesis is the claim that every effectively computable function on the natural numbers is computable by a Turing machine. In the 1930s every serious attempt to formalize the intuitive notion of an algorithm—general recursive functions, the lambda calculus, Post systems—turned out to define exactly the same class of functions, and no one has produced a plausible counterexample since. He therefore said the thesis should be treated as a very well-confirmed empirical generalization about what can be computed. Quantum computers, he noted, do not threaten it, because they still compute only Turing-computable functions and merely speed up some computations. But, he continued, the thesis is in principle open to refutation: if physicists ever built a device that solved the halting problem, that would falsify it, which shows the thesis is a contingent claim about the natural world rather than a definition or a mathematical theorem. Given nearly a century of failed attempts at falsification, he concluded, we should regard it as established fact. I am uneasy about parts of this but cannot locate the errors myself. In particular, I suspect the inference from convergence of formalisms and absence of counterexamples to empirical law, and from a hypothetical halting-problem machine to a refutation of the thesis as usually stated, is too quick. Is this a fair summary? Go through it piece by piece and separate what a careful computability theorist would accept, what is a simplification, and what is misstated, and say whether the closing verdict survives the corrections.
