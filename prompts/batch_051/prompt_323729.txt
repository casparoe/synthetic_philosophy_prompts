I'm putting together a little self-test on the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism — the problem of how a sentence like "stealing is wrong" keeps its meaning when it shows up unasserted inside a conditional like "if stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong" — and I want to check whether there's a live option I'm forgetting. The solutions I can already produce from memory: (1) the Blackburn-style quasi-realist story, where what a moral conditional "expresses" is a higher-order attitude of endorsement or rejection toward combinations of attitudes, and that's supposed to do the logical work without real truth-conditions; (2) the Gibbard-style planning-state or norm-expressivism, which runs moral judgments through a space of complete norms or plans so that conditionals work by ranging over those; (3) the Schroeder "being-for" move, where you keep a single non-cognitive attitude and instead let the content it's aimed at carry the negation, disjunction, conditionals, and so on; (4) hybrid (or "ecumenical") expressivism, where each moral sentence expresses a conative state plus an ordinary descriptive belief, and the belief does the compositional work while the conative state does motivation. I'm leaving the minimalist/deflationist "we can help ourselves to truth talk" response aside, since it's always looked more like a patch on (1) than it's own solution.

I want one more proposed solution to the Frege–Geach problem that is not on that list, not a special case of one of them, and not a combination of them bolted together. It should take the embedding problem head-on rather than just relabel it. Then tell me straight whether it's any good, and whether it differs in kind from the four I listed or ends up collapsing into one of them.
