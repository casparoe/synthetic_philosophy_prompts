I keep noticing that the debate over whether AI alignment is "solvable in principle" sneaks in a notion of "in principle" from computability theory without anyone saying so. There, a problem is solvable in principle if some Turing machine solves it, resource bounds be damned. And there's a result, Rice's theorem, that says any nontrivial semantic property of a program—a property of its input/output behavior rather than its source code—is undecidable: no general algorithm can inspect an arbitrary program and tell you whether it has that property. "This policy is aligned with its human operators' intent" looks an awful lot like a semantic property. So one could conclude alignment is unsolvable in principle, full stop. But that feels too fast: alignment isn't a property of the code alone, since it depends on the environment and on what the humans actually want, and we aren't limited to black-box inspection anyway—we get to build and train the system. Is there a real transfer of structure here, or is Rice's theorem just a seductive analogy? What would have to be true of the word "aligned" for the undecidability to actually bite, and does its context-dependence make the problem harder or easier than the computability analogy suggests?
