I keep running into what looks like the same loop in two places that rarely cite each other, and I want to know whether the resemblance is structural or merely verbal.

On one side there is Ian Hacking's account of looping effects in the classification of people. Hacking points out that a psychiatric or social category—say, a diagnosis—does not merely label a pre-existing kind. People learn the label, take it up into their self-understanding, and begin to act in ways that fit, resist, or complicate it. The classification then has to be revised to keep up with the people it classified, who have changed partly because of the classification. Unlike electrons, which do not care how physicists classify them, the classified human kind is a moving target: classification changes the classified, which changes the classification.

On the other side there is Goodhart's law from the economics of measurement, in its familiar rough form: when a measure is selected as a target, those being measured adjust their behavior to the measure itself, and the measure loses its original informational value. Nearby ideas—Campbell's law about quantitative social indicators, the Lucas critique about policy and behavioral relationships—gesture at the same pattern: the act of measuring or intervening changes the thing being measured.

The parallel I keep seeing is this. In both cases a representation of human behavior enters the social world, the represented people become aware of it and respond to it, and the representation is thereby made less accurate for its original purpose. Both seem to run a feedback loop through the represented person's knowledge of the representation. My question is whether this is one phenomenon in two idioms, or whether the two loops are only superficially alike.

Three things I would like the answer to sort out.

First, in the Goodhart-style case the response looks instrumental and often strategic: agents game the number because rewards are attached to it. In the Hacking-style case the response looks interpretive: people come to understand themselves through the label, and what changes is self-description and identity, not merely an optimized indicator. Is that a difference in kind, or only a difference in emphasis?

Second, if the structures really do match, what would have to be true of each side for results to transfer? For instance, should we expect looping effects to show something like Goodhart-style collapse, where a classification becomes increasingly useless as people accommodate to it? And should Goodhart-style gaming show something like Hacking's moving target, where the target changes because the population itself has changed?

Third, what does the parallel predict that turns out false on one side or the other? I am looking for the place where the analogy snaps—or an argument that it was no more than pattern-matching to begin with.

Please write your answer in American English.
