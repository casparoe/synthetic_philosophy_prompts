I have been arguing with a colleague about how to characterize the competition between the leading AI labs, and I want to know whether one characterization in particular is answerable to evidence at all. My colleague's view is that the race is a commitment problem in the sense Fearon uses in his work on rationalist explanations for war: the major labs would each prefer a verified and binding slowdown to an unchecked race, but none can credibly promise to comply, since the lab that quietly scales after signing stands to gain a lead that compounds, which makes the agreement worthless and prevents any serious proposal from surviving. I do not trust this as a diagnosis. In Fearon's framework, rational actors can fail to reach a mutually beneficial bargain for three reasons: private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and issue indivisibility. In a messy, fast-moving technology race, each of those stories can be told after the fact, and the choice among them begins to look like a matter of taste rather than inference.

There is one feature of the AI case that might make the question more tractable than it first appears. Unlike states concealing force strength, these labs publish a great deal about their methods and sometimes about their compute, and they recruit from the same small pool of researchers; the deepest uncertainty may concern not what each lab can do now but what the technology will do over the coming years, and the very object being raced for is partly produced by whoever reaches it and in what manner. I would like you to design the strongest test you can of the claim that this particular race is best described as a commitment problem rather than as a problem of private information or of an indivisible issue, specifying what observations would count for and against each diagnosis, and then being explicit about what even a favorable result would leave unsettled—for example, the level at which the relevant agents act, the difference between a commitment problem that institutions could solve and one they could not, and the difference between a commitment problem and a shifting coordination game. I am not interested in a survey of prior work; I want the operational reasoning itself. Please answer in continuous prose, as in a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
