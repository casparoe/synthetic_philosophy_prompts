I chair the priority-setting committee at a county health board. Four times a year we allocate about $9 million across roughly 30 proposed programs, and mostly we let a cost-per-quality-adjusted-life-year formula rank them and fund the top of the list. A QALY, for anyone reading this later, is something like a year in full health counting as 1, and a year with a mild condition counting as some fraction. I keep hitting cases where the formula says to do something that feels off: make a small number of identifiable people worse off in a serious way so that a very large number of people each get a tiny benefit they might not even notice. I've been reading about the "separateness of persons" objection to utilitarianism — roughly the worry that summing utility across people, as if a loss to one person gets cancelled by a gain to someone else, treats a bunch of separate lives like one giant life, when no actual person ever receives the combined total. I don't want to bring a slogan to my committee; I want something we can run.

Constrainsts: there are three of us on the committee, none of us trained in philosophy; we get one 45-minute meeting before the board chair signs off, and we can't send anyone home to read Rawls. I need the thing to be reusable every funding round, not a memo about one decision.

Give me a pre-sign-off audit checklist. For each program that ranks near the top, the checklist should run through a small set of tests for whether the way we aggregate benefits is treating people as one person. For each test, say what a clear pass looks like, what a clear fail looks like, and where we should mark it as a judgment call rather than a mechanical answer. Be explicit about which steps are dressed-up judgment and where the procedure will misfire if we use it mechanically. Also say what the checklist cannot settle, so we know when the board just has to decide as a board.

Then work the checklist through these three applications:
(1) A rescue treatment for one child with a rare disease versus a campaign that mildly eases allergy symptoms for 40,000 people. The formula favors the allergy campaign.
(2) A screening program where we know in advance that several thousand healthy people will go through painful false-positive scares and some unnecessary biopsies, so that about 30 people get an early cancer catch.
(3) My own pet proposal, which I want to approve: divert money from a smoking-cessation program that saves the lives of heavy smokers into small amenity upgrades across dozens of clinics, because the amenities score better per dollar and staff and patients will like them.

Format: after you give the procedure in plain English, put it in a table with one row per step and these columns: Step or test; What we literally check; What a clear fail looks like; What a clear pass looks like; Where judgment cannot be eliminated; And note for ourselves. Then give a second table for the three worked applications, with columns: Application; Which step flagged it; What the procedure says; Whether I'm going to like that; What remains unsettled. If a field doesn't apply for a row, write "n/a."
