I was arguing with a friend who works in data science, and he fowarded me a tech-blog piece about the "impossibility results" in algorithmic fairness. The article summarized the math like this: "Researchers proved a few years ago that it is mathematically impossible for a risk-scoring algorithm to be fair. They showed that if you require the algorithm's scores to mean the same thing for every group — that's calibration — and you also require it to make mistakes at the same rate for every group — equal false-positive and false-negative rates — then the two demands can't both be satisfied once the groups have different base rates, which they do for basically any real outcome, unless the algorithm is essentially perfect. So this is a no-go theorem for fair machine learning: any attempt to make a model fair is guaranteed to fail, and the honest options are to stop deploying these systems for high-stakes decisions or to admit that the demand for fairness was never mathematically coherent to begin with."

My friend thinks this is roughly right, and that people who keep talking about "debiasing" models are basically ignoring a proof. To me the conclusion sounds way stronger than the math could possibly support, but I don't know the technical details well enough to say exactly where the article goes off the rails. Could you do an accuracy audit of that summary? In particular: what did the actual results show, which fairness conditions were in tension and under what assumptions, what caveats or degenerate exceptions does the summary brush past, and does anything in the real theorems support the jump to "fair algorithms are impossible" or "we should stop deploying them"? I'd also like to know whether a researcher in this area would call the article a fair popularization, a simplification, or a distortion, and whether the line about fairness never having been mathematically coherent is a fair inference or a category mistake.
