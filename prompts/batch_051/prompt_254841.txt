Ordinary economic commentary treats a central bank that buys government bonds with newly created reserves — or that credits the government's account to finance spending — as printing money, and widely regards that as a dangerous debasement of the currency. Yet when a commercial bank issues a mortgage and credits the borrower's account with a deposit that did not previously exist, the same commentary treats the transaction as ordinary credit intermediation, even though most of what is called money in a modern economy comes into existence through bank lending of exactly this kind. Both acts create new monetary claims, and both place someone in a position to command real goods and services. Is the asymmetry in this ordinary judgment justified? Set out the strongest candidate basis you can find — the backing of the new claims, their reversibility, who first receives the new purchasing power, the constraints on creation, or any other difference — test it against close cases, and give a verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned.
