I've been keeping a list of my gut reactions about where the indicative/counterfactual line falls, and I want to know if there's one principle that gets them all, or almost all, and which of my calls it makes me abandon. All the example sentences are ones I made up.

1. Our basement flooded while we were out. I'll happily say "If the plumber got my voicemail, he's on his way." I'm not claiming he got it, and I'm not claiming he's on his way; I'm just ruling out the combination where he got it and isn't on his way. But "If the plumber had gotten my voicemail, he would be on his way" feels like it commits me to a fact about an alternative course of events, and I get nervous about asserting it in a way I dont about the first.

2. We know the mayor either resigned or was pushed out, but not which. "If he wasn't pushed out, he resigned" is something I'll say flat out, as if it's licensed by my evidence alone. "If he hadn't been pushed out, he would have resigned" sounds like a claim about his psychology or the pressure he was under, and I could accept the first while thinking the second is probably false.

3. Same texture in a murder mystery: if I'm sure either the cook or the gardener did it, "If the cook didn't do it, the gardener did" is just what my evidence supports. "If the cook hadn't done it, the gardener would have" feels like a causal guess about the gardener's backup plans, and I'd withhold it.

4. A coin was sitting unflipped on the counter. "If I had tossed it, it would have landed heads or tails" is fine, and "If I had tossed it, it might have landed heads" is fine. But "If I had tossed it, it would have landed heads" is one I won't assert. It doesn't feel exactly false, just unsettled.

5. This one is what makes me think the subjunctive is about taking the antecedent to be false. A friend's match was yesterday and I haven't heard the result. "If she won, her coach is thrilled" is fine. But "If she had won, her coach would have been thrilled," said while I still don't know the result, sounds to me like I expect she lost. Yet "If she were to win tomorrow, her coach would be thrilled" doesn't hint she'll lose; it just sounds like scenario talk. So I cant tell whether the "false antecedent" feel is really about falsity or about something about the past tense.

6. Then there's a pair I don't know what to do with. In this order I'm totally comfortable: "If the cup had been dropped, it would have broken. But if the cup had been dropped onto the rug, it wouldn't have broken." In the reverse order I'm less sure, but I don't flat-out reject it. They look like they should conflict, but mostly they don't feel that way.

So: is there a coherent account of the indicative/subjunctive boundary that captures these, or nearly all of them? Please state the principle (or a small set of candidates) precisely, say which of my verdicts it can't keep, and tell me what it predicts about the case I keep going back and forth on: "If she's already in Paris, then I'm a hedgehog," said by someone who is convinced she isn't in Paris. Is that just an ordinary indicative with a silly consequent, or is it doing something special to the boundary? And does the account treat it differently from "If she were in Paris, I'd be a hedgehog"?
