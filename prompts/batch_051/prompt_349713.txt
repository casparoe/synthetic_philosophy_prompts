So I've been going down a rabbit hole on laws of nature after building a little toy physics engine and realizing that the "laws" in my code are just rules I wrote down, and the simulation isn't really "obeying" anything in a deep sense. I've mostly ended up on the view that laws of nature are just efficient summaries of what actually happens — the best compression of the total pattern of events, not some extra thing out there that pushes events around. (I think people call this the Humean or best-systems view?) It's appeal to me is that it avoids spooky governing and matches how we actually write models: find the simplest description that fits the data.

But I've started to worry that I only know the arguments for it, because I went looking for them on purpose. Suppose I'm wrong. What would count against this view? I want the defeaters ranked from "that's just a papercut" to "this whole picture is dead." In particular: what does this view predict that its rivals don't, and what would be awkward-but-survivable vs fatal to observe or prove? I've seen people mention uninstantiated laws — like a law about a situation that never actually comes up — as a problem, but I can't tell if that's a real crack or just an edge case they've papered over. And more generally: have defenders of this view bolted on enough caveats that it can dodge any evidence at all? If so, is that a bug or a feature for a view like this? For what it's worth, I'd personally find it most persuasive if someone showed me a concrete physical situation where the best summary of the past gets the future wrong but a "governing" law would get it right — or something along those lines.
