I am trying to get clear on whether the therapy/enhancement distinction can do real normative work, and I keep returning to a passage in the President's Council on Bioethics report Beyond Therapy (2003), in the section they call The Limitations of the Therapy vs. Enhancement Distinction. As I understand their argument, the authors grant that the distinction looks at first like a clean one between restoring normal health and going beyond it, but then they argue that it is less clean than it appears: judgments about normalcy and health are partly social and historical, many interventions can be redescribed either as treating a condition or as augmenting a capacity, and the distinction by itself does not tell us which uses of biotechnology we should welcome. Their larger claim is that what matters is not whether an intervention restores or exceeds a baseline but what ends it serves and whether those ends are worth wanting.

I want a definition that can survive that skepticism, not simply repeat the normal-health baseline. Give me a working definition of enhancement, or of the therapy/enhancement contrast, and run it through these cases: a vaccine given to a healthy person against a future disease; orthodontic braces for a child with no functional bite problem; a stimulant taken by a student without ADHD to study; an antidepressant taken by someone with ordinary grief; growth hormone for a child predicted to be very short but otherwise healthy; a cochlear implant; a beta-blocker taken by a violinist before auditions; and a gene edit that would raise a healthy person's working memory. For each case say whether your definition classifies it as therapy, enhancement, both, or neither, and then say which verdict is least secure and whether the Council was right that the distinction's real value is mostly rhetorical.
