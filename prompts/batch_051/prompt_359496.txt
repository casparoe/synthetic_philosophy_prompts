Here are the criteria I was taught for reading causal structure from observational data on a set of measured variables with no unmeasured common causes. A variable X is a direct cause of a variable Y just in case X and Y are statistically dependent conditional on every subset of the other measured variables, including the empty set; if X and Y become independent conditional on any such subset, I am to conclude that there is no direct causal connection between them. The accompanying rule is that, among the graphs satisfying these dependence conditions, the correct graph is the sparsest one.

Now consider this case. A subscription company measures three variables across customers: how deep a discount each customer is offered (D), how strongly the customer has learned to wait for sales rather than pay full price (W), and whether the customer churns in the following quarter (C). The causal story is this. Deeper discounts reduce churn directly, by making the service cheaper. Deeper discounts also increase sale-waiting, because customers learn to expect future discounts. And sale-waiting increases churn, because customers who hold out for a discount are more likely to quit when none arrives. Suppose the two paths from D to C — the direct negative path and the indirect positive path through W — exactly cancel, so D and C are statistically independent in the data overall. Yet when the analysts condition on W, D and C become strongly dependent. A colleague who runs the standard search reports a graph with no D–C edge, in which D and C appear as independent causes of W.

The growth team says the search result is correct: D is not a cause of C, since it makes no overall difference to churn; an A/B test varying D while allowing W to respond finds no average effect. The pricing team says the search result is misleading: D is a cause of C, because the association appears once W is held fixed, there is a well-understood mechanism, and a separate test that fixes W finds that deeper discounts lower churn.

Go through the criteria one by one. Does D count as a direct cause of C under the procedure I was taught? Which team is right, and does the case show that the criteria themselves need to be revised? In particular, should the requirement of dependence conditional on every subset be retained as a necessary condition for direct causation, and does the sparsest-graph rule survive this case?
