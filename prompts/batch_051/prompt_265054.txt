I keep noticing that my judgments about killing insects refuse to settle into a clean pattern, and I would like help seeing whether a single principle lies behind them, or whether some of the judgments should be revised.

Let me set the cases out. Swatting a mosquito that lands on my arm strikes me as completely fine, and I do not pause over whether it suffers. Treating a child's head lice with a chemical that kills every louse on her scalp also seems fine. Fumigating a house whose floor joists are being eaten by a termite colony seems fully justified, even though the termites are merely doing what termites do. An apple grower who sprays to stop codling moth larvae from ruining the crop seems to me to be acting acceptably, despite the enormous number of insects killed, though I grow uneasy when the grower uses a broad-spectrum insecticide that drifts into a hedgerow and kills pollinators that were never attacking the crop. A neighbor who drenches a lawn in soil insecticide purely so the grass looks perfect, killing grubs and ground beetles, seems excessive; I want to say it is not acceptable, or at least far harder to justify. And a child on our street who collects Japanese beetles and slowly crushes them for amusement seems to me to be doing something genuinely cruel, and I am confident that is wrong.

The working rule I used to rely on was that killing insects is permissible when it serves a serious human interest, and wrong when it serves no interest beyond amusement. That rule is now wobbling. Swatting a mosquito protects me from a bite and from possible disease, but the particular mosquito on my arm is usually harmless, so on the rule it should sit uncomfortably close to the child with the beetles. The notion of a serious interest is also doing a great deal of work: it has to make the orchard legitimate while making the perfect lawn illegitimate, and I cannot see why a marketable fruit crop counts as serious while a purely aesthetic goal at home does not. If aesthetic goals count, then I owe an explanation of why amusement does not.

I would like you to try to extract a principle from these reactions, or a small family of candidate principles. For each candidate, identify which of my cases motivates which clause; tell me what the principle says about cases I have not faced, such as a mosquito-control program in a malaria region, an entomologist who kills a few rare beetles in order to describe them taxonomically, or a gardener who hand-picks tomato hornworms and drops them into soapy water; and state plainly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate.
