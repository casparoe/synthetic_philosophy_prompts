I am a doctoral student in political theory finishing a paper on Han Feizi and the ethics of public administration, and in three weeks I defend it at my department's interdisciplinary workshop. The thesis I am defending is that a neo-Han Feizi approach to public administration—one that specifies officials' duties in terms of incentive-linked, publicly auditable outcomes rather than appeals to their cultivated virtue—is both more candid about the problem of hidden action and more democratically accountable than Confucian-style rule by virtue, at least for high-discretion, low-visibility offices such as social workers, inspectors, and licensing officers. The argument runs in three steps. First, I grant the virtue ethicist's point that these are exactly the roles where judgment and sound character matter most, and where rules underdetermine correct action. Second, I claim this fact cuts in the opposite direction from the one Confucians draw from it: because virtue is inward and unverifiable, making it the basis of accountability hides official power, whereas incentive structures are public objects that citizens can inspect, criticize, and revise. Third, I take from Han Feizi the institutional lesson that a well-ordered state runs on the systematic operation of reward and punishment rather than on the personal example of the ruler, and I argue that democratic accountability requires something like a visibility-first principle: officials should answer for the consequences their discretion predictably produces, not for the quality of their inner lives. I openly concede that Han Feizi himself was not a democrat and that his machinery of reward and punishment serves a sovereign's control; my claim is that the machinery can be rehoused in democratic institutions without losing its administrative virtues.

The audience is about twenty faculty and graduate students: roughly half are normative political theorists, several with republican sympathies about arbitrary power; a third work in comparative ethics or Confucian virtue ethics and are invested in the idea that institutions fail without cultivated character; two are public-administration scholars who study performance measurement, gaming, and intrinsic motivation; and there is one historian of early Chinese political thought who will catch exegetical sloppiness about Han Feizi. I expect the Confucians to say that virtue is not a compliance technology; the republicans to say that an incentive regime is itself a form of domination; the public-administration scholars to say that low-visibility settings are exactly where metrics get gamed; and the historian to say that I have read a concern for citizen accountability into a text whose only interest is ruler control.

Please give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order they will arise, phrased as this audience would actually phrase them, with how likely and how damaging each is, what I should concede and what I should hold, a one- or two-minute reply in each case, and a note on where that reply is soft. Identify the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss, and end with the question I should be praying nobody asks.
