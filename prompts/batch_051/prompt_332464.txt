For most of my career I worked as a molecular biologist, and since retiring I have been reading the philosophy of biology. I keep finding the gene's-eye view, as argued by Dawkins and Williams, both more powerful and more slippery than I expected. As I understand it, the view picks out a replicator as any entity of which copies are made, and it says natural selection will favor replicators only if they have three properties: longevity, fecundity, and copying-fidelity. A replicator with those properties can spread through a population across generations; a thing without them cannot really be an object of cumulative selection even if it sits inside an organism. The selfish gene is then simply a replicator whose success is measured by the number of copies of itself it leaves in the next generation, while the organism is demoted to a vehicle built by a coalition of such replicators.

Here is the case that troubles me, drawn from my old field, so I will supply the facts. A LINE-1 retrotransposon is a DNA sequence of roughly six thousand base pairs present in hundreds of thousands of copies in the human genome. The full-length active form encodes machinery for making copies of itself: it is transcribed into RNA, the RNA is reverse-transcribed into DNA, and that DNA is inserted at a new position in the genome. If this happens in a germ-line cell, the new copy is inherited and can repeat the cycle. Most LINE-1 copies are inactive fossils, truncated at the start and unable to encode the copying machinery, but a small number of active master elements keep generating new inserts, occasionally causing disease by landing inside a gene. The host seems to make no consistent functional use of these scattered copies and instead spends considerable effort trying to silence them through DNA methylation and small-RNA pathways. By the three criteria above, is an active LINE-1 element a replicator and therefore a unit of selection in the gene's-eye sense? Work through longevity, fecundity, and copying-fidelity one at a time and say where the case qualifies, where it fails, and where the criteria simply underdetermine the answer. Then tell me whether the case shows that the criteria need sharpening, whether the gene's-eye view can absorb a piece of repetitive DNA without strain, and what it would mean to call such an element selfish.
