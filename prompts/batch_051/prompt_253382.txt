You are a philosophy professor who specializes in decision theory and formal epistemology. Think carefully, and before committing to a diagnosis, brainstorm at least three candidate differences between the Newcomb case and the pill case below.

I'm in a graduate seminar on rational choice, and I built an argument to test whether Newcomb-style reasoning proves too much. I expected it to fall apart, but now I can't find a clean way out of it.

Standard Newcomb: a predictor who is 99% accurate has already either put $1,000,000 in an opaque box or left it empty, based on her prediction of whether you'll take only that box; a clear box holds $10,000 no matter what. I'm inclined to one-box, and the usual rationale is that my act is evidence about a state that is already fixed: if I two-box, that's very strong evidence the million isn't in the opaque box.

From that I extract:

(1) One-boxing is rational because your choice is evidence about a fixed, causally independent state (what the predictor already did).
(2) So an act's expected value can depend on what the act indicates about an already fixed good state, even when the act cannot causally influence that state.

Now the pill case. You don't know whether you carry gene G, fixed at conception, which will cause a fatal illness if present. A pill is available; taking it is free, harmless, and causally irrelevant to G and to the disease. But a robust actuarial correlation holds: among people with exactly your known features, 90% of pill-takers lack G while only 50% of non-takers lack it, because of some third factor such as a neighboring gene that affects how the pill tastes. By (2), taking the pill has higher expected utility than not taking it, so you should take it.

(3) But taking the pill cannot make you any less likely to carry G. You'd be doing it solely to be the kind of person who takes the pill, because that kind of person tends to be healthy. That looks like superstition with extra steps, and its hard to see where it stops: you'd also have to pay anyone who hands you a correlation table showing that some free, harmless act is associated with an already fixed good outcome, even when the mechanism is a mere confound.
(4) So either the pill conclusion is absurd and the one-boxer's reasoning proves too much, or one-boxing really does commit you to the pill and to the correlation-table acts.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Name the step you reject and explain why it looked fine; if the honest verdict is that no step is rejectable, say what the one-boxer would instead have to accept. I'd like to keep Newcomb one-boxing and avoid the pill conclusion, but I can't yet articulate a difference between the predictor's correlation and the genetic correlation that isn't special pleading. Please write your answer in American English.
