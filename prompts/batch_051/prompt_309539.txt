I am writing a paper on Schopenhauer's theory of music, and I keep getting stuck on a specific passage in §52 of the first volume of The World as Will and Representation. In that section, Schopenhauer argues that music stands apart from the other arts because it does not depict the Platonic Ideas, the determinate grades at which the will objectifies itself in nature. Instead, he says, music is a direct copy or image of the will itself, which is why it can affect us so much more powerfully than the other arts. To make this vivid, he develops an analogy between the four voices of harmony and the main grades of the will's objectification: the bass corresponds to the mineral kingdom, the tenor to the vegetable kingdom, the alto to the animal kingdom, and the soprano to the human being. Melody, he claims, reflects the inner life of human willing and motivation through its deviations from and returns to a tonic, with suspensions and modulations corresponding to obstacles, striving, and resolution.

My advisor says this voice-to-grades analogy is just an illustration, an expository aid that should not be pressed too hard. I suspect, however, that it is doing real philosophical work, and that pressing on it causes trouble for Schopenhauer's central claim about music. Here is my worry. If the bass stands to the mineral realm as the soprano stands to the human realm, then music still seems to be a copy of a kind of hierarchy, a structure of nature, even if it does not depict individual Ideas in the way a painting or poem does. That looks as though music remains tied to the objectification of the will in nature, just at a higher level of abstraction, and so it is not obviously the unmediated expression of the will itself that Schopenhauer wants it to be. The analogy would then undercut the very uniqueness of music that it is supposed to illustrate.

Please make the strongest possible argument for that objection. In particular, spell out exactly what the analogy commits Schopenhauer to, why those commitments conflict with his own definition of music as a direct copy of the will, and how someone who wanted to defend Schopenhauer would have to reposition the analogy to avoid the problem. Do not try to soften the objection; instead, develop it as pointedly as possible while keeping it philosophically precise.
