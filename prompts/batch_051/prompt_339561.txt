I'm taking a course on the deep human past and the origins of inequality, and I'm drafting a term paper on whether the transition from foraging to farming deserves to be called a mistake. Before I present it, I want a consistency check on the cluster of commitments I've settled into. My commitments are these. First, for the earliest adopters of farming, the transition was a welfare decline: the archaeological record of early agricultural populations indicates reduced stature, more dental caries, more infectious disease, and more hours of repetitive labor than the foraging lifeways they replaced. Second, I take living hunter-gatherers as relevant evidence that pre-agricultural life was better than the standard grim picture suggests, since their substantial leisure, mobility, and relative egalitarianism support the idea that foragers can be in a meaningful sense affluent without accumulating much. Third, I accept that living foragers are not windows onto the Neolithic or Paleolithic past: they mostly live on marginal lands unwanted by agricultural states, and centuries of trade, displacement, and interaction with states have shaped their societies, so they cannot be read as unchanged survivors. Fourth, on inequality, I hold that durable and heritable forms of hierarchy, such as stable class distinctions, inherited status, and pronounced gender subordination, required the storable surplus and fixed property that farming made possible, and that foraging societies lacked the material basis for inequality of that kind. Fifth, I am nevertheless not willing to say the agricultural revolution was a mistake all things considered, because the later populations, institutions, and bodies of knowledge depend on it, and because I do not think the value of a historical transition should be judged solely by average individual welfare. I suspect some of these sit awkwardly together, but I cannot tell where the real fault lines are. Please audit the set rather than lecture on the topic: tell me which pairs genuinely conflict, which only appear to conflict, and rank the genuine conflicts by severity. For each genuine conflict, say which commitment would have to give and what the least costly revision would be, and then say whether a single revision to one commitment would clean up most of the set. I would prefer your answer as continuous prose, the way you might write an email or letter: no headings, bullet points, bold type, or tables.
