Aristotle's account of phantasia, the capacity standardly translated as imagination, rests on a three-way distinction among perception, phantasia, and thought. Perception requires a present sensible object. Phantasia does not: it is the capacity in virtue of which an image appears to a mind, and it operates when no object is present—in dreams, in remembering a face, and in deliberately calling a scene before the mind. Aristotle distinguishes phantasia from perception because it lacks perception's direct grip on its object and can be false where the perception of a proper object (such as the eye's perception of color) does not go astray. He distinguishes it from thought and belief as well, in part because many nonhuman animals, on his view, have phantasia without holding beliefs or exercising reason. Yet he also assigns phantasia an indispensable role in the intellect: on his account, the soul thinks in and through images, and no act of thinking is exercised without them, even when the object of thought is abstract.

Now suppose a counterfactual being: in every ordinary respect an adult human—normal senses, language, retained information, inferential competence—except that phantasia has been entirely excised. No image ever appears in its mind. It cannot picture an absent object, recombine remembered appearances into a novel one, or dream in images. Everything else is stipulated to remain the same, unless that stipulation is already incoherent on Aristotle's principles.

Work out, with reasoning, what this being can and cannot do, staying strictly inside Aristotle's framework rather than appealing to contemporary empirical findings about imagery or its absence. Go faculty by faculty—memory of particular events, deliberation about future particulars, desire and motivation, practical skill and craft, mathematical and abstract reasoning, and the acquisition of new concepts—and say for each whether Aristotle's commitments force the verdict impossible, impaired, altered, or unchanged, identifying the commitment that does the forcing. Then run the arrow in the other direction. If this being nonetheless reasons abstractly and deliberates successfully without phantasia, which specific part of the Aristotelian account breaks: the necessity claim tying thought to images, the distinction between phantasia and belief, or something else? If instead those activities collapse, what exactly has been shown—that phantasia is a material condition of thought, a dispensable accompaniment, or something in between? I want the chain of reasoning laid out step by step, with an explicit statement of where Aristotle's position compels a prediction and where the prediction depends on how charitably one interprets the claim that the soul thinks with images.
