I've been reading philosophy of biology since I retired, and a move I keep running into — from people who should know better — is this: natural selection is just gradient descent on a fitness landscape, the same blind hill-climber we train neural nets with. They use it to licence a strong conclusion: since a gradient-follower never looks ahead, any talk of organisms as 'designed' is empty, and evolution's lack of foresight is exactly what we should expect given the algorithm. But I've also heard it run the other way: deep learning shows a stupid local optimiser can produce extraordinarily intricate structure, so selection not having a plan is no objection to adaptation being genuinely design-like after the fact.

Before I take sides I'd like the analogy audited. Set out the correspondence table — what maps to the loss function, the gradient, the step size, the training run, the parallel restarts — and then the places the mapping breaks, separating disanalogies that actually matter for the design question (say, fitness not being a fixed objective, or recombination not being a gradient step) from the ones that are mere cosmetics. Does either conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is the phrase 'evolution is gradient descent' doing all the work?
