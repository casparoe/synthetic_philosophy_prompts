I'm a novelist, about a third of the way into a book where a family sues a county after a flood-control project backfires and ruins their farm. The county's lawyer moves to dismiss on sovereign immunity, and my protagonist—a stubborn farmer who starts reading law books at night—gets fixated on the old maxim that the king can do no wrong. Here's the thing I keep tripping on: if the state can't be sued without its consent, doesn't that mean the law is effectively commited to saying the state cannot do wrong, that there's no legal standpoint from which its acts can even be called violations? And if so, how can the same system also talk about constitutional rights against the state, or say an official acted unlawfully? I don't need the plot solved, but I want to know what the doctrine actually commits a person to. Is sovereign immunity a claim that the state is morally or legally infallible, or is it something narrower, like a rule about which remedies a court can give? And when my protagonist starts ranting in the deposition scene, what should he be getting right, and what should the county's lawyer be smugly wrong about?
