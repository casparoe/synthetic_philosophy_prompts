I am a novelist, and I have spent enough years around publishing to find something strange about the way a book's reputation forms. A handful of early readers — an editor, a scout, a lead reviewer — make large and highly visible gestures of commitment: a major advance, a starred review, a longlisting before publication. Then other people who privately admit the book struck them as mediocre fall into line, and within a season the title is simply known to be important. When I press individual critics, several will concede that their private judgment diverged from their public praise. The pattern reminds me of the information cascades studied by economists and decision theorists: each actor holds a private signal (their own reading of the text), observes the public judgments of those who moved earlier, and rationally discounts the private signal because the accumulated public record looks more informative than one's own reaction. The result is a consensus that can float free of any individual's actual assessment — an aggregate judgment that no one privately endorses.

I cannot decide whether this is a genuine instance of the model or only a loose analogy. On the side of the parallel: publishing is at least partly sequential, reputational actions are observable, and early movers anchor the choices that follow. Against it: reviewers all read the same book, so their private signals are not independent draws conditional on the book's quality; they may be almost perfectly correlated through the text itself. Dissent also carries reputational costs that have nothing to do with statistical inference from prior choices. I would like you to adjudicate whether the claim that a novel becomes canonical is an information cascade, a case of rational herding under payoff externalities, or neither — and whether the parallel is deep or superficial. Please state the mapping precisely, say which assumptions of the cascade model transfer to literary prestige and which fail, and name at least one thing the parallel would predict that turns out to be false in actual publishing.
