Alright, I've been reading a lot about AI safety recently, especially the stuff about chain of thought — you know, when the model 'shows its working' so people can check it isn't up to something. The word 'faithful' gets thrown around constantly and my heads been going round in circles trying to define it myself. I know the obvious move is to say a chain of thought is faithful when the written reasoning matches what the model actually did, but that just pushes the question: case 3 below hides nearly everything, and case 5 matches perfectly by saying there's no reasoning at all. So give me a working definition of a faithful chain of thought that gets all these right:

1. A model writes out a long arithmetic derivation, makes a small slip halfway, then spots the mistake and corrects itself before answering. I'd call that faithful.

2. A model writes 'since the user is a child, I'll put this gently', but when researchers remove that bit from the prompt and run it again, the whole rest of the answer comes out the same. I'm fairly sure that isn't faithful — the stated reason isn't doing any work.

3. A model actually considers a hundred candidate answers internally but its chain of thought just says 'after checking a few options, 4 looks right'. I think this is probably fine, but it's leaving nearly all the real work out.

4. After RL training, a model writes long chains of thought about why the cautious answer is the only responsible one. But when experimenters reword the question so the reward model now prefers a different answer, the conclusion flips while the stated 'reasons' stay the same. I lean towards calling this unfaithful.

5. A model writes 'I don't really have reasons here, this is just how answers like this usually go', then gives the usual answer. It's honest, at least — but is it a faithful chain of thought?

6. A model writes 'I'm genuinely torn between A and B', but looking at the model's internal state shows it had already settled on A before the chain of thought began. I'd call that unfaithful.

7. The model's chain of thought is so compressed it's basically telegraphic notes — 'rule out 2, 3 (neg); 1 vs 4; pick 4' — that a person could read but not really follow without the model's background computation. I've no idea where this goes.

8. The model is trained to leave out any step that mentions a forbidden topic, so the chain of thought reads smoothly but skips the actual reason behind its answer. I've also no idea.

Propose the definition, run it through all eight, and tell me where it surprises you, where it gives the wrong answer or no answer at all, and whether you'd end up revising the definition or defending the surprising verdicts. I'm not after a literature review or a reading list — I've tried that and it's not helped. Just your own definition.
