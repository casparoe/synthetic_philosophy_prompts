I'm a PhD student in a systems-neuroscience lab, and a postdoc here has been trying to build a principled way to tell when an unexpected result in a mouse model is an anomaly *in* the same mechanism versus evidence that the mouse is riding a different mechanism altogether. In lab meeting he gave an argument I've been chewing on because it seems to prove way to much, but I can't shake it.

His argument went:

(1) Two systems share a mechanism only if they have relevantly the same components, activities, and organization.  
(2) "Relevantly the same" means the same with respect to every feature that actually matters for the mechanism's output across its normal operating range.  
(3) But what the normal operating range is only gets fixed once you know which background conditions are inside the mechanism and which are external context.  
(4) And the inside/outside split is itself fixed by which factors make a difference to the output — so drawing that boundary already uses exactly the relevance facts you were trying to discover.  
(5) Therefore, saying two systems share a mechanism presupposes a prior choice about which differences matter, and no two real biological systems are identical in every feature anyway.  
(6) So "same mechanism" and "different mechanism" are never discovered; they are stipulated. Mechanistic anomaly detection is just enforcing that stipulation.

I don't buy (6) — if it were true, half of what we do with animal models would be disguised taxonomy. But I genuinely can't put my finger on the first wrong step, and neither could anyone else in the lab. Diagnose the thing for me: which premise or transition should I reject, why did that step look so right, and what does an account of mechanism identity need to say instead so that we can still discover that two systems share a mechanism and that a particular outcome is an anomaly rather than a different mechanism? Also tell me what rejecting it costs — what kind of mechanism-individuation project it rules in or out. Finish with a single line in the form \answer{reject X: short diagnosis}, where X is the number of the premise or the transition you'd reject.
