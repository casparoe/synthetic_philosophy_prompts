Two analysts on a working group I contribute to got stuck arguing about how to model a pair of rare post-surgical complications, call them A and B. Marginal rates for each are available from aggregate reporting, but there is no linked patient-level dataset from which their co-occurrence could be directly estimated, and no established physiological mechanism connects them. The working group needed a joint risk estimate for preoperative counseling. Here is the disagreement roughly as it unfolded.

Maya opened by saying that with the marginal rates known and no evidence or known mechanism linking A and B, the right default is to treat them as independent; any other joint model would be inventing correlation out of nothing.

Ron replied that independence is not the absence of an assumption but an extremely specific one. The space of joint distributions consistent with the two marginals is large, and almost every point in it involves dependence. "We don't know of a connection" is not evidence that no connection exists, he said, so the presumption of independence dresses up a substantive claim about the world as a neutral starting point.

Maya answered that they had to produce a number, and independence is the least committal choice: it is the maximum-entropy distribution when the only constraints are the marginals. Any move away from independence would require choosing a direction and strength of dependence, which would be importing more information than the data or the literature supplies.

Ron said maximum entropy is only as neutral as the constraints one chooses to impose. Fixing the marginals and stopping there is itself a modeling decision. A clinician's background knowledge includes latent common causes—overall patient frailty, hidden variation in surgical complexity, quality of postoperative care—that would plausibly make A and B positively associated across a heterogeneous patient population. Conditional on that background knowledge, he argued, the least informative well-calibrated prior would not be independence.

Maya countered that such background knowledge is vague and unquantified, and that the working norm in this kind of modeling is to start simple and add structure only when the data demand it. That norm, she said, is what keeps a group from overfitting an imagined dependence to noise.

Ron said this was now a different argument. Maya had started by claiming independence is the least committal representation of what is known, and had shifted to claiming it is a useful bias–variance heuristic. Those are separate theses. Moreover, the pragmatic argument cuts both ways: assuming independence will systematically pull the joint probability of A and B downward, and the joint probability is exactly the quantity the counseling tool exists to communicate. When the cost of underestimating co-occurrence is high, the computationally convenient default is not obviously the clinically appropriate one.

Maya conceded the cost asymmetry but said it was a reason to do sensitivity analysis around an independence baseline, not a reason to replace the baseline itself. Ron had not, she said, proposed an alternative default that was not at least as arbitrary.

Ron took that as a direct challenge. A better default is available at the level of the generating process, he said: model A and B as conditionally independent given shared latent factors, with population-level dependence emerging from variation in those factors. In that framework, independence is a special case at one endpoint—zero shared variation—rather than the neutral center of the space of models. The burden, he argued, is on anyone who puts the default at zero to explain why zero shared variation should be privileged.

Maya replied that the latent-process story is appealing but does not by itself commit one to any particular strength or direction of dependence, so as a default it is nearly as underspecified as simply saying "some dependence." Without linked data to estimate the shared variation, she said, she still needed a number, and anchoring at zero while stress-testing the result was the only disciplined procedure on the table. Ron answered that a disciplined procedure can be disciplined at the wrong coordinate, and that privileging zero shared variation is a choice, not an absence of one. They went in circles from there, and no one in the room could cleanly separate the points.

Please adjudicate this exchange point by point. For each move, say which claims are sound, which are overstated or confused, and where the two are talking past each other. Then state what a defensible version of a default-independence principle would actually say when used in this kind of applied modeling, what it is and is not a justification for, and what the procedure should do when the costs of the two directions of error are asymmetric.
