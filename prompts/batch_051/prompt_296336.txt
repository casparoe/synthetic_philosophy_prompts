Not a philosopher — I'm a backend engineer — but I've been chewing on an idea for a while and I want somebody to tell me if it's real or if I'm badly reinventing something obvious.

I've noticed there are two kinds of deterministic systems, and I've started calling them skippable and unskippable (no idea if theres real vocabulary for this). A system is skippable if, given the rule and starting state, you can jump straight to the state at time T without simulating every step. The bouncing-ball-in-a-box thing is like that: you can unfold the reflections and do one straight-line computation instead of stepping through the whole bounce history. A glider zipping across a Conway Life grid is another: after you spot the pattern, you know where it'll be in 10,000 generations, you just translate it.

Then there are the other ones. I got nerd-sniped last month and wrote a little 1D cellular automaton engine, and some rules are basically static forever: row 10,000 is just row 10,000. To know row 10,000 you have to run rows 1 through 9,999. You can rewrite it in CUDA or precompute chunks or whatever, but you're still doing the whole run, only faster. The shortcut doesn't just feel hard; it feels like it isn't there.

So here's the theory I want checked. The cheapest way to know the future state of an unskippable system is to run it. Therefore, for an unskippable system, there's no explanation shorter than the system itself. Any "theory" of it is at best a partial approximation, and the complete understanding of step T just is the run. So for systems that look unskippable to me — weather, an economy, maybe a brain — the idea of finding a compact theory that explains and predicts them is alot like asking for a shortcut that provably doesn't exist. Explanation bottoms out in simulation.

I keep flip-flopping. Sometimes it seems obviously true (there's got to be a length limit on how much a description can compress a system's behavior). Sometimes it seems obviously false, because we do have useful shortcuts for weather and economies, and I don't have to run a head-on collision in my physics engine to know roughly what happens. Maybe I'm conflating exact-state prediction with higher-level understanding, and those are different jobs.

So: is this distinction coherent, or is it shot through with holes? If it's already a named idea in CS theory or philosophy, tell me the name and where my version gets it wrong. And does the big leap actually follow — that explanation has a hard limit and is just re-running — or am I smuggling that conclusion in?
