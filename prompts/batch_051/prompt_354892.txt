After an argument with my advisor about whether historians can ever escape their own time, I wrote down the five things I keep coming back to, and I honestly can't tell whether they form a position or just a pile of intuitions. (1) There's no neutral, ahistorical standpoint: every historian writes from inside their own time and uses concepts and concerns the people they're studying mostly didn't have. (2) But historians should still avoid presentism in the pejorative sense—you don't condemn a twelth-century person for lacking modern ideas about human rights, and where you can, you should explain what people did from inside their own self-understanding. (3) At the same time I think some things are just wrong, full stop—the transatlantic slave trade, say—and saying it's wrong only by our lights is a cop-out. (4) I also think descriptions of the past are never purely neutral: terms like religion, state, slavery, economy already carry evaluations, so you can't fully seperate describing from judging. (5) But I'm not a relativist, because the evidence does push back and some readings of a period are just worse than others.

Do these hang together? I'd like an audit rather than a lecture: which of these genuinely conflict, which pairs only look like they conflict, ordered by how severe the tension is, and what the cheapest revision would be plus what it would cost. And be self-critical about your own verdict—where is your reasoning most likely to go wrong, and what argument would most likely change your mind about which commitment has to give?
