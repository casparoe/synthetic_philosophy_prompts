ok so I've been rereading Nick Bostrom's "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" and it keeps tripping me up in a place I didn't expect. Here's the passage:

"Suppose that these simulated people are conscious (as they would be if the simulations were sufficiently fine-grained and if a certain quite widely accepted position in the philosophy of mind is correct). Then it could be the case that the vast majority of minds like ours do not belong to the original race but rather to people simulated by the advanced descendants of an original race. It is then possible to argue that, if this were the case, we would be rational to think that we are likely among the simulated minds rather than among the original biological ones. Therefore, if we don’t think that we are currently living in a computer simulation, we are not entitled to believe that we will have descendants who will run lots of such simulations of their forebears."

Here's the pile of things I think I'm committed to; I want a consistency audit, not a tutorial:

(1) Bostrom's trilemma is sound, and I take the simulation prong seriously—I'd put maybe 30%-ish credence on our being in a simulation, which feels like enough that it shouldn't just sit inert in a corner of my worldview.

(2) I'm an expected-value longtermist: if an action has even a very small chance of steering an astronomically large future, that can swamp the near-term tradeoffs.

(3) Simulated people count. If this is a simulation, "the far future" just means the far future of the simulation, and the lives downstream of us inside it matter just as much as any base-reality lives would.

(4) Most ancestor simulations get switched off before the simulated civilization becomes spacefaring or posthuman. The simulators get their data and stop; they're not paying to grow a whole new branch of history.

(5) I think the reason to take (1) seriously is a self-sampling assumption: I should reason as if I'm a random observer (or observer-moment) from the appropriate reference class. I want to apply that uniformly, not just when it gives me a wow-sounding result.

(6) But when I actually do my longtermist prioritization, it comes out looking like a non-simulation longtermist's: focus on not dying off, then on the next centuries and beyond. I don't treat simulation scenarios as a special reason to change any of that.

Go through the set and tell me wheter it hangs together. Please don't just conclude that everything is in tension; I want discrimination. Structure the response as:

- "Ranked tensions" (~350 words): the genuine conflicts, each with the two commitments involved, how bad it is (say out of five), and what the cheapest fix would cost.
- "Apparent tensions that dissolve" (~150 words): pairs that look problematic but on inspection aren't, and why.
- "The cheapest single revision" (~150 words): the one change that cleans up the most, and what I'd be sacrificing by making it.
