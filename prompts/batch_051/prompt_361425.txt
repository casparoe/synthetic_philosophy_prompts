In a graduate seminar on the problem of evil, I pressed the evidential version of the argument: if an all-powerful and all-good God existed, we should not expect the sheer quantity and distribution of apparently pointless suffering we actually see — much of it horrendous, and much of it falling on children and animals, where the usual appeals to soul-making or free will seem least credible. A classmate responded, "God works in mysterious ways," and moved on as if that closed the matter. I don't think it does, but I also don't want to simply accuse him of changing the subject. I know the rough difference between a theodicy, which supplies actual justifying reasons, and a defense, which only shows compatibility; and I've heard the term "skeptical theism" for the move that, given our cognitive limits, we should not expect to see the reasons for particular evils. But I can't tell which of these — if any — the slogan is really asserting, and there seem to be several different claims hiding in it.

Lay out the distinct precise claims "God works in mysterious ways" could be making in this argumentative context. For each reading, say what kind of claim it is (a claim about God's nature, about our epistemic position, about the proper attitude toward evil, about the state of the evidence, or something else), whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or question-begging, and whether it actually answers or undermines the evidential argument rather than merely deflecting it. In particular, I want to know which readings commit to there being actual reasons for each evil that are simply hidden from us, which readings claim only that we are badly placed to judge whether any given evil is gratuitous, and whether the slogan can do any of this work without already assuming that God exists. Present the readings as a menu I can bring back to the seminar, and flag which reading the slogan would need to mean for my classmate's use of it to have any argumentative force.
