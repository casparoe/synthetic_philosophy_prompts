For nearly fifty years mathematicians have been unable to agree about whether computer-assisted proofs—the four-colour theorem, the Kepler conjecture, and, more recently, large machine-checked derivations—are really proofs. The early objections, about bugs in the code and trust in the case lists, have largely been answered: the computations have been reproduced, and in some cases fully formalised. Yet the philosophical standoff remains. One side holds that a proof is a certificate that can be checked even when no single human can survey every step; the other holds that a proof must be surveyable by a human mind, and regards an unsurveyable computation as a report of an experiment rather than a demonstration. My working hypothesis is that this is not a mathematical disagreement at all but a disagreement about the purpose of proof—whether its job is to guarantee truth or to produce understanding. Is that diagnosis right, or is there still a substantive question about reliability, fallibility, or what checking consists in that the two sides are really disputing? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, grounded in what each side actually says, and an honest account of what would count as progress: a distinction, a theorem, a reformulation of the question, or an argument that the impasse is permanent.
