In his 1968 paper “Prior Probabilities,” E. T. Jaynes argues, roughly, that when our information consists only of a specified set of constraints on a parameter, the unique prior that uses all and only that information is the one maximizing entropy subject to those constraints; any other prior would smuggle in assumptions for which there is no evidence. A friend has now walked me through an argument that this principle proves too much, and I need to know where it goes wrong.

Suppose our only information about a positive quantity θ is that it lies in the interval [0,1]. By Jaynes’s rule, the rational prior is uniform: p(θ)=1 on [0,1]. But the same information can equally well be expressed by saying that φ=θ³ lies in [0,1]. Applying Jaynes’s rule to φ gives q(φ)=1 on [0,1]. Since these priors express the same state of information, they ought to be related by the usual probability transformation: q(φ)=p(θ(φ))·|dθ/dφ|=(1/3)φ^(−2/3). They are not. So Jaynes’s rule yields two inconsistent “rational” priors from one body of information, apparently showing that maxent priors are representation-dependent. Since maxent is the most plausible route to objective Bayesianism, the conclusion seems to be that there are no rational priors at all.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like to keep the idea that maxent gives the right answer in simple cases like this, but I cannot see how to block the move from θ to φ without giving up the claim that priors represent information rather than arbitrary coordinate choices.
