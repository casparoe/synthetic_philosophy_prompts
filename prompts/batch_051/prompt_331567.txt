Epistemic status: armchair, and I keep going back and forth on this one, so I'd love someone to actually work it through.

I want to use the Challenger launch decision as a case study for interpretations of probability, because i think it's a rare case where the philosophical stance actually would have changed what people did. Nobody ever seems to do the mapping carefully, so: do it.

The facts as I'm taking them for the exercise: January 28, 1986. The night before launch, engineers at Morton Thiokol worried about the SRB field-joint O-rings, because the forecast was for unusually cold weather and cold makes the rubber lose resiliency. There had been O-ring erosion on warmer flights before, but no shuttle had ever launched below the low 50s (°F), so there was basically no empirical track record at anything like these temperatures. The engineers recommended delaying; NASA pushed back, including the infamous line about taking off your engineering hat and putting on your management hat, or something close to it, and Thiokol management reversed the recommendation. The launch went, the O-rings failed, and all seven crew members died.

Here's the part I want in the weeds. Take the proposition: 'at near-freezing temperatures, the field-joint O-rings will fail enough to destroy the vehicle.' Under frequentism, what even is that probability? Which reference class does it belong to, and what does the absence of prior cold-weather launches do to the estimate—should 'no data below 53°F' read as 'no evidence of danger' or as 'no reference class to even form a frequency in'? Under subjectivism, the engineers' worry looks like a legitimate degree of belief grounded in a physical model plus sparse evidence, but then so does a NASA manager's lower number grounded in a prior that the system is generally safe—subjectivism alone doesn't tell us whose prior to use, and maybe just relocates the disagreement. Under a propensity view, the probability is a physical property of the O-ring material at low temperature, which is nice because it makes the risk real whether or not it's been measured often enough, but then how is a propensity ever operationalized except through a subjectively chosen model?

What I'd like from you: (1) map the launch decision from the inside for each interpretation—what counts as evidence, what probability or range each would have assigned that morning, and what each would have recommended; (2) say which interpretations the two sides were tacitly running, and whether NASA's demand to 'prove it's unsafe' is fairly read as a frequentist burden of proof or just a misuse of frequentism; (3) after the disaster, which interpretation ends up looking best and which breaks, and whether the episode is an argument for seperating the metaphysics of probability from decision rules for engineering safety. If your answer is that the whole disagreement was really about decision theory or organizational incentives and not probability at all, defend that too—i genuinely don't know where the boundary should go.
