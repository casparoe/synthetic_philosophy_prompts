I'm a philosophy of language PhD student, and I keep going back and forth on whether there's a real parallel here or just a shared word. The absurdity doctrine says a court can decline to apply a statute's plain text when the literal result would be so absurd that the legislature couldn't of meant it. Davidson's principle of charity says that when interpreting a speaker you should pick the reading that makes them come out mostly rational and often right, instead of attributing crazy beliefs. Both seem to be the same rule: don't impute absurdity to an author if a more sensible reading is available. But the settings pull apart — a court is hemmed in by enacted text and the legislature's authority, while charity is supposed to be a precondition for interpretation at all, not a doctrine you invoke on special occasions. Is there something deep here about interpreting under a presumption of reasonableness, or does the analogy break as soon as you ask where the sensible reading comes from and who gets to decide it? If the comparison is confused, just say so.
