For an article on whether an endless life could be desirable, I have been reading both sides of the debate touched off by Bernard Williams's Makropulos case — the woman in Karel Čapek's play who, after several centuries, finds her deathless existence emptied of point. One analogy keeps reappearing in the critics' camp. A human life, they argue, is like a story or a sonata: its episodes derive their meaning from being gathered into an arc, and the arc matters only because it ends. An immortal life would therefore be a narrative with no cadence, and its pleasures would not accumulate but go flat. From this the critics conclude that eternal bliss is not a coherent hope, only tedium under another name.

I want an audit of this analogy rather than a verdict on heaven. If pressed, what corresponds to what — episodes to scenes or movements, death to the final cadence, desire to plot, the person to the protagonist? Which features of stories and sonatas does the analogy quietly import that a defender of eternal bliss need not accept: retrospection, selective editing, scarcity of time, themes engineered to demand resolution? Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are incidental, and distinguish the version of eternal life the analogy can actually wound — an indefinitely extended sequence of temporal human projects — from the stricter theological picture of a changeless enjoyment that has no episodes to close. Does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it? If the analogy fails, what would a better comparison for the defenders' claim be, and what does that better comparison license?
