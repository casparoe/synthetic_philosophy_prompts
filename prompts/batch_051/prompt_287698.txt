I keep coming back to the Chase and Sanborn coffee tasters case and I think there's a stronger anti-qualia argument in it than people usually say. Here's my reconstruction, with an example. Suppose a coffee taster says, after years on the job, that he used to like the taste and now he doesn't, and he insists the coffee itself hasn't changed. Dennett's point is that there are at least two readings: his coffee-quale changed, or his standards changed. Now:

(1) If qualia are real, then when a subject has a quale, introspection can tell them whether it stays the same or changes over time.
(2) The coffee taster cannot tell by introspection whether his coffee-quale changed or his standards changed.
(3) So if qualia are real, introspection is not authoritative about them.
(4) If introspection is not authoritative about qualia, then qualia are not the private, incorrigible felt qualities qualia-friends claim.
(5) Since private incorrigible felt qualities are the only thing the term 'qualia' was introduced to name, qualia are an incoherent posit.

Is this valid? If not, which step fails exactly, and what would a qualia defender's strongest repair be? If it is valid, say so and defend the conclusion. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
