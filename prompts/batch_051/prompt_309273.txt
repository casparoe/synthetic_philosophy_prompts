Fernand Braudel never lived to see the Anthropocene become a big deal, but the arguments it set off among environmental historians are basically on his home turf: slow structures, deep time, and whether events matter much at all. I keep flip-floping on how he'd come down. On one read he'd love it: humans messing with climate, soil, and coastlines is exactly the kind of slow-moving material force he thought historians ignored while they chased kings and treaties. On another read, his whole framework depended on keeping timescales apart — the near-still longue durée of geography and climate down below, the buzzing short term of events up top — and the Anthropocene collapses that distinction, because ordinary human activity is now supposedly doing geological work. So what do you think he'd have made of it? Would he take it as vindication of the longue durée, or as a category mistake, or some third thing? Give me your best guess at his actual likely position, and flag the place in your reconstruction where you're least sure.
