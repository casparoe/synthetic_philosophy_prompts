Karl Marx writes in the *Grundrisse* (Notebook VII, on the Chapter on Capital):

"But to the degree that large industry develops, the creation of real wealth comes to depend less on labour time and on the amount of labour employed than on the power of the agencies set in motion during labour time, whose 'powerful effectiveness' is itself in turn out of all proportion to the direct labour time spent on their production, but depends rather on the general state of science and on the progress of technology, or the application of this science to production."

Consider a present-day firm that licenses a large language model to draft legal documents. Two engineers who maintain the model subscription and two lawyers who supervise the outputs replace a department of forty junior lawyers; the firm's billings hold steady. Marx did not foresee this specific technology. Interpret the passage and apply it to this case. Give your response as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Cover: (1) what the passage literally claims about the relation between labour time and wealth; (2) whether the legal-services firm is an instance of the tendency Marx describes, and why or why not; (3) what the passage implies about where the value of the final legal documents comes from under Marx's categories; (4) the strongest objection to treating the firm as confirming the passage; and (5) what the passage's own logic commits Marx to saying about whether the two engineers and two lawyers are the workers whose labour time matters here.
