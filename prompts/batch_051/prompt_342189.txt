I've been teaching Goodman's new riddle this week — the grue/bleen thing — and i keep telling students the standard moral: seeing only green emeralds before time t gives you exactly the same evidence for "all emeralds are green" and for "all emeralds are grue," and no purely formal rule can tell you which predicate to project. Goodman's own fix is entrenchment: we've actually used predicates like green in past inductions and not grue-like ones, so green is the one we're entitled to keep projecting.

But someone pointed me at Solomonoff induction, which claims to do precisely the formal job Goodman said couldn't be done. The prior weights each computable hypothesis by two to the minus its program length, so the simpler hypothesis is massively favored; and green is obviously shorter to describe than grue, since grue is "green until some arbitrary time t, then blue," which needs the extra timekeeping machinery.

So here's what I want you to work through. Does Goodman's new riddle survive Solomonoff induction, or does Solomonoff just solve it by supplying the missing prior? Spell out which premise of Goodman's argument Solomonoff bears on. Then push back: the obvious Goodman-style countermove is that Solomonoff has only moved the problem into the choice of reference universal machine. The invariance theorem only pins down complexities up to a additive constant, and nothing formal says the "natural" machine is natural — so choosing it can look like entrenchment in computability-theory clothing. Is that countermove actually right, or does it overstate how much the choice of machine matters? Tell me wether Goodman is dead, hurt, or basically fine.
