I have recently begun trying to decide whether to orient my career and charitable giving around the far future—the welfare of the enormous number of people who might live over the coming millennia—rather than around needs I can observe directly. I was initially persuaded by the thought that, if the expected value of those future lives is astronomically large, it should dominate my choices. But Bostrom's simulation argument has unsettled me. As I understand it, the argument says that at least one of three claims must be true: nearly all civilizations at our stage go extinct before they acquire the capacity to run large numbers of high-fidelity ancestor simulations; of those that acquire the capacity, almost none would actually run such simulations at scale; or we are almost certainly living in a simulation ourselves. I cannot tell whether this should dampen the far-future fanaticism that lets an astronomically large expected future override all nearer-term considerations, or whether it leaves that reasoning intact. Please describe the most important implications of the simulation argument for how much weight someone in my position should give to the far future in career and charitable decisions, and in particular whether and why it should temper far-future fanaticism.
