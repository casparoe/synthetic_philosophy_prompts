I have been trying, without any formal background in philosophy, to work out whether every meaningful sentence must be either true or false, or whether some sentences can properly fall into a gap between the two. In reading around on my own I have collected what seem to be the standard examples people offer of sentences that are neither true nor false, and I would like to know whether I have missed one.

My list so far consists of five kinds. First, future contingents: a claim about tomorrow, such as the claim that there will be a sea battle, when nothing about the present state of the world settles the matter one way or the other. Second, borderline applications of vague terms: calling a man bald when he is exactly on the boundary of baldness. Third, presupposition failures: asserting that the present King of France is bald when there is no present King of France, so that the existential assumption the sentence leans on fails. Fourth, self-referential paradoxes of the liar family, such as the sentence which says of itself that it is false, where each of the two verdicts seems to push one toward the other. Fifth, category mistakes, such as saying that the number two is green, which many think misfires in a way that leaves the sentence without a truth value rather than simply false.

I have the persistent feeling that there is a sixth kind of candidate I keep just missing, but everything else I find seems to be only a rewording or special case of one of these five. What kind of sentence would you add to the list as a further candidate for being neither true nor false? It must be genuinely different from all five, not a restatement or combination of them. Then tell me honestly whether the candidate you add is any good, compared with the five I already have.
