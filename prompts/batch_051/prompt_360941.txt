Consider an asymmetry in how people usually judge automation. When a machine takes over one step of someone's work—checking dimensions, sorting parts, issuing a receipt—we call it progress, and the narrowing of the person's job barely registers. When machines and software take over every step of an occupation, so that the occupation itself disappears or shrinks to a shell—as happened to typesetting once desktop publishing absorbed its tasks—we describe the change as a social crisis, and the people who once did the work as its victims rather than as beneficiaries of progress. On the face of it, this should be strange. If a job is just its tasks plus a wage, a title, and a place in an organization, then losing the tasks one by one and losing the bundle should be two ways of losing the same things, differing only in number; ordinary judgment treats them as differing in kind. The puzzle is like a sorites problem run backward: no particular task is mourned, yet the occupation is mourned once enough of them are gone.

I want the strongest principle you can find that justifies holding both judgments at once—or, if you cannot find one, an honest argument that the asymmetry is an inconsistency we have learned to live with. To build that principle, say what a job is over and above its tasks: how it organizes time, identity, and standing; how it packages income, recognition, and the use of a person's capacities; and how the pace and visibility of a change affect what is owed to the people who bear it. Then test the principle against uneven cases: an occupation whose tasks have been ninety percent automated while its title, wage, and schedule remain; a job narrowed to a single task in which almost none of the person's skill is exercised; and a new occupation assembled from fragments of several automated ones. End by taking a position: keep the ordinary asymmetry as a rough practical judgment, replace it with something sharper, or give it up.
