I head the public-claims review desk at a mid-sized international development charity. My team checks roughly twenty public appeals, campaign statements and social-media posts a week before publication. Each piece is checked once, by a junior reviewer with no training in philosophy or linguistics, usually in under thirty minutes, and I sign off the final call. The stakes are real: a complaint to the fundraising regulator, a damaging news story, or a slow loss of donor trust. Our current standard is a single test: is any sentence literally false? Under that test, a piece of copy is approved if every sentence is technically true, even when experienced staff insist that the piece as a whole is misleading. I want you to write a decision procedure my reviewers can actually run, and to ground it in the distinction between lying and misleading rather than collapsing the two.

The working principle I want the procedure to embody is this. A lie is an assertion the speaker believes false. A misleading communication need not assert anything false: it is a communication that causes, or foreseeably risks causing, a reasonable member of the intended audience to accept as true something the author knows or should know is false, where the false content is not the literal content of the sentence but what the sentence, in its context of publication, licenses that audience to infer. The wrong in misleading lies in inducing a false belief while keeping the literal record clean. Please turn that principle into a procedure, without using the words implicature, pragmatics or any other theory jargon in the part my staff will read.

The procedure should:

- give the questions a reviewer should ask in order, with enough guidance for a junior reviewer to know what counts as a sufficient answer to move on;
- mark the stopping points where the piece is passed, and the stopping points where it is sent back for rewording and treated as we would treat a lie;
- identify the steps at which the reviewer must judge what a reasonable supporter would infer or what the author intended, rather than tick a box, and say what should happen at those steps;
- distinguish the literal content of the copy from content that is clearly implied, content that is merely weakly suggested, and material omissions, and say which of these the standard is meant to catch;
- be usable without survey data, linguistic training or more than half an hour of work, and fit on one side of A4 as written instructions.

Then run the procedure on these three pieces of draft copy:

1. We spend 0p of every pound on overheads; every pound you give goes to our programmes. The sentence is literally true because an unrestricted corporate foundation covers all our administration costs, none of the public's gifts is used for overheads, and the revenues are recorded that way.

2. Nine out of ten people who complete our six-week debt-counselling course are free of unmanageable debt one year later. The figure is accurate for clients who complete the course, but more than half of everyone who starts it drops out before completing it.

3. Your £10 a month puts a child in school. I approved this slogan myself and would like to keep it. It rests on the fact that the programme's total annual cost, divided by the number of children it keeps in school, comes to roughly £10 per child per month when spread across all regular donors; no individual gift is traced to a named child, and no child's place depends on any one gift.

Apply the procedure honestly to all three, including the third, where I want the procedure's answer and not the answer that flatters me. Report the verdict each procedure question returns, and end by saying what this procedure cannot settle for us and which decisions will still have to be made by a person. Write the answer in British English, and do not include references or a reading list.
