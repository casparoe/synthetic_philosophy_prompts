I've been teaching intro-to-proofs this term and inherited a syllabus from a previous instructor with a rule I've never had to enforce before. It says: 'An existence proof must actually exhibit the object in question; proving that the object must exist because its absence would imply a contradiction is not an acceptable proof.' The person who wrote it, in the mid-2000s, clearly meant to stop students from doing lazy indirect proofs and excluded-middle tricks. It never occurred to them that a student might get a computer to find the witness for them.

This term a student had to show that there exists a 512-bit binary string with a certain easily-checked property that has no known human-found example. She ran a SAT solver, got a satisfying assignment, and submitted the 512-bit string as her witness along with a one-page proof that her checker program verifies the property on that string. She says she's 'exhibited the object' exactly as the rule demands, even though she never actually read the string and even though the proof relies on the SAT solver and her checker being correctly implemented, which she hasn't proven. Half my TAs think this is obviously fine—it's as constructive as it gets—and I'm less sure, because the clause was meant to keep existence proofs surveyable and checkable by a human. How should I interpret the clause here? Argue it from its wording, its purpose, and what its author would probably have said if asked about machine-generated witnesses, and tell me which approach should control and why.
