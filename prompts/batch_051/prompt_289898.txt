A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about whether the behavioral and experimental literature on hyperbolic discounting dissolves the traditional philosophical problem of akrasia or merely re-describes it. His deflationary view was that apparent weakness of will is just a predictable reversal of preference as a nearer reward grows salient: the agent at the moment of action is not doing what she judges worse, but what her present-weighted self wants. I pushed back, because the regretful agent often treats her earlier judgment as still her own and the later action as a failure, not a change of mind. We did not settle it, and I would like to work through the sharper formulation below.

The classic Davidsonian account of akrasia runs roughly as follows. An agent judges that A is better than B, all things considered, yet intentionally does B. This is possible, on Davidson's view, because an all-things-considered judgment is not yet an unqualified practical conclusion: the agent must still move to the unconditional judgment that A is better without qualification. Akratic action arises when the agent fails to make that move and instead acts on a contrary unconditional judgment, a failure of what Davidson calls the principle of continence. The account thereby preserves both the reality of akrasia and its character as a practical-rational failure within a single agent.

Now add hyperbolic discounting. In the standard picture, an agent at an earlier time prefers a larger, later reward, but as the smaller, sooner reward becomes imminent its present value rises steeply, and preference reverses without new information and without any change in the judged merits of the options. The apparent weakness is thus spread across time-indexed selves with different effective discount rates, rather than located in a synchronic failure of practical reason.

Question: does the Davidsonian argument survive this development, does it require repair, or does it lose its object? I want an answer that does three things. First, identify the specific premise or step of the Davidsonian account on which hyperbolic discounting bears. Second, assess whether the account can be repaired — for example, by treating all-things-considered judgments as time-relative or by relocating the failure to an earlier self's failure to foresee and bind a later one. Third, say whether any such repair leaves a recognizably philosophical phenomenon of akrasia, or whether it quietly turns the problem into an engineering question about commitment devices and self-control.
