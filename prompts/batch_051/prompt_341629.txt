Over the weekend two friends of mine got into it about a decision theory exercise, and neither of them would let it go, so now I'm supposed to be the referee and I honestly can't tell who's right. The setup was two separate choices. First choice: a safe option of $1,000,000 guaranteed, versus a risky option with an 89% chance of $1,000,000, a 10% chance of $5,000,000, and a 1% chance of nothing. Second choice, made separately: an 11% chance of $1,000,000 with an 89% chance of nothing, versus a 10% chance of $5,000,000 with a 90% chance of nothing. My friend Nadia picked the safe million in the first choice but took the shot at five million in the second. Our other friend Theo said that's exactly the Allais pattern and its inconsistent.

Theo's argument: once you factor out the 89% chance of $1,000,000 that the first two options share, the first choice is really just "11% chance of a million" versus "10% chance of five million plus 1% chance of nothing"—which is the same comparison as the second choice once you factor out the 89% chance of nothing there. So by the independence axiom, whichever way you go on one, you have to go the same way on the other. He also said someone with Nadia's preferences could be drained by a clever series of trades, a money pump, because they'd pay to switch depending only on how the same underlying options get packaged.

Nadia's comeback: the sure-thing principle assumes the "common branch" is something you can just ignore, but when the common branch makes one option certain, it's not inert—certainty is a real feature of the option, not a presentation detail. In the second choice neither option is certain, so an 11% shot at a million versus a 10% shot at five million feels like an obvious lean toward five million, whereas in the first choice the 1% chance of walking away with nothing, when she could've had the million locked in, looms much larger. She also said Theo can't jump from "your preferences violate this axiom" to "therefore you'll be money pumped"—that requires her to be myopic and not to anticipate the sequence, and she's allowed to just refuse repackaged gambles that she can see are repackaged. Theo said refusing the trades is just admitting she has to armor herself against her own preferences, which a coherent set of preferences wouldn't need.
