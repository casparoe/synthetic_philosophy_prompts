I've been doing the reflective-equilibrium exercise with art: write down my actual verdicts on particular cases, then look for the rule that could earn the right to govern them. The rule I keep reaching for is a softened version of Tolstoy's infection theory. A thing is a work of art insofar as its maker has genuinely felt an emotion, embodied it in outward signs, and succeeded in making the audience feel that same emotion; the value of the work tracks the sincerity of the maker and the moral character of the feeling transmitted. This handles several of my verdicts cleanly. A half-trained singer at a funeral breaks the room with a simple song, and I have no hesitation calling it art, perhaps the truest art I know. A technically immaculate aria leaves me completely cold, and I keep wanting to call it excellent craft or entertainment rather than art. A novel that makes me feel something morally repellent I grant is art, but I call it bad art because of what it is doing to me.

The rule keeps failing, though, on the cases I find most embarrassing for it. A Duchamp-style readymade transmits no feeling in Tolstoy's sense, yet I am prepared to defend it as significant art. A sentimental commercial reliably produces in me exactly the emotion its director intended, and I refuse to call it art. A private journal entry of mine that conveys a very specific grief back to me years later sits exactly on the boundary. Is there one principle that gets all of these, or nearly all of them? If I keep the Tolstoyan principle, which of these verdicts does it force me to overturn, and what does the least-cost repair look like? I would also like the repaired principle to speak to two cases I keep turning over: a jazz improvisation that moves me unmistakably even though I later learn the player was mostly showing off technical facility rather than feeling anything; and a spare, emotionally empty proof or diagram that has the same formal elegance I associate with great art. I am less interested in a historical reconstruction of Tolstoy than in a defensible principle in that neighborhood.
