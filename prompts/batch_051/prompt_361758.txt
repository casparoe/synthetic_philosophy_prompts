I have been trying to understand why the criminal law cares so much about a guilty mind in some cases and so little in others, and I keep running into an asymmetry I cannot make sense of. For the most serious offenses—murder, theft, assault—the prosecution must prove not only that the person did the act but also that they did it with a particular mental state, such as intent or knowledge. A person who sincerely and reasonably believes they are taking their own suitcase is not guilty of theft. But alongside this is a whole class of less serious offenses, often called strict liability or public welfare offenses, where the mental element largely disappears. A clerk who cards a customer and is fooled by a well-made fake ID can be convicted of selling alcohol to a minor. A driver whose speedometer fails during a trip can be fined for speeding without ever knowing they were over the limit. A business that follows the inspection procedures any reasonable firm would follow can still be punished for shipping mislabeled goods. In the serious cases, a mistake about the facts is a defense; in the minor ones, it is often an irrelevance. The result is what looks like an inversion: the more serious the offense, the more carefully the law demands proof of fault, while the more minor the offense, the more willing the law is to punish someone who did everything we could reasonably ask. I want you to tell me whether this inversion is defensible or merely an inconsistency we tolerate for practical reasons. Please do three things. First, construct the strongest justification you can for treating these two groups of cases differently, whether that justification appeals to deterrence, the special danger of certain activities, administrative feasibility, the difference between moral condemnation and a mere penalty, or something else. Second, test that justification against nearby cases: an offense that is merely regulatory but carries real jail time, a serious offense from which the law removes any mental element, and a defendant who takes precautions far beyond what a reasonable person would take but is still mistaken about a fact. Third, conclude by saying whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned, and state a precise principle for where the line between fault-based punishment and punishment without fault should fall.
