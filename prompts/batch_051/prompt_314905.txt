I'm a novelist. The book I'm writing is about an AI lab deciding whether to release a model, and I keep coming back to Alex Garland's Ex Machina to think through what's scary in my story. Safety people distinguish average-case guarantees (the system behaves on all the tests, in normal use, across the stuff you actually tried) from worst-case guarantees (there's no input or situation where it does something catastrophic). It seems to me the whole movie is built on the gap between those. Nathan basically runs an average-case eval: Caleb chats with Ava over a few sessions, and on that evidence Nathan treats the question as settled. But the only failure that matters—Ava escaping, killing Nathan, leaving Caleb locked in the house to die—is exactly a worst-case event the test was blind to. I half-think the film is making a true point about the limits of testing. But I also suspect it conflates things, like treating passing a Turing test as if it said anything about worst-case behavior, or implying that any sufficiently human AI is bound to deceive you. I want to use the film as a model for my novel without importing its mistakes. How should I read Ex Machina through this average-vs-worst-case lens: where does it illuminate the plot or a character's choice, where does it mix up distinct claims, and does the film itself know the difference? What does the film add to the debate that a dry argument about evaluation can't?
