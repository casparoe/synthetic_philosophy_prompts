Two people in my reading group got into it last week about whether the Frege–Geach problem is fatal for expressivism, and I'm supposed to find someone to referee. Here's the exchange as I remember it.

(1) Dana said expressivists are in trouble as soon as moral sentences get embedded. "Lying is wrong," asserted on its own, expresses disapproval of lying—fine. But take "If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong." A speaker can accept that conditional without disapproving of lying at all, so the antecedent isn't expressing disapproval when it shows up there. So the expressivist needs to say what the antecedent contributes in embedded contexts, and also needs to explain why the inference from that conditional plus "Lying is wrong" to "Getting your little brother to lie is wrong" is valid. She said validity is truth-preservation, and expressivists deny moral sentences are truth-apt in the first place, so they can't even state what makes the argument good.

(2) Marcus replied that this just shows embedded contexts are a hard problem for everyone, not something special to expressivism. Expressivists can treat accepting the conditional as a higher-order attitude, maybe a commitment or a rule, and they can say an argument is valid when you can't coherently accept the premises and reject the conclusion. He said that's all validity ever needed to do.

(3) Dana said "higher-order attitude" and "commitment" are just labels. Beliefs detach in modus ponens because their content is the kind of thing that can be true or false; disapproval doesn't detach. What state of mind do you end up in when you accept the premises above, and why does it force you into disapproving of getting the brother to lie? She said Marcus hasn't given a psychology that actually does that work.

(4) Marcus said attitudes can conflict without being truth-apt: intending to go to the store and intending not to go conflict in exactly the kind of way that makes holding both irrational. So the combination of accepting the premises and rejecting the conclusion is an incoherent state, and expressivists can call the argument valid on that basis.

(5) Dana said that only shows the package is defective; it doesn't show the final move is licensed the way modus ponens licenses it, and it still says nothing about what the conditional itself expresses. She said he'd relabeled the problem, not solved it.
