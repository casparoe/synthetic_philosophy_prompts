A jurisdiction is considering the following statutory scheme for orphan works and abandoned software, and I want it analyzed as a piece of institutional design rather than judged in the abstract.

Any person may apply to the national copyright office for a certificate of orphanhood for a specific published work: a book, film, photograph, sound recording, computer program, or a separately copyrightable component of one. To obtain the certificate, the applicant must present the results of a certified search for the rightsholder. A certified search may be performed either by the copyright office itself, for a fixed fee, or by a licensed private search firm; in either case it must include at least three documented attempts: a query of the public rights database, an effort to reach the last known publisher or distributor, and a notice posted for ninety days in a national orphan-works registry. The applicant must also state the work's current annual licensing value and post a bond equal to one year's stated value, held in escrow.

Once a certificate issues, the work enters an orphan pool for twenty years. During that term, anyone may reproduce, distribute, adapt, or otherwise exploit the work without permission, provided they report each use within sixty days and pay a fee of five percent of gross revenue attributable to the use, or, for noncommercial use, a flat fee per copy or installation set by a schedule. Payments are credited to a work-specific escrow account. If a rightsholder surfaces during the twenty years and establishes their claim, they may collect the accrued escrow, may withdraw the work from the pool so that the compulsory license ends prospectively, and may recover the bond if they show the certified search was fraudulent or negligent; uses made before that point remain licensed and cannot support an infringement suit. If no rightsholder surfaces within the twenty years, the copyright lapses; the applicant's bond is returned, the applicant receives a bounty of ten percent of the accumulated escrow, and the rest of the escrow goes to a public digital-preservation fund. For works first published more than fifty years before the application, the search requirement is reduced to a single ninety-day registry notice and the bond is waived.

Software is treated component-by-component: a program and each separately identifiable library or other component may be declared orphaned individually, and a certificate for one component does not license the others.

Treat this as a mechanism. Walk through how it would actually behave: who would use it first and why; how rightsholders, would-be users, search firms, and bad actors would respond; where it would be gamed and whether the gaming is self-correcting; what happens with software whose owner is a dissolved company and whose components have different and partly uncertain rightsholders; what new intermediaries it would create; and whether the main failure modes can be fixed without discarding the scheme entirely.
