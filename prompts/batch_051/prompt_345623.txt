I came accross a pop-econ piece about something called "ergodicity economics" (associated with Ole Peters and a few collaborators), and I can't tell when it's being precise and when its smoothing things over. The piece summarizes the view like this:

"Classical economics tells us to maximize expected value, but that is mathematically wrong for any decision you'll face more than once. Expected value averages over thousands of parallel copies of you, each living out a different possible outcome, and then lets them pool their wealth at the end. Real life is one path through time, so what matters is the time average, not the ensemble average. Peters proved the point with a simple gamble: a fair coin is flipped, heads you gain 50%, tails you lose 40%. The expected value of a single flip is positive, so standard economics says a risk-neutral person should stake their whole bankroll, and keep doing so on every flip. But if you do that, your wealth almost surely collapses toward zero, because the time-average growth rate is negative. This shows expected utility theory is flawed, and that being risk-neutral is irrational. The same logic, the article concludes, is why poor people should not buy lottery tickets."

Then address the lottery-ticket point separately: does the negative time-average growth rate in the coin-flip game actually give anyone a reason to avoid lottery tickets, or is the article smuggling in a different argument?
