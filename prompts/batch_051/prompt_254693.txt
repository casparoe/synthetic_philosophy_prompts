I'm auditing a philosophy of art course and we spent a week on the paradox of fiction — the puzzle about how we can be genuinely frightened or saddened by characters we know perfectly well don't exist. But I keep bumping into a different thing, and I can't tell whether it's just a special case of that puzzle or a seperate problem with its own shape. Last week I rewatched a thriller I've seen maybe four times. I remembered exactly who lives and who dies, I remembered the twist, and still, in the scene where the killer is coming up the stairs while the main character hides in the closet, I was holding my breath and practically willing her to stay put, even though I knew she wouldnt. Same thing happens when I retell a near-miss story to a friend: I know the ending, I lived the ending, but the old dread creeps back into my chest while I'm telling it.

So my question isn't the usual one about whether I believe in the characters. I want to know: is there an established name for the fact that knowing the outcome doesn't shut off the feeling? If there is, I want to know what the name covers exactly and how it relates to the paradox of fiction — do the standard moves people make about fiction (make-believe, or the idea that emotions can run on thoughts rather than beliefs) already handle this, or is there a leftover problem with a different structure? And if there isn't a name, what would you call it, and how would you separate it both from the paradox of fiction and from the more boring case of being startled by a twist you didn't see coming?
