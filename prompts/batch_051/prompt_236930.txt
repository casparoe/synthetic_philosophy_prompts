I spent my working life in geochemistry and only got serious about philosophy after retiring, so apologies if this is less precise than it should be. Lately I've been reading Plato's Meno, and this passage has been stuck in my head (Plato, Meno 97e-98a):

"For these, so long as they stay with us, are a fine possession, and effect all that is good; but they do not care to stay for long, and run away out of the human soul, and thus are of no great value until one makes them fast with causal reasoning. And this process, friend Meno, is recollection, as in our previous talk we have agreed. But when once they are fastened, in the first place they turn into knowledge, and in the second, are abiding. And this is why knowledge is more prized than right opinion: the one transcends the other by its trammels."

Here is an argument I've been mulling over; I half-suspect it is too quick but I cant see exactly where.

(1) The passage takes the difference between true opinion and knowledge to be a difference in staying power: true opinions "run away," while knowledge is true opinion fastened down.
(2) If knowledge is just true opinion plus a fastening, then the only thing knowledge adds is retention: keeping hold of a truth you already possessed.
(3) If a thing adds only retention of some good, then its value is purely instrumental; it is valuable as a container, not for itself.
(4) So on the Meno's own picture, knowledge is only instrumentally better than true opinion: it is a better keeper of truths, not something better in its own right.
(5) Therefore the Meno's original puzzle—why knowledge is better than true belief—has a deflationary answer: there is no special epistemic value over and above keeping true beliefs around.

I keep squinting at (3). It sounds analytic, but maybe it smuggles in the conclusion by assuming that a "fastening" can only be a retention device, rather than something that changes what the belief is. But if (3) is fine, then the argument looks valid to me and the conclusion follows, even though it seems flatter than what Plato wants. Am I right that the argument is valid and the only place to resist is (3)? Or is there a subtler slip earlier, in (1) or (2)? Be specific about the step and, if it turns on "fastened" or "retention," say which reading of that term makes the premise true and whether the argument still goes through.
