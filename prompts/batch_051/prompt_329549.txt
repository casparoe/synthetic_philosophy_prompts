I spent nearly forty years as a physical chemist, and the habit I can't drop is the conviction that a good theory shouldn't depend on how you label the setup: coordinates, reference frames, which side of the equation you call baseline. So when I started reading philosophy around retirement, I got completely tangled in the debate about loss aversion and the endowment effect, because it feels like exactly that kind of frame-dependence — except now the frame is what you happen to own.

The side I find theoretically compelling goes like this. A preference ought to be a ranking of outcomes, not of descriptions of outcomes. If I end up with a mug or I end up with five dollars, the ranking shouldn't change because someone flipped whether the mug was described as mine at the start. So the fact that people who are just handed a mug will demand about twice as much to sell it as people who weren't handed one will pay to buy it looks like a mistake: the reference point — owning, not owning, a default — is leaking into the evaluation. The standard cleanups don't obviously rescue it. The mugs are randomly assigned, so owners aren't a self-selected group of mug-lovers; the stakes are too small for wealth effects to matter; the transaction costs in the experimental market are trivial and symmetric. Prospect theory's answer — that losses loom about twice as large as gains — seems to me like a description of the bug dressed up as a parameter. And if my preferences really do depend on the frame, then what I prefer is underdetermined, which should bother anyone who ever needed a unique solution to an equation.

The feeling I can't get rid of is this. I own my father's old brass telescope. It doesn't work well and it isn't worth much, but if you asked me to name a selling price, the number I'd genuinely give is far above anything I'd pay for the identical telescope in someone else's attic. That gap doesn't feel like a miscalculation; it feels like what it is to have had the thing, to have it woven into what I take myself to be doing. When I think about losing something I already have, the dread isn't just about the end state — it's about the transition, the change in what my life has accumulated. But the mug experiments were built, as far as I can tell, precisely to rule out those special stories about attachments and biography, and the gap shows up anyway for strangers and mugs. So either my attachment is the thing that has to go, a misfiring heuristic no more trustworthy than a visual illusion, or the theory's demand that only final outcomes count is too strong, or there's some reading on which both survive.

What I want to know is: in this clash, which side should yield, and what would justify yielding rather than just declaring a winner? If the endowment effect is a legitimate part of what I value, what exactly changes in the theory — reference-dependent utility, a broader conception of outcomes that includes history and ownership, something else — and how do I stop that from licensing every status-quo bias people have? If the attachment is the thing that should go, what's the argument that it's a misfire rather than a value, given that I can state the mug results and still feel the telescope? Is there a reading on which description-invariance about final states and genuine loss aversion are compatible, maybe because my owning the thing now is a real difference in the outcome, not just the frame? I'm less interested in which camp you're in than in what would actualy settle it: what evidence or argument would move me in each direction, and what would only beg the question. I dont have many years left for this stuff, so I'd rather skip the survey and go straight at the load-bearing point.
