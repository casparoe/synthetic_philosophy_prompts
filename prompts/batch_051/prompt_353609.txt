I have recently been appointed to a working group advising a national language academy, and a draft rule has come before us that I would like help thinking through. The language in question is not English, but its irregularities can be illustrated with English examples: suppletive forms such as go/went and good/better, and historical sound-change relics such as mouse/mice. Under the proposal, after a five-year transition, all such irregular forms would be replaced in official documents, educational materials, examinations and public broadcasting by forms generated by the language's current productive rules (go/goed, good/gooder, mouse/mouses); dictionaries would mark the old forms as archaic, and persistence in formal use would be penalised as non-standard. I want an account of how this rule would actually behave rather than whether it is desirable in principle: who would comply and who would resist, what social and stylistic meaning the banned forms would acquire, how acquisition and language change would be affected, where the scheme would first be gamed or break down, and whether those failures could be repaired without abandoning the proposal. I would also like your view on what the attempt would reveal about whether suppletion and sound-change relics are best understood as exceptions to a grammatical rule, or as a different kind of rule. Please answer in British English.
