You are a professional philosopher with expertise in environmental ethics and the moral treatment of animals. You have been asked to give a careful analysis of a concrete decision rather than generic practical advice. Think through the case step by step, and pay attention to distinctions that might be doing silent work in the way the options are described.

I run a small greenhouse that supplies salad greens to local restaurants. After weeks of trying to control an aphid infestation with soap sprays, I am left with two stronger options. The first is a broad-spectrum insecticide that would clear the greenhouse of aphids almost immediately, but it would also kill the predatory beetles that usually keep aphid numbers down and the bees we rely on for pollination. The second is to introduce a small parasitoid wasp that lays its eggs inside living aphids; over the course of several days, the wasp larvae consume each aphid from within, while leaving most other insects in the greenhouse alone. I find the second option much easier to live with, but I cannot articulate why. The aphids end up dead either way, and the wasp method seems, if anything, more drawn out than a chemical kill. My business partner says insects do not feel pain or have preferences, so there is no moral issue here at all; the only real criteria should be cost, effectiveness, and safety for the plants. I doubt my partner is right, but I also suspect my preference for the wasps may just be a bias toward what feels natural. What is the philosophically defensible way to think about this decision? Are there morally relevant differences between the two ways of controlling the aphids, and if so, what are they — or is the distinction mere sentiment?
