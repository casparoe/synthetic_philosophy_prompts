I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and lately I keep getting pulled into the argument about whether you can seperate the art from the artist. I've started writing down my own verdicts on specific cases, and they're a mess. Maybe you can tell me what principle I'm actually tracking, if any.

A photographer whose street portraits I loved was convicted of hiding cameras in a gym changing room. The portraits are still technically brilliant, but I can't look at them anymore; the same sneakiness that made the portraits good seems continuous with what made him a predator.

A novelist wrote a genuinely wise book about forgiveness, and then it came out that he had defrauded his own siblings out of their inheritance. I still think the book is wise and I'd recommend it, but only with a caveat.

A composer dead for three centuries left letters full of ugly race hatred, but the music I love is an abstract string quartet, so none of it is in the work. I listen without any guilt.

A pop singer I've loved for years drove drunk and permanently injured a pedestrian. Her songs are about heartbreak and have nothing to do with cars. I stopped listening for a year, but now I listen again; I just don't go to the concerts or buy the vinyl.

A film director screamed at and humiliated actors to get the performances he wanted. The movie is a humane story about caring for the vulnerable. I think the movie is a masterpiece and I still watch it, but I borrow the library's disc so the director gets nothing.

Then there's the one I can't make fit. I can't sit through the comedy special of a stand-up who was later convicted of sexual assault, even though the special is about loneliness and never hints at any of that. That feels different from the pop singer, and I don't know why, because neither crime is in the work. Maybe it's that the comedian's onstage persona is this intimate, confessional 'this is who I am' thing while the singer's persona is obviously a performance — but I don't really buy that distinction either, since both are performances.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? If you can state it precisely, do; if you think I've got two competing principles doing the work, name them and show which fits better. Which of my verdicts does it force me to revise, and why those? And what would it say about the case I'm currently stuck on: a beloved children's author who embezzled money from his own church's food bank and then wrote a series whose whole moral is that small lies always catch up with you. My kid loves those books, and I don't know whether reading them aloud is fine, fine-with-a-caveat, or not fine.
