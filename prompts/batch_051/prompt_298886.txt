A philosopher in my department works on whether moral responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise and has a track record of verdicts on Frankfurt-style cases. In the first, the canonical one, Jones decides on his own to embezzle; a hidden intervener prepared to ensure that he decides to embezzle would have stepped in if Jones had shown any sign of deciding not to, but the intervention never occurs. She judges Jones responsible: the mere unavailability of an alternative did not by itself remove responsibility. In a second case, the intervener gives Jones a drug before deliberation that permanently removes his capacity to refuse; Jones then weighs the considerations that remain and decides to embezzle. She judges Jones not responsible. In a third case, the only safeguard is a device that would have prevented Jones from acting on a decision not to embezzle; Jones never makes such a decision. She judges Jones responsible.

She is now asked about a fourth case. Jones deliberates in his ordinary way, and his reasoning from premises to decision is entirely his own. Unbeknownst to him, a device would intervene at the earliest stage of any movement toward refusing, preventing a contrary intention from even beginning to form; because Jones never moves toward refusing, the device is never triggered and his deliberation proceeds exactly as it would have without it. She has not yet offered a view.

What verdict should she give if she wants to cohere with her earlier rulings? Which earlier case is the closest analog? If more than one principle fits her precedents, state what principle each choice would commit her to, and describe the further case that would settle which principle she actually holds.
