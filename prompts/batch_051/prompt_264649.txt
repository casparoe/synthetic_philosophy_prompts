After forty years in geophysics I've started reading the philosophy I'd always meant to get around to, and Hume's Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding is the first thing that hasnt made me feel like I'd wandered into somebody else's seminar by mistake. Section VIII in particular stopped me cold. There's this passage:

"It is universally allowed, that matter, in all its operations, is actuated by a necessary force, and that every natural effect is so precisely determined by the energy of its cause, that no other effect, in such particular circumstances, could possibly have resulted from it. The degree and direction of every motion is, by the laws of nature, prescribed with such exactness, that a living creature may as soon arise from the shock of two bodies, as motion, in any other degree or direction than what is actually produced by it. Would we, therefore, form a just and precise idea of necessity, we must consider whence that idea arises, when we apply it to the operation of bodies."

Then he goes on to say that our whole idea of necessity just is constant conjunction plus the mind's inference from one thing to the other, and that we find exactly those two circumstances in human actions as much as in falling bodies. His payoff is compatibilist: "liberty" properly understood is only the power to act or not act according to the determinations of one's own will, as opposed to being stopped by external force. I want to keep that payoff. But when I pushed his definition, I got an argument that seems to prove way too much, and I can't find the hole in it.

(1) On Hume's picture, an act is free iff it's caused by the agent's own will and isn't blocked by external force.
(2) But internal causes can determine the will itself: an addict's craving, a post-hypnotic suggestion, a mood disorder, a manipulator who quietly installs your desires before you ever notice them. In such cases the person acts on their own will, with no external force at the time of acting.
(3) So on Hume's picture, the addict using, the hypnotized person obeying, and the person whose desires were engineered all act freely.
(4) But those are paradigm cases of diminished or absent freedom; if a notion of freedom can't tell them apart from ordinary choice, it can't do the work we need it to do in assigning responsibility.
(5) So either Hume's definition of liberty is wrong or incomplete, or free will in the sense relevant to blame has nothing much to do with doing what you will unconstrained. If I give up the first I loose the clean compatibilism I wanted; if I give up the second, I'm not sure why we'd ever praise or blame anybody.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If there's a way to keep step (1) without swallowing step (4), I'd like to see it spelled out.
