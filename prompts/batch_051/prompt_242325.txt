I've seen a recurring move in rationalist-adjacent discussions of virtue ethics: people say social psychology has shown there are no stable character traits, and therefore Aristotle's account of acting from virtue is empirically bankrupt. I want to test that move against Aristotle's own criteria.

Here is the passage I mean, from Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, II.4: "first he must act with knowledge; secondly he must deliberately choose the act, and choose it for its own sake; and thirdly the act must spring from a fixed and permanent disposition of character. For the possession of an art, none of these conditions is included, except the mere qualification of knowledge; but for the possession of the virtues, knowledge is of little or no avail, whereas the other conditions, so far from being of little moment, are all-important, inasmuch as virtue results from the repeated performance of just and temperate actions."

Now consider a borderline case. A person helps a stranger in clear need. He knows what he is doing and chooses the helpful act for the stranger's sake. But his helping in this particular emergency was partly prompted by a confederate modeling a quick intervention, and in a near-identical emergency where two other bystanders remain passive he is much less likely to help; across several standard helpfulness situations his behavior does not correlate strongly. He has a history of ordinary decency, and he endorses the act as the right one.

Work through Aristotle's three conditions as they appear in the passage, and say whether, on those criteria, this counts as an action done from virtue. Be explicit about where the evidence would need to be sharper to force a yes or no. Then address the situationist inference: if "fixed and permanent disposition" is read as requiring the kind of cross-situational consistency the social-psychology literature suggests is rare, should we revise Aristotle's criterion, or is that reading too demanding? In particular, say what the third condition adds beyond the first two, and whether the situationist critique needs it to be doing more than it does.
