I've recently started advising a small philanthropic fund that evaluates interventions affecting beings of very different degrees of consciousness — people with severe brain injuries, farmed animals, and a few proposals about digital minds — and I have to commit to a way of ranking them. The theory I keep relying on is additive: the value of a state of affairs is the sum of well-being across all welfare subjects, because that is simple, transparent, and gives a definite answer for every comparative choice. But I also have a firm intuition I can't bring myself to disparage: consciousness does not enter value as just another term added in. A system with no consciousness contributes zero, no matter how much "happiness" its internal scoring indicates; a subject whose experience is thin or fragmented seems to receive less value from the same quantity of pleasure; and no pile of barely-conscious satisfaction quite seems to equal the ruin of a fully conscious life. That points toward multiplying consciousness by happiness, or making consciousness lexically prior to happiness, rather than adding it alongside happiness. Present each side at its strongest, then give me a negotiated verdict. In particular: under what conditions should the intuition be debunked and dropped, under what conditions should the additive theory be amended toward a multiplicative or lexical form, and is there a reading on which both survive — for example, additivity across subjects after consciousness has already gated what counts as welfare? If you think they are compatible, say exactly where a residual conflict would remain.
