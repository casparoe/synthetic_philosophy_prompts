I recently became a parent, and in reading about water safety I came up against a legal pattern I cannot shake: in most common-law jurisdictions, a stranger who stands by and watches a small child drown in a shallow pond commits no crime, whereas a babysitter who watches the same child drown can be convicted of manslaughter. The difference between them is not that one moved and the other did not; it is that the babysitter stood in a recognized duty relationship to the child while the stranger did not. The same pattern recurs throughout criminal law: no one is obligated to rescue a stranger from a burning car, but a parent, a lifeguard on duty, a contractual caregiver, or the person who caused the accident may be punished for letting the same harm occur.

I understand that philosophers disagree sharply about whether omissions can be causes at all: some hold that absences are not the sort of thing that can enter into causal relations; others hold that an omission is a cause only relative to a background norm or expectation that picks out what should have happened; others still hold that an omission causes an outcome in exactly the same way a movement or collision does.

I would like you to reconstruct what this body of legal practice presupposes about causation by omission, and to say whether the presupposition is coherent. In particular: does the law presuppose that omissions are not genuine causes, so that the duty requirement does the causal work that absences cannot do? Or does it instead allow that omissions are causes, and treat duty as an independent filter on culpability? Can either reading be squared with the but-for test that courts use everywhere else, given that the child would not have drowned but for the stranger's inaction just as much as but for the babysitter's inaction? How much of the doctrine is compatible with several theories of causation by omission rather than forced by a single one? Finally, explain what would have to change in the law if we accepted the view that absences are never causes, and what would have to change if we accepted the opposite view that omissions are causes in precisely the same way that actions are.
