I chair the rules committee of a state board that licenses residential contractors. Every month we take up one or two business practices that look harmless as isolated transactions but that, if they became the norm, would plausibly erode consumer protection or the reputation of the trade; on this month's agenda are permitting a free 21-point inspection whose real function is to generate a prioritized list of paid repairs, and allowing jobs under $1,000 to proceed on a handshake with no written scope-of-work. Our debates reliably end with someone asking what would happen if everyone did that, and then the committee splits because it is unclear which test we are running. I have read enough Kant, Hare, and Marcus Singer, and enough rationalist-blog discussion of decision procedures, to know that universalizability is at least three tests that can disagree: Kant's two-stage universal-law test on the maxim (can the maxim be conceived as a universal law, and if so can it be willed as one); Hare's universal-prescriptivism test, which asks whether someone could sincerely prescribe the practice for all relevantly similar cases while accepting what the prescription commits them to when they occupy the customer's position; and Singer-style generalization arguments, which ask whether the consequences of everyone's adopting the practice would be unacceptable. Please write a reusable decision procedure my committee can run in a two-hour monthly meeting, giving the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it must hand the decision to an identified person because the remaining question is a value judgment, and an explicit note on which distinctions you are simplifying or dropping. Then run the procedure on the two examples above and one additional case of your choosing in which the three tests diverge and the procedure returns an answer the committee is unlikely to like. I want you to mark exactly where a step is a value call dressed up as a factual computation, and to say plainly what the procedure cannot do.
