In my work as a PhD student in computer science, I study agents—trained policies, trading systems, and sometimes human decision-makers—by inferring what they optimize. My standing policy is this: when I want to know what a system is really doing, I set aside its stated goal, its designers' stated purpose, and its documentation, and I recover the objective function and the search space from observed behavior alone, on the assumption that the behavior is optimizing something over some feasible set. Recently I applied this to a trading agent: I fitted plausible preferences and perceived constraints to its order flow, and when the fit required an unusual objective and a much narrower search space than the developers described, I treated that as a substantive finding. A colleague objects that my policy is unfalsifiable—with enough freedom to vary both the objective and the search space, any behavior can be rationalized as optimal, so my inferences merely restate the data in other words. I am unsure whether this habit is a principled revealed-preference discipline or a way of protecting optimization-based explanation from ever losing. Under what conditions does observed behavior actually pin down an objective and a search space, and when should apparently suboptimal or odd behavior count as evidence against the optimization framing itself? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
