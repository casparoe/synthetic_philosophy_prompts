I keep finding myself persuaded by the instrumentalist philosophy of science usually associated with Milton Friedman's methodological essay — the so-called F-twist — and then rebelling against it in a specific kind of case. On the one hand, the position has real systematic virtues. It gives a clean decision procedure for model choice: rank models by predictive success, not by how closely their assumptions resemble the world. It explains why deliberately simplified models so often outperform more realistic ones out of sample, and it treats realism about assumptions as an aesthetic prejudice rather than an epistemic requirement. Friedman's own examples — leaves arranged as if each were trying to maximize sunlight, an expert billiard player who plays as if solving the physics of ideal collisions — show how a knowingly false assumption can be the cheapest route to accurate prediction.

On the other hand, I have an intuition I cannot get rid of. Suppose a simulation of a housing market reproduces observed price dynamics and neighborhood tipping points by assuming that every household is an identical, fully informed, perfectly optimizing agent facing no credit constraints, no moving costs, and no discrimination, even though the best available evidence says actual households use rough heuristics, face severe financial constraints, and respond to race and class composition in ways the model encodes nowhere. The instrumentalist says: if it predicts, stop complaining about the agents. But it seems to me the model has not merely risked predictive failure; it has failed as an explanation, because the respect in which its assumptions are false is exactly the respect in which it claims to tell us why prices and segregation move as they do. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want a negotiated verdict. Concretely: (1) Make the strongest case that the intuition is a leftover demand for assumptions to mirror the world and should be debunked. (2) Make the strongest case that the instrumentalism must be amended, for example by adding a structural or mechanistic correspondence condition for models offered as explanations rather than as forecasting tools. (3) Make the strongest case that the two are compatible, perhaps by distinguishing Galilean idealizations that isolate a genuine causal factor from substantive falsehoods that replace the real mechanism with a fictitious one, or by distinguishing explanation from prediction, and say how the distinction could be drawn without collapsing back into naive realism. What would justify each of the three moves, and which do you think we should accept?
