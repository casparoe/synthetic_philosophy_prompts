I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep turning over a lunch argument I witnessed between two colleagues, Priya and Tom, about the two-envelope paradox. The setup they agreed on: two envelopes contain amounts of money, one exactly twice the other; you pick one and, before opening it, you're offered the chance to switch to the other envelope. Should you switch?

Priya opened with the familiar expected-value argument. Suppose you open your envelope and find $x. The other envelope, she said, contains either $2x or $x/2, and since nothing distinguishes those possibilities, each has probability 1/2. Switching therefore has expected value (1/2)(2x) + (1/2)(x/2) = 1.25x, which beats the $x you keep by staying. Since this holds for whatever $x you find, you'd switch every time. She conceded that this conclusion is absurd, because after switching you could run the same argument and switch back; her takeaway was that expected-value reasoning gives wrong answers for one-shot gambles and needs to be replaced by some less expectation-maximizing norm.

Tom said the problem is not expected value but the 50/50 split. Once you condition on having seen a particular $x, the probability that the other envelope contains $2x rather than $x/2 depends on how the envelopes were filled — on a prior distribution over possible amounts. Getting 50/50 for every possible $x would amount to a uniform distribution over an unbounded set of amounts, which is not a coherent probability distribution. With any actual filling rule, he said, the amount you observe carries information about whether you are holding the smaller or the larger envelope; switching will be favorable for some observed amounts and unfavorable for others, with no blanket 25% advantage.

Priya came back with a version of the argument that never opens the envelope, so no prior over amounts is needed. Let $X$ be the amount in the envelope you are holding. The selection process makes it equally likely that you hold the smaller or the larger of the two prepared amounts. If you hold the smaller, the other envelope contains $2X$, so switching gains $X$. If you hold the larger, the other envelope contains $X/2$, so switching loses $X/2$. The expected gain from switching is therefore (1/2)X − (1/2)(X/2) = X/4, which is strictly positive. Only the coin-flip, she said, no assumption about the distribution of amounts, and switching still comes out strictly profitable.

Tom said a simulation would settle it. Choose a distribution for the smaller amount — uniform over the integers from 1 to 1000, say — put twice that in the other envelope, randomly assign which envelope you receive, and always take the other one. The average net change over many trials converges to 0, not to X/4 or to any 25% edge. The reason, he said, is that Priya is using one symbol X for two different quantities. In the branch where she holds the smaller envelope, the X in her gain term is the smaller amount, call it a, and the gain is a. In the branch where she holds the larger envelope, the X in her loss term is the larger amount, 2a, so the loss she writes as X/2 is really a. Her simplifying step, which combines the two X's to get X/4, is where the error occurs; if you plug the branch-specific values back in, the expected gain is (1/2)a − (1/2)a = 0.

I followed the algebra in each turn but cannot tell how much each move actually got right, and I suspect someone is right for a reason the other has not quite named. Then give a bottom-line verdict on who is right about which specific point, and say what, if anything, both Priya and Tom are missing.

Lead with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence, then give the point-by-point reasoning; do not preface it or restate the question.
