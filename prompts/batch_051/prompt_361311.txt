In Computing Machinery and Intelligence (1950), Alan Turing writes: "Instead of attempting such a definition I shall replace the question by another, which is closely related to it and is expressed in relatively unambiguous words." Please argue that this substitution—replacing the question of what thought is with the imitation-game criterion—commits Turing to reading the later sections on learning machines not as an engineering afterthought but as an epistemic claim about how intelligence must be cultivated: start with a childlike machine and educate it, because the relevant capacities are not to be specified in advance in a definition. Then address the strongest objection to that reading, namely that the imitation game itself may presuppose the very ability to learn that the child-machine proposal tries to explain. State clearly which part of your argument depends on the original 1950 text and which part is a rational reconstruction.
