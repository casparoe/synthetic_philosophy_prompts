I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on oaths and the ethics of promising, and we have recently been working through the archaic Greek material on swearing by the Styx. A parallel keeps nagging me, and I cannot tell whether it is genuinely illuminating or only a verbal echo.

In the picture we get from Hesiod, the gods' most solemn oaths are sworn by the river Styx. What strikes me is that such an oath binds the gods not because they are virtuous or because some higher authority stands over them, but because the penalty is built into the oath itself: once the vow has been registered, the sanction follows as a kind of automatic consequence, and even the most powerful of the gods cannot simply set it aside. The oath is credible because the incentives have been rearranged, not because the promisor is good.

This looks to me structurally identical to the way a commitment device works in economics and game theory. An agent who cannot be trusted puts something at stake so that defection becomes strictly worse than performance; the threat of the penalty, rather than the speaker's character, supplies the credibility. On this reading, swearing by the Styx is just the gods' way of posting collateral, and the Styx functions as an enforcement technology.

But the analogy already feels strained in two places. A commitment device binds because the parties expect the penalty to be carried out; the Styx oath is supposed to bind because perjury is a cosmic offense whose punishment simply follows from it. And ordinary promises — including oaths between people — seem to bind even when there is no sanction attached and nobody will find out, which the commitment-device model cannot explain. So maybe the parallel captures only oaths that are already enforceable, and misses what makes an oath an oath in the first place.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a list of pros and cons. Please (i) state the proposed mapping as precisely as you can, (ii) say what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer between the two domains — for example, does the economic idea that better collateral makes a promisor more credible transfer to the thought that the Styx oath is the strongest vow the gods can swear? — and (iii) identify at least one prediction the parallel makes that comes out false. Keep the whole answer under 700 words.
