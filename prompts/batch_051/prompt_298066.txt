I am a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course in ancient philosophy, and I am marking a short-answer question from the midterm on Aristotle's logic and metaphysics. The question asked students to reconstruct the sea battle argument from De Interpretatione 9 and to explain what Aristotle gives up in order to keep the future open. Below are the exam question and one student's answer; I would like a second opinion before finalizing the mark.

Exam question: In De Interpretatione 9, Aristotle uses the sea battle to ask whether statements about the future are already true or false. Reconstruct the argument that moves from the assumption that every statement is true or false to the conclusion that all future events are necessary. Then explain Aristotle's solution: what exactly does he refuse to assert about future contingent statements, and does that refusal succeed in keeping the future open? Be precise about which logical principles are and are not being surrendered.

Student answer: Aristotle starts from the principle that every declarative sentence is either true or false. If future-tense sentences are included, then either 'there will be a sea battle tomorrow' or 'there will not be a sea battle tomorrow' is true already. Suppose the first is true. Then the battle cannot fail to occur; for if it did fail, the sentence would not have been true, and a truth is something fixed. So the truth of statements about the future seems to force the future to match them, and fatalism threatens. Aristotle responds that this is too strong: for future contingent statements, neither the affirmation nor the negation is true at the time it is uttered. He therefore rejects the law of excluded middle for this class of statements, so that a disjunction like 'there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there will not be a sea battle tomorrow' is not true until the day arrives. This shows his real concern is epistemic: since we cannot now know which disjunct will obtain, we should not assign a truth value now. Once we see that our evidence is incomplete, the fatalist conclusion is blocked and deliberation remains meaningful.

Keep your entire assessment to a maximum of 400 words.
