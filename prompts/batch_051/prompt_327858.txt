My neighbor sits on the board of a small historical society that has just inherited a large, unevenly organized collection of local records, and the board is deciding what to preserve and digitize. The most influential member argues that limited storage and cataloging time force them to prioritize clearly significant material — letters between prominent residents, official town records, documents tied to well-known events. I have been uneasy with this because the material he calls insignificant — rent ledgers, shop inventories, wage records, the recollections of people who never held office — is often the only surviving trace of how most of the town's inhabitants actually lived, and keeping only what already looks significant seems to decide in advance what future historians will be able to find. He replies that some documents really do matter more than others and that no archive can keep everything. I cannot tell whether he is making an unavoidable practical point or a mistake about what an archive is for, and I do not know what principle could settle it. How should I think about this, assuming we do have to choose?
