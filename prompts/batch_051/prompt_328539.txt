A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether grief should count as an emotion at all. He insisted it is not an emotion but a temporally extended process structured by a continuing attachment, while I kept treating it as a paradigm emotion; neither of us could say what would settle the question, because we were each importing different criteria for what an emotion has to be. The argument also touched on fear of flying in someone who fully believes flying is safe, the difference between an irritable mood and an episode of anger, and why we feel fear for characters we know are fictional. It became clear that any position on these disputes is really a position about the desiderata a theory of emotion must satisfy, and we never got those out on the table.

So, before I read further in this area: write out the desiderata for an adequate theory of emotion, where adequacy means the theory has to underwrite work in both philosophy of mind and moral psychology, explaining what emotions are, how they interact with judgment and action, and how they can be assessed as fitting or unfitting. I want the specification, not a survey.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and state whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of some camp. At minimum, address the following:

- Recalcitrant emotion: a person trembles and avoids a flight while sincerely judging that flying is as safe as sitting in her own living room. Any theory must explain how the emotion and the considered judgment can diverge, on pain of simply identifying emotion with evaluative belief.
- Intentionality: anger is at an insult, fear of a dog, sadness about a loss. A theory must explain this aboutness, and not in a way that makes the emotion identical to a judgment about the object.
- Fit and evaluation: an emotion can be apt or inapt, warranted or unwarranted; fear of a real threat differs from fear of something harmless. This normative dimension must be preserved without collapsing it into either brute causation or moral assessment.
- Bodily involvement and valence: emotions feel like something and involve the body, yet indigestion, shivering, and the startle reflex must come out as non-emotions. Mere bodily arousal plus felt valence is therefore not sufficient, and the theory has to say what more is needed.
- Moods versus emotions: free-floating anxiety has no determinate object, while an episode of anger is about something. A theory must either assign moods a determinate object, explain how they are quasi-intentional, or deny that moods are emotions, but whichever option it takes, it must respect the phenomenologically vivid line between an irritable mood and an angry episode.
- Emotions in non-linguistic animals and preverbal infants: dogs and infants plainly have fear and anger. A theory cannot require linguistically articulated propositional content without becoming empirically and intuitively revisionary, so it must specify the minimal kind of content or appraisal emotion requires.
- Fictional and absent objects: we fear the monster in the film while fully believing it does not exist, and we grieve over losses in the past. An adequate theory must explain directed affect at known-absent or known-nonexistent objects without attributing to us a belief that the object exists or that the danger is real.
- Episode, disposition, and process: an episode of anger differs both from a disposition to anger, such as irritability, and from a drawn-out process such as mourning. Whatever the theory says about whether grief is an emotion, it must explain why grief resists classification as a single discrete episode and what that implies for the ontology of emotion.
- Cross-cultural variation: which states get named and grouped as emotion categories varies across languages and cultures. A theory should be consistent with this variation without being forced to declare emotions either entirely socially constructed or entirely fixed by biology.

Then do two things explicitly. First, flag any subset of these desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. For instance, a state essentially about an object may resist being one and the same as a mood that has no object; if so, say what has to give and what the cost is. Second, identify the single requirement that does the most work excluding the currently popular families of theory.

Finally, give me no more than a short paragraph each on how the judgment, perception, and pure feeling families of theory fare against the list, with honest attention to which of their failures are fixable by refinement and which are structural.
