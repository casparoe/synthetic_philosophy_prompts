After forty years in geochemistry I’ve finally gotten around to reading Berkeley seriously, and the thing that surprises me is how much of his thinking is about money. He spent a good chunk of the 1730s arguing that Ireland should set up a national bank and issue paper notes backed by land, and that people make a category mistake when they treat gold and silver as if they were the wealth itself rather than a way of counting claims on wealth. I’m about to interview a philosopher of economics who defends a reconstructed Berkeleyan position: roughly, that money is best understood as a social scorekeeping device for claims on real productive capacity, and that modern fiat is closer in spirit to Berkeley’s plan than to the gold standard.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that dont just let her restate the view. I want you to press where it hurts: why exactly a scorekeeping device maintains trust when the issuer has a bad harvest or a war, whether backing notes with land really solves anything that commodity backing didn’t, what the view says about inflation and whether it has any non–ad hoc stopping rule, how it handles international payments when foreigners won’t hold your paper, and whether Berkeley’s immaterialism does any real work in the monetary argument or is just biographical decoration. For each question, include the answer you predict she’ll give and a follow-up keyed to that answer. Then commit: identify the two questions that you think generate the most serious problems for the view, and explain why you picked them.
