After retiring from a career in biostatistics, I have been spending my time on the philosophy of law, and one problem has come to trouble me more than any other: the treatment of naked statistical evidence. For forty years I moved unthinkingly between a population frequency and a statement about an individual case; the law, by contrast, often refuses that move. In the familiar blue-bus scenario, a pedestrian is injured by a bus, and the only evidence tying the injury to a particular company is that that company operates eighty percent of the buses on the route—enough, arithmetically, to clear the civil preponderance standard, and yet courts and commentators have balked at imposing liability. In the gatecrasher scenario, an organizer can prove that more than half of a crowd entered without paying but cannot identify which attendees those were; it is more likely than not that any given attendee gate-crashed, but the law hesitates to find a randomly selected attendee liable. The same reluctance deepens in criminal cases, where a defendant may be shown to belong to a small class almost all of whose members are guilty, and still not be convicted on that showing alone. I cannot yet tell whether this resistance reflects a principled requirement of legal proof, a widely shared cognitive bias, or a confusion about what a frequency does and does not establish.

Before I read any further in this literature, I would like the specification rather than the survey. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of how a legal fact-finder should handle naked statistical evidence must satisfy. For each requirement give the case or consideration that motivates it: the scenarios it must decide correctly or explain away, the distinctions it must not collapse (preponderance versus proof beyond a reasonable doubt; civil liability versus criminal punishment; the probability that a proposition is true versus the propriety of acting on it; actuarial risk versus particularized proof; selection of the appropriate reference class, if you think that belongs on the list), and any results from probability, statistics, or decision theory that the theory must accommodate or explicitly rebut. Then indicate which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are commitments of particular camps, flag any subset that looks jointly unsatisfiable, and identify the single requirement that does the most work to exclude candidate theories.

I am not asking for a bibliography or a summary of named authors; I want the criteria and their justification. I would also be glad to learn whether your list itself is inconsistent—that is, whether no single account of legal fact-finding can satisfy everything you have laid down—since that would be as informative as a positive theory.
