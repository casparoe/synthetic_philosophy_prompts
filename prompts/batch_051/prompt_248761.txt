Consider a now-familiar pattern in platform moderation. Platforms take down recruitment videos produced by violent extremist groups and explicit hate propaganda when those files are uploaded without comment or context, but they permit the same clips when a journalist, researcher, or counter-extremism channel reposts them with captions or commentary that condemn, document, or analyze the material; many platforms also write exceptions into their rules for documentary, educational, or newsworthy use. On its face the pattern is strange. The repost shows the same images and conveys the same ideas to audiences that may include exactly the people the original was designed to recruit, and the condemning commentary is easily skipped. So the practice appears to presuppose that the harm of propaganda does not lie in its content reaching a mind, but in something about the act, its framing, or its uptake.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of harm, speech, and platform responsibility as precisely as you can. Then do four further things. Say which rival views the practice quietly rules out — for instance, a pure causal-exposure view on which any reproduction of the material is harmful, and a content-neutral view on which the platform should carry the original too. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined even after reconstruction. And say what would have to change in moderation practice if the presupposed view were replaced with a straightforward causal-exposure view.

Answer as exactly five numbered claims, each one to two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
