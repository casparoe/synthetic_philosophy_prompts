I have a rule I've caught myself following, and I want to know if it's actually defensible. When a doubt is one of those all-encompassing skeptical scenarios — the world just started five minutes ago with fake memories and fake fossils, I'm a brain in a vat, the people around me don't have inner lives — I don't treat the scenario as very unlikely. I don't assign it a probability at all. I just refuse to open the question, and I move on. My friend says that's the intellectually cowardly move: if I can't rule the scenario out, then I should give it some probability, however small, and if I won't even do that, I'm not being honest about my ordinary confidence. My reply has been that any inquiry into these things would have to use the very faculties being questioned — my memory to check whether my memories are broadly reliable, my senses to test my senses, my sense of other people to judge whether other people have minds — so the inquiry is rigged from the start and the correct response is not to run it.

But I also notice my rule has no obvious way to stop protecting bad certainties. Someone equally convinced that a family member would never lie to them in a serious matter could use the same move: refuse to look at the evidence, because the evidence might be part of the deception or because weighing it would already be disloyal. When I step back, I can see that their refusal looks pathological while mine looks like common sense, but I can't say what the principl difference is. Is my policy defensible as stated? If so, what distinguishes the questions I get to table from the ones I don't, other than my own comfort? And from the inside, how would I know if I drew the line in the wrong place?
