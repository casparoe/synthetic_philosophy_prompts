I have recently become a trustee of a small private foundation left by my great-uncle, who spent his later years living, with some strictness, by the principles he hoped the foundation would teach. The governing deed contains this clause:

"The Trustees shall practise a strict frugality in the administration of this Foundation, and no part of the income shall be laid out upon any scheme, however plausible or improving, the like object whereof might be accomplished at a smaller charge; for a charity which lives plainly is itself a lesson in the virtue it exists to diffuse among the young, namely habits of economy and provident living."

The Board must now decide a matter the Founder could not have imagined. A digital agency proposes a series of short video lessons on budgeting, thrift, and the avoidance of debt, placed before young people through paid social-media creators. The fee would consume nearly half of one year's income and would be, by a wide margin, the largest single commitment in the Foundation's history; but the agency's figures indicate that it would also reach, for each dollar spent, far more young people than anything else the Board has identified. The alternative, a school lecture series with printed materials, would cost a tenth as much and reach perhaps two percent as many. One faction on the Board reads the clause as a constraint on our own conduct: the Foundation must be frugal, so commissioning a glossy campaign is disqualifying even if it is instrumentally efficient. The other reads it as a directive to make the world more frugal: refusing the cheap-per-person campaign would waste most of the audience we could have reached, which is the greater extravagance.

I am less interested in what a court would decide than in what any coherent reading of the clause would say. Is the clause best read as requiring the Foundation to be frugal, or as requiring it to promote frugality? When the Founder wrote that the charity's plain living is itself part of the lesson, was he making the manner of the Foundation's spending non-instrumentally important, or merely describing a channel of communication? How much work should be done by the words "the like object whereof might be accomplished at a smaller charge"—and do algorithmic placements change what counts as "the same object"? If the act-constraint reading and the goal-maximizing reading genuinely diverge here, should we conclude that they are competing interpretations of one value, or that they are two values that happen to share a word?
