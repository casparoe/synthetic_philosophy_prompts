I'm a philosopher, and a colleague of mine in the bio department keeps insisting that moral considerability should scale with brain size—or, being a bit more careful, with cortical neuron count. His line is that if we could pump a mouse's neocortex up to human neuron counts, we'd have to start treating it like a being with serious moral standing, and not merely because it would now be smarter: the brain itself would be doing moral work. I want to know weather that's a claim we can actually test rather than just knock around in the seminar room.

Design the best empirical test—or a small battery of tests—you can for the claim that brain size (or neuron count) is morally relevant in its own right, not just a proxy for sentience, preferences, self-control, or whatever else we already happen to care about. Be concrete: what would you compare, manipulate, or measure? Say what pattern of results would push you toward "yes, the brain is doing the moral work itself," and what pattern would push you toward "no, it's only a stand-in for the real bearers of moral status." Then be honest about what even a clean result would leave unsettled. In particular: suppose every capacity we independently cared about tracked neuron count perfectly. Would neuron count then be morally relevant, or just impossible to distinguish from a proxy? Is the leftover merely a practical limitation of the lab, or is there a normative residue in the phrase "morally relevant" that no experiment can touch? I want you to mark the border between the testable part of the claim and the untestable part as precisely as you can.
