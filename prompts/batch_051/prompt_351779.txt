I have been reading Blaise Pascal's Pensées in a philosophy elective, and I have been trying to turn one section of it into something more general, which I would like assessed honestly. The section I have in mind is the one where Pascal says that justice is open to dispute while force is not, and that because we could not make what is just strong, we made what is strong just. As I understand him, he is not merely saying that might wins in practice. He says the sword gives a real right, and that if we did not grant force the authority to define justice, we would have to face the fact that force and justice simply point in different directions. Then, closely afterward, he writes that custom is the whole of equity for the sole reason that it is accepted, and that if you trace custom back to its first principles you destroy it. In other words, the foundation of a legal order cannot be looked at directly, not because it is bad or good, but because the looking itself undoes the authority.

I have been calling it the backward-titling effect. The idea is this: any sufficiently stable arrangement of force, once it has lasted long enough and organized enough of ordinary life, is experienced by the people living under it as having the right to be what it is, and this experience is not a separate belief they add on afterward. It is more like the background condition for finding their own activities intelligible, so that when the arrangement is challenged, the challenge feels unjust even to people who admit the arrangement began unjustly. The origin does not get forgiven; it gets forgotten, and the forgetting does the work of justification. I think this explains something Pascal gestures at but does not quite say: why people can be sincere when they defend a system they would have condemned if they had seen it at its founding, and why the worst thing you can do to an institution is not call it wrong but ask for its title.

I want to know whether this is a coherent claim as I have stated it, or whether I have just re-described ordinary legitimacy, ideology, or rationalization under a new name. Is my formulation saying anything that Pascal's own claim does not already say, and where does it break? I am especially unsure about the phrase "the forgetting does the work of justification," because forgetting sounds like an absence and justification sounds like a reason, and I am not sure an absence can be a reason. Please be direct about which parts of this would survive contact with the strongest objections and which parts would not, and tell me if the idea has already been worked out in a way I should be wary of accidentally reproducing.
