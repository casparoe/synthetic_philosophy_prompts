Consider this passage from Maimonides, *The Guide for the Perplexed*, III.32, in M. Friedländer’s translation:

“It was in accordance with the wisdom and plan of God, as displayed in the whole Creation, that He did not command us to give up and to discontinue all these manners of service; for to obey such a commandment it would have been contrary to the nature of man, who generally cleaves to that to which he is used; it would in those days have made the same impression as a prophet would make at present if he called us to the service of God and told us in His name, that we should not pray to Him, not fast, not seek His help in time of trouble; that we should serve Him in thought, and not by any action.”

Now consider the following argument someone might build from this passage:

(1) According to Maimonides, the sacrificial commandments were given because the Israelites could not suddenly abandon the mode of worship to which they were accustomed.  
(2) If a divine command is given because of a condition of its recipients that could in principle change, then the command is given for the sake of something other than itself.  
(3) If a divine command is given for the sake of something other than itself, then it is a concession to human weakness rather than a permanent feature of the Law.  
(4) Therefore, on Maimonides’s view, the sacrificial commandments are concessions to human weakness and are not permanent features of the Law.  
(5) But Maimonides also holds that the commandments of the Torah are binding for all time.  
(6) Therefore, Maimonides’s account of the sacrifices is in tension with his commitment to the eternity of the Law.

I cannot locate the fault: each step looks plausible, yet the conclusion seems stronger than what the passage actually licenses. Is this argument valid? If it is not, identify exactly which step fails, and state whether the failure is logical or arises from an ambiguity or unsupported premise.
