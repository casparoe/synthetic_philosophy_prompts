I teach a big intro to political philosophy course, and a few years ago I put this line in my syllabus:

"A curve will be applied to an exam only if it raises the final letter grade of the student with the lowest raw score on that exam."

The idea, obviously, was a little Rawlsian difference principle for grading: departures from a flat scale are only justified when the worst-off student is made better off by them. Not a perfect analogy, but the students get it. Anyway, this term something came up that the clause clearly wasn't written for. The final was harder than usual, and by my usual criteria a curve would be warranted. The lowest raw score belongs to a student who didn't sit the exam at all because of a mental health crisis; university rules give a zero for no-shows unless there's a formal accommodation, which this student didn't have. The second-lowest raw score belongs to a student whose apartment building caught fire the week of the exam, and they took an emergency make-up under genuinely bad conditions and scored poorly, but their overall grade still passes. For every other student the curve would lift things by about a third of a letter grade.

If I apply the clause mechanically, the zero is the lowest raw score, and curving the exam wouldn't raise the zero to a D, so the clause forbids the curve. But that seems to make the whole class hostage to a zero that isn't really the relevant kind of disadvantage the principle was meant to track. I could treat the zero as a penalty rather than a "position" in the class's distribution, and ask whether the curve benefits the worst-off among those who actually took the exam. But then I'm sort of deciding who counts as least advantaged before applying the difference principle, which feels question-begging.

So here's what I want help with: does the clause, read as a mini difference principle, require me to include the zero when identifying the least advantaged? If not, what principle of exclusion am I implicitly using, and is it one Rawls would accept? And should the particularity of the disadvantage—no-show due to mental health vs. taking the exam in a hotel after a fire—matter to who counts as the "least advantaged" in a local distribution like this, or is that exactly the sort of information the veil of ignorance is supposed to make us ignore?
