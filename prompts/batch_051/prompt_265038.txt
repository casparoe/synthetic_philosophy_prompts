I have been trying to get clear about what Heidegger means when he says that we come to see our tools most explicitly not when they work but when they fail. My current paraphrase is that in absorbed work a tool is ready-to-hand: it does its job without coming into focus, as part of a whole network of tasks, materials, other people, and purposes. When something goes wrong, the tool becomes present-to-hand, an object of explicit attention, and along with it the network itself becomes visible — the workshop, the dependencies on suppliers, the deadlines, the point of the whole activity. To test the boundaries of this idea, I propose four criteria for a genuine breakdown of equipment in this sense. C1: the equipment was functioning transparently immediately before the episode. C2: the episode interrupts ongoing practical activity rather than being deliberately sought out. C3: the user's attention shifts from the work to the tool itself. C4: what becomes visible is not just the isolated tool but the wider web of use and dependence in which it was embedded.

The borderline case is one a colleague in an engineering department described to me. A machinist runs a CNC mill that is fitted with a predictive-maintenance system. One afternoon the system pauses the job, reports that a bearing is about to fail, and instructs the machinist to stop and schedule a replacement. The machinist sets down the workpiece, reads the diagnostics, and for the first time in months sees the mill as a piece of machinery inside a production schedule — suppliers, downtime costs, maintenance staff, the purpose of the order. The machine never refused the work and never broke in use; the monitoring system preempted the actual failure. Does this episode count as a breakdown of equipment in the sense specified by C1 through C4? Go through the criteria one by one, say which are satisfied, violated, or genuinely indecisive, and then say whether the indecisive ones reveal an ambiguity in the phenomenon or a defect in my criteria. I especially want to know whether a merely anticipated failure can do the same revelatory work as an actual one, and whether the emphasis on breakdown overstates the role of the tool's material resistance as opposed to any interruption that makes the network of purposes explicit.
