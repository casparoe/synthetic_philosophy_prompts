I'm one of three people on the grant committee of a small family foundation. We meet quarterly to choose which of a dozen applicant organizations will receive funding, and our routine is fixed: each of us reviews the same full application packet on our own, but one of us scrutinizes the finances most closely, another the program design and staff, and the third — my role in this cycle — the organization's community relationships and its record on previous grants. We deliberately keep our individual notes private until the meeting. Then we go around the table in the same order each time, starting with the chair, and each person states a recommendation before any open discussion begins. This week we considered an organization I came in ready to fund; my read of their community ties and past-grant performance was strongly positive. The chair recommended rejecting. The second member — who, as far as I knew, had drawn an area that should have left him favorably disposed — also recommended rejecting. By the time it reached me, I had largely convinced myself that two independent negative conclusions outweighed my one positive slice of the evidence, and I recommended rejecting as well.

I have been turning it over since, not because I felt pressured or cowed — the three of us are collegial and the chair does not pull rank — but because I cannot tell whether my reasoning at the time was actually justified. Once I knew how the first two had voted, should I rationally have discounted my own information to the point of voting with them, or was I manufacturing a reason to avoid being the lone dissent? And ought a procedure like ours to worry us even if every member is competent, honest, and sincerely trying to make the best grant? I would rather understand the structure of what happened in that meeting than be told simply to trust my gut or to speak up more.
