I was arguing with a friend who edits a small regional paper about how aggressively newsrooms should fact-check stories that sound too wild to be true. He kept saying he was just following Hume's rule about miracles, but it seemed to me that the policy was really just a way to never have to revise what the paper already "knows." So I wanted to turn it into a short parody piece.

Write a parody of an internal memo from the editor-in-chief of a small regional paper announcing a new "No Miracles" editorial policy, explicitly grounded in David Hume's argument about testimony in his essay on miracles. The memo should be deadpan and corporate, with a subject line like "Updated policy on astonishing claims" and a few numbered subsections—e.g., what counts as a "law of nature" for editorial purposes, the minimum number of independent witnesses required, why rival miracle reports cancel each other out, and the form staff fill out when they have a story involving an improbable event. The satire should target the way people wave the slogan extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence around as a way of never having to update on surprising but well-attested reporting; in particular, exaggerate the move of quietly treating very improbable but lawful events as if they were miracles, and the circularity of using the editor's own past experience as the uniform experience every report has to beat. It should be funny to someone who knows Hume's essay (the falsehood-would-be-more-miraculous test, the love of wonder, the rival-religions point) and readable to someone who doesn't. Dont include actual quotations from Hume—paraphrase instead. Keep the memo under 650 words, not counting the subject line.
