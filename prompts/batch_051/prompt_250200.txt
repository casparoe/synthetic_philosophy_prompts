I run a small philosophy discussion group and two of the regulars have been at each other's throats for months about conspiracy theories. One's a generalist: she thinks the category conspiracy theory itself carries an epistemic defect — that any claim about a secret group orchestrating events is suspect as a kind, because of how such claims tend to handle counter-evidence. The other's a particularist: he thinks that's a category mistake, that each theory stands or falls on its own evidence, and that the standing suspicion of the category is mostly a move people use to avoid engaging with awkward theories. They've agreed to stop re-running the same argument and to produce an adversarial collaboration statement instead.

Your job is to draft that statement. I want: (1) a numbered section of claims both can sign, including claims about the history (official narratives have sometimes been false — Tuskegee, MKUltra, tobacco companies and cancer) and claims about what they are not asserting; (2) a disagreements table, with each row worded so that the generalist would accept the phrasing of her side and the particularist would accept the phrasing of his; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions that, if settled, would actualy move at least one of them; and (4) for each crux, a concrete test, case, or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If part of their dispute is just about how to define conspiracy theory, say which part and why. The hard bit is finding formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted, not just listing topics. Write the whole thing in British English.
