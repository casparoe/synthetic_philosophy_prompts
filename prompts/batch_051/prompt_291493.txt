I am a retired hydrogeologist. Since stepping away from field work, I have been reading decision theory, the philosophy of probability, and the literature on risk, and I now serve as the scientific member of a three-person regional authority that rules on water-quality and land-use permits. Our enabling statute instructs us to invoke the precautionary principle wherever a proposed activity may cause serious or irreversible harm, but beyond that sentence it is silent. The practical burden has fallen to me to turn that sentence into something the three of us can apply consistently, and I find that most available guidance is either slogan or evasion.

I would like a reusable decision procedure, not an essay on the history of the principle. The constraints are these: permits arrive about monthly; we have at most half a day of deliberation per application; the dossier is almost always applicant-supplied and incomplete; we cannot commission new studies; the chair is a retired judge with no scientific training who must sign the order; and our rulings are effectively unreviewable for years. The procedure should tell us, in order, which questions to ask, what evidence counts as enough to trigger precaution, and when we should approve, approve with monitoring, defer pending data, or refuse.

At least four distinctions must survive in the procedure, and I want to see how they are handled: first, the difference between a measurable risk with a reasonably known distribution and severe uncertainty about what the possible outcomes even are; second, the asymmetry between missing evidence of harm and evidence of no harm, without imposing a burden of proof so heavy that no novel substance could ever pass; third, irreversibility and the option value of waiting, set against the cost that waiting itself imposes on others; and fourth, the inconvenient fact that the status quo is not risk-free, so caution must not quietly harden into a bias toward whatever already exists. Please also say plainly where judgment cannot be eliminated and how the procedure misfires if someone pretends otherwise.

Then apply the procedure to these three docket sketches: a fungicide used for a decade in a neighbouring state, where one meta-analysis reports a weak and statistically fragile association with childhood illness near treated fields; a proposal to store a novel persistent fluorinated compound for fire suppression, with no long-term toxicity data, where the applicant argues that the absence of demonstrated harm should count in its favour; and a detection at parts per trillion of an old industrial solvent in groundwater, made visible only by new instrumentation and far below any established effect level. I expect at least one of your outcomes to be one my panel would rather not reach, and at least one to run against the applicant. Conclude by stating what the procedure cannot do, and which question it deliberately refuses to answer.
