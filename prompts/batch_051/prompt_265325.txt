I've been trying to reverse-engineer my advisor's theory of how practices go from totally respectable to beyond the pale. She works on what she calls moral revolutions — slavery, dueling, footbinding, that kind of thing — but she refuses to state her view in the abstract; she just hands down verdicts on individual cases and lets me guess. Here's my best reconstruction of her track record:

Dueling among English gentlemen: she says the moral and religious arguments against it were fully developed by 1750 and did nothing on their own. What ended it was a flip in what high-status men found honorable; once a duel could make you look kind of absurd rather than brave, the practice collapsed fast. She doesn't treat that one as an economic story at all.

British abolition: here she's got a Williams-ish, material-interest line. Her version is that abolition passed when the planters lost their grip in Parliament and compensation made the bill palatable, and that half a century of moral argument mostly supplied fine words for an interest shift. But she also won't say it was pure economics — the empire really did lose money on abolition, and she thinks the moral campaign did genuine work by making pro-slavery votes embarrassing to hold. So that one is interests, with a dash of honor.

Footbinding in China: she says it unraveled within a generation because natural-foot societies and marriage market pressures turned bound feet into a badge of backwardness. Not because anyone came up with a new argument about women's bodies; the arguments were old, and the status/emotion cascade did the work. Pure honor, basically.

Public executions in Europe: she says the reformers' cruelty arguments were mostly decoration. The spectacle stopped when states had enough police and prison capacity that they no longer needed a crowd to watch order being restored. Which sounds almost functionalist, or at least capacity-and-interest driven.

Next term she's writing about a smaller, more recent case: why dog meat went from a normal, if declining, part of the diet in South Korea in the 1980s and 90s to a banned practice in 2024, by which point consumption had already fallen something like 85% from its peak. She keeps poking at the fact that the ban came after the practice had shrunk, not before, so the simple interest story doesn't fit cleanly; but the honor story has a timing problem too, since the shame seems to have been around for a while before the ban. My guess is she'll make it a status story — that dog meat became embarrassing in a wealthy, pet-owning, globally watched country, and the ban mostly ratified a shift that had already happened in kitchens and restaurants. But I can also see her going Williams-ish on it, saying the last producer lobby got small enough to be beaten and the legislature only moved once nobody important was left to fight.

So: predict what she'll say was the main driver in the dog-meat case, say what theory she's implicitly running — honor, interest, arguments-as-decoration, or some hybrid — and tell me which further case or fact would most cleanly settle which rule she's actually using. I'm less interested in South Korea than in the structure: where does her track record genuinely pin her down, and where is she still ambiguous? And when you answer, please seperate what's actually settled about these historical cases from what's still genuinely contested among people who study them — don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
