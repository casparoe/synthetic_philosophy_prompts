Since retiring from a long career in the natural sciences, I have been working through some classical literature, and I find myself puzzled by the Orpheus and Eurydice story. The version I first absorbed, and the one that seems to dominate the popular imagination, is roughly this: Orpheus descends to the underworld, moves its rulers with his music, and is permitted to lead Eurydice back on the condition that he not look behind him; at the last moment he looks, and she is lost forever. Recently, however, I have met other ancient tellings that do not fit this pattern. In a speech preserved in Plato's Symposium, the gods send Orpheus away with only a phantom of Eurydice, because he had contrived to enter Hades alive rather than die for love as Alcestis did; in that version his rescue never comes close to succeeding. Elsewhere the story is allowed to continue past Orpheus's death to a final reunion, or left in permanent separation, or turned into an allegory about self-control, the limits of art, or the impossibility of retrieving the dead. My scientific habits keep insisting that there ought to be a determinate answer here: which of these is the canonical version of the myth, and should the others be treated as later corruptions or distortions of an original story? Is there a principled method—perhaps something like textual criticism, or a theory of how oral literature is transmitted—by which one could reconstruct the original Orpheus story behind the surviving variants, and would that reconstructed story deserve to be called the true myth? I would be grateful if you would also state how confident you are in your claims and identify the point you regard as least certain.
