Consider how readily we draw moral lessons from exemplary lives, from ancient moral biography and mediaeval hagiography down to modern schoolroom accounts of great figures. A colonial administrator is admired for his administrative energy while his cruelty is set aside as a prejudice of his age; a celebrated reformer is praised for her courage while her habitual dishonesty to her family is quietly omitted from the standard account. The same person who happily extracts a virtue from such a life will usually insist that it is intellectually disreputable to extract only a scientific theory's successful predictions and discard its failures, or to judge a firm's strategy by counting only the quarters in which it happened to work. Both practices select the favourable parts of a whole and set the rest aside. Is there a principled difference that justifies this asymmetry, or is moral learning from biography a form of cherry-picking we tolerate mainly because it supplies useful exemplars? Set out the strongest defence of the asymmetry you can construct, test it against the hard case of a virtue exercised in the service of an unjust cause, and say whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned.
