You are a professional philosopher specialising in the metaphysics of causation, equally at home with counterfactual, interventionist, and physical-process theories. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the strongest arguments for each side before committing to a verdict.

I am torn between a theory I respect and an intuition I cannot shake, and I want your help in working out what each resolution would cost.

On reflection, I find a broadly counterfactual or interventionist account of causation compelling. On this view, C is a cause of E when, relative to a suitable model and the background circumstances, an intervention on C would change E or its probability; in the case of an omission, the omitted event's occurring would have made the relevant difference. The account is attractive because it is unified and principled: it applies the same basic test to positive and negative causal claims, it links causation tightly to prediction and control, it lets the choice of model rather than mysterious metaphysical glue determine which of many conditions counts as the cause, and it handles cases that energy-transfer or productive views find awkward. The plant died because the gardener failed to water it; the bridge collapsed because the engineer never ran the stress test; the patient died because the nurse omitted the second dose. In each case the counterfactual is clean, and the causal attribution seems correct.

But I also hold a firm intuition that I cannot talk myself out of. An omission is an absence, a not-occurring, and an absence is not the sort of thing that can make anything happen. The plant died from desiccation, not from a non-watering; the patient died from the disease, not from the dose that never arrived. The idea that a nothing caused a something feels like a category mistake rather than a slightly figurative way of speaking. Worse, the counterfactual account taken neat seems to prove too much. If the gardener's failure to water caused the plant's death, then so did the failure of every other person on Earth to water it, since for each such person it is true that had that person watered it, the plant would have survived. The view would then need extra machinery — salience, normalcy, duty, contrast classes — to keep only the gardener's omission causal. Once that machinery is added, the theory may no longer be the simple, unified view that attracted me.

I do not want a survey of the literature or a list of positions. I want you to do three things. First, present the strongest case you can for the claim that the counterfactual or interventionist account should treat omissions as full causes, and that my intuition is a vestige of a push-and-shove conception of production that should be debunked. Second, present the strongest case for the claim that the intuition tracks a real metaphysical distinction and that the theory should be amended or restricted — for example, by treating omission statements as contrastive explanations, as normative or responsibility ascriptions, or as mere counterfactual dependence without causation. Third, give me a negotiated verdict: which side should yield on which issue, what would justify each possible move, and what would have to be different for the opposite resolution to be right. In particular, address the overflow problem directly: can the counterfactual view handle it without giving up the virtues that made the view attractive, and does the distinction between causing and allowing do real work here, or does it merely change the subject?

Take the space you need, and commit to a specific answer about what should be given up.
