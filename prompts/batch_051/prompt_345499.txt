I have been working through the section of Hume's Treatise of Human Nature, Book III, Part II, entitled Of the obligation of promises, and I find the opening argument much more compressed than it first appears. The passage runs as follows:

> THAT the rule of morality, which enjoins the performance of promises, is not natural, will sufficiently appear from these two propositions, which I proceed to prove, viz. that a promise wou'd not be intelligible, before human conventions had establish'd it; and that even if it were intelligible, it wou'd not be attended with any moral obligation.
>
> I say, first, that a promise is not intelligible naturally, nor antecedent to human conventions; and that a man, unacquainted with society, could never enter into any engagements with another, even tho' they could perceive each other's thoughts by intuition. If promises be natural and intelligible, there must be some act of the mind attending these words, I promise; and on this act of the mind must the obligation depend. Let us, therefore, run over all the faculties of the soul, and see which of them is exerted in our promises.

What puzzles me is that Hume seems to be running together two claims I would have wanted to keep apart. One appears to be a claim about meaning or conceptual possibility: before some human convention exists, the words 'I promise' would have no determinate content, so that a promise could not even be understood. The other appears to be a claim about obligation: even if the words were understood, no natural act of the mind could make the utterance binding. Yet the passage slides quickly from the first to the second, and the proposed test — running over the faculties of the soul to find the act of mind that answers to the words 'I promise' — looks as though it is being asked to support both claims at once. Could you help me reconstruct exactly what the argument at this stage is supposed to establish? In particular: what does Hume mean by saying that a promise is not naturally intelligible, and how is the absence of a suitable act of the mind meant to show that? Does the argument bear on what the words mean, on when they create obligations, or on both, and how do those readings fit together? I would also be grateful for an account of what the thought experiment about two people who could perceive each other's thoughts by intuition is meant to rule out, since that is the detail I find hardest to place within the larger argument.
