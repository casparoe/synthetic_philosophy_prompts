In my epistemology course we've spent several weeks on radical skepticism, and I've realized that I respond to the standard skeptical argument with a policy I can't yet defend. The argument runs: if I know I have hands, then I know I'm not a brain in a vat; I don't know I'm not a brain in a vat; therefore I don't know I have hands. I don't assess the premises neutrally. I take my knowledge that I have hands to be more certain than the second premise, so I run the argument backward: the conditional seems right and I do know I have hands, so I conclude that I must know I'm not a brain in a vat, and only then do I ask why the second premise seemed plausible. Is this a defensible epistemic habit, or is it begging the question and rationalizing after the fact? If it is defensible, how should it be stated so that it doesn't license me to keep any conclusion I happen to favor whenever a valid-looking argument challenges it, and where does it fail?
