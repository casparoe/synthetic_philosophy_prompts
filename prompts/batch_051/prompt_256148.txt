A national government, persuaded by the argument that leisure is not recovery time for work but an activity pursued for its own sake, creates a Leisure Dividend: a monthly payment to every citizen that may be spent only on activities meeting four statutory tests, drawn from the tradition of thinking about play. The subsidized activity must be freely chosen, oriented to no external result, marked off from ordinary routines, and governed by its own internal rules rather than by some goal outside itself. To police this, each recipient submits a weekly log describing how the Dividend was used, and a Leisure Board audits a random sample of logs; any activity judged instrumental, however subtly, such as socializing for career advantage, exercising for productivity, cooking for the household, or making art for sale, forfeits that month's payment. The Board is explicitly instructed to distinguish genuine leisure from both recuperative downtime and idle aimlessness, and to treat coercion arising from the audit process itself as outside the definition of freely chosen activity.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a question about whether leisure should be publicly valued. Analyze how the institution would actually operate. What incentives does the Leisure Dividend create for how citizens spend their time, choose activities, and describe those activities in their logs? What happens to the intrinsic character of an activity once it must be narrated, defended, and audited? How would recipients game the audit, and how would auditors, knowing they are being gamed, escalate their scrutiny? Does the scheme's reliance on an external reward and coercive verification undermine the very quality it is meant to protect, and can it be redesigned to avoid that failure without giving up either the subsidy or the definition of leisure as an end in itself?
