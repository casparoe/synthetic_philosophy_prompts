I've been developing a little theory about Hume on miracles that I'm calling the Independence Threshold, and I'd like an honest assessment of it. The standard gloss of Hume is that a miracle report can never be rationally believed because it's always outweighed by our uniform past experience of nature's regularities. I think that frames the problem wrong. The real bottleneck, on my view, isn't the prior probability of the event described; it's the prior probability that this specific report would have arisen through some ordinary non-miraculous process. So a report should move you to belief exactly when P(the report exists | the miracle didn't happen) is lower than P(the relevant law was suspended), where the second quantity is your antecedent willingness to think the world is the sort of place where that law sometimes fails.

The upshot is what I call the Independence Threshold: testimonial evidence for a miracle only accumulates across witnesses if each witness's report would be generated independently conditional on the miracle not having occurred. Five people all coming out of the same revival tent with the same story give you basically zero extra evidential weight, because one shared social cause could explain all of them. But a single witness with a long, checkable track record of being right about ordinary factual matters could in principle have an error rate lower than your prior on the law-violation, and then their report rationally wins. I think this is a clean fix for Hume, because it saves his point about human credulity while blocking his stronger conclusion that no testimony can ever establish a miracle.
