I have been tinkering with a toy simulation in the spirit of the careenium. It consists of a large grid of identical, simple, locally interacting elements—call them simmballs—each of which updates its state according to a short rule that refers only to its immediate neighbours. When the grid is large enough and run long enough, robust coarse-grained patterns emerge: whorls, fronts, and closed loops that persist for thousands of updates, drift through the grid, split, and merge. I have learned to read some of these loops as symbols, because one particular loop reliably precedes a striking macro-level event in which the whole grid locks into a low-entropy lattice configuration that no individual simmball rule mentions. A colleague now argues that the loop is not merely a convenient summary of the underlying traffic. He claims the symbol is causally in charge: it pushes individual simmballs around, so that the best explanation of the lattice is that the coarse pattern directed the micro-level states rather than riding on top of them. I suspect the claim may have less empirical content than he thinks, but I want to test it rather than dismiss it.

Design the strongest concrete test you can of the claim that a coarse-grained symbol in such a system is causally effective over its own substrate. Specify the manipulation or measurement you would perform, the outcomes that would favour the causal reading and the deflationary reading respectively, and where the two readings remain hardest to separate. Then be explicit about what the test would and would not settle: could any observation distinguish a symbol that acts as a cause from a symbol that is merely a compressed description of causes operating one level down, or does the distinction outrun the data?
