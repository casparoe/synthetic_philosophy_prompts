I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about when the word "genocide" should apply, going by my gut on particular cases. My verdicts are all over the place and I want to know whether there's a principle that captures them, or which ones I should give up. Heres my list.

The Holocaust: yes, obviously, the paradigm. Rwanda in 1994, killing Tutsis: yes. Srebrenica in 1995: yes, even though it was one town and several thousand men and boys. Armenia under the Ottoman Empire: yes. The Holodomor, the Soviet famine in Ukraine: I end up saying yes, but it feels different—nobody was rounded up into camps, the weapons were grain requisitions and sealed borders, and lots of people frame it as an attack on a class, kulaks, rather than a nation. I still call it genocide.

Then the no side. The Atlantic slave trade: no, even though the death toll was enormous, because the point was to extract labor, not to make a people disappear. The Khmer Rouge killing city dwellers and former officials as class enemies: no, even though the numbers were huge, because class and politics feel like the wrong kind of group. But then the Khmer Rouge killing Cham and Vietnamese: yes, no hesitation. I'm aware how inconsistent that is.

Forced removal of indigenous children into residential schools: I want to say yes, or at least genocidal, even though the body count was much lower, because the point was to dissolve the group into the settler population. A policy of sterilizing an indigenous group so it eventually dies out: yes, obviously genocidal. But a state that systematically expels an ethnic group from its land, destroys its shrines, and bans its language, while killing almost no one—I keep going back and forth. Some days that's just ethnic cleansing and not genocide; other days it seems like exactly what "destroying a group" ought to cover.

Part of the problem is legal: the Convention only protects national, ethnic, racial, and religious groups, so class and political groups are out. But my actual usage doesnt track that cleanly—I count the Holodomor partly because it hit a nation even if the official target was kulaks. The principle I keep reaching for is that genocide isnt about how many die, it's about whether the project is to make a group disappear as a group, rather than to exploit, punish, or displace the people in it. But that principle might be doing no real work, and it doesn't explain why I treat the Holodomor differently from Cambodia's class killings. I also notice I'm quicker to reach for the word when I already dislike the regime, which makes me trust my own verdicts less.

So: is there a coherent principle behind these verdicts, or close? If you had to state the principle and then apply it to the expulsion-and-banned-language case, what would it say? And which of my verdicts does it force me to drop?
