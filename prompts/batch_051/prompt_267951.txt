In my school's law club we run a mock "Internet Supreme Court" where our advisor, a retired First Amendment lawyer, gives us her rulings on hypothetical posts about a local restaurant, Cafe Borealis, and we try to predict her next one. So far she's ruled that "Cafe Borealis is a total rip-off, I hate it" is protected opinion; that "three separate employees told me the owner doesn't pay overtime" is not protected because it asserts a checkable fact; that a bare one-star rating with no words is protected because ratings are subjective; that "word around the kitchen is that the chef has hep C" is not protected because even though it's phrased as a rumor it still carries a checkable health fact; that "the owner is a greasy weasel" is protected as name-calling hyperbole; that "I called the health department and they told me the place has accumulated twelve violations" is not protected because the number of violations is checkable; and that "Cafe Borealis will be gone by June, mark my words" is protected as a forecast.

Our next case is splitting the club: the same site's editor posts "Based on the city inspection records I reviewed, Cafe Borealis will lose its license by summer; I give it a 1 out of 10." Half of us think that's protected because it's still a prediction plus a rating, like the bare star rating and the bare forecast. The other half think it's not protected because it leans on an outside factual basis, like the overtime post, the rumor post, and the inspection post. If we want her to stay consistent with herself, how should she rule, what's your best guess and why, which earlier case is the closest analag, and what principle would we be adopting with each of the two possible rulings? If the old cases don't actually settle it, tell us what future post would force her to choose between the rules.
