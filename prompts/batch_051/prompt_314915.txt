You are a political philosopher specializing in democratic theory. I am writing a paper on who should have the vote in a modern representative democracy, and in particular on the cluster of questions around compulsory voting, lowering the voting age, and enfranchising long-term non-citizen residents. Before I evaluate existing proposals—theories that ground the franchise in being subject to the laws, in having one's interests affected, in political competence, or in having a stake in the polity—I want the specification that any adequate theory of the franchise would have to meet. Write the list of desiderata, with the case or distinction behind each: which cases the theory must classify correctly or at least handle gracefully (young children, adolescents, adults with severe cognitive disabilities, resident non-citizens, emigrants, prisoners, people who do not yet exist but will live under current decisions), which distinctions it must not collapse (who is eligible to vote versus how much weight a vote has versus who may stand for office versus whether voting may be made compulsory), and which empirical findings about political judgment, cognitive development, or voter behavior it cannot simply contradict. Begin by stating, in a sentence, what you take the point of a vote to be, since the desiderata will track that. For each item on the list, say whether you regard it as a fixed point or as a negotiable preference of one camp, and flag any pair of items that cannot be jointly satisfied; that tension-spotting is the part I most want, so give it real attention. Then, in at most a sentence per family of view, note which single requirement does the most to exclude the proposals I named. Think carefully, and be explicit about how confident you are in each entry and where you are making a judgment call. If I have set the task up badly—for example, if "who should get a vote" cannot be separated from "which electoral system and which offices are being voted on," or if writing a neutral list of desiderata already begs the question against one family of view—say so plainly rather than writing the list as though the problem were straightforward.
