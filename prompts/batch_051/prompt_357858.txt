I'm in a third-year module called Persons and Community in Chinese Philosophy (we've just done Mencius and a bit of Confucian role ethics, and the lecturer keeps framing everything as 'individualism versus collectivism'), and I've come up with a distinction of my own that I'd like someone to kick the tyres on before I build my essay around it.

I call it the knot-self. The individualist picture, as I understand it, says a person is a continuous chooser — a thread — and that the thread is already there before it enters any relationship; relationships are just ties it later chooses to tie or untie. The collectivist picture we keep getting given is the mirror image: the thread is nothing over and above its ties. My thought is that both are wrong because both keep the thread in the picture. What if a person just is the knot — the point where several strands of obligation, to parents, siblings, teachers, friends, maybe ancestors and future students, are pulled tight enough that you can point to something and say 'that one'. The knot is what individuates you; it's not that there is a self first and strands then attach to it. So 'becoming yourself' isn't about discovering a hidden thread or choosing your affiliations from nowhere, it's about tending to the knot: deciding which strands should take the strain and which should be let slacken. I think Mencius is groping towards something like this with the four sprouts, except he wants the strands to be in the grain from the start.

Where I think the idea earns its keep is that it makes individuation a matter of degree. Some knots are tighter and more distinct than others; a person can loosen into something barely more than the surrounding net. That seems right to me in a way the binary 'either a self or a mere bit of a group' never is. It also, maybe, explains why ritual matters: repeated bowing and set forms of address are literally how the strands get pulled taut.

But I can't tell whether I've actually said anything new or just re-described relational selves, Ubuntu, and Confucian role ethics with a rope metaphor. And the view might have a problem with the hermit or the monk who cuts every strand: if they stop being a knot, have they stopped being a person, or thinned into a different kind of thing? I also suspect its not clear what 'tightness' or 'taking the strain' could mean once I stop speaking in metaphor, and the whole theory might be unfalsifiable — any bit of autonomy I point to gets re-described as a choice about which knot to pull.

Give me the strongest reconstruction of the knot-self, tell me whether it collapses into something already on the shelf, and then hit it with the three best objections — and say whether any of them is fatal rather than just embarrassing.
