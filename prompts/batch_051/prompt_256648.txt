I am in my final year of school, where I am studying both Latin and Italian, and I have become stuck on a question that began as a passing remark from my Latin teacher. She said that there is no year — and perhaps no century — in which Latin became Italian, because languages change so gradually that any line between them is drawn afterwards by historians and nationalists rather than by the speakers themselves. I find the underlying picture genuinely compelling: each generation could understand the one before it, whilst the familiar changes (the collapse of the case system, the rise of articles, the slow shift in word order) accumulated in small steps, and 'Latin' and 'Italian' are names we impose on a continuous stream. The theory has the virtues I tend to favour: it is simple, it unifies a great many scattered facts, and it tells me not to look for a sharp boundary where there is none.

And yet I cannot make the contrary intuition give way. When I put a passage of Cicero next to a paragraph from a modern Italian newspaper, it seems plainly true that these are two different languages, not merely two points on a single continuum. It also seems as if there should be a fact about when the one stopped and the other started — or at least a fact about which texts count as Latin and which as Italian — even if nobody at the time noticed the change taking place. I have heard it said that this is only the heap paradox reappearing, or like asking exactly when a child becomes an adult, and that the intuition is simply the mind insisting on a boundary where nature has none. But I am not sure that response engages the intuition rather than explaining it away.

I would like you to set out the strongest case for each side, and then to assess the possible resolutions and what each would cost. If the intuition should be given up, I want to know what sort of debunking story would justify that and what it would have to explain. If the theory should be revised, I want to know what revision would be needed — for instance, whether 'being the same language' might track something social or literary rather than purely structural — and what would count as evidence for it. Is there a reading on which both survive, so that Latin and Italian are genuinely distinct without there having been a moment of transition? Please separate what is settled among people who study this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Please write your answer in American English.
