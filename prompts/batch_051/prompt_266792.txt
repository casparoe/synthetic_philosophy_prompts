I'm a novelist working on a book set in a small country that's about to issue its first official apology for a massacre carried out by a previous government forty years ago, and the scene keeps stalling because I cant get clear on what the characters are actually doing when they apologize. The foreign ministry lawyers want the statement to say the state "deeply regrets" what happened and refuse to allow the word "apologize," since they say that word could be read as admitting legal liability and trigger compensation claims. The survivor groups say that without the word "apology"—and without the current head of state kneeling, or at least visibly weeping—the whole thing is just public relations. Meanwhile the main character, the prime minister, who was a teenager at the time of the massacre, keeps asking what "we" refers to when she says "we apologize": she didn't do the killing, most citizens didn't, and the people who did are dead or in hiding. And there's a second thing she keeps circling: the same state will not apologize for the surveillance and detention practices it is running right now, which makes her suspect that distance, not contrition, is what makes an apology possible.

I don't want the novel to have a thesis exactly, but I want to understand the machinery of these rituals well enough that the character's confusion reads as real confusion and not fake naivete. So: what philosophical commitments does the practice of an official state apology presuppose? In particular, what does it presuppose about who "we" are—a nation as a person that persists across generations, an institution that can inherit something like guilt, a merely convenient shorthand? Does it presuppose that groups can be morally responsible, or only that they can take on remedial obligations? And what is the act supposed to do: wipe something clean, make a promise, revise the story a people tells about itself, redistribute standing? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory behind the practice, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with or gets bolted on, and then say what would have to change in the ritual if one of those presuppositions were dropped—say, if only individuals turned out to be moral agents, or if responsibility could not be inherited across generations.
