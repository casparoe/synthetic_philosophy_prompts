I've been trying to think through whether AI development between countries or labs is best modeled as a race or as a bargaining failure, and I keep coming back to Fearon's rationalist explanations for war. As I understand it, Fearon's move is: war is costly, so there should always be some negotiated deal both sides would prefer to fighting; a rationalist explanation of war therefore has to point to whatever stops the bargain from being found or kept. He gives three candidates — private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems (one side can't credibly promise not to use a future advantage), and issue indivisibility (though he seems to think this last one is mostly fixable with side payments). People have started borrowing this to talk about AI races, but I'm not sure the framework actually survives the transplant.

In Fearon's setup the actors are states, the disputed object is territory or policy, and the costly outside option is a discrete event called war. In an AI race the actors are often private labs with investors who want them to win outright, the prize — being first to transformative AI — looks indivisible and winner-take-all, and the costly alternative to agreement isn't a single destructive event but the race itself: everyone skimping on safety to go faster. Does Fearon's argument survive this? I'm interested in which premise actually does the work in the original, which one breaks down or changes meaning once you move to AI races, and whether there's a repaired version that says something weaker but still useful — or whether the framework is just doing a different job in the AI case than it does for interstate war. I cant tell if I'm confusing levels of analysis or if the racing frame really is a bargaining problem in disguise.
