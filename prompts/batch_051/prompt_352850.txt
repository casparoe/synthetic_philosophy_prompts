I'm in a philosophy of AI course and we keep going back to Dennett's True Believers essay and his thermostat example, where he argues that even a simple thermostatic device can be treated as if it wants the room to be warmer and believes the room is too cold, because doing so helps you predict what it will do, even though no one thinks the thermostat has an inner mental life. That move always made sense to me for dumb regulators. But I'm trying to work out what happens when the system isn't a bimetallic strip but a big language model trained to produce exactly the kinds of sentences people use to self-ascribe beliefs. Suppose the model has no separate truthfulness or reasoning objective, just next-token prediction over a corpus full of I think, I believe, I'm not sure, and so on. To make it concrete: the model controls a smart-home thermostat and can answer natural-language questions about why it changed the setting. When you ask it why it turned the heat on, it says it believed the room was getting too cold. Treating it as a believer seems to license stronger predictions than Dennett's original as-if stance: we'd start expecting it to have a coherent belief set, to update when contradicted, to be able to report its evidence. But the actual system may just be producing the string that is statistically most likely after why did it turn it on. Does the intentional stance still work the same way here, or does it stop being a harmless predictive stance and turn into a substantive claim about the model's internal states? More precisely: can we separate the stance's predictive usefulness from the question of whether the model actually has beliefs, and if we can't, what exactly breaks in Dennett's original argument once the system starts talking as if it believes things?
