I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm trying to write a piece on the current consciousness science slugfest—integrated information theory, global workspace, predictive processing, illusionism, panpsychism, the lot. What keeps tripping me up is that every camp has it's own private definition of what would count as "explaining consciousness," so I can't seperate real progress from sales pitch. Before I write anything, I want the spec sheet: the list of desiderata an adequate theory of consciousness has to meet, where "adequate" means good enough for a reporter who has to sort out which of these views are doing actual work.

Here's the passage I keep coming back to, from David Chalmers, "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (Journal of Consciousness Studies, 1995):

"If these phenomena were all there was to consciousness, then consciousness would not be much of a problem. Although we do not yet have anything close to a complete explanation of these phenomena, we have a clear idea of how we might go about explaining them. This is why I call these problems the easy problems. Of course, "easy" is a relative term. Getting the details right will probably take a century or two of difficult empirical work. Still, there is every reason to believe that the methods of cognitive science and neuroscience will succeed."

I read that as Chalmers drawing a line: phenomena like reportability, access, integration, and wakefulness will eventually get a mechanistic story, and if that were all consciousness was, there'd be no deep problem left. So the question is whether that line is doing real work or whether it's just an assumption a theory is allowed to reject. I want you to do four things. First, take a stand: is the easy/hard line itself a non-negotiable desideratum for any adequate theory, or can a good theory collapse it—and if it can, what does it have to do to earn that? Second, build the full desiderata list for a theory of consciousness: the cases and phenomena it has to get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, the neuroscience it can't contradict, with the reason each one is a requirement. Mark each as non-negotiable or camp-specific, and flag any pair of requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied. Third, briefly score the current contenders—IIT, global workspace, predictive processing, illusionism, panpsychism—against the list. Fourth, tell me how confident you are in the list overall and single out the one requirement you're least sure of.
