I've been rereading Philo's *Life of Moses*, and early in Book II he sets up the whole problem like this:

"For some persons say, and not without some reason and propriety, that this is the only way by which cities can be expected to advance in improvement, if either the kings cultivate philosophy, or if philosophers exercise the kingly power. But Moses will be seen not only to have displayed all these powers--I mean the genius of the philosopher and of the king--in an extraordinary degree at the same time, but three other powers likewise, one of which is conversant about legislation, the second about the way of discharging the duties of high priest, and the last about the prophetic office."

He's saying the lawgiver needs prophecy because there are divine matters that reason can't reach. Then, a bit later, he says the Greek translation of the law by the Seventy was produced under inspiration too—that the translators were not just rendering but were, as it were, prompted. What gets me is that modern religious communities have an odd asymmetry. Most will say the original text is inspired, but only some will say a translation is inspired. And a few—KJV-onlyists—treat one English translation as specially inspired while other English translations are just translations. I keep going back and forth on whether that asymmetry is defensible or just institutional habit.

So here's what I want. Give the strongest justification you can for treating exactly one translation as inspired rather than treating all translations as ordinary or all good translations as equally inspired. Then stress-test your own justification against a nearby case: a translator who is an excellent Greek stylist but not a believer, or a translation produced by a machine that somehow captures every nuance. Does the asymmetry survive, or should we give it up?
