I'm a retired chemist, and I've spent the last couple years going down the philosophy of science rabbit hole. Right now it's the simultaneous discovery thing: Newton and Leibniz, Darwin and Wallace, and the question of wether a discovery was just going to happen and whether the name attached to it matters at all.

A friend of mine, retired geologist, has been collecting priority disputes forever and has very settled verdicts. Here's what he says about the three I've argued with him about:

- Newton versus Leibniz on calculus: split it. The fact that Newton had fluxions in his private papers earlier doesn't add to his share, since Leibniz got there on his own.
- Priestley versus Lavoisier on oxygen: Lavoisier gets it, not Priestley. Priestley actually made the stuff, but he didn't know what he'd made, and discovery is knowing what you've got.
- Darwin versus Wallace on natural selection: Darwin gets the main credit and Wallace a footnote. Wallace had the right mechanism, but Darwin had the whole theory, the evidence, and the book. The joint announcement was still the right move.

So here's the new case I want to predict before I call him. Two labs, working independently, come up with the same correct and fairly complete explanation for the same phenomenon at basically the same time — say, how a particular molecular machine resets itself, but the details dont matter. Lab A posts a preprint first, complete but plain, and nobody pays attention. Lab B publishes three months later in a major journal with a cleaner writeup, and the field starts citing Lab B as the discoverers.

Which way does my friend go: shared credit, or does Lab B get the discovery? Which of his three earlier verdicts is the closest analog, and what principle is he actually running? If his old verdicts underdetermine it, tell me what principle each answer would commit him to, and what extra case would settle which one he's really using.

And since I'm still translating my old bench-scientist habits into philosophy: answer in American English.
