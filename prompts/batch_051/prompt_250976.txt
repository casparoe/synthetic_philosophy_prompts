I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've been reading about a dispute in mathematics that looks, from the outside, as though it should have been settled decades ago but somehow hasn't been. In the 1970s, Appel and Haken proved the four-colour theorem by reducing it to nearly two thousand cases and checking those cases on a computer. Many mathematicians objected that this was not really a proof, because no human could survey it; the reply was that the computer had produced exactly what a proof is supposed to produce, namely certainty that the theorem is true. Thomas Hales's proof of the Kepler conjecture drew the same objection, and even the Flyspeck project, which later produced a formal verification of that proof down to machine-checked axioms, did not end the argument so much as relocate it: now the dispute is over whether a machine-checkable certificate that no one can read is a proof, or something else mathematics has learned to accept. The two positions have been well rehearsed for roughly fifty years, and I don't see anyone moving.

I find this strange because it looks like my kind of problem. I spend my working life deciding whether software can be trusted, what a test suite actually establishes, and when a machine-checked proof is stronger than a code review. Nothing about a verified exhaustive case analysis makes me want to say the four-colour theorem is unproved. Yet I can also feel the pull of the other side: a certificate that no one can read doesn't tell me why the theorem is true, and it changes what mathematical activity is for.

I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not a verdict on who is right. Is the disagreement substantive — about how reliable computation is compared with human checking? Is it verbal — about the word 'proof'? Is it a disagreement about standards of evidence, or about which cases count as data in mathematical practice? Or are the two sides answering different questions under one heading — one about certainty, one about understanding, one about how a community accepts and builds on results? I have a hypothesis, and I want you to test it rather than assume it: the word 'proof' is doing two jobs that normally travel together but come apart exactly in the unsurveyable cases — a verification job, in which a proof is a checkable guarantee of correctness, and an explanation job, in which a proof lets a competent reader see why the result is true. If that is close to right, the debate stalls because each side is defending one of those jobs while using the same word, and it will only move once the jobs are separated. Ground the diagnosis in what the two sides actually say rather than a caricature, tell me where that hypothesis holds up and where it breaks, and say what would count as progress under the diagnosis you end up defending — a distinction, a formal result, a change of mathematical practice, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected.
