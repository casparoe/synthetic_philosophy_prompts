I am currently taking a philosophy of science course, and we have recently covered Hempel's raven paradox. I would like to work through a variant of the paradox in which the background facts and the sampling procedure are fixed exactly, because I suspect the standard moves look different once selection is made explicit. Please give a careful, numerically explicit analysis.

The variant is this. An ornithologist knows with certainty that a remote island contains exactly 100 birds; exactly 20 of them are ravens; and exactly 40 of them are black. She is testing the hypothesis H that all ravens on the island are black. Let x be the number of non-black ravens, so H is the claim that x = 0, while the alternatives are x = 1, ..., 20. For any fixed x, the birds on the island fall into four groups:
- black ravens: 20 − x
- non-black ravens: x
- black non-ravens: 20 + x
- non-black non-ravens: 60 − x.

Now compare three pieces of evidence.
(1) The ornithologist draws a uniform random sample of 10 birds, without replacement, from the 20 ravens, and all 10 are black.
(2) She draws a uniform random sample of 10 birds, without replacement, from the 60 non-black birds, and none of the 10 is a raven.
(3) A field assistant deliberately searches until he finds a bird that is non-black and not a raven, ignoring any non-black ravens he may pass, and brings it back; the ornithologist knows that this was the selection rule.

For (1) and (2), compute the probability of the observed result conditional on x = 0, x = 1, and x = 2, and then calculate the likelihood ratios P(observation | x = 0) / P(observation | x = k) for k = 1 and k = 2. Binomial-coefficient expressions are acceptable, but please also give approximate decimal values. State whether each observation confirms H on a Bayesian analysis, and compare how strongly the two observations confirm H. For (3), determine whether the deliberately presented non-black non-raven confirms H at all, and explain the contrast with (2).

Finally, discuss what this variant does to the original paradox. Does Nicod's condition still force the conclusion that any observed non-black non-raven confirms 'all ravens are black', no matter how the instance was selected? Does the logical equivalence of 'all ravens are black' and 'all non-black things are non-ravens' force procedures (1) and (2) to be evidentially equivalent? In your assessment, does the paradox survive this variation, or is it dissolved once the sampling protocol and background counts are fixed? Defend your assessment.
