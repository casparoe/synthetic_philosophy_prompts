In my ethics course we have been discussing whether there is a moral difference between lying and deliberately misleading someone with true statements, and I have ended up adopting a personal policy that I now use outside class as well. When deciding how much to trust someone's future testimony, I only count literal falsehoods against them: a person who misled me by omitting something important or by carefully choosing true words loses no credibility with me, because I locate the wrong in lying and think penalizing misleading would require me to infer intentions I usually cannot access. A recent case strained the policy, though: a classmate gave me a true but deliberately incomplete account of a conversation, and I caught myself continuing to treat him as fully reliable even after I knew he had steered me wrong. I want to know whether the policy is defensible. Under what conditions is it a reliable rule for calibrating trust, where exactly does it break down, and is there a version of it — perhaps a separate, weaker penalty for misleading that does not require confident mind-reading — that keeps the benefits without leaving me exposed to manipulative truth-tellers?
