You are a professional philosopher who works on the theory of knowledge across traditions, including Indian Buddhist epistemology. I am preparing a seminar presentation in which I defend a position reconstructed from Dharmakīrti's account of perception, inference, and momentariness, and I would like a rigorous consistency audit of the view I am inclined to defend. I am not asking for citations, bibliography, or textual excavation; I want you to treat the numbered claims below as my own stated commitments and examine how they fit together.

The view:

1. There are exactly two instruments of knowledge: perception and inference.
2. Perception is wholly nonconceptual. It is an unmediated causal registration of a unique particular and has no propositional content.
3. Inference yields knowledge only when its reason satisfies three conditions: the reason is present in the subject of the inference; it is present only in cases similar to the property to be proved; and it is absent in cases where that property is absent.
4. Memory and testimony are not independent instruments of knowledge. When they yield knowledge, they yield it by reducing to inference, or occasionally to perception.
5. The ultimately real is what has causal efficacy. Whatever lacks causal efficacy is only a conceptual construction.
6. Everything real is momentary, because a permanent thing could not produce an effect either all at once or in stages. Any particular therefore exists for no more than a moment.
7. Concepts and words take universals as their objects. Universals are not ultimately real, but they are indispensable for thought, communication, and ordinary action.
8. It is rational and practically justified to act on a sound inference, such as expecting fire on a distant mountain from the sight of smoke, even without having perceived the fire.

Please do the following. First, distinguish the commitments that are genuinely in tension from those that only appear to be in tension on a first reading. Second, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say which commitments conflict, what would have to give, and what the cheapest repair would be. Third, identify the single revision that would do the most to restore coherence to the view, and be explicit about what that revision would cost elsewhere in the view. Think carefully, and where an apparent tension dissolves under a clear distinction, spell out that distinction rather than conceding the conflict.
