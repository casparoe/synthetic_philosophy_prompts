I've been going around in circles about the special composition question — the question of when a bunch of things add up to a single thing — and I've started writing down my gut verdicts on concrete cases. I'm hoping you can tell me what principle, if any, I'm implicitly using, or what principle I should use, even if it means overturning some of my calls.

Here are the cases. The grains of sand in a sandbox: no object, it's just some sand. A sandcastle built from those very grains: an object. The LEGO bricks in a box: no object; the finished house built from them: an object. A hammer: an object. Its head and handle lying next to each other: no object; fitted together they compose the hammer. A mountain: I want to say it's a single thing even though it's really just a big pile of rock and dirt — I can't say what it has that the sand heap lacks. A cloud: I flip-flop, sometimes it seems like a thing and sometimes just a bunch of water droplets. A living cat is obviously an object, but I also think the dead cat is still an object, a body, even though its no longer alive. And my cells obviously compose me.

My rough rule was something like: some things compose an object when they're arranged in a way that gives them a unified structure or function — that's why the sandcastle, the house, and the hammer count, while the heap and the loose bricks don't. But the mountain seems to break that rule, since it's just a heap with no function, and the corpse breaks it too, since it no longer has the cat's function. The cloud breaks it in another way: it has a shape but no function and no sharp boundaries. So: is there a real account of composition that delivers my verdicts, or close to them? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And what does the principle you end up with say about cases I haven't settled — a swarm of bees, a wave, a fossil, and a pile of sand that's halfway to being a sandcastle? If the whole way I'm setting this up is confused, or if my verdicts are just inconsistent in a way no interesting principle can fix, I'd rather you say so plainly than hand me a principle that's been gerrymandered to fit. And please be honest about how much the cases actually pin the principle down — I'd rather hear 'your cases don't settle between these two views' than get a false sense of precision.
