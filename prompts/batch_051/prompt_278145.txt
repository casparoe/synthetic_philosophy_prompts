I am preparing a graduate seminar on a question that keeps splitting our department: whether the elegance of a scientific or mathematical theory is ever, by itself, evidence that the theory is true. A string theorist in the group argues that the pre-data elegance of general relativity, the Dirac equation, or gauge theory carried genuine evidential weight, while a statistician counters that what we call elegance is largely an aesthetic selection effect that biases which theories get developed and tested. I would like to turn this into a structured exercise in comparative judgment rather than another exchange of the usual platitude that elegance is a helpful heuristic but not decisive.

Below are six candidate accounts of how elegance might bear on truth. Sort them into tiers by how much evidential force you think each actually has, and give each a score from 0 to 10, where 0 means the fact that a theory is elegant does nothing to raise the probability that it is true and 10 means it would be decisive. By elegance I mean the perceived combination of simplicity, symmetry, economy of principles, and the sense that the pieces could not have been arranged otherwise; I am asking only about evidential weight, not about elegance's value in guiding research, its aesthetic worth, or its pedagogical usefulness.

(a) Elegance as a proxy for parsimony: elegant theories tend to have fewer free parameters, entities, and ad hoc additions, and a theory that needs less to fit the same data is better confirmed on ordinary Bayesian grounds.

(b) Elegance as a mark of hidden constraints: tightly coherent mathematical structure is often a symptom that symmetries or consistency conditions are silently excluding many nearby alternatives, concentrating probability on the structure that survives.

(c) Elegance as compression: an elegant theory represents many apparently heterogeneous phenomena in a few principles, and the fact that the data can be compressed at all is evidence of some common underlying order.

(d) Elegance as a track record: because elegant theories such as the Dirac equation have repeatedly turned out to be correct, there is an inductive reason to trust future elegant theories.

(e) Elegance as a selection artifact: scientists preferentially develop, publish, and pursue elegant theories, so the historical record of elegant successes partly reflects which theories got a chance to be tested rather than which were antecedently more likely to be true.

(f) Elegance as cognitive projection: what humans find elegant is shaped by our perceptual systems and mathematical training, so the feeling of elegance tracks features of our own architecture more than features of the world.

Please rank the six from most to least evidential force, assign them to tiers (for example, strong, moderate, weak, none or negative), and give each a numerical score. Note any ties. Then tell me whether your ranking would change for an immature field that has little data and no strong constraints yet, and why. I am particularly interested in which of these accounts are doing genuine evidential work and which only explain why elegance feels like evidence.
