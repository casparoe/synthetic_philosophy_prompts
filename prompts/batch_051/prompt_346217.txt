I'm a retired geologist, so be patient if I mangle the philosophy jargon. I recently read Searle's 'Minds, Brains, and Programs' and then went back to Dennett's two-bitser argument, which he uses in a few places including 'Evolution, Error, and Intentionality.' The move Searle makes is something like this: a system can have all the right formal machinery—rules for manipulating Symbols that look like Chinese characters—and still not understand a word. He says the human in the room has exactly what a digital computer has, and the human doesn't understand Chinese, so neither does the computer. Dennett's two-bitser is different: he points to a vending machine that can't physically distinguish a US quarter from a Panamanian quarter-balboa, yet we say it's a quarter-detector. The machine's intentional state—if you want to call it that—is real enough, but it's derived from our purposes, not from the machine's inner discrimination.

Now here's what's bugging me. Searle's argument was set up for the old-style AI: a programmer writes explicit rules for manipulating discrete symbols. Today's LLMs aren't built that way. They're trained on enormous corpora of human text to predict the next token; the 'rules' are distributed weights, the 'symbols' are continuous vectors in a high-dimensional space, and noone writes down what the system should do with a Chinese character. Does Searle's argument survive this changed assumption? Which premise is actually under pressure—the formal-symbol-manipulation premise, the syntax/semantics premise, or something else? And does the two-bitser become stronger in this setting, because the LLM's fluency seems to force us to treat its intentionality as derived from human text in much the way the vending machine's is derived from human design? Or is that just a category mistake? I want to know if the original/derived intentionality distinction still does philosophical work here, or whether it needs repair—maybe a weaker version that still captures what Searle was worried about even if his original argument doesn't survive the change in how the systems are built.
