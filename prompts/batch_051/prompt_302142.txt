In a seminar on the moral psychology of prosperity, we read the chapter of Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments where he discusses the poor man's son whom heaven, in his phrase, has visited with ambition. Smith argues, in effect, that the rich man's possessions are an immense machine that yields only slight bodily conveniences; the ambitious man gives up a real tranquillity he could already have had for an imagined elegant repose; and when he has finally obtained it, he discovers that riches and greatness are mere decorations of little practical use. But then Smith immediately adds that this is nonetheless a useful deception: nature has imposed it on us because it rouses and keeps in continual motion the industry of mankind, and without it the earth would remain uncultivated and population would not increase.

A classmate built the following argument out of this passage, and I cannot decide which step to reject. (1) If the apparent value of abundance beyond what it buys is merely a useful illusion, then no one has a genuine reason to pursue more than enough wealth to secure the goods wealth purchases. (2) Smith regards the ambition of the poor man's son as part of a providential order that benefits everyone. (3) If we approve of a desire only because it produces good side-effects, we are not granting that the object of that desire has genuine worth for the person who has it. (4) So Smith must say abundance itself has no genuine worth beyond the conveniences it buys. (5) But then, by the same reasoning, we could not genuinely admire productive industry; we could only recommend it as a socially useful delusion. (6) That seems to prove too much, because we do want to say that successful productive ambition, and not merely its effects, can be an estimable trait. (7) Therefore the idea that wealth's value is an illusion must be false. I am tempted to reject step 3, but I worry that doing so collapses the distinction between a trait's being useful and its being admirable. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does, and what is the least costly way out?

Please answer as a moral philosopher who works on Smith and on the ethics of prosperity. Start by brainstorming the main candidate diagnoses, then evaluate them. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, and then give your reasoning with no preamble and no restating of the question.
