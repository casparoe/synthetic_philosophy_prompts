I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am working on a long reported essay about whether history is "locked in": whether the broad features of the present — industrialized economies, the modern state, organized science, large-scale markets — were robustly likely to emerge, or whether they rest on accidents that could easily have gone otherwise. Over several months I have been interviewing two camps of researchers who have been arguing past each other for years. The contingency camp holds that path dependence is pervasive: small early differences are amplified by self-reinforcing mechanisms such as increasing returns, network effects, and institutional inertia, so that many outcomes are frozen accidents and alternatives were genuinely live. The robustness camp holds that many of these outcomes are overdetermined by deeper pressures — geography, demography, ecology, convergent functional needs — and that when one path is blocked, functionally similar substitutes usually arise; on their view, rerunning history from an identical starting point would differ in detail but converge on broadly similar large features. One recurring obstacle in my reporting is that the two sides often use "path dependence" to mean different things: one means acute sensitivity to initial conditions, while the other means a durable causal chain that, once started, is hard to reverse, which need not imply that the outcome was improbable.

I want you to draft the joint statement that such a collaboration should have produced: first, a numbered list of claims both sides can sign about what path dependence is, what needs explaining, and which mechanisms (increasing returns, lock-in, convergence, selection pressures) are real; then a table of the disagreements that survive, each stated neutrally and then restated in wording its own side would accept; and for each disagreement, the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side — together with a concrete case or line of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Please make at least one crux the familiar keyboard layout whose arrangement is often claimed to be an inferior standard that got locked in, and make at least one other crux a case from outside economics, such as the independent emergence of agriculture in several regions or the convergent evolution of similar forms in separated lineages. The table should make clear where the residual disagreement is substantive and where, if anywhere, it has been merely terminological. Format the document as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table with the columns: neutral statement of the disagreement, each side's preferred formulation, the crux, the test case, and each side's prediction.
