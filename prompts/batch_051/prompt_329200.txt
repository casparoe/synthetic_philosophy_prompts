I hold the following views, and I would like an audit of whether they form a coherent package and, if not, which of them is doing the damage.

(1) The strong principle of identity of indiscernibles: if two objects share all purely qualitative properties, including relational ones, then they are identical.
(2) Black's two-sphere world is possible: a world containing exactly two iron spheres that are perfect qualitative duplicates of each other, two miles apart, and nothing else.
(3) In that world, the spheres are numerically distinct; it is not a case of one sphere appearing twice, nor of a single scattered object whose parts are qualitatively alike.
(4) In applying (1), impure properties are inadmissible. Properties such as being identical to a, or being the sphere two miles from a, cannot be invoked to make the spheres come out discernible; that would trivialize the principle.
(5) Nonetheless, the spheres are weakly discernible: the relation being two miles from is symmetric, irreflexive, and holds between them. I have been treating this as what reconciles (1) with (2) and (3).
(6) I reject primitive thisness: there are no haecceities, and no two possible worlds differ merely by a permutation of qualitatively identical individuals.

I am worried that (6) conflicts with (3), though that may be an illusion, and I am more worried that (5) cannot do its intended work given (4). Are these six commitments jointly stable? If not, which must be revised, and which apparent conflicts dissolve on closer inspection?
