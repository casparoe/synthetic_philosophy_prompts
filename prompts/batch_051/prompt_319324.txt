I'm a PhD student working on the epistemology of machine learning, and I've been trying to sharpen a view I'll call the legibility ideal. The basic thought is that, for high-stakes domains, it is not enough that a system's outputs be correct or well calibrated; the system should also make the operative grounds of its outputs available in a form that a competent human could in principle follow, reconstruct, and contest. By operative grounds I mean the actual route by which the system's considerations issued in the verdict, not a post hoc rationale that merely coheres with it after the fact. I am still trying to work out whether the ideal is right, where it bites, and what it actually demands in practice.

I keep reaching for the second game of the 2016 Go match between AlphaGo and Lee Sedol as a test case, and in particular what commentators came to call move 37. The salient facts, as I understand them, are these. Early in the middle game, AlphaGo played an unusual shoulder hit on the fifth line, a move that, as I understand it, its own policy network reportedly assigned only about a one-in-ten-thousand probability of being played by a human. Expert commentators at the time read it as a probable mistake; Lee Sedol left the board for some minutes, apparently unsettled; and as the game developed the move proved pivotal, so that it is now widely cited as a public case of machine creativity in a domain long thought to require deep human judgment. What interests me is that the move's initial rejection and its later vindication both unfolded without any human being able, at the time, to reverse-engineer the search that produced it.

I would like you to treat this episode as a stress test for the legibility ideal, and to run the analysis in both directions. First, apply the ideal to the episode: identify the relevant parties, say what would have counted as understanding move 37 at that moment, and specify what each party should have wanted or done by the lights of the ideal, including whether it licensed or condemned deference to a black-box move whose justification no one could yet state. Second, turn the episode back on the ideal: does the case show a genuine and unavoidable tension between legibility and correctness, and if so, does that refute the ideal, restrict its scope, or suggest that the ideal was misstated from the start? In both directions, be disciplined about the mapping; in particular, distinguish understanding the move's justification from merely predicting the move or producing moves like it, and say plainly where the ideal is silent about the case rather than forcing it to speak.
