I keep coming back to a move Alan Turing makes in his 1950 Mind paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence, and I can't tell whether it has a name. He spends the paper proposing that the question whether machines can think be replaced by the imitation game, which is designed so the interrogator can't see or hear the contestants and has to judge purely on written answers. Then near the end, in the section on extra-sensory perception, he doesn't dismiss the idea. He says something like the statistical evidence for telepathy at least is overwhelming, and that these phenomena are disturbing because they seem to cut against all our usual scientific assumptions. But he doesn't treat this as a reason to reconsider the imitation game as a criterion. His fix is practical: if telepathy is real, you just put the contestants in a room shielded from it and run the game as before.

The pattern I'm interested in is this. A person sets up a test or criterion. Some phenomenon shows up that would, on the face of it, interfere with the test or threaten its validity. Instead of revising the test, or saying what the phenomenon implies for the original question, the person adds a barrier so the phenomenon can't reach the test, and then treats the matter as handled. The phenomenon isn't denied—in Turing's case it's actually taken seriously—but it also isn't integrated into the theory. It's quarantined. The test itself stays exactly the same, just with a new wall around it.

I know this is somewhere near ad hoc auxiliary hypotheses, Popper's immunizing stratagems, and Lakatos's protective belt, but none of those seem to fit exactly. Ad hoc hypotheses usually change the theory or add an assumption to explain away a failed prediction. Turing doesn't change the imitation game or the criterion of thinking one bit; he adds a physical constraint to the environment. And he isn't responding to a failed prediction either—he brings up ESP himself and then fences it off.

So my question: is there an established name for this exact move—acknowledging an anomaly, granting that it is serious, and then building a quarantine around the test rather than revising the test? If there isn't, what would you call it, and how would you distinguish it from a plain ad hoc auxiliary hypothesis and from a Lakatosian protective belt? I'm less interested in the label than in getting the edges of the concept right, because I see the same shape in a lot of arguments, not just Turing's.
