Over the past few years I have been piecing together a view I call blame capital, and I would like an honest assessment of it rather than a sympathetic reconstruction. The core claim is that blaming someone is less like issuing a verdict than like drawing on an account. To blame a person for violating a norm, you need a positive balance in that same norm's account, where the balance is your own history of compliance with the norm minus your history of violating it, weighted by how recent the lapses are and by how costly it was for you to comply. The accusation itself can still be true when the speaker's balance is zero or negative; what it lacks is the authority to function as blame rather than mere description. This would explain why a habitual tax evader's denunciation of a colleague's expense fraud leaves us cold even when the facts are exactly right, and why the same words from someone who has visibly paid a price to stay honest land differently.

The view makes several predictions I find clarifying. It makes entitlement norm-specific: someone might be entitled to blame others for dishonesty but not for disloyalty, because their record differs by norm. It makes entitlement restorable: a person who lied once twenty years ago and has since been scrupulous has, on this accounting, rebuilt the account, whereas someone who lied last week probably should not yet speak. And it allows standing to be underwritten collectively, as when a member of a group that has borne a wrong lends force to an outsider's otherwise weak criticism. A friend has pressed me on the point that a lifelong conformist who never faced serious temptation would accumulate outsized capital, and I am unsure whether ease of compliance should matter in the accounting. What I want to know is whether there is a real phenomenon here or only an extended metaphor. Has something like this already been worked out under another name? And where does the view break: give me the strongest case in which a person with no balance should clearly still be entitled to blame, or a person with a large balance clearly should not.
