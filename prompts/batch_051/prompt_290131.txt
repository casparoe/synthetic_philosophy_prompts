In a seminar on dynastic wealth and equality of opportunity, a classmate brushed aside the case for heavier estate taxes with the old proverb "from shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves in three generations." His gloss was that family fortunes mostly dissipate on their own within a few generations, so taxing large estates at death attacks a problem that largely solves itself—and that singling out the rare dynasty that does survive is driven more by envy than by a concern for equal opportunity. A week later I was reading Andrew Carnegie's essay commonly reprinted as "The Gospel of Wealth" (originally published as "Wealth" in the North American Review, 1889) and came across this passage, which seems to me to pull hard in the opposite direction:

"The growing disposition to tax more and more heavily large estates left at death is a cheering indication of the growth of a salutary change in public opinion. ... Of all forms of taxation, this seems the wisest. Men who continue hoarding great sums all their lives, the proper use of which for public ends would work good to the community, should be made to feel that the community, in the form of the state, cannot thus be deprived of its proper share. By taxing estates heavily at death, the state marks its condemnation of the selfish millionaire's unworthy life."

I now suspect the proverb is being asked to do much more work than it can bear, but I cannot yet say exactly what it would have to mean in order to work as a reply to Carnegie or to a modern equality-of-opportunity case for inheritance taxation. Lay out for me the distinct precise claims "from shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves in three generations" could be used to express—about reversion of family wealth to the mean, the effects of unearned wealth on heirs, the survival rates of family firms, the historical frequency of multi-generational fortunes, or any normative claim that natural dissipation makes taxation unnecessary. Present them as a menu, and say for each which are trivial, which are substantive and plausibly true, which are empirically doubtful or false, and which are value judgments rather than empirical claims. Then state which of those readings would actually license dismissing inheritance taxes, which would instead support Carnegie's position, and which readings his passage does not touch but a modern defender of the proverb would still need to establish.
