I've been retired from chemistry for a few years now and got pulled into philosophy through the back door, mostly by way of behavioral economics. A friend of mine whose an economist keeps insisting that the endowment effect is just a mistake: people overvalue what they happen to own because they're loss averse, and that's a bug in the machinery. My own intuition is messier. When I sold off my old lab after retiring, I put a price on a spectrophotometer I'd modified over the years that was maybe three times what I'd have paid for an identical used machine, and honestly that felt sane to me. I knew its calibration quirks, I'd built some of the modifications myself, and it carried a bit of my working life with it. But I also admit that with something like a mug I might show the same gap for no good reason at all. So I'm somewhere in the middle.

The claim I want your number on is this: considered across typical cases, the endowment effect (roughly, the gap between willingness-to-accept and willingness-to-pay for the same object) is more often a legitimate preference — the thing really is worth more to you once it's yours — than it is a cognitive error or artifact of the experimental setup. I'm at about 0.55; my economist friend is at about 0.15. Give me your own probability, but decompose it into the relevant sub-questions and give a rough probability for each: what a defensible baseline for "true" preference even would be, how much of the WTA/WTP gap is experimental artifact (transaction costs, demand effects, unfamiliarity with the task), whether ownership can legitimately change an object's value (through information, modifications, identity, attachment), and whether the loss/gain asymmetry itself is rationally defensible given uncertainty about the object's value. Then say how those weights combine, tell me which sub-question my friend and I are most likely actually disagreeing about, and what sort of evidence — I dont mean citations, but an experiment or argument — would move your number the most.
