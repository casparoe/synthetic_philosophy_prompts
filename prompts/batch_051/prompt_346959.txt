Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether games can be art. She kept insisting that whatever is artistic in a game arrives through the parts games borrow from older forms — image, story, music — and that the interactive remainder is just the machinery of a toy. I found myself defending the opposite view: if games have a distinctive aesthetic value, it must reside in the structure of the choices they offer. By the end of the argument I had assembled a small theory, and I want it checked.

I have been calling the concept agency texture. A game possesses distinctively ludic aesthetic value to the extent that its decision points exhibit two properties together. The first, branch salience, is the degree to which, at a choice point, several future paths present themselves to the player as live and genuinely distinct options whose differences matter, rather than as one obvious move accompanied by window dressing. The second, trace legibility, is the degree to which the player can afterwards see enough of the causal structure to understand what would have happened under the unchosen branches — not necessarily a full counterfactual replay, but enough that the choice was experienced as a choice between real futures rather than between opaque buttons. Chess on a bare board has high agency texture; a lavishly produced game with a fixed narrative can have high cinematic texture while remaining thin in agency texture. Puzzle games can be aesthetically strong without any story because their agency texture is dense. Heavy randomness can either sharpen or flatten the texture, depending on whether the player can hold the range of possible outcomes in view.

The theory is meant to single out what, if anything, is artistic about games specifically, rather than about images, prose, or music that games happen to contain. The artistry would consist in designing a possibility space that the player encounters as a field of available, intelligible alternatives.

I am aware this may be a dressed-up version of the old design maxim about interesting choices, and that there may already be a literature around agency as the aesthetic medium of games. I also fear it collapses the difference between aesthetic experience and planning. I want an honest assessment. Reconstruct the concept in its strongest form, say whether it already exists under another name, and then give me the three most serious objections. Which of them, if any, are fatal rather than merely uncomfortable? And what is the clearest example of something my theory either wrongly ennobles as good game art or wrongly demotes?
