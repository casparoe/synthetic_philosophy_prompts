Since I retired from physics I've been reading philosophy of time, and I keep tripping over the same equation in two places that couldn't be farther appart. Gödel found a rotating solution to Einstein's field equations — the whole universe turns relative to the local inertial frames — and in it the light cones tip so far that there are closed timelike curves through every point: a future-directed path can loop back and meet its own past. Gödel took this to show that time can't be objective, because you can't carve such a spacetime into a single global "now." Fine; that part I followed. What has been buzzing in my head is the way physicists deal with the loops, which is not to forbid them but to impose self-consistency: whatever travels around the loop has to arrive back at the starting surface in the same state it left, so the allowed states solve ψ = Uψ, where U is the evolution operator around the loop. That's a fixed-point equation — the same shape as the self-consistent field and renormalization-group equations I used for thirty years. But it also looks to me to be exactly the shape of Gödel's diagonal lemma: for any arithmetic predicate F(x), there's a sentence G such that G ↔ F(⟨G⟩), meaning G says F holds of G's own code. A self-consistent history through a time loop and a self-referential sentence are both fixed points of a map; in both cases the interesting question is whether a solution exists and whether it's unique.

So what I want to know is whether this is a real identity of structure or just me pattern-matching on the word "loop." If it's real, what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer? For instance, diagonal fixed points aren't unique, and sometimes none of them decide the sentence the way you wanted; would that predict that histories through a closed timelike curve are underdetermined, or does something on the physics side — unitarity, contraction of U, energy conditions, whatever — force a unique fixed point? And conversely, is there anything from the physics side, say the fact that you can approximate a self-consistent field by iteration, that transfers back to logic and tells you something about how to construct diagonal sentences? I'm trying to figure out where the analogy is load-bearing and where it actually breaks.
