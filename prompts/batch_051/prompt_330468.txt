Two friends of mine, Mara and Devin, spent most of a dinner arguing about whether a person who wants to minimize the suffering their diet causes should eat beef rather than chicken. Neither is a vegetarian; both agree that animal products can be justified if the expected harm per calorie is small enough. I listened and honestly could not settle it, so I am writing the exchange down from my notes and would like a referee's report.

Mara opened with the body-count argument: beef beats chicken on a per-calorie basis, since one steer produces on the order of hundreds of thousands of edible calories, while a broiler produces only a few thousand, so replacing beef with chicken means hundreds of chicken lives for the same amount of food; moreover, cattle can spend most of their lives on pasture, while essentially all broilers live in crowded sheds. Devin replied that the pasture point is doing too much work: most beef is grain-finished, not grass-finished, cattle convert feed to meat much less efficiently than chickens (roughly six kilos of feed per kilo of gain, against under two for a broiler), and once you count the field animals killed growing feed, grain-finished beef probably causes more crop deaths per calorie than chicken. Mara conceded the point about feedlots but said the relevant comparison is the one a shopper actually faces: grass-finished beef exists, costs more but not absurdly more, requires no row-crop feed, and avoids the annual harvest that kills field animals, so the choice between grass-finished beef and factory chicken favors beef. Devin then challenged her on two fronts: a cow is more cognitively complex than a chicken, so one cow death is not obviously better than many chicken deaths; and even grass-finished beef uses far more land per calorie, meaning it displaces more wild animals than chicken feed crops, which is good only if wild lives are on balance not worth living. Mara answered that if you take wild-animal welfare seriously the land argument is genuinely ambiguous: a hectare of pasture supports less animal life than native habitat, and feed fields are hostile to small animals for much of the year, so on a net-negative view of wild lives replacing rich habitat with simple pasture could reduce total suffering and give beef an unexpected advantage, while on a net-positive view chicken's higher yield per hectare is better because it spares habitat—either way, the original question is now about wild-animal welfare, not cows versus chickens. Devin closed by saying that once you have let wild animals and land use in, you have to include climate: beef's methane and land-use costs are a diffuse, expected-value term that affects enormous numbers of animals, and could dominate the direct death accounting, so the claim that beef causes less suffering than chicken is underdetermined until someone fixes the accounting boundary, the welfare scale, and the model of wild lives.

Please adjudicate the exchange. For each move, say what is right about it and where it goes wrong, and rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what both of them are still missing.
