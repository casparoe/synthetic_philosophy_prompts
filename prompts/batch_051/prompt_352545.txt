I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a piece on rational herding. The standard account comes from Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch's 1992 Journal of Political Economy article, A Theory of Fads, Fashion, Custom, and Cultural Change as Informational Cascades. The paper opens by defining a cascade as a situation in which someone who has observed earlier choices rationally decides to imitate the person ahead and disregard his own private evidence. The authors argue that such cascades can form even among fully rational individuals, because early actions carry information but do not reveal it perfectly, so later decision-makers can rationally judge that the accumulated public behavior outweighs their own signal.

I want to know whether that argument survives a development the 1992 model did not anticipate. In many modern settings—online reviews, medical crowdfunding, prediction markets, social media health advice—the public record includes not only the sequence of choices but also the reasons people give, how confident they say they are, visible disagreement, and the previous accuracy of the participants. When actors can observe not just what predecessors did but also why they say they did it, does the original cascade argument still go through? Which premise is actually under pressure? If the argument needs repair, what is the strongest repaired version, and what residual claim should be abandoned or renamed?
