I teach an undergraduate seminar on Schopenhauer, and I keep finding that students identify as 'pessimists' without distinguishing the diagnosis of suffering from the metaphysics of the will, or either of these from the proposed remedy. I want a handout that forces them to locate themselves by their answers to concrete cases, not by which label sounds best.

Please write a decision tree for the question: if suffering were inseparable from willing, what, if anything, would be the right response? The tree should lead a student from an initial answer about the character of suffering and striving to one of seven leaves, each leaf carrying the name of a position (or the word 'unnamed' where nobody defends it) and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. The top of the tree must separate two claims students commonly run together: the empirical claim that desire arises from felt lack and therefore tends towards renewed dissatisfaction, and the metaphysical claim that the inner nature of the world is a blind, non-rational will. The tree should therefore find room for someone who accepts the first claim but rejects the second, and for someone who accepts the second but rejects the first, and it should say what each combination amounts to.

The leaves must include at least the following. First, Schopenhauer's own position: the diagnosis, the metaphysics, and the conclusion that denial of the will — not suicide and not mere aesthetic respite — is the only final release. Second, a naturalistic pessimism that accepts the diagnosis and the futility of striving but treats these as contingent features of human psychology rather than as a metaphysical will, and that stops short of the ascetic conclusion. Third, a Nietzschean-style tragic affirmation that accepts something like blind striving and suffering but answers with affirmation rather than denial. Fourth, an antinatalist leaf that locates the remedy in preventing new sufferers rather than in altering one's own willing. Fifth, a providential or theodic position that denies the initial premise by treating suffering as ultimately compensated.

Also mark as logically possible but, on your account, unoccupied or unstable any combination of answers the named leaves leave empty, and diagnose in one clause whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. In particular, the combination that accepts the metaphysical will and the diagnosis but still regards suicide as a genuine escape should be flagged, and your tree should separate it from Schopenhauer's view by the argument that suicide is itself an act of will rather than its denial.

Every branch question must be answerable by a student who has never heard of the label it leads to, and should use concrete cases where possible. The audience is second-year undergraduates who have read only a little secondary material on Schopenhauer. Use at least three of the following as nodes or sub-nodes: a case about music or scenery that temporarily quiets one's wanting, where the question is whether the experience reveals something about the world or only provides respite; a case about compassion, in which one person says that another's pain is in some sense not a separate reality from their own, where the question is whether this is insight, sentiment, or confusion; a case about someone in chronic pain who refuses suicide on the grounds that it would not touch what is really wrong, where the question is whether the refusal is coherent and under which metaphysics it would be; a claim about a vine or root pushing blindly into soil, where the question is whether the language of striving is metaphor or a description of the inner nature of things; and the observation that satisfaction, once attained, lasts only a brief interval before a new want appears, where the question is whether this shows anything about the structure of willing or only about how some people happen to feel.

Finally, flag any two leaves the tree cannot really separate with the available questions rather than pretending they come apart.
