I write about data and criminal justice for a general-audience magazine, and last month I sat in on a working session between two people in a county pretrial office — a data analyst, Maya, and a policy director, Dev — who got into it over their new recividism risk score. Both were sharp, and I kept flipping on who had the better argument, so I could use a referee.

The tool scores every defendent from 1 to 10, and anyone above a cutoff is flagged for a more restrictive pretrial hold. The vendor's fairness report claims three things about decisions made at that cutoff: calibration (among people flagged high risk, the same share actually reoffend within two years in each racial group, and same among people flagged low); equal false positive rates (among people who would not reoffend, the same share get flagged in each group); and equal false negative rates (among people who would reoffend, the same share slip through in each group). The county's two main racial groups reoffend at different base rates.

Here's roughly how the argument went.

Maya: "Calibration is the one that matters. A score has to mean the same thing for Black and white defendants, otherwise you're comparing apples to oranges."

Dev: "Calibration is just consistency. A tool can be perfectly calibrated and still flag Black defendants at twice the rate of white defendants, so calibration is compatible with exactly the harm we're worried about. Fairness has to constrain the errors. Equal false positive rates are the minimum — don't be more willing to wrongly lock up one group."

Maya: "Equal false positives alone still lets the false negatives swing wildly apart when base rates differ. You'd just be moving the error around. The only coherent package is equalized odds: equal false positive and equal false negative rates."

Dev: "Fine, equalized odds. But the vendor also claimed calibration, and there's a theorem that you can't have calibration, equal false positives, and equal false negatives all at once unless the groups reoffend at the same rate or the tool is essentially a perfect predictor. Our groups don't reoffend at the same rate, so the vendor is promising a mathematical impossibility. The real fight is over which property to drop."

Maya: "Drop calibration. Equalized odds is the fairness requirement; calibration is a statistical nicety. A score can be uncalibrated and still treat the two groups' errors equally, which is what fairness demands."

Dev: "I don't think calibration is a nicety. If the score isn't calibrated, then a 7 doesn't mean the same thing in each group, so you could satisfy equalized odds by using different risk thresholds for different groups — and that's a kind of double standard. Calibration stays."

Maya: "But equalized odds just is the pair of equal error rates. If you keep calibration, the theorem says you can't have that pair either. So calibration and equalized odds conflict too. What's left is choosing which error is worse, and that's a moral decision we've been pretending was a technical one."

Dev: "If the data can't support calibration and equalized odds at the same time, the honest move is to make the tool matter less in bail decisions, not to relabel whichever metric survives as 'fair.'"

I couldn't tell which claims were solid and which were just rhetorical. Go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what. Where does the impossibility result actually cut against each of them — does it vindicate one side, or does it show that the whole hunt for a single "correct" fairness metric was the mistake? And suppose the county has to set actual bail policy next week: what should it do with the theorem in hand?
