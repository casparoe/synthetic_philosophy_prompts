I've been going around in circles on whether not doing something can really be a cause. Like if I promise to water my neighbors plant while they're away and I forget, and it dies—did my forgetting cause the death, or did the dry soil cause it and my failure is just, I don't know, background? I've seen people argue about this online enough to know there are a few main camps, but every quiz I can find is about free will or the trolley problem, never about omissions specifically.

Could you make me a "what do you actually believe about omissions?" quiz for a small reading group I'm in? Eight to ten questions, each one a short concrete case where someone didn't do something and then something happened (bad, good, or neutral). The answer choices should track what people actually say about the case, not which label sounds nice. Then give me a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions.

The positions I want it to sort between are roughly these: (1) an omission is a genuine cause, full stop, just as much as an action is; (2) an omission can't be a cause, because a cause has to be an actual something rather than an absence, but citing the omission can still explain why the thing happened; (3) omissions don't really cause or explain anything, talk about them is mostly a social way of flagging that someone was expected to act and didnt; (4) whether an omission counts as a cause depends on what you're contrasting it with, so the same failure can be a cause under one comparison and not under another. Treat these as the target boxes, but dont use the labels in the questions.

Please work in at least these cases: a bodyguard who doesn't show up and the client gets shot; a surgeon who skips antibiotics and the patient dies of an infection; a stranger who could have shouted a warning about a falling piano but stays quiet; a parent who decides not to vaccinate their kid and the kid gets measles; and one where the bad outcome would have happened anyway no matter what the person did, so that people who only count an omission as a cause when it actually makes a difference can show their colors.

The questions should all be answerable by someone who doesn't know any of the jargon. At the end, tell me which positions your scoring key can't cleanly separate, and for any answer pattern that maps onto a spot nobody seems to defend, say whether you think that's because the view doesn't make sense or just because nobody bothered to take it up.
