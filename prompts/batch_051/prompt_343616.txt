I am putting together an essay on the distribution of consciousness across animals, and the discussion keeps oscillating between two positions I find unsatisfying: a cortex-centered view that would deny sentience to octopuses and birds despite strong behavioral evidence, and a permissive view that would award sentience to nearly anything that can learn. A colleague has proposed a way of cutting through this, and I am not sure how much weight it can bear. Her proposal is that the best single behavioral marker of minimal phenomenal consciousness is trace conditioning rather than delay conditioning. In delay conditioning the conditioned stimulus overlaps with the unconditioned stimulus, and in humans it can apparently be acquired without awareness of the contingency; in trace conditioning the conditioned stimulus ends before the unconditioned stimulus arrives, so the animal has to bridge a temporal gap, and the human evidence suggests this requires awareness that the first stimulus predicts the second. She argues that trace conditioning therefore demands a representation of something absent, which is about as close to a functional signature of a subjective point of view as behavior can get, and that any animal that passes a well-designed trace-conditioning task should be credited with at least minimal consciousness, while animals that only manage delay conditioning should not. On her line, mammals and birds would be included, many invertebrates known only for simpler learning would be excluded, and cephalopods would remain an open case to be settled by the task itself.

I would like you to tell me how one would actually test whether trace conditioning is a good marker for the distribution of consciousness, rather than just a marker for one kind of associative learning. Design the strongest concrete test or observation you can, whether comparative, developmental, or based on human manipulations, and state precisely what pattern of results would count in favor of the marker, what would count against it, and what a positive result would still leave unsolved about consciousness in animals. I want a committed response: choose a definite test and a definite interpretation of its likely outcomes, with reasons, rather than laying out options for me to pick among. Please write it as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter or an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
