I'm a physics undergrad and I've settled into a policy that I'm starting to feel uneasy about. Whenever interpretations of quantum mechanics come up—Copenhagen, Everett, Bohm, whatever—I refuse to assign any of them any confidence at all. My line is: the standard ones agree on the predictions for every experiment anyone knows how to run, so no possible evidence could favor one over another, and having a 'leaning' is really just an aesthetic preference for one kind of story. I'm not exactly a shut-up-and-calculate type, because I do think the measurement problem is a real problem. I just think the only rational stance is flat, permanent agnosticism—no credences, no betting odds, not even a gut lean.

Then at a department happy hour a friend cornered me with two things I couldn't answer. First, she said, if I'm so hard-nosed about unobservable structure, why do I go around talking about the wavefunction like its a real thing out there, when that's also an interpretive commitment? Second, she said people used to say atoms were unobservable and no evidence could settle it, until Brownian motion turned unobservable into measurable; how do I know interpretations won't pull the same trick later? That second point felt unfair to me because atomic theory eventually made distinctive predictions, while interpretations are built to match the same formalism—but im not sure that difference is as clean as I want it to be. Is my policy defensible? Under what conditions could an interpretation deserve more or less confidence, and how would I tell from the inside whether I'm being disciplined or just refusing to think? Please give the answer in American English.
