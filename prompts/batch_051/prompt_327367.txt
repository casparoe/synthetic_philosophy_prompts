I've been thinking a lot lately about a question I don't have good language for: when we build something — a tool, a system, a piece of software — do we build our values into it so that the thing itself keeps enforcing those values later, or is the thing just a neutral instrument that does whatever people choose to do with it?

Last week I heard this argued out in concrete terms, and I couldn't settle it. Two friends were talking about an apartment building that had replaced its physical keys with a phone app. Tenants open every door through the app, and the landlord can revoke a tenant's access remotely, at any hour, with the change taking effect almost instantly. Daniela said the lock itself was political. Marcus said only the landlord's decisions were political, and the lock was just a tool. Their exchange went roughly like this.

Daniela: The lock is designed so that someone without a smartphone and a registered account cannot be a tenant at all. No written rule excludes those people. The design excludes them before anyone makes a decision. That's what I mean by a tool having politics.

Marcus: That's just capability. A staircase also excludes people who can't climb stairs, but nobody says a staircase is political. The political question is whether the building provides a ramp or discriminates in its rules. The tool just is what it is.

Daniela: The staircase example proves my point. Stairs become a political matter precisely because the design fixes who can get in; the argument is literally about the stairs. A thing doesn't stop being political just because it's silent and mechanical.

Marcus: Then you're using the word so loosely that every object is political — hammers, doorknobs, eyeglasses. If everything is political, the word does no work. Politics has to involve decisions between people, interests, power, not the shape of metal.

Daniela: But the lock does involve power between people. With a physical key, the tenant holds part of the control: getting the key back, or changing the lock, is slow and visible. With the app, the landlord can switch off a tenant's access in a second, silently, perhaps by mistake, perhaps right in the middle of a dispute. The lock doesn't cause that, but it makes that power cheap. Power is partly about what a design makes easy.

Marcus: The landlord could already lock you out by changing the lock. The app only makes it more convenient. What makes the act wrong is the lease and the law, and those apply either way. If the landlord overreaches, the problem is the landlord, not the gadget.

Daniela: But the cheaper and less visible an act is, the more often it happens and the less it gets challenged. A design that makes certain uses of power effortless changes how tenants and landlords actually behave, even if no new policy was ever announced. After a while, the tool starts functioning as the policy.

Marcus: That's still people choosing to behave differently. The tool isn't making them. A decent landlord stays decent with an app, and a bad one was already bad with keys. If we blame the lock, we let the people off the hook.

Daniela: I'm not letting anyone off the hook. I'm saying the lock pushes in one direction: it makes it easier to use access as a weapon and harder to be the kind of landlord who has to sit with a decision before acting. That is exactly what I mean when I say a designed thing can have politics — it favors some ways of living together over others, whether or not the designers ever declared that.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Please go through their exchange move by move. For each step, say who is right about which specific point, which claims are wrong or only partly right, and what both of them are missing. Then say who is more right overall, and why.
