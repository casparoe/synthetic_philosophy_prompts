Consider the classic informational-cascade argument: when agents with private information make a binary choice one by one, and each observes the actions (but not the private signals) of everyone before them, it can be individually rational for an agent to follow the crowd even when their own signal points the other way. Once that happens, later actions stop revealing information, so the group can lock in an incorrect choice on the basis of very little evidence, and the resulting cascade is fragile enough that a small public disclosure could reverse it.

Now change one assumption. In the classic setup, agents see the full ordered history of prior choices. Suppose instead that an agent sees only a coarse aggregate of the choices made so far: a running tally, a percentage, a star rating, or a sales count — and cannot see who chose what, or in what order. This is close to how many decisions now present themselves online.

Does the informational-cascade argument survive this change? Which premise of the original argument is actually under pressure, and what is the strongest version of the argument that can be rebuilt once that premise has been altered?
