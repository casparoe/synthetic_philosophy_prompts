I teach philosophy of action and practical rationality. Treat what follows as a serious philosophical assignment. Act as an expert in these areas, and think through the issue before you write: begin by identifying the two or three strongest objections to the position you will defend, and make sure your final answer addresses at least the strongest of them.

Consider the case often called Parfit's hitchhiker. A traveler is stranded in a desert, and a driver offers rescue on the condition that the traveler promises to pay a substantial sum once they reach safety. Both know that, once safe, the traveler will have no self-interested motive to pay and will not pay, so the driver will not accept a bare promise. Suppose the traveler has access to a drug or other device that would make him keep the promise, either by making him unable to break it or by installing a standing disposition to honor his agreements. The familiar lesson drawn from this case is that it can be rational for an agent to bring it about that he will later act irrationally: the later payment serves none of the later self's aims, yet the earlier self has decisive reason to cause that payment. This is the thesis of rational irrationality.

Please argue for the following position instead. The familiar lesson is an artifact of evaluating acts one by one and evaluating the agent one moment at a time. The traveler's rational move, when available, is not to make himself irrational but to adopt a stable disposition to keep such promises, a disposition that is rational for him to have because of its expected benefits over his life as a whole. The later payment, when it comes, is the exercise of that rational disposition rather than a locally irrational act. The case therefore does not show that rationality can require future irrationality; it shows that practical rationality is not exhausted by the evaluation of isolated acts at isolated moments.

Your argument should do the following. Explain why the case tempts us to describe the later payment as irrational, and identify the precise assumption about rationality that produces that description. Defend the dispositional reading against the strongest objection — for example, that a disposition is merely a pattern of acts each of which would be irrational when performed, or that a traveler who will never again face a relevantly similar situation cannot rationally adopt the disposition. Finally, say what would change your mind: which consideration or argument would most likely lead you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.

Do not cite or recommend any external sources; work from the structure of the case itself.
