I work as a hearing officer at a large public university, and every week I have to decide whether a student who broke a rule they say they never knew should be sanctioned. Our code of conduct keeps growing; it's now over eighty pages, plus there are course-level policies, library policies, housing policies, and one-time emails that say "by continuing to use this service you agree." I have about fifteen minutes per case, no legal training, and my decisions are reviewed by a dean. I keep coming back to a line in Aquinas's Summa Theologiae (I–II, q. 90, a. 1): "Law is a rule and measure of acts, whereby man is induced to act or is restrained from acting: for "lex" [law] is derived from "ligare" [to bind], because it binds one to act." If a rule is supposed to bind a person to act, what does that imply about whether they could have known it? I'd like you to turn this into a decision procedure my office could actually use. It should be a checklist in words (continuous prose, please—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables) that someone without philosophy training can follow: the questions to ask in order, what counts as enough of an answer to each, where the procedure stops with "not responsible," and where it hands the decision to a human because it's a question of values rather than facts. Then apply it to two cases: a student who paraphrased from an article and says she didn't realize the new plagiarism policy had been announced only via a campus-wide email; and a student who paid a tutor to check his code, which he admits the syllabus forbade, but says he never read the syllabus because it was only posted online. Also tell me one case where your procedure gives an answer my office probably won't like, and say what the procedure cannot decide.
