I follow a standing policy in my intellectual life, adopted after I became sympathetic to hinge epistemology in the tradition of Wittgenstein's On Certainty, of declining to give evidence for what I take to be hinge commitments. When a statement such as that the world was not created five minutes ago complete with apparent memories, or that my senses and memory are broadly reliable, comes up in ordinary conversation and someone demands that I justify it, I do not offer reasons. I treat the demand as misfiring: the statement, I say, is not something evidence supports or could overturn; it is part of the fixed background against which evidence gets a grip in the first place. In practice I explain why the request is ill formed or change the subject, and I regard the person pressing me as having made a mistake about the kind of claim I was making rather than as having raised a live doubt. I follow this policy in everyday argument but suspend it when I am doing philosophy, where I let myself ask which of these commitments could be wrong. A friend recently asked how I know I am not misapplying the policy to protect a belief that is merely contested — say, a belief about the reliability of a particular institution, a scientific claim I find congenial, or my confidence in someone's testimony. I could not answer, and it now seems to me that, from the inside, the policy may be indistinguishable from refusing to defend whatever I most need to keep fixed. Is the policy rationally defensible? What would a defensible version look like — in particular, how could one distinguish a genuine hinge commitment from an ordinary belief that has been promoted to that status for emotional or social reasons? And if I cannot yet state such a test, am I entitled to keep following the policy in the meantime?
