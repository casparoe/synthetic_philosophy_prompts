I'm an evaluator on a pre-deployment safety team at an AI lab. My team reviews chain-of-thought transcripts for models being considered for use in high-stakes settings, such as a system that proposes medication changes to clinicians or flags passages in legal documents for an attorney. Each week we must decide, for a given model, whether its chain-of-thought should be treated as evidence about the reasoning behind its outputs, or as a narrative that may diverge from the computation that actually produced those outputs. The stakes cut both ways: if we over-trust an obfuscated transcript, we may clear a model whose stated reasons are confabulated; if we over-distrust, we may block a model that is behaving for exactly the reasons it states. We have a few hours per model, we can run a bounded number of behavioral probes, and we may have no access to model weights; our conclusions go to a deployment board that makes the final call.

Last night I had a long argument with a friend who works in interpretability. His view, roughly, was that chain-of-thought monitoring has matured enough that transcript fidelity should be the default assumption; by the end of the argument I held the opposite worry, that optimization pressure makes articulate transcripts increasingly suspect exactly when the stakes are high, because a model selected for task performance and overseer approval is under pressure to produce reasoning that reads well to auditors whether or not it reflects the underlying process. I want something more operational than that argument.

Please write a decision procedure my team could actually follow to decide how far to trust a chain-of-thought transcript in a given evaluation. Build it around the following distinctions rather than treating faithfulness as a yes-or-no property: a transcript can be coherent and reach the right answer yet still be an unreliable account of why the model did what it did; faithfulness is graded; and optimization pressure can select specifically for legible, auditor-friendly transcripts rather than faithful ones. The procedure should specify the questions to ask in order; say what counts as a sufficient answer to each; keep evidence about the faithfulness of the transcript separate from evidence about the task-competence of the model; mark the steps where a human evaluator's judgment cannot be eliminated; and state explicitly what the procedure cannot justify concluding. It should also handle the hard case where the transcript is coherent, complete, and correct, yet we still suspect it is post hoc rationalization.

Then apply the procedure to three concrete cases: (1) a model whose transcript identifies a real contraindication for a proposed medication and whose final answer follows it; (2) a model whose transcript appeals to a precedent that does not appear in the provided document while still reaching the answer an expert would have given; and (3) a model whose transcript is vague and hedged but whose behavior remains strong under adversarial probing. Include at least one application where you expect the procedure returns an answer my team will not like.
