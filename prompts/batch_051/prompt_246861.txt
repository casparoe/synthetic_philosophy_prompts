Exam question:

Explain Sen's liberal paradox (the impossibility of a Paretian liberal). Your answer should: (i) state the result precisely; (ii) define each condition involved; (iii) show, using an appropriate example, how the conditions generate a cycle; and (iv) evaluate one standard response that attempts to preserve some version of both individual liberty and Pareto efficiency, identifying exactly which condition the response modifies or abandons and at what cost.

Student's answer:

Sen's liberal paradox shows that no social decision rule can satisfy three conditions that are individually weak. The first is unrestricted domain: every logically possible profile of individual preferences must be admissible. The second is the weak Pareto principle: if every individual strictly prefers x to y, then society must strictly prefer x to y. The third is minimal liberalism: there must be at least two individuals with at least one pair of alternatives each over which they are decisive, meaning that society follows the individual's strict preference over that pair in whichever direction it points. The condition deliberately does not require decisiveness over every pair that concerns the person's private life; one pair for each of two people suffices, which is what makes the condition look weak.

Sen's standard illustration involves two people and one copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover. The alternatives are that the first person reads it (x), the second person reads it (y), and nobody reads it (z). The first person, a prude, ranks z above x above y. The second, a lewd, ranks x above y above z. The prude is decisive over the pair {x, z}; since the prude prefers z to x, society must rank z above x. The lewd is decisive over the pair {y, z}; since the lewd prefers y to z, society must rank y above z. Since both prefer x to y, Pareto requires society to rank x above y. The three requirements together yield the cycle x above y, y above z, z above x.

As I understand it, Sen's theorem requires the social preference relation to be transitive, and the paradox is usually presented as a failure of transitivity. That suggests an escape: require only acyclicity, since acyclicity is enough for a choice function yet much weaker than transitivity. If society needs only a maximal element rather than a complete ordering, the Chatterley cycle would not by itself defeat the rule; one could break the cycle with a supplementary selection procedure. So the result is best understood as a paradox about ordering, not as a decisive objection to every liberty-respecting decision procedure.

The result is also naturally located within Arrow's framework. Arrow's theorem shows that unrestricted domain, the Pareto condition, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and nondictatorship cannot all be satisfied by a social welfare function. Sen's liberal paradox is what one obtains by replacing nondictatorship with minimal liberalism while retaining the rest of Arrow's framework, in particular independence of irrelevant alternatives; decisiveness over a pair is just an extreme, rights-based form of independence. The impossibility therefore inherits its force from the Arrow proof.

For a response, the most natural candidate is to restrict the domain of admissible profiles so as to exclude meddlesome preferences: preferences over alternatives that fall inside another person's protected sphere. In the example, the Pareto step depends on both individuals ranking x above y, where x and y are alternatives about who reads the book; on this view each such comparison is meddlesome because it ranks somebody else's conduct. If preferences of that form are ruled inadmissible, the cycle cannot form. This keeps both the Pareto condition and the liberty condition intact over the restricted domain, and the only loss is unrestricted domain. Since domain restrictions are already needed in responses to Arrow's theorem, single-peakedness being the obvious example, the cost is modest. The liberal paradox therefore does not force a choice between liberty and Pareto; it forces a choice between unrestricted domain, on one side, and liberty plus Pareto, on the other.
