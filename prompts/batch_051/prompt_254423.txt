I spent my career in the natural sciences and have only recently begun reading formal epistemology. A limiting case of the Condorcet jury theorem has been bothering me, and I would be grateful for a careful, step-by-step assessment of the following argument. If it fails, I want to know the exact step at which it fails and why; if it goes through, I want to know which informal statement of the theorem is misleading and how it should be amended.

(1) Let n voters decide a binary question by simple majority rule. Suppose the voters are mutually independent, and each voter has probability p_n = 1/2 + 1/n of voting for the correct answer.

(2) The Condorcet jury theorem says, roughly, that if voters are independent and each votes correctly with the same probability p > 1/2, then the probability that the majority is correct tends to 1 as the number of voters increases.

(3) For every n, p_n is greater than 1/2. An electorate of size n with competence p_n therefore satisfies the theorem's condition at each stage of the sequence.

(4) It follows from the theorem that, as n grows without bound, the probability that the majority is correct in this sequence of electorates tends to 1.

(5) But that cannot be right. In this sequence, each individual voter becomes arbitrarily close to a fair coin as n grows; the individual edge over chance is only 1/n. A majority of near-fair coins should be, at best, slightly more reliable than a coin toss. It would be strange for a vanishing individual advantage to compound into near-infallibility.

(6) Therefore, either the Condorcet jury theorem is false as usually stated, or its ordinary statement silently assumes something that this sequence violates.

Am I right that one of these steps is too quick? If so, which one, and what exactly is the hidden assumption? If instead the argument is sound, how should the informal statement of the theorem be qualified?
