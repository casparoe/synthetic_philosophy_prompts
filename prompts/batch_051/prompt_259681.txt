I'm the president of my high school's philosophy club and next tuesday I'm running a 40-minute session on speech acts — the idea that saying something can be doing something, like when saying "I bet you five bucks" is itself the bet. The audience is like a dozen kids, mostly sophomores and juniors, no philosophy background, and a few of them already use "performative" the way people do on tiktok to mean fake or just for show. I already get the concept, so don't explain it to me — I need you to plan how I should teach it.

You're an expert at teaching philosophy to teenagers. Before you write the actual plan, brainstorm where high schoolers genuinely get tripped up on this, not the textbook version, the real version. Think carefully, then write the plan in exactly these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. Entry point (2-3 sentences): what I should put on the board or ask in the first two minutes to make the idea feel like a puzzle instead of a definition.
2. Order of moves (150-200 words): the sequence I should introduce the pieces in, numbered, and for each move what it depends on from the previous move.
3. The carrying example (100-150 words): pick one example to carry the whole 40 minutes. Say what it has and what it can't have, and if you think "I now pronounce you..." is the wrong carrying example, say why or swap in a better one.
4. Misconception map (150-200 words): the wrong ideas students will bring in or form halfway through, each with roughly when it shows up and where in the sequence I should head it off. Make sure you deal with the "performative = fake/for show" usage they already bring, and the one about sincerity making the act work or not.
5. Check for understanding (2-3 sentences): one short thing I can ask at the end that would tell me whether they actually got it rather than just nodded along.
6. Cut list (50-100 words): what to leave out at this level, and the one sentence I can say to the kid whose already got their hand up asking about it.
