I'm a retired physical chemist, and since leaving the bench I've been working through a stack of books on astrobiology and philosophy. One thing that keeps bugging me is the Great Filter argument. The way it gets presented—at my local reading group, in TED-style talks, in a couple popular books—is as if the history of life is a series of filters: dead chemistry to replicating molecules, replicating molecules to simple cells, simple cells to complex cells, complex cells to multicellular life, multicellular life to toolmaking intelligence, intelligence to a civilization that expands across the galaxy. At each step most lineages fail to get through; the fractions that pass are small; so the total fraction that makes it all the way is the product of those small numbers. Then the argument turns to the apparent silence of the universe and says: since nobody seems to have made it through the whole train of filters, at least one filter must be extremely fine, and it might well be one of the later ones—so we should not be optimistic about surviving our own filter. People at the reading group say this like it's arithmetic.

But I've spent a lot of my life running actual filtrations—preparative column stuff, membrane separations, size-exclusion. And real filters don't work the way this story needs them to. The fraction that gets through depends on what's being loaded, on the pressure and temperature, on how clogged the filter already is, and on whether the stuff upstream is clumpy or homogeneous. Filters in series are not usually independent draws. And in a real lab you decide what the pores are sized for; nobody sized the pores for "intelligence" or "civilization." So I want somebody to take the analogy seriously. Give me the correspondence table—what is the feed, what are the pores, what's the filtrate, what's the retentate, what would count as pressure or loading. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which breaks matter for the conclusion people actually squeeze out of it, namely that the hard step is probably ahead of us so we're probly doomed. Does that conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the filter picture carrying more weight than it's rated for? If there's a better analogy for the Great Filter reasoning, I'd like to hear that too.
