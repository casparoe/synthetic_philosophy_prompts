Over breakfast my roommate, who just got an A in a graduate decision theory course, tried to convince me that whether expected utility maximization is rationally required was settled long ago, and I've been turning it over all day. Here's the summary he gave me, as close to word-for-word as I can remember:

"Look, it's just a theorem. If your preferences are consistent — and by consistent I mean they don't cycle, so you never prefer A to B, B to C, and then C to A — then the standard representation theorem says you can be represented as maximizing the expected value of some utility function. So consistency and expected utility maximization turn out to be the same commitment: once you've ruled out cyclic preferences, the theorem gives you expected utility maximization for free, and there's no seperate question of whether a rational person has to accept any further axioms. The only thing left to argue about is what goes into the utility function — that is, which outcomes you value and how much."

Something about this felt too clean to me, especially the idea that a representation theorem could by itself settle what it is rational to do, but I couldn't put my finger on exactly what was bugging me. I'm not disputing that some set of consistency conditions gets you expected utility maximization; I just suspect he's quietly sliding between "can be represented as maximizing expected utility" and "must obey these axioms on pain of inconsistency." Is this a fair summary? Walk me through it piece by piece and say what a careful defender of coherence arguments for expected utility maximization would accept, what she'd call a simplification, and what she'd flatly deny.
