Physicists sometimes talk about a final theory of physics — an end to the process of finding ever deeper laws. Consider this working definition: a final theory is one that (i) accounts for all known forces and particles within a single framework, (ii) is mathematically consistent, (iii) has no adjustable free parameters at the fundamental level, and (iv) is empirically adequate, in that it contains at least one solution that matches all observations. Now suppose a candidate theory satisfies all four clauses. Its fundamental equations are fixed and parameter-free, and one of its many low-energy vacua reproduces the particles, forces, and constants we observe. But the theory turns out to have an enormous landscape of such vacua: the measured constants are environmental accidents that vary from vacuum to vacuum, the theory does not select our vacuum over the others, and from inside our vacuum no experiment can distinguish it from rivals that share our local physics.
