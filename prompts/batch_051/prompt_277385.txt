I'm taking a seminar on ancient ethics, and we've spent the last two weeks on Book II of the Republic. We read the passage where Glaucon explains the common view of justice's origin, and I pulled up the Shorey translation on Perseus:

"…to avoid the one and take the other determine that it is for their profit to make a compact with one another neither to commit nor to suffer injustice; and that this is the beginning of legislation and covenants between men, and that they name the commandment of the law the lawful and the just, and that this is the genesis and essential nature of justice—a compromise between the best, which is to do wrong with impunity, and the worst, which is to be wronged and be impotent to get one's revenge. Justice, they tell us, being mid-way between the two, is accepted and approved,"

—Plato, Republic, Book II, 359a (trans. Paul Shorey, via Perseus Digital Library)

A study guide I was using glossed the passage in a way that seemed plausible but maybe a little too tidy. It said something like: According to Glaucon, justice is a compromise. Everyone would prefer to commit injustice and get away with it, but nobody wants to be on the receiving end, so people agree to rules that protect them from each other. Justice is therefore valued purely for its consequences, not for itself. The story of the ring of Gyges is then meant to prove that no one is just willingly, since anyone who could get away with injustice would do it.

I suspect this gloss flattens the passage in a couple of places, but I'm not sure whether my unease is justified or just a beginner's over-reading. Could you give me an accuracy audit of this summary against the actual text? In particular, I'd like to know: Does Glaucon actually assert that injustice is by nature good, or is he reporting a view he wants Socrates to refute? Is it right to say justice is valued "purely for its consequences" on this account, or does the text say something more specific about the lesser evil and the inability to do injustice? And does the ring of Gyges prove that nobody is just willingly, or does it serve a different argumentative function in Glaucon's challenge?
