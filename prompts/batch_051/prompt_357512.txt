My friend and colleague, who died last week, was a lifelong epiphenomenalist about consciousness, and I'm the one giving the eulogy. The room will be half philosophers of mind (they'll catch any slip) and half family (they won't know the term and mostly need to hear that he was warm, not a position paper). Write the eulogy, max 650 words. It has to honor what he actually believed: no line can imply his experiences caused anything — not "his love made him write those letters," not "his grief softened him," not "the music moved him to tears," and certainly not "he is somewhere now, thinking of us." "He lives on in our memories" is okay only if it means we remember him, not that some part of him is still doing something. He held that experiences are real and vivid but causally inert, an accompaniment to what the brain does; our talk of them, including a eulogy, is itself part of the physical story. But he would have hated a eulogy that reduced him to his neurons too. The thing he found astonishing was that consciousness is real, obvious from the inside, and does nothing. Make the mixed room feel why that view isn't cold, and why losing the comfort of "he's watching over you" doesnt cancel what he was. After the speech, give me a separate note of at most 150 words: flag where you leaned on a contested reading of epiphenomenalism or took a liberty with it, and say in a sentence or two what about the paradox of phenomenal judgment is genuinely settled versus still contested among people who work on it.
