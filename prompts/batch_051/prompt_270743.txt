I'm teaching The Handmaid's Tale in a course where we've also been reading some archival-studies material — the claim that archives aren't neutral storage, that deciding what gets preserved, described, and made findable is itself an exercise of power, and that the silences in a collection are often as informative as the documents themselves. I keep coming back to how the novel reaches us: a mess of cassette tapes found later in someones footlocker, no dates, no real name, arranged by people the reader never meets, and then the Historical Notes epilogue turns it over to a room of scholars at a Gileadean studies conference who treat her voice as an artifact. The keynote speaker spends a weird amount of time joking about her and trying to pin down the identity of her Commander, and the audience laughs. That ending always read to me like Atwood showing preservation curdling into another kind of silencing.

What I'd like is an actual reading of the novel's frame through an archives-and-power lens, not just the easy line that the winners write history. Inside Gilead, women's access to reading and writing is one of the first things cut off, the Bible becomes a controlled text delivered through male readers, and the bodies on the Wall work as a kind of public record whose meaning the state curates. After Gilead, Offred's story survives only by accident and gets curated by people who seem more interested in the artifact than in her. So: does the novel dramatize the idea that an archive is an instrument of power, and where does it complicate that idea rather than illustrate it?

I want the reading to be honest in both directions. Offred is editing, omitting, reconstructing from a damaged memory — her tapes are already a shaped archive before any historian gets near them, so does that undercut the neat claim that the epilogue scholars simply colonize her voice, or does it deepen the point? And does the book let anything of her escape the archivist's frame, or is the last sting that even the reader gets put in the archivist's position, wanting to extract something usable from her? By the end I want to know whether Atwood adds something to the debate about archives and power that the theory on its own doesn't, or whether she's mainly staging what the theory already says.
