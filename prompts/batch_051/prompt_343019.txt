As someone who teaches philosophy of language, I have come to distrust the ready-made positions in the prescriptivism–descriptivism debate, mostly because my own judgments refuse to line up behind either side. I would like to know whether there is a single principle that captures them, and if not, which of them I should give up. Here are the verdicts, roughly in order of confidence. I regard the split infinitive as fully grammatical, and the familiar rule against it as tracking nothing real. I likewise regard sentence-initial "hopefully" as unproblematic, and I treat "try and fix it" and "if I was you" as matters of register—acceptable in speech, to be avoided in careful written prose. Yet "between you and I" still strikes me as a genuine error in standard English rather than a stylistic blemish, and I would mark "less than ten items" as wrong in any edited context, though I cannot say why its wrongness differs from the acceptable informalities I just listed. I do not call nonstandard constructions like "I don't have none" or invariant "be" errors at all: I take them to be well-formed in grammars other than my own, and I would fault a student only for using them where standard edited prose is expected. But then "the green big dragon" feels ungrammatical in a different and stronger way, as if no amount of usage could ratify it, and I suspect it tracks something about how adjectives are ordered rather than a convention. Finally, there is a pair I cannot reconcile: I accept "here's an invite" without resistance, while "this policy impacts everyone" sets my teeth on edge and I privately mark it as bad, even though both are cases of shifting a word's category. I do not know whether my principle ought to respect that irritation.

Is there an account of what "bad grammar" consists in that delivers these verdicts, or nearly all of them? If there is, the account must also tell me what to say about a case I keep wavering on: a question such as "whom do you think is coming?", which my students produce when they are trying to be careful. I am inclined to treat it as the clearest error in the lot, but I worry I do so only because effortful politeness annoys me. Please state the principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate if any can be kept, and test it against at least one case I have not listed. Finally, I would like to know what could change the account you give: which of my verdicts, if it turned out to rest on a false empirical belief about actual usage or about how one of these constructions works, would most destabilize the principle, and where you take the proposed account to be least secure.
