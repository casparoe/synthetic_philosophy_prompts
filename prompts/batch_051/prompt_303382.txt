You're a political philosopher working on the moral economy of the welfare state. Think carefully, and maybe start by writing down the distinctions people might be implicitly tracking and then testing them against edge cases, before you commit to a verdict.

I keep going back and forth on whether we have a real double standard about welfare. Let me set it up with two people, same town, same year.

A is claiming unemployment benefit while doing cash-in-hand work. Over eighteen months she under-reports her income, keeps claiming the full benefit, and ends up with roughly £9,000 the state wouldn't have paid her. When she's caught it's treated as benefit fraud: prosecution, a criminal conviction, maybe a suspended sentence, the local paper runs a story, and the word "scrounger" gets used, or at least impled.

B is a self-employed builder. Over the same period he under-reports his income to the tax office by about the same proportion, so he avoids roughly £9,000 in tax. He's caught too. He pays the back tax plus a penalty, no conviction, no front page, and most people I know treat it as "just how small business works." If I'm honest, they'd nearly all call A a fraudster and barely shrug at B.

The state seems to run the same asymmetry: it pours resources into catching and prosecuting benefit cheats over amounts that are tiny next to unpaid tax. And on paper B's lie looks at least as bad to me: he isn't poor, he could have paid, and the public services he skipped out on were being funded honestly by everyone else. Yet the social meaning is completely different.

What I want to know is whether anything actually justifies treating A and B differently, or whether this is just class bias with a badge on. The candidates I can think of go like this. Maybe a benefit is a kind of gift from the community, so abusing it is like stealing from a needy stranger, whereas tax is a burden, so avoiding it is just defending what's already yours — but that restates the attitude instead of justifying it. Maybe it's about need: A took from a pot reserved for the poor, B from the general pot — except both reduce what the state can spend on the poor. Maybe it's conditionality: B doesn't owe the state job-search meetings, work placements, or availability requirements, only money, while A owed her time and her availability too — but both owed honest records and a chunk of their income. Or maybe we just resent someone looking "idle on our dime" more than someone "working their books," which would be a social status thing, not a principle.

Give me the strongest principle you can build that could justify the asymmetry as it actually operates — steelman it harder than I just did — and then test it against nearby cases: a landlord who claims housing benefit for tenants who don't exist; a farmer claiming a subsidy for fields he doesn't farm; a millionaire claiming a carer's allowance; a poor freelancer who evades tax because otherwise she can't make rent. Then tell me straight: should we keep the asymmetry as it is, try to reverse it, or flatten it, and what would the moral and legal books look like if we followed your answer through?
