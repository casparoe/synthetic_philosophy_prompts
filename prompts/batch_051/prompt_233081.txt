I've been trying to determine exactly what the Buddhist doctrine of anattā commits one to, and I keep constructing an argument that seems to show the Buddha's teaching of anattā is inconsistent with his teaching of kamma. Here is the argument:

(1) According to the Buddha, none of the five aggregates — material form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and consciousness — is a self, and no self exists apart from or over and above the aggregates.
(2) So the person is nothing more than the stream of aggregates.
(3) The stream of aggregates is a succession of distinct momentary states: no single element of the stream is identical to any later element, since each arises in dependence on the preceding one and then ceases.
(4) If no single element persists from one moment to the next, then no one persists through time; there is a series of lives but no single being that lives them.
(5) But the doctrine of kamma holds that the being who acts now later reaps the fruit of that act — in this life or a future one.
(6) Therefore anattā and kamma are inconsistent.

My suspicion is that step (4) is doing the real work and that the move from "no single element persists" to "no one persists" is too quick, but when I try to press that objection I'm told that a causally connected series is not a being who can deserve anything, and I cannot say why that reply fails. So tell me: exactly which step should be rejected, and on what reading of it? I want a committed diagnosis, not a survey of possible readings. If the flaw is an equivocation, name the term and give both senses. If the argument has a salvageable core, state the strongest version that does go through and specify the premise that would have to be added or reinterpreted to make it valid. If you think it is valid and the inconsistency is real, defend that rather than hedging.
