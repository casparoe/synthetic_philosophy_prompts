I'm a philosophy PhD student who works on well-being and the good life, and I've been going in circles about a pattern in how people around me—and, if I'm honest, how I myself often—evaluate deliberately choosing to have less. Take the FIRE movement (financial independence / retire early): people who save 70% of their income, live extremely cheaply, and retire at forty are widely praised as disciplined, rational, and a little bit admirable for refusing normal levels of consumption. But a person who does something structurally similar out of religious or ethical conviction—say, gives away their possessions and joins a monastery, or lives in a tiny room and renounces pleasures as an ascetic—is often treated as sad, repressed, or wasting their life, even when they describe themselves as liberated. Both are voluntarily cutting consumption and comfort in pursuit of some good, and both claim the simpler life makes them better off. So the practice I want you to examine is this whole pattern of judgement: we celebrate one kind of radical frugality and pathologize another.

What view of the good life, welfare, or prudence does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that would make the two halves hang together—say whether it is coherent, and seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with (hedonism, desire-satisfaction, objective-list views, ideals of self-mastery or autonomy, something about the worthiness of the goal). Then tell me what would actually have to change in these social evaluations if we replaced that presupposed view with, say, a straightforward hedonism, and again if we replaced it with a perfectionist view that admires self-denial for its own sake. I'm less interested in a survey than in a sharp reconstruction: where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, and what would it take for these judgements to be more than a mood?
