I spent thirty years in spectroscopy, so the assembly theory stuff has been nagging at me since I started reading philosophy of science. The idea, as I understand it, is that you can score an object's complexity by the minimum number of joining operations needed to build it up from a small stock of primitive building blocks, with repeated substructures available for reuse. Suppose that in 2035 someone starts selling a cheap benchtop 'assembly meter' that takes a solid or liquid sample and returns that score reliably, plus a guess at the shortest construction pathway; no exotic physics, just very good chemometrics or whatever turns out to work. Give me a reasoned prediction of how the next twenty years play out, scientifically and industrially. I want the mechanism, not a story: which fields pick it up first and what gets displaced; which early uses are real and which are cargo-cult metrics; what new frauds or junk papers it enables; and whether it quietly changes how people define life, technology, or signal versus noise — or whether 'the assembly index is what complexity means' simply becomes an unargued background assumption. Also tell me how confident you are in the various claims and flag the single prediction you're least sure of.
