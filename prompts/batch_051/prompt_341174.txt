I’m in a graduate seminar on the unity of the proposition, and I’ve ended up genuinely torn between two ways of resolving Bradley’s regress. On one side I’m attracted to the Fregean idea that propositional unity comes from a distinction in kind between saturated and unsaturated components: the concept is essentially incomplete or predicative, so it doesn’t need a further relation to connect it to the object; it simply takes the object to form a complete thought. This feels like a solution because it stops the regress by denying that the relation itself is another thing waiting to be related. On the other side I have a firm intuition I can’t dismiss: when I consider the proposition that Socrates is wise, there is still a unity there that Frege’s machinery doesn’t fully explain—the function-argument combination can look like a description of what the unity does, not an account of what it is. F. H. Bradley’s Appearance and Reality, in the chapter on relation and quality, pressed something like this: if a relation is something, it seems to be a further constituent that needs relating; if it is nothing, it cannot relate. Frege’s unsaturated concept is supposed to escape that dilemma, but I’m not sure it escapes my intuition.

I don’t want a verdict; I want a negotiated treatment. Give me the strongest possible version of the Fregean position and the strongest possible version of the intuition that propositional unity is not exhausted by the saturated/unsaturated distinction. Then tell me what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be amended or abandoned, and whether there is a reading of “unity” on which both can survive. Be honest about which feature of the problem the Fregean account can’t reproduce and what the intuition might be tracking if it isn’t tracking a further entity.
