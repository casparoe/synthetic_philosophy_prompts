I have been reading about the void-for-vagueness doctrine on my own, not as part of any course, and I have talked myself into an argument that seems to prove far too much. I want you to tell me whether the argument is valid, and if it is not, exactly which step fails and why. Here are the steps as I understand them.

(1) The doctrine says a criminal law is void for vagueness when it fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what it prohibits, so that the person has to guess at whether some conduct is criminal. The doctrine also has a requirement about preventing arbitrary enforcement, but set that aside, since my argument concerns notice only.

(2) In practice, a criminal law cannot do its work without general classifying terms such as vehicle, dwelling, weapon, obscene, or threatening. Any such term has fuzzy edges: there are borderline cases in which competent speakers would in good faith disagree about whether the term applies.

(3) So for every criminal law, there is some possible piece of conduct near a boundary such that a person of ordinary intelligence cannot know in advance whether the law covers it.

(4) But if there is conduct a person of ordinary intelligence cannot classify in advance under a law, then the law leaves that person to guess at what it prohibits.

(5) Therefore every criminal law is void for vagueness. That cannot be the doctrine's actual consequence, or criminal law would be impossible. So one of these steps must be the culprit.

I half suspect the culprit is step (2), because the argument might be overstating how vague ordinary legal terms are. But if step (2) is where it fails, that feels too easy, and I want to know whether there is a deeper mistake later in the argument.

Please structure your answer in the following sections, in this order, with roughly the lengths indicated. Write in British English throughout.

- Verdict (one paragraph): State whether the argument goes through. If it does not, name the step where it first fails, and mention any further step you would also challenge.
- The main failure (up to 250 words): Explain exactly how that step fails, and on what reading of it. If the argument could be repaired by restating the step, say how.
- The other steps (two short paragraphs): Go through the remaining steps and say whether each is sound, and if one looks suspicious but is not, why it is not the real problem.
- What the standard actually requires (up to 150 words): Say what the fair-notice side of the doctrine must require, if it is to avoid the conclusion in step (5).
