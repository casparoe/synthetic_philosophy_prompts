I work on a policy research team that advises governments on the governance of frontier AI systems. Our team operates under an informal but firmly enforced norm: no written analysis goes out unless it is anchored in at least one historical analogy, drawn in practice from a fixed stock of three — nuclear weapons, the printing press, and the Industrial Revolution. We treat these analogies as carrying real causal structure rather than merely rhetorical color. The nuclear-weapons analogy imports state secrecy, arms-race dynamics, deterrence logic, export controls, and a picture of a small number of decisive actors. The printing-press analogy imports a nearly opposite apparatus: decentralized diffusion, cheap copying, the slow democratization of knowledge, the difficulty of suppressing information, and benefits that arrive only after prolonged disruption. The Industrial Revolution tends to serve as a middle case, capturing economy-wide transformation, labor displacement, and long time horizons.

Lately I have been struck by how much of our analysis is settled by the choice of analogy before any AI-specific evidence enters the picture. We will draft one section under a printing-press frame and another under a nuclear frame, and their conclusions pull in different directions without our ever stating what would make one frame the correct one. I would like you to help me understand what this practice commits us to. What view of technology, and of historical change, does it presuppose — for instance, the idea that a technology carries an intrinsic character that keeps its social and political effects stable across centuries? Is the practice internally coherent when it switches between analogies within a single document? And if we abandoned the underlying view in favor of the idea that a technology's consequences are settled mostly by surrounding institutions, choices, and distributions of power, what would our norm of mandatory historical analogy have to look like afterward?

Please answer as a philosopher of technology and of history. Begin by brainstorming the implicit commitments of the practice; then distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; and finally identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that more than one defensible view could license it. Think carefully before you conclude.
