Take Nozick's experience machine. The classic version: you, an adult, get to plug in for life and design the experiences. Nozick expects you to refuse, because you'd want to actually live in contact with reality and be a certain kind of person, not just have the feelings. Fine.

Now change the setup. The society licenses parents: would-be parents submit a life plan for the child, and the licensing board approves or denies it in order to protect the child's future. A couple applies to raise their child inside a permanent, upgraded experience machine from infancy. The machine isn't crude: the body develops normally, some of the caregivers and other children are real and not simulated, and the simulated content is as rich as any biography. But the menu of possible futures was fixed by the initial setup and can't be altered from inside; the child never learns that an outside world exists until age 25, at which point they can choose to leave with no penalty. The board has to decide whether this plan violates the child's right to an open future—or whatever you think is the better name for that idea.

Here's what I actually want. Run the Nozick-style analysis and the open-future analysis side by side and tell me whether they come apart on this case. My working hypothesis is that they do: Nozick's point is about whether I want a simulated life for myself, while the open-future point is about the child never having had the chance to evaluate the simulation from outside, even if the simulated life is satisfying. But maybe the second is just the first point in kid-shaped packaging. Take a definite position on whether the board should deny the license, and make the stronger claim about which argument does the real work; if you think they don't come apart, say explicitly why the distinction is empty here.

One more subquestion to fold in: what would have to be true about the child's exit options at 25 for the open-future objection to collapse?
