I'm a journalist who covers AI and the ideas around it for a living, and I've been chewing on the debate approach to AI safety — the proposal that you take two much-smarter-than-human models, have them argue both sides of a hard question while a weaker judge (a human, or a small model) picks the winner, and trust that the truth wins because a lie can always be exposed by a short, checkable rebuttal. The more I think about it, the less I belive the setup, and I've got a homemade diagnosis I want someone to kick the tires on.

I've been calling it the fog advantage. Here's the theory. A judge has a finite audit budget: they can really verify a handful of steps before they start taking the rest on faith. So the side that wins is the side whose decisive points sit above that threshold and whose weak points sit below it. A debater willing to lie can produce fog on demand — long, plausible-sounding sub-arguments, credible digressions, expert-flavored detail — each of which costs the judge real effort to clear even when it's beside the point. Fog is cheap to generate and expensive to dispel, so a shameless debater can push their actual flaw down below the judge's audit depth. The honest debator can't do the same, because they can't just relocate the hard parts of the truth. Concretely: two models are debating whether a patch to a trading system is safe. The honest side points at a money-printing edge case in four steps any competent judge can follow. The dishonest side doesn't deny it; it emits a forty-step story about how the upstream cache layer normalizes balances, where step fourteen quietly assumes a currency conversion that never actually happens. Each of the forty steps sounds like normal engineering. To expose the lie, the honest side has to walk the judge through the entire cache layer for real, which is exactly the long foggy explanation it was trying to avoid, while the liar just keeps saying "yes, and therefore." The judge can't grade forty-step arguments, so the lie survives.

My claim is that this is a structural limit rather than a practical annoyance: below the budget needed to chew through all the fog, extra judge time or extra rounds get absorbed without changing the result, and debate ends up selecting for whoever is least bothered about padding rather than whoever is right.
