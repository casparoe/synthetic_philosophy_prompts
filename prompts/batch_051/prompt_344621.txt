I recently read a piece arguing that the cluelessness objection is the deepest problem for consequentialism, and I want an accuracy audit of the argument as the article presented it. The decisive paragraph ran something like this:

"Consequentialism says an act is right just when its consequences are better than those of every alternative. But consequences never come to an end: the person you help today may commit a serious harm next year, and that harm changes the course of other lives, and so on to the end of history. Since we can see only the first few links in the chain, and since the links we cannot see would also have to be counted, we can never know which act is actually right. On the theory's own terms, then, ordinary options come out indistinguishable, and the theory is condemned to cluelessness. This is not merely a practical inconvenience; it shows the theory is untenable, because a moral theory that cannot in principle guide a single decision is not a moral theory at all."

I find this persuasive when I read it quickly, but the conclusion does more work than the premises literally state. Go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts are accurate, which parts are subtle distortions, and what a defender of consequentialism would say in reply. In particular, I want to know whether the slide from the epistemic claim that we cannot know which act is actually right to the verdict that the theory is untenable is licensed by the accurate parts, or whether it silently assumes that a criterion of rightness must also be a usable decision procedure. And if the distortion is removed, does a serious residual problem remain for consequentialism? If so, state precisely what that problem is.
