I coordinate public consultation meetings for a municipal planning department. We run these roughly monthly, often on decisions with high stakes and polarized participants—housing density, flood-control measures, siting of social services. The department wants a reusable facilitator’s procedure grounded in Habermas’s ideal speech situation, so that meetings are less dominated by the loudest, most persistent, or most institutionally powerful voices. I am not a philosopher, most participants are not, and each session is constrained: usually two hours, one facilitator, no legal power to exclude people except on narrow safety grounds, and a department official who must sign off on any departure from standard practice.

Please translate Habermas’s ideal speech situation into a practical decision procedure I can run in each session. In his early formulation, the ideal speech situation includes the requirements that every subject capable of speech and action be allowed to take part in a discourse; that everyone be allowed to question any assertion whatever; that everyone be allowed to introduce any assertion whatever; that everyone be allowed to express their attitudes, desires, and needs without hesitation; and that no speaker be prevented, by internal or external coercion, from exercising those rights. Treat those requirements as the design target, not as a description of any real meeting.

The deliverable should be a checklist or sequence of tests in order, with, for each: the question to ask before or during the meeting; what counts as a sufficient answer; where the procedure stops with a “no” or shifts the meeting to a different format; and where it must hand the decision to a person because a Habermasian condition conflicts with another value or cannot be checked empirically. Mark clearly which steps are genuine judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Also identify which distinctions in Habermas’s original account must survive the simplification and which can be dropped for a two-hour meeting with non-specialists.

Then run the procedure on three scenarios: (1) a meeting about whether to build a flood-retention basin that would require removing a community garden; (2) a meeting at which a small group of repeat speakers tries to define the agenda before others can speak; and (3) a meeting where an expert consultant’s technical model is repeatedly challenged by residents as biased, and the facilitator must decide whether to treat the challenges as obstruction or as legitimate questioning. Include the likely answer for each, and identify at least one scenario where the procedure gives an answer the department or I will not like. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do—particularly what it cannot guarantee about power, sincerity, or consensus.
