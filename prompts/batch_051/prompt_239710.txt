I'm preparing for a qualifying exam in philosophy of biology, and one of the study aids I'm using—a prep book written by a philosopher I trust—summarizes Machamer, Darden, and Craver's mechanistic account of explanation as follows. They argue that biological explanation proceeds by specifying the mechanism responsible for a phenomenon, where a mechanism is a collection of entities and activities whose organization produces that phenomenon. For them, explanation is an ontic matter: it is the mechanism in the world, rather than anyone's representation of it, that does the explaining. From this, the book draws the conclusion that a written description is explanatory just in case it truly reports the mechanism's parts and operations, and that what marks off a complete explanation from a sketch is that the complete one has filled in every black box and traced the mechanism down to its molecular constituents. Mechanism schemas, accordingly, are useful heuristics for guiding discovery, but not explanations in their own right, since they contain black boxes and stop short of the molecular level.

This summary is admirably clean, but something about it feels too strong, and I would rather not memorize a distortion. Is it a fair account of their view? Please separate the accurate claims from the overstatements or subtle misreadings, address what Machamer, Darden, and Craver actually say about bottoming out and about the status of mechanism schemas, and explain what the ontic character of explanation does and does not commit them to.
