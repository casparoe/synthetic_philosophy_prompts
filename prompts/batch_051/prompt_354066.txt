I am assembling material for a seminar on formal theories of truth, and I would like a dialogue that forces a disagreement into the open rather than burying it in a footnote. The disagreement concerns the diagnostic lesson of Curry's paradox and Yablo's paradox. Curry's paradox begins with a sentence that asserts of itself that if it is true, then some arbitrary statement P follows; from the transparency of truth, together with something like the structural rule of contraction or an equivalent detachable-conditional move, P is derivable without any appeal to negation. Yablo's paradox instead uses a countably infinite sequence of sentences S1, S2, S3, and so on, where each Sn asserts that every later sentence in the sequence is untrue. The contradiction is reached in two stages: assuming any Sn is true forces Sn+1 to be both true and untrue, so each Sn must be untrue; yet since every later sentence is thereby untrue, the content of each Sn is nonetheless satisfied, so each Sn is also true.

Please write a dialogue of roughly 900 to 1,100 words between two logicians, A and B, standing at a whiteboard during a coffee break at a workshop. A holds that Yablo's sequence is a genuine liar-style paradox involving no self-reference and no pejorative circularity, and that it therefore shifts the burden away from self-reference as the source of semantic paradox: any adequate solution must revise the logic—plausibly by rejecting or restricting contraction, or by restricting the truth schema—rather than merely banning self-referential sentences. A also insists that Curry's paradox already pointed in this direction, but that its self-referential form let self-reference theorists off the hook; Yablo removes that excuse. B resists the inference. B concedes that the two-stage derivation is valid, but argues that the sequence as a whole can be specified only through a fixed-point-style construction, and that the property of being one of the Yablo sentences quantifies over a totality that includes itself; the circularity has been relocated from the individual sentence to the sequence-defining predicate, not eliminated. On B's view, Curry's paradox, being self-referential, is compatible with a circularity-based diagnosis, and Yablo is the first serious candidate counterexample to that diagnosis—but a failed one, so A is too quick to abandon self-reference or circularity as the common culprit.

Both characters should be equally competent and charitable, and neither should be a strawman. Each should restate the other's position in terms the other accepts before objecting to it. The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth, with each character responding to the other's actual arguments rather than delivering set speeches, and it should push toward the question of what circularity amounts to in this setting and whether it is even a well-defined diagnostic category. End without a tidy resolution, but with each position made more precise under pressure.
