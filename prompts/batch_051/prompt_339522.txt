I write about science and ideas for a living, and I've been poking at a story about moral responsibility in supply chains when the same question keeps coming up: when is someone complicit in a wrong they didn't actually cause? My editor wants me to have a clean test for it, but the deeper i go the messier it gets. Here are five situations and what my gut says about each.

First, a guy agrees to drive a friend to a house so the friend can steal a laptop. They get lost, the friend finds another ride, and the burglary happens exactly as it would have anyway. The driver's contribution made zero difference to the crime, but I still think he's complicit.

Second, a lookout for a robbery falls asleep before anyone ever needed his warning, and the robbery goes off without a hitch. I think he's complicit too, even though he literally did nothing.

Third, a retired teacher owns a few thousand dollars of a mining company through an index fund. The company has been bribing officials abroad for years, and she knows what's reported about it, but her stake is microscopic and she never votes her shares. I dont think she's complicit in the bribery.

Fourth, a user retweets a cruelly mocking post about a private person. The post was already going viral, and her retweet didn't measurably change how many people saw it. I still think she's complicit in the harassment.

Fifth, a payroll clerk at that same mining company notices that some of the "consulting fees" she processes look exactly like bribes. She keeps processing them because her job is to process forms, not ask questions. I think she's complicit.

My working rule was that complicity requires making the wrong more likely. That handles the shareholder the way I want, but it can't handle the driver, the lookout, or the retweet. And the payroll clerk is exactly as replaceable as the shareholder, so the same rule would either let both off or catch both, and I badly want to treat the two of them differently.

So: what principle, or small set of principles, captures all five verdicts? If none can, which of my verdicts should I drop, and why? Then apply your principle to one more case I'm genuinely stuck on: a bartender hears two regulars plan to jump a man after they leave, and he neither warns the man nor calls anyone. Is the bartender complicit in the beating, or just a lousy bystander?

Give me your answer as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, with no introduction or conclusion. Make one of the five points a confidence note: how sure you are about the principle you end up with, and the single claim you're least sure of.
