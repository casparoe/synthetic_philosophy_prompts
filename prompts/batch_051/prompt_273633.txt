A friend and I spent most of last weekend arguing about whether she was wrong to break a promise in order to help another friend through a crisis. I won't revisit the judgment itself; what I want to understand is a move she made over and over, and that I've since noticed in a lot of moral argument. Every time I offered a general principle — that promises bind unless a substantially greater harm would result, for example, or that one should not treat a person as a mere convenience — she refused to apply it. Real cases, she kept saying, are richer than any rule, so no rule could settle this one. But when I pressed her to say what feature of the case made the principle misfire, she couldn't name one. She would answer only that I had to look at the whole situation, or that this case was simply different. The same pattern appeared later, when we disputed whether a white lie to a child is acceptable: I offered a principle, she called it too crude for the case, and again no specific feature emerged. Is there a name for this position, or for this thing people do? My question is not really about terminology. I want to know whether an insistence that each situation is richer than any rule is a defensible moral view, or whether it is better described as a refusal to give reasons, a kind of special pleading, or a way of trusting an intuition one will not articulate. If it is defensible, what separates it from the person who merely asserts that his case is special without saying how? What test would let me tell apart a genuine, feature-rich objection to a principle from a bare claim of uniqueness, and should the answer differ between my two examples?
