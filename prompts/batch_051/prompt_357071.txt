Just got out of a long argument with a friend about Mill's Utilitarianism. He keeps saying that on any consequentialist picture, virtues and duties can only ever be instrumental: their value is derivative from the happiness they tend to produce, and Mill's line about virtue becoming "part of the end" is just a psychological consollation. I think there might be a coherent middle reading where virtue starts out as a means but becomes finally valuable, not merely instrumentally, once a person actually loves it for itself. We kept circling around this bit from Chapter IV ("Of What Sort of Proof the Principle of Utility is Susceptible"):

"Virtue, according to the utilitarian doctrine, is not naturally and originally part of the end, but it is capable of becoming so; and in those who love it disinterestedly it has become so, and is desired and cherished, not as a means to happiness, but as a part of their happiness."

My friend reads that as: "part of their happiness" is just a roundabout way of saying it still tracks happiness, so whatever value virtue has is instrumental through and through. I'm tempted to read it as: the virtue is now valued for itself by the agent, and that is exactly what a consequentialist can offer without making virtue independently binding. Which reading is closer to what Mill is actually committed to in that chapter, and does either reading leave room for duties that aren't merely derivative? Also, please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know Mill well, and flag if one of these readings is a minority view.
