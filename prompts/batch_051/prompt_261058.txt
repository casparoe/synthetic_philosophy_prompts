I was arguing with a friend about whether sophisticated agents are immune to money pumps, and it sent me back to a passage in Frank Ramsey's essay Truth and Probability. My memory of it is that Ramsey says you can measure a person's degree of belief by proposing bets and seeing what odds she accepts, but that this method is only sound when the stakes are small enough that the utility of the money itself doesn't distort the choice. I find that weirdly relevant to money pumps, because the textbook diachronic pump keeps each individual step tiny—trade a little, pay a small fee—so that the agent's preferences, not the money, do the work.

Here's the variation I want worked through. Suppose an agent has preferences A over B, B over C, and C over A, but she is sophisticated: she knows her own preferences are intransitive and adopts a standing policy of refusing any sequence of trades she recognizes as a pump, even though she still sincerely has each pairwise preference when the alternatives are presented in isolation. Does the money-pump argument still show she is irrational? Does the small-stakes caveat Ramsey adds make the pump more or less effective against someone like this? I'd like you to run the standard analysis plus the variation, and if you think the setup is confused—for instance, if you can't have the pairwise preferences and the standing policy without already abandoning intransitivity—say so plainly rather than pretending the prompt works. Also, if your answer depends on fine details about how trades are framed, flag those rather than hiding them.
