In my philosophy of law seminar, the instructor has spent the term issuing verdicts on sentencing cases. She is strikingly consistent, but no one has yet succeeded in stating the rule she is applying. Her record so far is this. She held that a young drug courier with no prior record, who had been trafficked and coerced into the role, should receive a suspended term with treatment. She held that a first-time shoplifter with a steady job should receive a fine and community service, not jail. She held that a now elderly and housebound fraudster who had spent decades taking the savings of pensioners should receive the maximum available term. She held that an offender who had become quadriplegic after serving about a third of a sentence she had earlier called proportionate to the offense should be released. And she held that a repeat burglar whose break-ins had grown increasingly violent and whose risk of re-offending was high should receive the top of the range for the current burglary.

Next week the seminar takes up the following case. A remorseful eighteen-year-old acted as a look-out in a series of armed robberies. He played a peripheral role, used no violence himself, had no record, and is judged a low risk. On any ordinary desert scale, his conduct warrants roughly eighteen months. But the city is in the middle of a robbery wave, and the prosecution offers credible evidence that a conspicuously harsh sentence—say, six years—would measurably reduce the number of young people willing to serve as look-outs. The class agrees that the forward-looking case for the longer sentence, taken on its own, is strong. Both sentences are legally available; she is deciding what sentence ought to be imposed.

Predict her verdict. Lay out the two or three most plausible rules that fit her record so far—pure consequentialism, pure retributivism, and limiting retributivism, which treats desert only as an upper bound, are the natural candidates—and state what each rule prescribes for the look-out case. Identify the closest earlier case, say which rule best explains the whole record, and give your best guess about what she will say and why. Finally, describe a further case, different from the look-out case, that would most cleanly settle whichever ambiguity your analysis leaves open.
