You are a professional philosopher specializing in bioethics and the concept of death. Begin by brainstorming the different ways one might draw the line between life and death, then work through the case below carefully. I care more about a criterion-by-criterion analysis than about a single yes-or-no, and I want you to flag any place where the standard is doing real conceptual work versus merely ruling by stipulation.

The standard I have in mind is the two-pronged one roughly codified in American law and clinical practice: a human being is dead when either (a) circulatory and respiratory functions have irreversibly ceased, or (b) all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem, have irreversibly ceased. Death is therefore supposed to be reachable by two routes: heart–lung failure or whole-brain failure.

Now consider this patient. A woman in her forties suffered a severe hypoxic brain injury. Fourteen months later, clinical examination and imaging indicate that her cerebral cortex has been largely destroyed: she shows no signs of awareness, no goal-directed movement, no ability to communicate, and no realistic prospect of recovering any of these capacities. Her brainstem, by contrast, is substantially intact. She breathes on her own, cycles between sleeping and waking, opens her eyes, gags and coughs, digests food, and maintains her own heartbeat and blood pressure. A feeding tube supplies nutrition, but no machine breathes for her and no drug supports her circulation.

Is this woman dead under the standard as stated? Go through each prong and say precisely where she falls. Then address the harder question: does this case show that the line has been drawn in the wrong place—for instance, because what matters for a person is consciousness rather than the brainstem's autonomic housekeeping? Or does the fact that she is plainly a living organism settle the matter in favor of the standard? If the criteria need revision, propose and defend the best revision; if they do not, defend them against this case.
