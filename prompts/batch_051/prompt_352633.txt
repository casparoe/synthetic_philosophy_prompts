A friend has been drafting a paper on how much one should be willing to pay for very large possible payoffs, and he produced an argument that now strikes him as proving too much, though he cannot find the faulty step. I reproduce it here with his numbering:

(1) A rational agent should choose, among the acts available, one with maximal expected value: the sum over possible states of the value of the act's outcome in that state, weighted by the probability of the state.

(2) Value is unbounded above. For any possible outcome, however much value it contains, there is a further possible outcome containing still more — for instance, more lives worth living.

(3) From (2), for any finite sacrifice S and any positive probability p, there is a possible outcome O such that p times the value of O exceeds the value of S. So for any ordinary, high-probability good you might pursue, there is a merely possible good so astronomically valuable that even a small chance of realizing it has higher expected value.

(4) Therefore, under (1), ordinary high-probability goods are never what you ought to pursue: whatever finite good an act would secure, some long-shot, low-probability, astronomical outcome outbids it in expected value.

(5) Nor can the pattern be contained. For any cost a stranger asks you to pay, she can describe a possible outcome of arbitrarily enormous value and link it to your paying her. If you assign any positive probability to her story, (1) directs you to pay; and since such stories can be multiplied indefinitely, (1) directs you to hand over everything, claim by claim.

(6) A theory with that consequence cannot be correct: it is exploitable, and it leaves no decision-theoretic role for the goods whose reality we know most directly.

(7) Hence at least one of (1), (2), or the bridge from (2) to (5) is false. My friend's worry is that the argument proves too much: it does not merely recommend one controversial choice, but empties the theory of any sane guidance.

I would most like to keep (1) in some recognizable form, and I am prepared to give up (2) if that is what the argument really requires. What I cannot yet see is whether rejecting (2) actually blocks step (5), or merely shrinks the stakes while leaving the fanatical structure intact. Which premise or step should be rejected, and what does the repair cost? Please answer in American English.
