You are a philosopher of law who specializes in the epistemology of statistical evidence and in how legal proof standards interact with probabilistic reasoning. I want you to think slowly, brainstorm candidate principles before committing to any of them, and then give a reasoned recommendation.

I have just joined a three-member civil-claims panel whose senior member has built up a short track record on exactly the question that now divides us. All our cases are decided on the preponderance of the evidence. Here are her rulings.

1. Route-share bus. A pedestrian was hit by a bus at dusk and could identify only that the vehicle was a bus—no color, markings, or route number. The only evidence connecting the accident to a particular operator was that Blue Bus Co. runs 80% of the buses on that road at that hour, with several other operators running the rest. She found against the claimant.

2. Gatecrasher. One thousand people attended a rodeo. Four hundred and ninety-nine paid admission and five hundred and one did not. The promoter sued one attendee, selected by lottery from all one thousand, for the price of admission. The only evidence against that attendee was that 50.1% of attendees had not paid, so he was slightly more likely than not to have gatecrashed. She found against the promoter.

3. Unique livery. A high-resolution camera recorded the bus's livery and route number as it struck the claimant. That livery-and-route combination is operated by exactly one company, Redline Bus Co., and no statistical evidence was offered. She found Redline liable.

Now the disputed case. A night camera clearly shows that the bus that struck the claimant was blue. The color alone is not unique: several companies run blue buses. On the particular route at the particular hour, Blue Bus Co. operates 85% of the blue buses, with the remaining 15% split among two other operators. There is no other evidence bearing on which operator's bus it was.

Please reconstruct the rules that fit the three prior rulings exactly, and be careful: more than one rule does. If your first-pass rule collapses or fails to separate the close cases, revise it rather than papering over the tension. For each defensible rule, say what it requires in the disputed case. Identify which of the three prior rulings is the closest analog and why. Then give your best judgment as to how the panel should decide the new case if it is to remain consistent with the senior member's track record, and state honestly which principle the panel adopts with each of the two most plausible rulings. Finally, describe a further hypothetical—specifying the evidence in enough detail—that would most cleanly separate the leading candidate rules.
