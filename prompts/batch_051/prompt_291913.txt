I left a demanding career about eighteen months ago, and now that I am deciding whether to take on another all-consuming commitment, I have had to make explicit what I believe about the value of effort and struggle. Before I circulate this position to a discussion group, I want a consistency audit. Here is what I find myself committed to.

1. A good life is good in virtue of how it goes for the person living it, and how it goes is ultimately a matter of felt experience: enjoyment, fulfillment, loving relationships, and the absence of avoidable suffering. Achievements, money, and standing matter only insofar as they make a difference to what a life feels like from the inside.

2. Effort is not good in itself. By effort I mean exertion against resistance, the felt strain of doing something hard. It is a cost, and it is worth incurring only when it is likely to buy something of value: skill, health, completed work, or being someone others can rely on. I reject the idea that grind is good simply because it is grind.

3. Yet I cannot bring myself to say that a life containing no real struggle would be just as good as the same life with struggle. Someone who never has to push through anything hard seems to me to be missing something important — not merely taking a more efficient route to the same destination.

4. The case that keeps returning to me is this. Picture two people who, ten years on, are in the same place: the same skill, the same standing among colleagues, the same comfortable daily life. One got there through years of grinding against real resistance; the other coasted or was carried. I find myself thinking that the first person's life contains something good — something that belongs in the accounting of how well their life went — that the second person's lacks, even if their later felt experience is indistinguishable.

5. I also hold that some goods are constituted by difficulty rather than merely produced by it. Tested trust is one: a person who has shown up for you only when it was easy is not the same object of trust as someone who has shown up when it cost them a great deal. Mastery is another: an ability installed all at once, without the long, unglamorous practice, would not be the same good. The difficulty is part of what makes these goods what they are.

6. None of this makes me hostile to ease. Rest, play, and idle time are themselves good, and I think the culture of relentless productivity is a real pathology. At the same time, I think a life built mostly around comfortable consumption would be empty.

What I need to know is whether this set hangs together. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, and which pairs only appear to conflict? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity, say in each case what would have to be given up and what the cheapest repair would cost, and then identify the single revision that would do the most to make the whole view coherent. I would rather find the cracks now than have someone in the group find them for me.
