So in my English class we were reading about this wierd thing called imaginative resistance. The basic idea is that people will happily accept all sorts of impossible stuff in a story—dragons, time travel, whatever—but if the narrator says that, like, a mother did the right thing when she killed her own baby, readers kind of refuse to make it true even inside the fiction. They don't just find it disturbing, which would be normal; they start saying the story doesn't make sense or that the author cheated. My teacher said philosophers are split on weather this is a special feature of moral concepts or just people being squeamish.

Here's what I keep wondering. Suppose that starting tomorrow, that resistance completely disappears for everyone on earth. Not that anyone becomes evil or stops caring about right and wrong in real life, just that when a novel, an ad, a movie, or a thought experiment says "in this world, X is right," people can enter that frame as easily as they imagine a world with cheap teleportation, even when X is something they would normally find monstrous. Give me your best argued prediction of how this plays out over the next twenty years. I care more about the chain of reasoning than about whether we end up in a better or worse world: what happens to fiction and art, to advertising and propaganda, and to the way philosophers use wild made-up cases to test moral theories? Which of your predictions would you actually bet on, and which ones depend on assumptions about how much imagined scenarios leak into people's real moral commitments? What would have to be true about human psychology for your whole prediction to be wrong?
