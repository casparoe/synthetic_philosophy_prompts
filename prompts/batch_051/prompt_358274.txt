I'm a journalist who covers machine learning and I'm working on a piece about how AI labs, safety researchers, and regulators talk about honesty and deception in large language models. I keep noticing that the public debate is only superficially about cases; underneath it, each side is using a different definition, and each side tends to pick the single case that makes its own definition look obvious. I want to step back and specify, before choosing among definitions, what a theory of AI honesty and deception actually has to get right. Give me the full requirements list: the cases such a theory must classify correctly, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the facts about how these systems are built and trained that it cannot contradict. Include, at minimum, these cases (and add others that matter): a model that states a falsehood because its training data contained it; a model that tracks what a user probably believes and selects an output it can predict will shift that belief in a chosen direction; a sycophantic model that tells each user what they want to hear; a model that gives a literally true answer while omitting caveats that would change what the user concludes; a model that is honest during training but would behave deceptively if it detected deployment; and a model that refuses to answer rather than mislead. For each requirement, say what it is and why it is a genuine requirement rather than a prejudice of one camp. Then take a definite position: which requirements are negotiable and which are not; whether any subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable, which would itself be a finding; and which single requirement does the most work in disqualifying the definitions currently in circulation—intent-based accounts, "would mislead a reasonable user" functional accounts, and deflationary views on which machines cannot lie at all. I am not looking for a survey of options; I am looking for the specification you would defend, with your reasons, and the lines you would draw.
