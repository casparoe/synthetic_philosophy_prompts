I am taking a seminar on social epistemology this term, and the unit on moral testimony has left me holding a set of commitments that I am now trying to turn into a coherent position for the final session. Here is the set as it currently stands. (a) Moral testimony can, in the right circumstances, give the hearer a justified true moral belief, so it is a source of moral knowledge in at least a minimal sense. (b) Moral understanding — the grasp of why a moral claim holds — cannot be transferred by testimony; it has to be built up first-personally through the hearer's own reasoning. (c) There is a genuine asymmetry between moral and non-moral testimony: there is something off about forming a moral view purely on another person's say-so that is not off about forming, say, a geographical or medical belief in the same way. (d) It is nevertheless fully rational, in some cases, to defer to a recognized moral expert on a difficult applied question — the permissibility of a particular research policy, for example — rather than working the question through oneself. (e) We are criticizable for outsourcing a moral judgment precisely when we could have thought the matter through ourselves, and blameless when we could not. (f) Knowledge and understanding are separable, in the sense that one can know that p without understanding why p.

Before I present this, I would like an audit of the whole set. Please go through the commitments and tell me which are in tension with which, which pairs only look as though they conflict, and which tensions are the severe ones rather than the easily repaired ones. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would cost. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the set hang together. I suspect some of the apparent clashes dissolve once the distinctions are drawn carefully, and that the real problem, if there is one, lies somewhere I am not seeing.
