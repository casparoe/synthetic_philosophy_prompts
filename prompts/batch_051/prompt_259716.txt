I'm trying to figure out whether everyday conventions like tipping, queueing, and jaywalking are actual moral obligations or just local stuff you go along with. My gut verdicts are a mess: tipping 20% in the U.S. because a server's wage depends on it feels required; tipping in Japan, where it isn't the custom and might confuse or offend, feels wrong to insist on; not tipping on a first U.S. visit because you genuinely didn't know feels like a mistake but not a wrong. Cutting a visible line feels wrong even when the line is badly formed and everyone's a tourist; merging late at a "lane closed" sign feels a little bad but I do it and figure everyone does. Jaywalking across an empty street at 3 a.m. feels like nothing; doing it in front of parents with small kids feels slightly wrong because it's modeling something; doing it in a city where everyone does it feels fine, but in a city where it's harshly fined I'd stop even though I can't tell if that's morality or just fear of the fine. Is there a single principle that captures all of that, or near enough, and which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? And what would it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a visitor who learns that the local tipping norm is one she thinks is exploitative (it lets restaurants pay servers below minimum wage) — should she follow the local norm or refuse? Finally, tell me how confident you are in the principle you land on and which point you're least sure about.
