I've been doing some tutoring for a few years — mostly middle-school math and science, plus some friends' kids — and I've been noticing that wrong answers aren't all the same kind of thing. I have strong gut feelings about which errors are just slips or guesses and which ones show that the kid basically has a whole alternative picture they're reasoning from, but I can't say what the line is. Here are some cases I keep going back to.

- A sixth grader insists heavier objects fall faster. I dropped a flat sheet and a crumpled ball of paper and she said the paper is slower because it's lighter. When I showed her a video of a feather and a hammer dropped in a vacuum, she said the video was probably faked. This one feels like a genuine alternative theory.
- A five-year-old says "I goed to the park." This doesn't feel like a theory at all; if anything it's smart — the kid over-applied a rule that usually works.
- A college student adds 1/2 + 1/3 and writes 2/5. When I ask what 1/2 + 1/2 is, they happily say 2/4, which is 1/2, and they don't see anything odd about adding halves and getting a half. This feels theory-like, because they keep making the same move and it all hangs togehter for them.
- A seven-year-old says the moon is following the car. Not a theory; it's more a trick of perception than a belief.
- A biology student tells me plants get most of their mass from soil and water. When I point out that the soil level in a pot barely changes and water has no carbon in it, they still don't buy that the mass is coming from the air. This feels theory-like too.
- A kid calls a whale a fish because it lives in water. Not a theory; he's just using the word with a different category boundary.

The pair that really bugs me is the five-year-old's "goed" and a different math student who says division always makes numbers smaller. When that student sees 4 ÷ 0.5 = 8, he says dividing by less than one is basically multiplying, so it isn't really division. I want to call his division rule a misconception, but I don't want to call "goed" one. Yet both are over-extending a rule that worked in all the cases they'd met. What's the difference supposed to be?

My working hypothesis is that a genuine alternative theory has to be something like a coherent causal story that ties several cases together, while an over-applied rule is just a bug in an otherwise fine machine. But the division case already breaks that, since it looks like a single rule and I still want to call it a misconception. Can you extract a principle that captures which errors count as real "alternative theories" and which are just slips, word choices, or harmless overgeneralizations? I'd rather have one clean principle that forces me to revise a verdict or two than a long list of conditions that saves all of them. And what would the principle say about a student who announces that a fraction of a fraction is always smaller than both of them? Also, where do I have to give something up?
