A pacifist denomination has in its membership covenant the following rule: "No member shall manufacture, sell, repair, or knowingly supply any instrument whose primary purpose is the taking of human life, nor knowingly assist another in doing so." The authors of the rule were thinking of weapons and battlefield equipment. A member of the denomination is a machine-learning engineer who maintains a widely used open-source object-tracking library, released under a permissive license. She started the project for civilian uses, such as wildlife monitoring and assistive robotics. She later learns, from a journalistic investigation, that a defense contractor has incorporated the library into the target-lock system of a loitering munition, where it helps keep the weapon fixed on a human target as it descends. The contractor downloaded the code on its own, pays her nothing, and has never contacted her. She is now deciding whether to keep merging bugfixes and performance improvements to the tracker.

Does the rule, read in its own terms, make her continued work a case of knowingly supplying an instrument whose primary purpose is the taking of human life? Or does the generality of the code block that description? I would like the answer to compare at least three readings: a literal reading of the words, a purposive reading of what the covenant's authors were trying to prevent, and a reasonable-member reading of what someone in the community would understand themselves to be committed to. Where those readings diverge, say which should govern and why, and whether the rule can be restated so that it handles dual-use software without absurdly forbidding every general-purpose tool.
