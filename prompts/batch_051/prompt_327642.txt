For an epistemology handout for second-year undergraduates, write a decision tree that sorts a reader into one of six named positions on the sceptical challenge from Descartes to Hume. The organising angle is the problem of the criterion: the difficulty that any proposed standard of knowledge must be justified either by another standard, by its own verdicts, or by particular examples of knowledge that the standard itself is supposed to validate. But the tree should proceed through two concrete episodes rather than through labels the student may already know: (i) Descartes's evil-demon thought experiment; and (ii) Hume's observation that sceptical arguments lose much of their force as soon as one leaves the study for backgammon or ordinary conversation. Summarise each episode briefly in your own words so the handout is self-contained.

Each branch node should be a forced-choice question that a student can answer without knowing which position it leads to, and without already holding the label. The questions should ask what the respondent finds unacceptable, indispensable, or too revisionary in a proposed response, not whether they would call themselves a foundationalist or a sceptic. Include at least one node on whether the evil-demon scenario shows that no belief about the external world can be known with certainty; at least one node on how, if at all, a criterion of knowledge could be justified without circularity; and at least one node on whether a theoretical conclusion in favour of suspending judgement could govern, or be expected to govern, the ordinary confidence of common life.

Build the tree from logical space first: list the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they jointly define, locate the six positions among those combinations, and note any combinations that are unoccupied, with a one-sentence diagnosis of why each is unoccupied (incoherent, merely unattractive, or historically unexplored). The six leaves are: (1) Cartesian foundationalism, the view that the cogito is an indubitable first truth and that a non-deceiving God underwrites the reliability of clear and distinct perception; (2) methodism as a general response to the problem of the criterion, the view that the question what a person knows cannot be addressed until a criterion of knowledge has been settled; (3) particularism, the view that particular judgements such as the judgement that one has hands are better known than any proposed criterion that would cast doubt on them; (4) Pyrrhonian scepticism, the view that for every argument there is an equal opposing argument, with the result that judgement should be suspended across the board; (5) Humean naturalism, the view that sceptical reflection exposes reason's inability to justify our fundamental beliefs while custom and instinct cause those beliefs all the same; and (6) Humean mitigated scepticism, the practical stance that concedes the failure of rational justification but responds with methodological caution, probabilistic reasoning, and confinement of enquiry to common life.

For each leaf give the name and a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment. Where two leaves cannot be cleanly separated by your tree, say so explicitly and identify the further question that would separate them. Aim for no more than eight branch nodes, and present the final tree as a branching list in which every branch is phrased as a question to the reader. Do not append a bibliography or further reading; the diagnostic instrument itself is the deliverable.
