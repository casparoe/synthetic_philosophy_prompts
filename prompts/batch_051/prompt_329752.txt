I teach philosophy, and I've been keeping a file on a friend of mine, Dana, who teaches evidence over at the law school and sits as a part-time magistrate in small civil cases. She's got a real committment about when statistical evidence can carry a case, and I'm trying to get it into a clean rule before I write something up. All the cases below are civil, preponderance of the evidence.

Her track record so far:

1. Blue bus. A car is forced off the road by a bus; the driver only knows it was a bus. Eighty percent of the buses that run on that street at that hour belong to Blue Bus Co. Nothing else. Dana finds for the defendant.
2. Gatecrasher. A rodeo operator can prove a thousand people entered the grounds and nine hundred ninety-nine paid, but a computer glitch ate the list of who paid. They sue one attendee, picked at random, for the ticket price. Dana finds for the defendant.
3. Courier depot. A courier company's regional depot loses 60% of the packages that pass through it, according to the company's own tracking. The plaintiff's package went through that depot and disappeared. No other evidence. Dana finds for the defendant.
4. DNA. In a hit and run, the victim scratched the driver; skin under her fingernails gives a single-source DNA profile. It matches the defendant, with a random match probability of one in five million. No alibi, no other evidence. Dana finds for the plaintiff.
5. Attendant eyewitness. A delivery van clips a parked car. The only evidence is a parking attendant who says it was the defendant's van; under similar lighting and distance, this attendant's identifications are right 75% of the time. Dana finds for the plaintiff.

The new case: a jewelry store camera caught a burglar from behind, and the only distinctive thing is his gait. A gait analyst says the video shows the defendant's walk. This analyst's positive identifications are correct 51% of the time and wrong 49% of the time in blind validation studies, and there's no way in a given case to tell which kind you're looking at. Nothing else ties the defendant to the burglary.

I want to predict what she'd say and, more importantly, extract the rule she's running, because I can see at least two ways to come down. If you think her five rulings already force one answer, say so; if they underdetermine it, tell me what each choice would commit her to.

Here's the form I want it in. First, a table with these columns: candidate_rule (a short name plus a one-sentence statement), fit_with_her_past_cases (how it explains the five rulings above), and prediction_for_the_new_case (liable or not, with a clause). Then after the table, a JSON object with fields: best_guess, closest_past_case, principle_if_she_says_yes, principle_if_she_says_no, and further_case (a one-line description of a case you'd run by her next to settle which rule she's really applying).
