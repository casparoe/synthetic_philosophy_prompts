Consider the letter-string analogy microdomain made famous by the Copycat project. In the source analogy abc → abd, the change is naturally read as "replace the final letter by its alphabetic successor," and a target such as ijk → ijl is handled unproblematically. But change the target so that it contains runs of identical letters, such as mrrjjj, and the mapping becomes underdetermined. "Final letter" can be read as the final token, so that only the last j becomes k; or as the final letter-type, so that the whole final run jjj becomes kkk. The scope of "rightmost" and "successor" can shift in parallel ways, depending on what the target string makes salient. The Copycat tradition treats this underdetermination as evidence for fluid concepts: conceptual structures such as letter, group, successor, and rightmost are not fixed slots but patterns that slip and re-align under pressure from the target. The claim to be assessed is that this fluid, context-sensitive conceptual slippage is the correct account of human analogy-making in such microdomains, and that no fixed-rule or context-free mechanism captures the phenomena equally well.
