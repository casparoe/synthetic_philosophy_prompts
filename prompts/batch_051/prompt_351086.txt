I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been running a reading group at my company on the ethics of belief. We read W. K. Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief" (1877) and William James's "The Will to Believe" (1896), and the group split into two camps that have now been arguing past each other for months. I want you to write the document they should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration: a joint statement that both camps could actually sign, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than papered over.

The Clifford camp holds, roughly, that belief is never a purely private matter, that we have a standing duty to proportion belief to evidence, and that failing this duty is itself a wrongdoing. The James camp holds, roughly, that in options which are living, forced, and momentous and which the intellect cannot by itself settle, our passional nature may legitimately decide; it says it is denying the universality of Clifford's rule, not the value of evidence. The Clifford camp offers the following passage as the canonical statement of its demand, and I want the entire document organized around interpreting, testing, and applying it:

"The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him."

The document should have these parts.

1. Interpretation. State, in language both camps accept, exactly what the passage does and does not assert: whether it is a claim about moral blameworthiness, epistemic rationality, or both; whether it concerns the process by which belief is formed or the content of the belief; and whether it is meant to govern all beliefs or only beliefs that bear on action. Both camps should be able to sign this section without foot-dragging.

2. Agreed claims. A numbered section of substantive claims both camps can sign without hedging. Avoid making these so abstract that they say nothing; they should cover the social consequences of belief, the reality of motivated reasoning, the difference between inquiry and belief, and any other ground the two positions genuinely share.

3. Disagreements. A table with one row per genuine disagreement: the question in neutral wording; the strongest formulation each side would accept for its own position; and the strongest formulation of the other side's position that it can state without caricature. Where a disagreement is really only about wording, record it as resolved and give the shared statement.

4. Cruxes. For each crux — a sub-question such that a settled answer would move at least one camp — give the sub-question, a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Apply the quoted passage to the shipowner case and to at least one case the James camp would put forward as a difficult test of the Clifford policy. I suspect one crux will concern whether the origin of a belief is what matters even when the option is of the living, forced, momentous kind, and another will concern whether the line between options the intellect can settle and options it cannot is itself principled, but do not force these if the texts support better cruxes.

Please keep the document focused on the two essays themselves; I do not need a bibliography or external references.
