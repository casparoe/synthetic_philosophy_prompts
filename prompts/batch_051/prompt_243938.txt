You are an expert in decision theory and the philosophy of rational choice. Before answering, work through each case below in your own words, identifying its causal and evidential structure, and be explicit about which points you are assuming rather than establishing.

Here are four decision problems and my considered verdicts. I am aware that (1) and (3) seem to pull in opposite directions, and that (1) and (4) may look inconsistent. I would like a single principle of rational choice that captures all of them, or a principled account of which of my verdicts to give up. State the principle precisely, identify what each case contributes to motivating it, and say what the principle predicts about a further case.

1. A supremely reliable predictor—right in nearly every case—presents me with a transparent box containing $1,000 and an opaque box containing either $1,000,000 or nothing. She has already placed the million in the opaque box just in case she predicted that I would take only the opaque box. I may take only the opaque box or both boxes. My verdict: take only the opaque box.

2. A penalty version of Death in Damascus. Tomorrow I must be in one of two equally convenient cities. An enemy who is an infallible predictor of deliberate choices, but who cannot predict the outcome of a genuinely chancy device, has already gone to the city where he predicted I would settle. If I arrive in that city, he will harm me; otherwise I am safe. My verdict: rather than settle on either city by deliberation, I should toss a fair coin just before leaving and follow its result.

3. A gene causes both a mild desire to smoke and a certain disease, and the smoking itself has no causal effect on the disease; the correlation runs entirely through the gene. I know I carry the gene, smoking would give me genuine pleasure, and the smoke itself carries no further medical risk. My verdict: smoke.

4. The same as (1), except that I can see whether the million is in the opaque box before I choose. In the case where I see that it is there, my verdict: take both boxes.

What principle of rational choice, if any, captures all four verdicts without being merely gerrymandered to fit them? If one exists, state it precisely and identify the work each case does in motivating it. If none exists, say which of my verdicts I should revise first and why, and state the alternative principle. Finally, construct a fifth decision problem of the same general kind that I have not listed, say what your preferred principle predicts for it, and note whether that prediction seems independently plausible.
