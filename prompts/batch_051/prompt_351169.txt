I'm on the advisory board of a private foundation that funds basic science, and in three weeks we vote on whether to renew a 12-year grant to a research programme in cognitive neuroscience. I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the early LessWrong days, so I keep trying to run this as a Lakatos question, but I'm going in circles and need a second brain.

The programme's hard core is the claim that the brain is fundamentally a prediction-error minimizer and that perception, action, and learning are all approximate Bayesian inference. The protective belt is where they absorb anomalies: precision weighting, hierarchical message passing, specific proposals about dopamine and attention, that sort of thing. In the first six years the programme made a few genuinely surprising novel predictions that panned out, and it unified a bunch of scattered findings. In the last three years, though, the successes have mostly been after the fact: a result shows up, and within a month there's a new auxiliary tweak that explains it. Two of their headline recent predictions turned out to be retrodictions, and one failed replication was handled by adding a new precision-weighting parameter that looks suspiciously like an epicycle. The teams response is that this is exactly how a progressive programme protects it's hard core during a rough patch, and that killing it now would be the classic mistake of abandoning a programme right before it turns around. Half the board agrees with them; the other half thinks we're watching a degenerating problemshift (mostly post-hoc explanation rather than novel prediction) in real time and wants to cut funding.

Please draft the short decision memo I'll circulate before the vote. You're an expert on Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes who also writes clear, actionable institutional memos. Start by brainstorming the options (renew, cut, renew with conditions), steelman the strongest version of each side, then commit to a concrete recommendation. The memo should have an actionable bottom line and should engage honestly with the strongest consideration on the losing side. If you recommend conditions, make them specific enough that the researchers could actually tell whether they're meeting them.
