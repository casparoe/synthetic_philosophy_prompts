Suppose that a new drug (call it the veil) comes into wide use tomorrow. It is cheap, safe, non-addictive, and has no side effects beyond the intended one. A single dose suppresses the felt component of sadness and grief for exactly seven days: the bereaved person remains fully functional, can recall the loss in detail, can judge that they are grieving, and can still feel anger, fear, shame, and love; but the heavy, painful, aching quality of grief is absent. When the dose wears off, the grief returns at what appears to be its original intensity, as though it had been held in storage, and the spent week neither accelerates nor reduces the overall course of mourning. Predict, with explicit reasoning, how mourning practices, psychotherapeutic work, and social norms around grief and loss change over the next two generations. Distinguish three kinds of effect: those that follow fairly directly from the drug's mechanism (delay without processing), those that follow from changed incentives, and those that depend on cultural contingencies and could easily have gone otherwise. For each link in your reasoning, say whether it is robust or speculative. Conclude by judging whether the veil makes the world better or worse, on the assumption—which you may reject—that grief has some value as well as a cost.
