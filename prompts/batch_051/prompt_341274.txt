The fairness-based case for taxing inherited wealth runs roughly as follows. Large estates passed from parents to children give some adults advantages they did nothing to earn; taxing such transfers—bequests and substantial gifts—is a permissible way to keep competitive starting positions fair. Suppose we accept an empirical picture in which most of the advantage that affluent parents transmit to their children is conferred not through transfers of financial assets but through earlier parental investment: the postcode that determines schooling, private tuition, informal networks, and the habits and dispositions cultivated in childhood; actual inheritance, on this picture, explains only a modest share of intergenerational persistence. Does the equal-opportunity argument for inheritance taxation survive this picture? Say which premise of the argument the picture bears on, whether a weaker version can still support estate taxation, and whether that weaker version can avoid committing its proponent to much broader intervention in child-rearing. If it cannot, say what that shows about the original argument.
