So there's this condition I read about, pain asymbolia. People with it can still feel pain — they can point to exactly where a pin is jabbing them, tell you its sharp and its in the top of their foot or whatever, maybe give it a 7 out of 10 — but they're not bothered. They don't pull away, they don't want it to stop, they can chat through the whole thing. And that sent me down a rabbit hole about whether pain is really bad in itself. I keep coming back to this argument:

(1) If a sensation is bad in itself, then when someone has that exact sensation, they're in a bad state just by having it.
(2) Pain asymbolics have the exact same pain sensation as the rest of us — same location, same intensity, same sharp or burning feel — but they aren't in a bad state. They weren't just being tough: they honestly don't mind.
(3) So the pain sensation isn't bad in itself.
(4) The only difference between them and us must be some extra reaction — the unpleasantness, the wanting it to stop, the inner alarm.
(5) So what makes pain bad is that extra reaction, never the raw sensation. The same pin in your foot could be bad for you but not for them, and the difference has nothing to do with the pain itself.

I keep feeling like (2) or (4) is doing more work than it should, but I can't say exactly where it goes wrong. Go through it step by step: is the argument valid? If not, which step is the weak one, and does the conclusion survive in any form?
