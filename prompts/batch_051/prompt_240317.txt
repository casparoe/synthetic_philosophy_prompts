Six weeks ago I agreed to give the keynote address at Ellersdale's sesquicentennial, and since then I have made things considerably harder for myself. Ellersdale was incorporated in 1874, and the official founding story—the one on the plaque outside the courthouse and in the schools—turns on three fixed points: a charter signed by the first selectmen, a flag sewn by the women of the first winter, and a Founder's Day procession the town has supposedly kept up in unbroken line since 1875. After accepting the invitation I began reading Hobsbawm and Anderson, and then I checked the local historical society's archive. It now seems clear that the flag was almost certainly made for the 1910 reunion, that the procession in anything like its present form dates only from around 1925, and that the founding date of 1874 was less the birth of a community than a consolidation of three older settlements whose boosters wanted them narrated as a single settlement from the start. I find I cannot stand at the podium and simply recite the plaque version of our history; but I also do not want to spend the town's anniversary scolding my neighbors or implying that, because the founding story was assembled later, there is nothing real here to honor.

I would like you to write the address itself. It should run about eight minutes. The audience will be mixed: the mayor and council, local schoolchildren, families whose surnames appear on the founding plaque—the mayor is a direct descendant of the first signatory—and, because Ellersdale has a college, several historians who specialize in the region and will notice any fudged claim or evasive phrasing. The speech must be an anniversary address, not a historiography lecture, and it should be warm enough that the families receive it as commemoration rather than correction. But every sentence must be one I can deliver in good conscience after reading Hobsbawm and Anderson. Please let the ideas of invented tradition and imagined community do the structural work: find a way to honor a community whose continuity is real in practice even though its charter, flag, and festival are later inventions, rather than resting on claims about 1874 that I can no longer make.
