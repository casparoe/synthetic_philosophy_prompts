After two years of running a combined philosophy and cognitive-neuroscience reading group on split-brain patients and the unity of consciousness, I have two members who have settled into opposed camps and agreed to an adversarial collaboration. The first camp — call it the two-streams view — holds that under at least some experimental conditions a fully commissurotomized patient hosts two distinct centers or streams of consciousness, one per hemisphere, each with its own experiences and intentions, so that the speaking left hemisphere’s denials and confabulations are one subject’s failure to access the goings-on of another. The second camp — the single-subject view — denies that any second subject appears; it holds that there remains one subject whose conscious contents can be partially disunified or unequally available for report, and that the right hemisphere’s above-chance responding and affect are better explained by states that are either not fully conscious or are conscious in that subject but not reportable through speech. Both sides accept the broad experimental picture — lateralized stimulus presentation, hemispheric disconnection, the left hemisphere’s verbal confabulation about actions the right hemisphere initiated, and the right hemisphere’s accurate forced-choice performance — and they disagree sharply about what that picture licenses.

Write the document their good-faith adversarial collaboration should have produced, using the following structure. First, a numbered list of claims both camps can sign: the agreed empirical facts, methodological constraints on what can be inferred about consciousness from task performance, formulations of shared terms both endorse, and the views both regard as refuted or out of scope. Second, a disagreements table: for each genuine point of divergence, a statement of each side’s position in wording that side would accept as its own rather than a caricature, plus an agreed description of what would count as movement by at least one side. Third, a crux list: the sub-questions stated precisely enough that a determinate result — an experiment, a lesion or imaging finding, a clinical observation, or a conceptual argument — would settle them, where settling each crux would move at least one camp; for each crux, specify in advance the kind of case or evidence both sides accept as bearing on it, and record each side’s prediction of what it would show. If any central claim resists translation into terms the other side accepts even as a statement of disagreement, say so explicitly and explain whether the two camps are nevertheless talking about the same target. Finally, state whether any portion of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive — for example, a difference in how liberally to apply the word subject or the word conscious to hemisphere-driven behavior — and, if so, give the joint restatement of what the non-verbal residue would be.

Keep the document self-contained and do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the participants know the literature, and you may refer to positions and arguments informally by description.
