For an essay I am writing for my high school's philosophy club, I have been reading about consciousness and the self, and I have provisionally come to accept something like Thomas Metzinger's self-model theory of subjectivity. On this view, what I call my self is not a thing, a soul, or a substance, but the content of a continuously updated self-model that the brain constructs in order to represent the whole organism to itself. The part of the theory that most interests me is the claim that this self-model is phenomenally transparent: I experience only what the model represents, and never the model as a model, in roughly the way I look through a window and see the garden rather than the glass. I find the position convincing because it explains at once why the self feels so immediate and undeniable and why it can be experimentally distorted, duplicated, or made to drift outside the body.

What I am now worried about is that I may have adopted a theory that could not be shown to be false, and I do not know whether that worry is justified. So I would like to know: what would count against the claim that the self is the content of a transparent self-model? I am not asking for the familiar complaint that the theory denies the self or conflicts with how things feel, since the theory seems to predict that things feel exactly this way. I am asking what findings, arguments, or conceptual results would force a committed defender of the view to revise or abandon it, and which objections they could reasonably dismiss as not touching the core claim. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and then say honestly whether the theory is genuinely exposed to evidence, or whether it is so well insulated that no result could touch it—and, if it is insulated, whether that is a feature of the view or a serious problem with it.
