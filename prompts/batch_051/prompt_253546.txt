A couple of us in my department were talking through Origen's account of Scripture, specifically the part in De Principiis Book IV where he says the divine Logos arranged the text so that certain "stumbling-blocks," impossibilities, and apparent absurdities would be woven into the narrative. The point, as I understand him, is that these features are placed there intentionally to interrupt a merely surface-level reading and force the reader toward a spiritual or allegorical sense. My friend Aya, who works on early Christian hermeneutics, said this gives a principled reason to refuse the literal sense in specific cases: a passage that would otherwise be unworthy of God or historically incoherent is not an embarrassment but a signal. Daniel, who does more modern Protestant thought, pushed back. It went roughly like this.

Daniel said: fine, but Origen's criterion is really just a negative one. He never gives an independent rule for separating the stumbling-blocks from the passages meant to be read as history. Aya replied that the criterion is not supposed to be independent of the reader's formation: what trips up a beginner and what trips up a mature reader can differ. Daniel said: then the procedure has no teeth. A fundamentalist can just say "this apparent absurdity doesn't trip me up; I submit to it as mystery," and Origen can't say anything except that the fundamentalist is insufficiently formed. Aya said that's not a bug; the pedagogy is the criterion. Origen wants the reader to grow into a capacity to see the spiritual sense, not to apply a mechanical test. Daniel said then you've conceded the key point: the literal sense is never ruled out by the text itself, only by the reader's sense of what God would or wouldn't do, which is exactly the move Origen's critics call arbitrary. Aya said no, the point is that for Origen the spiritual sense is revealed through the literal, not behind it, so the reader's formation is part of the argument, not an escape hatch. Daniel ended by saying that if formation is doing that much work, Origen's position collapses into a tradition-dependent reading community, which is fine as sociology but not as an account of the text's meaning.

I want to adjudicate this properly, because they both made decent points and I kept going back and forth. Go move by move: which claims are right, which are wrong, and where do they each overreach? What is Origen's view really committed to, and what would he need to add to answer Daniel's objection without giving up Aya's point about spiritual maturation?
