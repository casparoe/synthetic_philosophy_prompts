Last night a friend and I had a long argument about where normal grief ends and major depressive disorder begins. I kept coming back to the way the DSM-5 sets up mental disorder in general: it describes a mental disorder as a syndrome marked by a clinically significant disturbance in cognition, emotion regulation, or behavior, a disturbance that reflects a dysfunction in the psychological, biological, or developmental processes underlying mental functioning, and that is usually tied to significant distress or disability. Here is what bothers me. The old bereavement exclusion is gone, so a person whose bereavement has looked depression-like for only a couple of weeks can, in principle, receive a major depressive disorder diagnosis. But if the disturbed mood is the normal operation of grief after a loss, how is it at the same time a dysfunction? That made me wonder whether the DSM’s diagnostic practice is smuggling in a value judgment under the word dysfunction. I want to know what theory of mental disorder this practice actually presupposes. Is it naturalist in the strong sense, treating disorder as a purely factual failure of biological or psychological function? Is it normativist, treating disorder as whatever a society judges to be bad enough to count as illness? Or is it best read as a hybrid such as the harmful dysfunction account? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the DSM’s definition-plus-bereavement practice, say what the practice strictly requires as opposed to what it merely fits with, and tell me what would have to change in the bereavement rule if the presupposed view were replaced by a purely normativist theory.
