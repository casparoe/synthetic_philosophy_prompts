I am a high school student on a robotics team, and we are building the decision-making module for a simulated search-and-rescue robot. Our current module chooses each action by multiplying, for every potential victim on its map, the probability that the person really exists by the probability that the robot could reach and save them if they do exist, summing these products, and then taking the route with the largest total.

During a test run, the robot had enough battery to reach only one of two sites. At one site the map indicated a single victim with near-certain confidence and the robot was certain to arrive in time. At the other site the map indicated three possible victims, each with only moderate confidence, and the robot had a small chance of reaching the site too late to help. The module sent the robot to the second site because the sum was larger. Some teammates argued that this is wrong: a person at the first site would be abandoned despite being almost certainly real, while the three signals could easily all be false. They also argue that the code is treating uncertainty about its own sensors as if people came in fractions, when a 45 percent chance of a victim is not 0.45 of a victim, since each person is either there or not. Others reply that the code is simply following the probabilities the map provides and that ignoring the chance to help three people would be an arbitrary bias in favor of certainty.

We tried two fixes. First, we added a rule that the robot must always go to any site containing a victim whose probability of being present is above a cutoff, but the choice of cutoff felt arbitrary, with different thresholds sending the robot different ways for no reason any of us could explain. Second, a teammate suggested that instead of adding up expected survivors, the robot should choose the action that gives the worst-off potential victim the best chance of being saved. We could not decide whether this solved the problem or simply replaced one questionable arithmetic with another.

The competition will test the robot on scenarios we have not seen in advance, and the judges expect us to explain the principle behind its choices, not just report the outputs. We are therefore not looking for a one-line tie-breaker for this single case. What should the decision rule actually be? Should we keep maximizing the expected number of survivors, should we refuse to abandon a near-certain victim no matter how many less certain victims could be helped, or should we adopt something in between? And is there a principled way to determine how much uncertainty in the map should matter when it conflicts with the number of people who might be affected?
