I'm a software engineer on an alignment team, and next month I'm presenting to our team's weekly safety reading group. I don't have a formal philosophy background; my case comes from the engineering side, and I expect the sharpest pushback to land wherever my concepts are least precisely defined. The talk argues that the debate approach to scalable oversight has a structural weakness that obfuscated arguments exploit, and that the standard response—train the judge on harder cases—doesn't close the gap.

The thesis I'm defending is roughly this. Debate is meant to transfer the epistemic advantage of superhuman debaters to a human judge by exploiting an asymmetry: the judge doesn't have to generate the correct answer, only to recognize which side exposes flaws in the other. An obfuscated argument is one whose decisive flaw is real but hidden inside material the judge cannot evaluate—too much length, too many nested sub-arguments, or locally plausible steps where the actual error sits in the inferential connection between them. If a debater can choose the vocabulary, branching structure, and level of abstraction of its argument, then with enough capability it can, for any fixed human judge and any fixed time budget, produce a defense of a false conclusion that the judge will accept—not because the argument is sound, but because the expected time for the judge to reach the flaw exceeds the time the debate allows. The judge's verdict then tracks what fits a bounded evaluator's attention, not what is true.

My six-step argument, as I intend to present it, is this. First, debate's guarantee is conditional: if an answer is flawed, an honest debater can expose the flaw to a judge who can follow the exposure. That is not the same as claiming the judge can always tell which debater is being honest. Second, obfuscation attacks the condition. A dishonest debater can restructure the flawed inference so that every sub-step the judge actually checks looks valid, while the invalid step sits in a branch the judge accepts on trust or skips as routine. Third, from the judge's side this is a search problem with an adversarially chosen haystack. The judge must allocate finite attention, and the debater who constructs the argument has every incentive to put the flaw where the judge is least likely to look. Fourth, fine-tuning the judge on cases where it was fooled doesn't solve this. It trains the judge against the previous round's obfuscations while giving the obfuscating debater a gradient toward the next ones; the training distribution is generated by the same adversary the training is trying to defeat. I think the debater wins in the tail of this arms race, though I'm not sure how to make that rigorous. Fifth, the debate protocol by itself therefore doesn't reliably transfer truth to the judge; at best it transfers the debaters' superior ability to compose locally acceptable but globally misleading arguments. Sixth, I'll close with a proposal: require debates to be locally verifiable—decomposed into short, independently checkable steps with no unverifiable cross-references—and penalize depth and branching. But I'll concede immediately that this imposes a legibility constraint that may exclude sound arguments which can't be decomposed that way, so the fix may cost exactly what debate promised to preserve.

The audience is about fifteen people, but the real challenges will come from a few directions. Two ML engineers on the team implemented a reproduction of the standard debate training loop—two models argue, a judge model is fine-tuned on human decisions, then debates are sampled to find judge failures. They'll say obfuscation is just a data-collection problem: the judge failed because it hadn't trained on enough long, messy, adversarial cases, and training on those failures is precisely how the gap closes. A theory-oriented colleague does formal alignment work; she'll accept my examples but want a formal claim—she'll ask whether my argument shows anything beyond the familiar point that boundedly rational judges can be fooled, and whether I have a worst-case bound or only an intuition pump. One teammate with a philosophy background will press the opposite flank: if peer review, courts, and every other fallible epistemic institution can also be defeated by clever adversaries, then showing that debate is fallible doesn't show it's worse than the alternatives, and I owe a comparison. The rest are generalists who will want concrete examples of obfuscation and plain definitions of my terms.

I want a question-period prep sheet, not a revision of the talk. For each objection: who it is likely to come from, how they would phrase it themselves given the assumptions they're bringing, how serious it is, what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, and a reply I can fit into two or three sentences. Please order the objections so that the most likely and most damaging come first, and give me at least one objection from each of the three audience types above. Then tell me the objection I should be most afraid of—the one that, if anyone thinks to ask it, most cleanly cuts through my argument—and how I should answer it even though my position is weakest exactly there.
