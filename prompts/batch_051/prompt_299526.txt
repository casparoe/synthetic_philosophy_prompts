My philosophy teacher has spent this term on scientific change, and she tends to work through examples. She has said that the discovery of Neptune was a model of rational science: a mature theory's predictions were tested against observation, an anomaly in Uranus's orbit was resolved by finding a new planet, and no conceptual upheaval was involved. She has also said that the geologists who rejected Wegener's continental drift in the 1910s and 1920s were being reasonable rather than dogmatic, since his proposed mechanism was physically implausible and independent evidence was scarce. And she has said that the Michelson–Morley null result did not by itself refute the ether, because physicists could reasonably add auxiliary assumptions such as length contraction to preserve the theory, at least provisionally. Next week she will give her verdict on the replacement of Newtonian gravity by Einstein's general relativity: the case presents the unexplained perihelion shift of Mercury, the apparent bending of starlight observed during the 1919 eclipse, and a deep change in how space, time, and gravity are understood. Predict what she will say. Lay out at least two accounts of scientific rationality that fit her first three verdicts — something like a Popperian stress on bold conjecture and critical testing, and a Kuhnian distinction between routine normal science and revolutionary change — explain what each predicts about the relativity case, say which prediction you favor and why, and name one further historical case that would most cleanly separate the two.
