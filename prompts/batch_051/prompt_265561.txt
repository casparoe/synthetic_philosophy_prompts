I spent my career in X-ray crystallography and only got into philosophy after retiring, so apologies if I mangle the terminology. The standard argument for signing up for cryonics is, as I understand it, an expected-value bet: the chance of revival is small but not zero, the value of getting an extra stretch of life is enormous, and the premiums are modest enough that the bet looks favourable. I've heard that version a thousand times and it always seemed coherent enough.

What's been bothering me is that the story of what revives you has changed under everyone's feet. When I first looked into this in the 90s, the promise was biological repair: thaw the tissue, mend the damage, wake up the same biological person. The revival route people now take most seriously, though, is destructive whole-brain scanning — slice the frozen brain, image every connection, then run the resulting mind as an emulation. The catch is that the original brain is destroyed by the scan, and the people pushing this tend to shrug at that part.

So my question is whether the standard expected-value arguement survives that change. If "revival" means destroying the thing I was trying to save and constructing something new with my memories and behaviour, has the original bet quietly smuggled in a claim about what makes the outcome me rather than a copy? I'd like you to say which premise is actually under pressure — the probability, the value of extra life, or the identity of the thing that wakes up — and whether there's a repaired version of the argument that still justifies signing up but only says something weaker. Please don't just say "it depends on your theory of personal identity." Tell me whether the destructive-scan story turns the bet into a bet on my survival at all, and what the argument has to assume for the expected value to be doing any real work.
