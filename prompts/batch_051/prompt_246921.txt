Ok so we've been doing interperonal comparisons of utility in my well-being and public policy class, and I'm trying to sort out what I actually think by going with my gut on cases.

- I have no problem saying someone with a toothache is better off than someone who just lost their mother.
- I'm also fine saying a $50 parking fine hurts a broke student more than it hurts a millionaire. I'd even say, for policy purposes, it hurts the student something like ten times as much.
- But ask me whether the grief is twenty times the toothache or fifty times, and I balk. I want to say its just much worse, not a precise multiple.
- With a migraine and a panic attack, both self-described 10/10s, I can say both are worse than a stubbed toe. But if someone insists the panic attack is worse than the migraine, I hear myself saying "worse for who?" — like I've stopped comparing the experiences and started comparing how the two people handle them.
- I don't think it makes sense to say one person's life is 1.7 times better than another person's, period. But I do think it makes sense to say that a change would improve one person's life more than it would improve another's.
- Still, I don't want to be a skeptic. Choosing to build a wheelchair ramp instead of a public library leans on the thought that the ramp does more good for its users than the library would for its users, and I think that reason is legitimate, not just a preference pretending to be a fact.

Is there a principle that captures when I'm willing to make an interpersonal comparison and when I'm not, or close? What does that principle force me to give up in the list above — especially the "ten times" for money but no multiple for grief? And what does it predict about the case I keep flip-flopping on: comparing one person's chronic depression to another's chronic back pain?
