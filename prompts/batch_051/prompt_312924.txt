I am writing a novel whose central conceit is a character trained as a court-appointed truth auditor: she perceives a distinct sensory signature when a sentence fails to be either true or false. I want the episodes in which this occurs to stand up to philosophical scrutiny, because the book's world is meant to contain genuine truth-value gaps, not merely situations where the characters cannot tell what the truth is. To that end, I have drafted six episodes, each built around a sentence I propose to present as neither true nor false:

1. A future contingent: early in the novel, a seer declares that the exile will return before the river freezes, at a moment when, in the novel's metaphysics, the future is not yet fixed.

2. A vague border case: the narrator describes a town that straddles the boundary between regions by asserting that Sere is in the highlands, where Sere is neither clearly highland nor clearly lowland, and the story treats the question as having no sharp answer.

3. A failed presupposition: an accuser states that the steward has stopped stealing from the granary, though the steward never stole from it in the first place.

4. An empty definite description: a town crier announces that the thirteenth magistrate of Sere will preside at dawn, but Sere has had exactly twelve magistrates and has no thirteenth.

5. A category mistake: a book of divinations contains the sentence that the number of the harvest moon is blue, crossing a boundary the world's language treats as absolute.

6. A self-referential ledger entry: the truth auditor is asked to rule on a document whose final line says that this very entry is not true, where the phrase refers to the whole entry containing it.

Please rank these six episodes into three tiers: those you would defend as genuine truth-value gaps, those defensible only under a specific account of the underlying phenomenon that you should name and briefly justify, and those better treated as something else, such as false, true but unknown, undefined, or context-sensitive. Within each tier, order the cases from most to least defensible. Then state, concisely, the test you are using to separate absent truth values from hidden ones, and apply it consistently across the six cases. I am less interested in doctrinal labels than in the criterion itself: what makes a sentence fail to have a truth value at all, as opposed to having a truth value that no one is positioned to determine? I want a committed assessment, with each episode placed definitively and defended, rather than a survey of possible positions.
