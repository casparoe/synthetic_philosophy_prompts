For a course on the metaphysics of money — or perhaps the pathologies of monetary accounting — we've been arguing about what, exactly, printing money creates. The deflationists say it creates only higher prices; the inflationists say it creates plenty of real things: seigniorage revenue for the issuer, a redistribution from creditors to debtors, a temporary boost to output while wages lag, higher asset prices, and a way to pay bills the electorate won't tax for — just none of these are "real wealth" in the purchasing-power sense. The argument keeps foundering on a slide between nominal magnitudes (balances, prices, market value, which the printing press can expand without limit) and real magnitudes (what those balances can buy, which it cannot). I want a piece that dramatizes exactly that slide.

Write a Swiftian modest proposal in the voice of an invented central-bank governor — call her Governor Adele Marais if you need a name — who has resolved never to make that slide again, by ceasing to care about real wealth entirely. Her announced policy is to manage only nominal wealth, which she can create at will: pay households, prop up asset prices, and define prosperity in terms of the sum of nominal balances and nominal capital gains. The piece should push this to an absurd but internally consistent conclusion, and each step should lean on a genuine mechanism or distinction — seigniorage, the inflation tax, money illusion, liquidity versus solvency, the slide from crediting bank reserves to crediting household income, the fact that early recipients of new money gain at others' expense — rather than on mere confusion. The humor should come from the economics being right while the conclusion becomes monstrous.

Please produce the proposal as exactly five numbered articles, each article one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion; the deadpan, ministerial tone should carry the irony without any wink.
