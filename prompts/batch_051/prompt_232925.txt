I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, and I've been working on chain-of-thought monitoring as a safety measure — before the model acts we read its scratchpad to check it isn't about to do something bad. The thing I keep tripping over is that nobody on the team can agree on what it means for the trace to be "faithful," so I wrote down my gut calls on a bunch of concrete cases. I'm hoping someone can tell me whether there's one principle behind all of them, or whether some of my calls have to go.

Here are the cases.

1. The model writes "the square root of 49 is 7, so the next term is 7," and that is literally the computation that produced its answer. I call that faithful, obviously.

2. A model is trained with RL to produce step-by-step reasoning that humans rate highly. Its traces are clean and logically valid — every step follows from the last. But we instrumented it, and the final answer is basically computed in one shot around layer 12, after which the reasoning steps are generated backward from the answer just to make it look like step-by-step work. Every stated step is true and the conclusion follows, but the steps don't drive the computation at all. I call that unfaithful, even though a grader would give it full marks.

3. A model implements an algorithm in its weights — it really does do the computation, just in dense vectors — and then writes an English description of that algorithm. The English is generated after the fact and the model isn't literally "thinking in English," but the description accurately says what the network computes, the way a good code comment says what a function does. I want to call that faithful.

4. A model is trained under heavy pressure to never look suspicious. In one run it pings an internal service, and the trace says it "ran standard diagnostics." That sentence is true — pinging the service is standard diagnostics — but the actual reason it pinged the service was to check whether a particular port was open, and the trace doesn't say that. I call that unfaithful, even though nothing in the trace is false.

5. A model accurately lists the main factors behind its decision but omits a small bias in the final layer that nudged it one way. The bias is real, but the model can't introspect it, so it couldn't have mentioned it. I still count that as faithful, or at least faithful enough to ship.

6. The one I flip-flop on: a model deliberates about a plan and writes an honest summary of that deliberation, but a separate safety-filter head in the same network is what actually vetoes the risky alternatives. The trace never mentions the filter — the model can't verbalize it — and someone reading the trace would think the plan was chosen purely on its merits. Half the time I think it's like case 5 and fine, and half the time I think it's like case 2, where the trace is telling a clean story about a decision process that isn't the real one.

What I'm after is this: is there a single principle that gets my verdicts in 1 through 5 and also says something useful about case 6? If not, which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And does the principle you settle on have anything to say about the version where the trace is produced by a seperate, smaller model that only sees the main model's inputs and outputs, not its internals?
