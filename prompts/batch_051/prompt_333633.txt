I have been reading through a debate about the practical pressures states face when deciding which communication systems get official standing, and I want a careful analysis of a concrete proposal rather than a general essay. The proposal is a three-part recognition test. A government will grant a communication system the status of a recognized language — with the right to court interpreters, public documents, mother-tongue schooling, and funding for documentation and preservation — only if the system satisfies all of the following: (1) children can and do acquire it as a first language without explicit instruction, reaching the full adult system; (2) it has been transmitted within a community across at least two generations of users; and (3) it supports open-ended, compositional expression, including novel sentences about absent, hypothetical, and abstract states of affairs. The drafters intend this to admit natural spoken and signed languages while excluding writing systems, codes, memorized formulas, engineered systems, and pidgins that are still stabilizing.

Treat the test as a mechanism whose enactment would reshape the behavior of communities, schools, linguists, and officials. Give a committed account of how it would actually play out, taking definite positions rather than canvassing possibilities. Work through its application to a home sign system (a gestural system a deaf child creates without access to an existing signed language), to an emerging sign language in a community with many members and now in roughly its second generation, to an established national sign language, and to a community that deliberately standardizes and teaches children its sign system to make sure it qualifies. What does the test incentivize: in how communities let acquisition happen versus teach deliberately, in how linguists document and describe systems, in how governments contest or phase in recognition, and in what counts as a "generation"? Where does it produce false positives or false negatives, and where does it smuggle in a contested theoretical criterion of what a language is? End with a definite verdict on the scheme's main failure modes and on whether amending the clauses could fix them without turning the test into something only already-standardized languages can pass.
