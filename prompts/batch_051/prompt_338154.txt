I've been trying to get a handle on the old future contingents debate — the sea battle thing — and the more I read the more it looks like a two-thousand-year stalemate, not a debate waiting on new facts. Aristotle's version in De Interpretatione goes roughly: if it's true now that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, then it seems that outcome is already settled now, so 'whatever will be will be' stops being a banalilty and turns into fatalism. The anti-fatalist line is that before the battle happens 'there will be a sea battle' isn't yet true or false — there's no fact of the matter until the future arrives. Then the fatalist says that just gives up bivalence, or makes truth weirdly time-dependent, and back and forth it goes. Nobody is introducing new evidence; their disputing what 'true' and 'will' and 'settled' have to mean, not what the facts are.

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict on who's right. My hunch is that the impasse is substantially a verbal tangle around 'settled': one side means causally settled (the present state of the world fixes the outcome), the other means semantically settled (there's already a determinate truth-value for the claim). If that's right, they're partly answering different questions. But I'm not fully sold, because even after pulling those apart there's a stubborn remainder: whether truth in general needs something present to make it true, and whether a branching-time semantics (where the future is open but claims get truth-values relative to histories) actually breaks the deadlock or just restages it with fancier machinery.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it a substantive dispute about temporal metaphysics, a semantic dispute, a clash of bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, or an ill-posed question? And given your diagnosis, what would actual progress look like — a formal result, a distinction, a change of question — or should we expect this one to be refought forever, and why?
