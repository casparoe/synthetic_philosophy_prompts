I chair a hearing panel for my state's Board of Nursing, and I have to circulate a written opinion to the panel before we reconvene on Thursday. The case we're deciding: a nurse with a spotless twenty-year record gave a patient the wrong medication, and the patient had a severe reaction and died. The nurse had just come off a double shift, she was covering a third nurse's assignment after a scheduling mix-up, and the two drugs involved had nearly identical packaging. Nobody on the panel thinks she was impaired or even reckless in the everyday sense; the hospital's own root-cause analysis blamed chronic understaffing and the look-alike packaging. The problem is our practice act says the board shall discipline any licensee who administers medication to the wrong patient, and it does not mention intent or state of mind at all.

That has split us. Two members read the statute as strict liability: the harm is undisputed, she caused it, the statute doesn't ask about fault, and the public needs a bright line so nurses can't argue their way out of every medication error. One of them keeps adding that licensure discipline isn't punishment at all—it's just public protection—so there's no question of punishing someone without fault to begin with. The rest of us, me included, are uneasy: it feels wrong to suspend or revoke a license over an error any nurse could have made under those conditions, and whatever we call it, discipline would follow her for the rest of her career. I need a short memo from you, written as my draft decision for the panel, that lands on a concrete outcome—revoke, suspend for a specified period, probation, letter of reprimand, or no discipline. Don't just split the difference into vague monitoring conditions. Give the strict-liability reading its strongest version first, so the members who favor it feel there position was taken seriously, and then explain why the final recommendation is the right one given both the public-protection aim of the statute and the usual idea that punishment is for fault.
