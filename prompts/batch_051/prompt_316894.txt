I have been trying to reverse-engineer a friend's implicit principle about the difference between lying and merely misleading, because her track record is almost consistent but keeps threatening to split in two. Over the past year she has given me these verdicts. If a woman lies at the door to save the people hiding upstairs, my friend says the lie is plainly right, though she is insistent that it is still a lie. If someone tells a friend that an unflattering haircut looks good, she says that is fine, at most trivial. If a used-car dealer, asked whether a car has ever been flood-damaged, answers "It has had two careful owners and starts first time, every time" — true statements picked so that the buyer will infer the car was never flooded, when in fact it is a flood car — she says that is deeply wrong; but when I asked her whether the dealer's answer was exactly as wrong as the flat "No, it has never been flooded" would have been, she said she could not confidently rank them. If a colleague, asked "Did you send the reference letter?", answers "I wrote it this morning" when she has written only half of it and has not sent it, my friend says that is wrong, but mildly — the wrongness, she says, is in the misleadingness. And if a manager in a disciplinary meeting says "I do not recall any previous warnings" while the personnel file recording those warnings lies open in front of everyone, so that no one is or could be misled, my friend will not give a verdict; she says only that that one is different.

The case I plan to put to her next is this. At a dinner party, a guest knocks a glass of red wine across a tablecloth, and the host immediately says, in front of everyone: "It is fine — it is only the cheap one from the market." The tablecloth is actually a family heirloom, and everyone present, including the host, knows it; she knows the sentence is false and knows no one believes it. She is not trying to change anyone's mind about the tablecloth; she is trying to release the apologizing guest from being the center of attention. I want to know how she will file the host's remark — as a condemned lie, as a harmless or even kind piece of social strategy, as a lie that is technically wrong but excusable, or as something outside the lying/misleading ledger altogether.

I care less about the yes/no than about the inverse problem. State the rule she seems most likely to be running; state the other rule or rules that fit her recorded verdicts at least as well; give your best prediction of her verdict on the dinner-party case and say how confident you are; and name the single further case that would best settle which rule she is actually running, with the verdict each candidate rule would give on it. Then mark what is broadly settled in philosophical discussion of lying and misleading off from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus. Please answer in these sections, in this order: (1) Prediction — her likely verdict on the dinner-party case and the feature of the case doing the work (about 100 words); (2) Candidate rules — the two or three most plausible principles that fit her whole track record, with the points where each sits awkwardly (about 250 words); (3) Best guess and confidence — which rule you would bet on, why, and your honest level of confidence (about 150 words); (4) Decisive further case — one further case that would best separate the candidate rules, with what each predicts (about 120 words); (5) Settled vs. contested — what is widely agreed versus genuinely disputed in the lying/misleading debate, placing each candidate rule on the appropriate side (about 120 words).
