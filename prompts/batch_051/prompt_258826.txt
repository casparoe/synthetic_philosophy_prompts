I am a historian of economic and political change, and I have a working command of the basic machinery of deterministic chaos and nonlinear dynamical systems at the advanced-undergraduate level: phase space, sensitive dependence on initial conditions, Lyapunov exponents, attractors and their basins, bifurcations, and structural stability, though I do not want a mathematics tutorial. Within history there is a long-running controversy over contingency, understood roughly as the question of whether large outcomes are best explained by the amplification of small and often accidental causes — the for-want-of-a-nail style of explanation — or whether deep structural constraints make the broad shape of events robust to such perturbations. I want you to restate the main positions in that controversy in the vocabulary of dynamical systems, one by one, with the mathematical terms made to do genuine work rather than serve as ornament.

The positions to translate, in historians' own terms, are these four. First, small-cause contingency: situations that differ only in some trivial particular can be driven onto drastically different trajectories, so particular events, choices, and individuals cannot be eliminated from the explanation. Second, structural necessity: large constraints of demography, geography, institutions, or class select heavily among the trajectories actually available, so the broad outcome would have survived a wide range of perturbations to initial detail, and perturbations alter at most timing or local color. Third, path dependence: an early, perhaps accidental, difference locks in through self-reinforcing feedback, making the system progressively harder to shift even though the eventual state traces back to that early accident rather than to deep structure. Fourth, contingency as openness: before the fact, more than one outcome was genuinely possible, and the later state is not something that could in principle have been recovered by projecting forward from prior conditions, because the causal sequence was unsettled until it occurred.

For each of the four, tell me three things: what translates cleanly into the dynamical vocabulary, and what the translation clarifies; what can be expressed only with distortion, and exactly where the distortion enters; and what has no expression in the vocabulary at all. Then give the residue report. Across the four translations, does the residue show places where the dynamical framework is blind to something the historical position gets right, or places where the historical position was less precise than it looked? Where the translation would smuggle in a dynamical assumption — for example, that the history in question is a deterministic system whose phase space and equations are given in advance — rather than honestly restating the historian's claim, flag that explicitly. And do not simply identify the historians' word structural with the dynamical notion of structural stability; any overlap is something to be earned, not assumed. I am not asking for a general essay on whether history is a science; stay inside the translation task and let the residue do the arguing.
