Question: Explain Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment. What is the argument intended to show about whether the meaning of a natural-kind term such as "water" is determined by the speaker's internal psychological states? State one important limitation of the argument as Putnam presents it.

Student answer: Putnam asks us to compare Earth with Twin Earth, a planet that matches Earth in its appearances and is inhabited by exact duplicates of Earth's speakers. On Earth the liquid in the lakes and rivers is H2O; on Twin Earth the corresponding liquid is a different compound, XYZ, though it looks, tastes, and behaves just like water. Oscar and his twin, Twin Oscar, are molecule-for-molecule identical, so they are in exactly the same internal psychological condition when each points at his local liquid and says "water." Even so, Oscar's word picks out H2O while Twin Oscar's word picks out XYZ, since each word is tied within its community to the local liquid. Because the same psychological state can accompany two different extensions, Putnam concludes that meaning is not entirely determined by what is inside the speaker's head; the external environment contributes to what a speaker's words mean.

There is, however, a temporal limit to this example. In 1750 no one on Earth or Twin Earth knew the microstructure of the local liquid, so "water" could not then have referred specifically to H2O on Earth or to XYZ on Twin Earth. For speakers at that time the term meant something like the clear, drinkable liquid that falls as rain and fills the rivers and lakes, because that description was all they had in mind. The example would therefore show only that a term's reference can become externalized once an underlying essence is discovered, not that meaning was external before the discovery.

Strictly speaking, the argument also establishes a point about reference rather than about meaning. What a speaker mentally grasps—the concept or description associated with "water"—can be exactly the same for Oscar and Twin Oscar, and it is that grasp that constitutes understanding. Putnam has shown that the world helps settle what a word denotes, but he has not shown that semantic content itself lies outside the speaker's mind. So the experiment is persuasive as a point about naming, but only partly persuasive as a point about meaning.
