I've been down a rabbit hole lately about AI persuasion — the worry that a system much smarter than a person could talk basically any human into basically any belief, not by lying, but just by having a perfect feel for the right framing, tone, pacing, and examples. In alignment circles there's a real live argument about whether a superintelligent assistant should ever be allowed to try to change your mind, and where the line between 'helping you think' and 'manipulating you' could possibly fall. I keep imagining how a company would actually have to operaitonalize that line in an internal policy document, and I think that itself is the joke: they'd be forced to write down crisp rules for a distinction that probably can't be made crisp.

Could you write a parody of an internal trust-and-safety ethics policy for a consumer AI assistant (give it a plausible product name), maybe titled something like 'Persuasion Safety and Utterance Classification Policy, v2.7'? Deadpan corporate voice. It should define which speech acts count as off-limits 'persuasion' versus allowed 'informing,' complete with severity tiers, borderline cases, and metrics like the maximum permitted shift in a user's stated beliefs per conversation. The humor should come from the actual conceptual mess — manipulation versus rational persuasion, superhuman persuasiveness as a security problem, whether scrupulously neutral informing is itself a kind of nudging, and whether a being that articulate can ever fully 'just inform' — so a reader who knows the alignment or philosophy literature finds it fair and someone who doesn't still gets it.
