I have just been assigned an upper-level seminar on mental representation, and in preparing it I have spent several weeks rereading the visual mental imagery debate between the depictive (or quasi-pictorial) accounts associated with Stephen Kosslyn and the descriptive (or propositional) accounts associated with Zenon Pylyshyn. What has struck me on this rereading is how strange the state of the debate is: the two sides share a large body of experimental results—mental rotation times that rise with angle of rotation, image-scanning times that rise with distance scanned, activation of retinotopically organized visual cortex during imagery—yet they draw opposite conclusions about what those results show. Kosslyn takes them to establish that imagery employs depictive representations with a genuinely spatial format; Pylyshyn takes them to show, at most, that subjects know what the experimenter expects and that their performance reflects tacit knowledge and task demands rather than the format of an underlying representation. Each side has accused the other of question-begging, of explaining performance with a mechanism that merely redescribes it, or of resting on an unargued analogy between mental images and visible pictures. There was also, along the way, a familiar methodological worry that behavioral data alone cannot discriminate between the formats, because any behavioral regularity can in principle be mimicked by a suitably designed model in the rival format. And then, at some point, the field largely stopped treating the imagery debate as a live theoretical dispute: people went off and built models, ran imaging studies, and adopted whichever framework their lab favored, without the underlying disagreement ever being settled.

I want to know, with the benefit of hindsight, what kind of disagreement this actually was. My working hypothesis is that Kosslyn and Pylyshyn were to a large extent answering different questions under the same heading—Kosslyn asking what kind of representation best explains and predicts imagery performance, Pylyshyn asking what behavioral and neuroscientific data can validly license in the way of conclusions about representational format—but I present that only as a hypothesis, and I may be over-rationalizing a messier dispute. Please do not write me a literature survey or a catalog of experiments. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement: is it verbal, is it a disagreement about what counts as an explanation in cognitive science, is there a hidden empirical question that could have decided it and did not, or is something else going on? And I want an explicit statement of what, if anything, would have counted as progress—a decisive experiment, a formal result, a redrawn distinction, a change of question—or, if you think no progress should have been expected, an argument for why not.

I am asking for a committed response: take a definite position on the diagnosis and defend it, including an honest treatment of the strongest rival diagnosis.

Please structure the response as follows, with rough lengths:

- "The debate in one paragraph" (about 200 words): state each side's core commitment and what each side takes the shared data to establish, without hedging.
- "Two or three failed diagnoses" (about 300 words): identify the diagnoses you considered and rejected (for example, that it was merely verbal, merely about the epistemology of introspection, or merely a dispute over the interpretation of one experiment), and say briefly why each fails.
- "The diagnosis" (about 500 words): state what kind of disagreement this was and argue for it. Say where each side went wrong, and distinguish your diagnosis from the hypothesis I floated above.
- "What would have counted as progress" (about 250 words): given the diagnosis, state concretely what could have moved the debate, or argue that nothing could have.
- "Implications for how I teach this" (about 150 words): say in a compressed form how the seminar should frame the debate so that students neither mistake it for a settled empirical question nor dismiss it as mere mutual misunderstanding.
