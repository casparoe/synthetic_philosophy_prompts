I’m a novelist writing a scene in which Leibniz is brought forward to the present and shown Max Black’s thought experiment about a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres. I don’t want him to sound like a caricature repeating the identity of indiscernibles. In his fourth letter to Clarke, Leibniz argues that there cannot be two individuals that differ only in spatial position and are otherwise perfectly alike, because God would then have no sufficient reason to place one here and the other there; he concludes that such cases never occur in nature. Black’s imagined world seems designed to test precisely that principle. How would Leibniz respond in character? Would he treat the two spheres as an impossible description, as a description that covertly assumes a distinguishing property such as being the one in this region, or as a genuine counterexample that forces him to qualify the principle? And what does the contrast between his theological setting and Black’s a priori thought experiment reveal about what the identity of indiscernibles is actually doing in each case? Stay faithful to Leibniz’s actual commitments, but give me a response that would be psychologically credible for him rather than a mere restatement of the principle.
