In my philosophy of mind course, I keep returning to a passage from T. H. Huxley’s essay on animal automatism in which he compares consciousness to the whistle of a steam locomotive. The whistle is produced by the engine’s machinery and may register its activity, but it does not turn the wheels; on Huxley’s proposal, consciousness is likewise a collateral effect of neural processes rather than a cause of bodily conduct. The puzzle I want to press is this. When I introspect on a case like deciding to raise my hand and then raising it, it seems overwhelmingly as though the conscious intention is the cause of the movement. But when I examine the relevant neural events, I find no obvious gap waiting to be filled by a mental cause; the motor output appears to have sufficient physical antecedents of its own. So there is an asymmetry between the first-person causal story, in which consciousness does real work, and the third-person physiological story, in which it need not. A sharper asymmetry lurks nearby: we treat our introspective reports about our own mental states as highly trustworthy, yet if epiphenomenalism were true, my confident report that my intention caused my action would itself be caused by unconscious neural events rather than by the fact it reports. But then the very report that consciousness is causally inert would be produced by a mechanism that does not track its truth.

I want the strongest case for thinking this asymmetry is justified rather than merely something we find introspectively compelling. Does the first-person perspective give us a kind of evidence about mental causation that third-person physiology cannot override, or is the apparent causal power of consciousness exactly the sort of introspective datum an epiphenomenalist should dismiss as a by-product? Is there a coherent position that keeps the reliability of introspection while denying that my conscious intention causes the movement? And if not, which should go—ordinary trust in introspection or the idea that the physical world is causally closed? Please do not settle for the easy answer that they are just different levels of description; I would like the answer to face the paradox that an epiphenomenalist explanation of judgment seems to undercut the grounds for accepting epiphenomenalism.
