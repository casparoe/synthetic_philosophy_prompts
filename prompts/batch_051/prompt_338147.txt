I am drafting a novel in which a public agency must decide whether to adopt an opaque predictive system for its most consequential decisions. As preparation, I have written out the commitments about legibility and accuracy that I want the book to take seriously before I build the plot around them, and I would like an audit of whether they actually hang together. My commitments are these. (1) A high-stakes automated decision is not genuinely justified unless the people bound by it can understand the grounds on which it was made. (2) I have no objection in principle to opaque models: when an opaque model is reliably and substantially more accurate than any interpretable alternative in a low-stakes setting, it would be irrational to insist on the interpretable one. (3) Only models that are natively interpretable, or trained with legibility as a direct objective, can satisfy the demand in (1); post-hoc explanations of an otherwise opaque model cannot. (4) To count as legible, the explanation a system gives must track what actually determined its output; a plausible-sounding reconstruction is not enough. (5) It would be unfair to hold machines to a standard of legibility that we do not hold human experts to, since the expert hunches of physicians and judges are often just as opaque. (6) In medicine, criminal justice, and comparable high-stakes settings, I would accept even a noticeable loss in predictive accuracy if that is the price of genuine legibility. Which of these commitments are in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity and say, for each, what would have to give.
