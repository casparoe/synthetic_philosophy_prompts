I've got a homemade principle I keep falling back on whenever I'm torn between two moral theories, and I want someone to pressure-test it properly. I call it the 'agreed-core' strategy, or sometimes 'residue-first'. The rule is: when you're morally uncertain between two or more live theories, don't start by asking which one is right, and don't start by trying to weigh their verdicts against each other. Start by asking what every theory you're taking seriously already agrees you should do, and just do that part first. Only the leftover choice — the residue — gets handled by expected choiceworthiness or hedging or whatever your usual method is.

The payoff, as I see it, is that the first stage needs no intertheoretic comparisions at all. You aren't saying how many utils equal a broken promise; you're just intersecting the theories' verdicts. So you dodge the most impossible-looking part of the moral uncertainty literature.

Example: I'm 60/40 between act-consequentialism and a fairly demanding deontology, and I'm deciding whether to lie to a donor to get a larger gift for an effective charity. The consequentialist says lie; the deontologist says don't. Agreed-core says: don't start there. Both theories agree I should spend the afternoon writing honest appeals, calling donors who haven't been asked yet, and so on. Do all that first. The contested act — the lie — only becomes live once the agreed core is exhausted, and by then the marginal stakes have shifted.

My broader claim is that most real decisions have a surprisingly large agreed core, and that the disagreement between moral theories mostly concerns a thin slice of the things you could do. So confining the uncertainty to that slice makes the different ways of resolving it converge in practice.

What I want to know is whether this is already a named position, or close to one. It feels like it's in the neighborhood of overlapping-consensus moves in political philosophy and of "just do what every theory permits," but the two-stage/partitioning feature is the bit I think might be new. My real worry is the partition: you can't actually perform 'the part everyone agrees on' and then separately decide the rest, because the action is one indivisible thing — lying-for-a-larger-donation versus not doing that. Am I smuggling in a status-quo bias or an act-description choice while pretending to be neutral? And is there a class of cases where the agreed core is empty, or so small the strategy collapses back into ordinary hedging? Be candid: where does this work, where does it stop being distinctive, and what's the strongest version of it?
