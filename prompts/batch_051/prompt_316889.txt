I am a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I am trying to get clearer about a methodological dispute in the scientific study of consciousness. The dispute concerns heterophenomenology, the approach associated with Dennett that treats a subject's introspective reports as data to be interpreted while remaining deliberately neutral about whether those reports refer to any actual private experiences. On this approach, the researcher reconstructs an account of how things seem to the subject from what the subject says, but refuses to treat those sayings as authoritative descriptions of experience itself; the method presents itself as the maximally neutral starting point for a science of consciousness because it assumes only that subjects produce report-like behavior, not that their reports are true. A colleague in my lab argues that this neutrality is illusory. On his view, by bracketing the truth of first-person reports, heterophenomenology quietly changes the explanatory target from consciousness to verbal behavior, and thereby builds a deflationary or illusionist metaphysics into the method from the start. I want to take that charge seriously rather than let it float as rhetoric.

Please act as an expert on philosophy of science and the methodology of consciousness research. Your task is to design a concrete test of the conceptual claim that heterophenomenology is, or is not, a neutral and non-question-begging way of collecting the data for a science of consciousness. I would like you to specify a study in which a heterophenomenological protocol and at least one more first-person method (for example, a micro-phenomenological interview aimed at eliciting the fine-grained felt structure of an episode) are applied to the same participants reporting the same kinds of experiences, such as ambiguous percepts, illusions, or cases where experience and task performance come apart. Specify the setup, the pattern of results that would favor the charge of theory-ladenness, the pattern that would favor the claim of neutrality, and the main confounds. Be precise about what such a study could and could not settle: which part of the dispute is operationalizable, and which residue no experiment can reach—perhaps the very standard of what it is for a method to be neutral, or the question whether first-person reports should count as evidence about experience at all. Begin by brainstorming two or three candidate operationalizations, and then develop the strongest one in detail; think carefully before committing to a design.
