I recently spent an evening arguing with my father, who is trying to persuade me to save less of my income and spend more of it now. When I said I was saving for a future I might never see in a form I could enjoy, he quoted Keynes at me: "In the long run we are all dead." The strange thing is that I had just been reading Keynes's essay Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren, and I came away from it convinced that Keynes was not running the line in my father's direction—but I cannot say exactly how far the slogan and the essay pull apart, or which of them better captures what Keynes thought about how one should live.

I would like you to lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be expressing. It seems to me the content changes completely depending on how long the "run" is taken to be, whose death is doing the work, what kind of concern the long run is being used to dismiss (a prediction? a standard for judging policy? a reason for present action? a justification for discounting future satisfactions?), and whether the claim is meant as a prudential norm or an empirical observation. For each candidate claim, please say whether it is a triviality, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something that is simply false, and tell me which reading would actually support my father's conclusion that I should spend rather than save. Then assess the relationship to Keynes himself. He wrote the line in one context and the essay in another; is my father using the slogan in a way that is continuous with the argument of Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren, or has the line come loose and turned into the kind of attitude its author was criticizing? I would especially like to know whether that essay commits Keynes to a particular account of the good life—for example, that it consists in present enjoyment rather than postponed satisfaction—or only to a much narrower historical thesis about the economic problem and the possibility of leaving it behind.

Where interpreters of Keynes disagree about the essay and the slogan, please keep those disagreements visible: distinguish what is settled from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus.
