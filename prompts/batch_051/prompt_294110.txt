I spent most of my career doing statistical mechanics and then information-theoretic work on inference, so when I retired I thought I'd finally read the philosophy of probability properly, not just the physics of it. Lately I've been working through the objective Bayesian literature, especially the maximum entropy crowd (Jaynes and the people after him), and I keep going back and forth on whether MaxEnt really gives you rational priors or whether it just takes the subjectivity and hides it somewhere nobody looks. I'm sketching a short essay with the working title "Maximum entropy: objective priors or subjective constraints?" and I'd like comments on my notes before I write the whole thing out.

Here are the notes so far. Overall thesis: the maximum entropy principle does not eliminate the subjective ingredient in prior selection; it relocates it into the choice of constraints, the base measure, and the coordinate system, so it is objective only relative to choices it cannot itself justify. Argument 1: the die example. If you're told a six-sided die has an expected value of 4.5, MaxEnt gives probabilities proportional to exp(λk), and this is usually treated as a triumph. But why is the constraint written as the mean of k? If someone had instead chosen to constrain the mean of k² or of log k, MaxEnt would return a different distribution, even though the underlying data are not obviously better summarized one way than the other. The objectivity is doing its work in the decision about which function of the outcome to constrain, and the entropy principle is silent on that. Argument 2: the continuous case is worse. To maximize differential entropy you need a base measure, and the natural-looking flat measure is flat only in the coordinate system you happen to be working in. If the same physical setup is described with x or with y = log x, and each coordinate is given its own flat "uniform" base measure, you get genuinely different distributions over the same possibilities, not the same distribution relabeled. Jaynes tried to fix this with transformation-group arguments leading to something like Jeffreys' prior, but that is an extra principle, something that doesnt follow from entropy alone, and it does not cover every case. Argument 3: in realistic problems the constraints are not known exactly; they are estimates with error bars. MaxEnt plugs in point estimates and outputs a distribution that is often much sharper than the evidence supports, which is an odd kind of humility. The steelman I'm trying to be fair to is that objective Bayesians do not claim to get priors from nothing; they claim that once the evidential constraints and the right symmetries are fixed, entropy picks the least committal distribution. My worry is that "the right symmetries" and "the evidential constraints" are doing all the philosophical work, while entropy is just a bookkeeping rule.

What I'd like from you: comments on the notes, especially accuracy. Am I mischaracterizing the MaxEnt position or botching the die example? Are these objections old news, or would a philosophy reader find them interesting? Is my steelman fair or a strawman? Dont worry about citations—I want conceptual comments, and be direct. Also, since I can't easily check all this from my armchair nowadays, say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure about.
