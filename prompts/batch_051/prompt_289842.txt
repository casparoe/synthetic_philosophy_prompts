I've been turning over a problem that keeps surfacing in rationalist-adjacent discussions of religion, usually in the form of asking whether petitionary prayer is supposed to work as a causal lever on the world. The standard response is that prayer changes the person who prays rather than changing God, but I've been rereading Aquinas and I think he's after something more specific. In the Summa Theologiae, II-II, Q. 83, Art. 2, "Whether it is becoming to pray?", he writes:

"Divine providence disposes not only what effects shall take place, but also from what causes and in what order these effects shall proceed. Now among other causes human acts are the causes of certain effects. Wherefore it must be that men do certain actions, not that thereby they may change the Divine disposition, but that by those actions they may achieve certain effects according to the order of the Divine disposition: and the same is to be said of natural causes."

I take this to say more than that prayer has psychological side effects. On this account, a petition is one of the secondary causes through which God's chosen effects come about; asking really is part of the causal order, even though it introduces no change in God's knowledge or will. But that only sharpens the question I want to work on. If a petition functions as a pre-appointed secondary cause, does its specific content matter, or would any act that occupied the same causal slot—a wordless intention, a vow, a fixed ritual—serve equally well? And if the content does matter, what exactly is left for that content to do, given that it cannot inform God of anything new and cannot move God's will?
