I've long treated physicalism as the default metaphysical setting, the way a lot of people in this corner of the internet treat naturalism: not a claim I could verify with a single experiment, but the standing rule that new explanations should not smuggle in anything spooky and that physics is, in principle, in charge of the inventory of what there is. The motivation is mostly unification and causal closure — the sense that we should not have to add non-physical causes to get minds, values, or anything else, and that the special sciences are layers over the physical rather than rivals to it.

The trouble begins when I try to make the word 'physical' precise, which I gather is Hempel's dilemma. On one reading, 'physical' means 'what current physics says exists,' in which case physicalism is almost certainly false: current physics is known to be incomplete and its ontology is historically unstable, so anchoring the doctrine to it makes the doctrine far too fragile. On the other reading, 'physical' means 'what a completed, ideal future physics says exists,' in which case I no longer know what claim physicalism is making — it seems to collapse into the assertion that everything will turn out to be posited by the final scientific theory, whatever that is, which rules out almost nothing and may even be compatible with things I wanted physicalism to exclude. Yet I cannot shake the intuition that physicalism is supposed to be a substantive, possibly false, empirical-seeming thesis about minds and causation, not a promissory note that whatever the last physics says will get labeled physical. I want to know whether that intuition is what I should give up, or whether the dilemma presents a false choice about how the reference of 'physical' is fixed. Present the strongest versions of each move — deflating the intuition, amending how physicalism should be formulated, or rejecting the dilemma's two horns — and say which you would take and what evidence or argument would make you switch.
