As a PhD student working on nineteenth-century British moral and political philosophy, I have been reconstructing the argument of Mill's The Subjection of Women against appeals to the nature of women, and my reconstruction seems to prove far more than I can accept. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

(1) Observed conduct can count as evidence for a natural disposition only if, roughly, the conduct has been observed under sufficiently varied conditions, or at least under conditions not uniformly arranged to produce it.

(2) The traits that defenders of female subordination attribute to women's nature—docility, domesticity, self-abnegation, aversion to public competition—have been observed only within a single, nearly universal system of subordination. That system penalizes their absence and rewards their presence, and there has been no society in which women were allowed to develop under conditions of equal freedom.

(3) Therefore, the observed distribution of those traits gives no evidence about women's natural dispositions.

(4) But the same problem appears completely general. Human beings are never observed outside some system of institutions, incentives, and expectations; every human group is observed under culturally specific arrangements, and many of those arrangements are coercive or inculcated from birth.

(5) If the principle in (1) is sound, then by (4) it is hard to see how any claim about human nature—or about the natural dispositions of any socialized group—could be warranted by observation.

(6) Yet Mill's own case for liberty and for women's emancipation seems to depend on affirmative claims about human nature: that human beings have capacities for self-development, judgment, and choice that are stunted by custom and coercion, and not merely created by another set of arrangements.

(7) So Mill's critique of appeals to nature either undermines his own positive program or must be qualified in a way that seems to take back its force.

I am reluctant to give up (1) or (2), since they appear to be the engine of Mill's argument. But I cannot see how to keep them without granting the generalization in (4) and (5), and thereby disarming the appeals to human nature on which Mill's own program relies. Which step should be rejected, and what would rejecting it cost? Is there a principled difference between the appeal to nature Mill attacks and the appeal to human nature he needs?
