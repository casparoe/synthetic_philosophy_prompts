I've spent a long evening going round in circles on a Bayesian blog and I've ended up with an argument that seems airtight but flys in the face of how I actually update, so I'm hoping someone can localise the break. Take something I'm confident I know, like that I left my keys on the kitchen table. (1) If I know the keys are on the table, then they're on the table. (2) If they're on the table, then any observation that seems to show they aren't is misleading. (3) If I know that a particular observation is misleading, then I may rationally dismiss it. (4) So when I actually get an observation that seems to show the keys aren't on the table, I may rationally dismiss it and carry on believing. (5) The same reasoning applies to everything I know, not just mundane facts: my belief that a particular commit introduced a regression, or that a design decision was the wrong one. (6) So once I know anything, I could never be rationally required to update on counterevidence about it. That seems to prove too much, because it turns every known belief into a closed loop and makes testing against the world pointless. I'd rather keep (1) and (2), since they look like plain facts about knowledge and truth. Which step do I reject, and what do I lose by rejecting it?
