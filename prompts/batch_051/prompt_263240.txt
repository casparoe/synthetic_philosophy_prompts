I am a third-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of religion course on Christian doctrine. I have been asked to lead a one-hour study session for fellow undergraduates who have no background in formal logic and who know the doctrine of the Trinity only in its creedal form. The idea I want them to understand is why the doctrine, stated as one God in three persons, is not an elementary breach of the logic of identity, even though the statements that the Father is God, that the Son is God, and that the Father is not the Son appear to force a contradiction through the symmetry and transitivity of identity. Please plan the explanation rather than write it out. Set out five things. First, the order in which the component distinctions must be introduced and what depends on what — for example, the distinction between the "is" of identity and the "is" of predication, and the distinction between person and nature or substance. Second, the single worked example or analogy that should carry the whole hour, and what it must make vivid and what false pictures it must not suggest. Third, the predictable misconceptions the group will bring or form — for instance, that the three persons are three roles or masks worn by one person, that they are three gods, that they are three parts composing a whole, or that the words "is God" can only mean numerical identity — and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than untangled later. Fourth, the final check that would show they understand why the offending syllogism does not go through, rather than merely accepting that the doctrine is a mystery. Fifth, what I should openly say I am leaving out at this level. Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
