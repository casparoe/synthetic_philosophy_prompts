You are advising the director of a comparative governance institute that is about to fund three years of conceptual work on what should count as corruption across different political cultures. The institute keeps running into the same unresolved disagreements in its research program: whether vote-buying in clientelist systems is better understood as corruption or as a poor constituent’s strategy for extracting resources; whether favoritism toward kin counts as corruption when the official’s community does not regard it as private, self-interested conduct; and whether the very category ‘corruption’ is a Western legal transplant that distorts how accountability actually works outside rule-of-law bureaucracies. The director wants to fund theoretical clarification rather than fieldwork or legal drafting, and she can support work on two of the following four questions.

(A) Intention or institutional drift? Should corruption be defined primarily by an officeholder’s corrupt intent in a discrete exchange, or can a system count as corrupt when formally clean rules — revolving-door career incentives, campaign finance, internal performance metrics — predictably pull decisions toward private-regarding outcomes even without a quid pro quo?

(B) Private gain or particularistic obligation? Does corruption require the official to gain privately, or should the concept also cover favoritism toward kin, party, faction, or an ethnic or regional constituency, even when the official gains nothing personally? In other words, does the concept need a worked-out account of what makes a benefit ‘private’ rather than ‘political’ or ‘communal’?

(C) Universal or parochial? Are anti-corruption standards universal because every society has standards against the abuse of entrusted power, or do they smuggle in a specific Western picture — impersonal bureaucracy, the separation of office from personal networks, adjudication by formal rules? Can a cross-cultural concept of corruption avoid being both imperial and vacuous?

(D) Descriptive or evaluative? Should ‘corruption’ function as a relatively neutral analytical category, or does calling a practice ‘corruption’ already settle the normative question against it? This matters most for clientelism and gift-giving, which defenders sometimes present as redistribution, insurance, or relationship-building in low-trust settings.

For each question, briefly say what three years of conceptual work would try to establish and what would count as progress. Then defend a ranking of which two the institute should fund, using importance, tractability, neglectedness, and how many downstream empirical disputes each would settle. State explicitly which question you would deprioritize and why, and what new evidence or argument over the next couple of years would make you swap your top choice for your second choice.

End with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing your ranking of all four, such as \answer{B > A > D > C}.
