I am a retired physicist who has only recently begun reading the philosophy of quantum mechanics seriously, and I have come to hold a particular view about the Born-rule problem in the many-worlds interpretation. I am not asking for a survey of the landscape; I want to know what would count against the view I describe below. My view is this: if one accepts a unitary-only ontology in which every outcome of a measurement is realized on some branch and nothing ever collapses, then the Born rule should not be postulated as a fundamental probability law. The squared-modulus weights can instead be recovered from the structure of the theory together with rational constraints on an agent who knows the branching structure. Such an agent assigns equal credence to the fine-grained branches and then coarse-grains them into outcome types; additivity then forces probabilities proportional to the squared moduli, which is the unique additive measure preserved by the branching dynamics, in line with Gleason's theorem. On this view, probability is not a matter of how often an outcome occurs in a single history; it is a constraint on rational credence across branches, all of which are equally actual.

I would like a map of this position's exposure. Which formal results, arguments, or thought experiments would most seriously undermine it, for example challenges to branch counting, to the additivity assumptions, to the appeal to Gleason's theorem, or to the claim that rational credence is the right place for probability to enter at all? Which objections should a defender of this approach concede genuinely count against it, and which would they properly dismiss? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether the position still risks anything empirical or conceptual, or whether it has been insulated by its qualifications into something that cannot be refuted. I would also like your own assessment of the single strongest defeater and whether I should find it persuasive. Please provide the answer in American English.
