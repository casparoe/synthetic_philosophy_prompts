For the past several years I have served as the academic member of my state's Information Practices Board, a body that must rule on proposals by public agencies to collect or use personal data where no statute plainly settles the matter, and I want to think through our next case before I draft the memorandum. The Board reasons by analogy: each decision is argued by locating it among the cases we have already decided, and I suspect our precedents, taken together, do not yet determine a single rule. Three sensibilities compete behind the table: a German-style conviction that citizens retain informational self-determination and that the purposes of collection must be specified narrowly in advance; an American-style confidence in transparency and open records as a check on government, which we honor by insisting that what the state does with data be open to inspection; and a recurring temptation, which several of my colleagues admiringly call social scoring, to let pooled data generate its own suspicions and sort citizens accordingly. Our most-cited rulings are these. (1) We approved, on strict conditions, a police request for the real-time location of a named kidnapping suspect from his mobile provider: access was limited to seventy-two hours, logged throughout, and the suspect was notified as soon as doing so would not endanger the child. (2) We rejected a police request for six months of historical location records, with subscriber names attached, for every telephone that had connected to a tower in a district where several street robberies had occurred, on the ground that it subjected thousands of people to a search on the strength of a correlation and without individualized suspicion. (3) We approved, subject to conditions, a welfare bureau's request to receive from the revenue office a map of declared incomes by census blocks of not fewer than one hundred households, to be used only to select neighborhoods for fraud auditing, provided the methodology was published in advance, the aggregate map itself was released as an open dataset, and individual tax files never left the revenue office. (4) We rejected a city's proposal for a resident responsibility index, which would combine school truancy, housing-code complaints, clinic visits, and utility arrears into a score influencing public-housing transfers and some school placements; a caseworker could inspect the underlying records, and a resident could appeal an underlying record but not the composite score, while the city intended to withhold the scoring formula as a trade secret.

The case now before us is a Finance Ministry tax-compliance program. Banks would automatically report each taxpayer's year-end account balance and total annual credits and debits to an independent Statistical Bureau. The Bureau would pseudonymize the records and apply a published, annually audited risk model that flags the two percent of files displaying the largest unexplained gap between reported income and bank activity. Only for flagged files would the Bureau disclose the taxpayer's identity to auditors; before any assessment was finalized, the auditor would have to give the taxpayer the flagging reasons and the underlying bank figures, which the taxpayer could correct or rebut. Unflagged records would be destroyed after five years. The program contains no general notice to the public that their banks are reporting these data, although anyone flagged would of course learn of it before an assessment.

I have not yet decided what the Board should do, and I would like you to work through the question as if you were drafting the memorandum for me. To remain consistent with our own precedents, should we approve the program, reject it, or approve it only with conditions? Identify the closest earlier case and explain why it, rather than its main rival, is the better analogy. If the precedents underdetermine the answer, set out the alternative principles that would each reconcile them, state what each principle requires in the present case, recommend one reading of the tradition and defend it, and specify a further case, not before the Board, that would most cleanly separate the candidate principles from one another.
