Several organizations now run intensive workshops that teach rationality as a trainable skill through repeated, standardized exercises they explicitly compare to martial-arts katas. Participants drill discrete routines in artificial settings: they assign numerical probabilities to trivia questions and check their calibration over hundreds of trials; they state in advance what evidence would change their mind before entering a disagreement; they practice generating the strongest version of an opposing view until it becomes a reflex. The programs claim these routines, once automatized, transfer to business, personal life, and science. Yet the same programs also warn that human reasoning is highly context-sensitive and easily derailed by situational triggers. What philosophical commitments does this training paradigm presuppose about the nature of rationality? In particular, does it presuppose that rationality decomposes into context-free sub-skills that can be trained in isolation and then transferred, and that being reasonable is more like a technique than like a character trait or a body of subject-matter knowledge? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is underdetermined such that rival views of rationality could license the same drills.
