I find the propensity interpretation of fitness compelling, and I can state its appeal cleanly. Define fitness as actual reproductive success — the number of viable offspring an organism in fact leaves — and natural selection threatens to collapse into a tautology: those who leave the most offspring are, by that very definition, the fittest, so the principle of natural selection merely restates the data. Define fitness instead as an objective probabilistic disposition, a weighted expectation over the possible reproductive lives an organism might live in its environment, and natural selection becomes an empirical claim again: organisms with higher expected reproductive success will tend, across populations and over the long run, to be better represented in later generations. The view also preserves the thought that a well-adapted animal can be unlucky — struck down before mating — and still have been fitter than the one that survived and bred.

And yet I cannot shake the intuition that the fitness natural selection actually acts on is realized differential reproduction. Natural selection has no purchase on a propensity that never manifests: populations change because particular organisms actually survive and reproduce, not because of what they might have done. If the fitter type can be eliminated by drift while leaving fewer descendants, in what sense was it fitter? I do not want to be told which side wins. Walk me through what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that gives, what it would take for the propensity view to be amended or abandoned, and whether there is a reading of 'fitness' — or of what it means for natural selection to 'act on' a property — on which both survive.
