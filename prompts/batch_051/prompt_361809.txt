For the past four decades, a debate over Libet-style experiments has been stuck on the same set of findings. In the standard paradigm, participants watch a spot rotating on a clock face and are asked to flex their wrist whenever they choose, while noting where the spot was when they first felt the conscious urge or intention to move. An EEG readiness potential recorded over the motor cortex begins several hundred milliseconds before the act, and the reported time of the urge falls much later, roughly a fifth of a second before movement. One side reads this as direct evidence that the brain decides before the person consciously does, so that the felt intention is epiphenomenal or a post hoc confabulation. The other side replies that the task probes only a trivial self-initiated twitch, that the readiness potential is not a decision but accumulating neural activity approaching a threshold, and that free will belongs in the temporally extended processes of planning, weighing reasons, and refraining, including the capacity to veto an action in its final stages. The same data, the same methodological objections, and the same counterexamples have been traded for decades without either side moving.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is largely conceptual: the two sides use free will, conscious intention, and decision to mean different things, and they disagree about what could possibly count as evidence against free will. In particular, I suspect one side takes free will to require that a fully formed conscious intention initiate the action from scratch, while the other side takes free will to be compatible with nonconscious preparation so long as the person's reasons and capacities govern the action. But I can also see a hidden empirical and methodological dispute underneath, over what the readiness potential actually is, whether the clock-report method reliably measures the time of an urge, and whether there is any neural marker of vetoing. I would like a diagnosis rather than a survey: is this a verbal dispute, a dispute over standards of evidence, a substantive empirical dispute in disguise, or several such disagreements layered together? Be concrete about what each side is committed to, and then say what would count as progress given the diagnosis, whether a conceptual distinction, a sharper experiment, or a reformulation of the question, or argue that no such progress should be expected and why.
