I am a software engineer working through some probability machinery for a reasoning engine, and I have managed to derive something that seems obviously wrong. I am hoping someone can tell me exactly where the derivation breaks.

I was reading about uncertain conditionals and came across the claim, associated with David Lewis's paper Probabilities of Conditionals and Conditional Probabilities, that a certain very natural rule about the probability of conditionals has a devastating consequence. I do not have a philosophy background, so I reconstructed the argument in my own terms, and I want to know whether I have faithfully represented it and, if so, which step I should reject.

Let P be a probability function over a finite set of states. Let A⇒C be the indicative conditional "if A, then C." Consider the following rule:

CPR: For all events A and C with P(A) > 0,
P(A⇒C) = P(C | A).

This looks like a clean spec for reasoning under uncertainty: the probability of an indicative conditional is the corresponding conditional probability. I also assume that if I learn an event E, my updated probabilities are given by conditionalization, written P_E(·) = P(· | E), and that CPR remains a valid constraint after such updates.

Now let A and C be any events such that P(A ∧ C) > 0 and P(A ∧ ¬C) > 0.

1. Conditionalize on C. The updated function is P_C. Under P_C, C has probability 1. Since P_C(A) > 0, CPR applies, so:

P_C(A⇒C) = P_C(C | A).

But in P_C, C is certain, so P_C(C | A) = 1. Hence:

P_C(A⇒C) = 1.

Equivalently, P(A⇒C | C) = 1.

2. Conditionalize on ¬C. Under the updated function P_¬C, C has probability 0. Since P(A ∧ ¬C) > 0, we have P_¬C(A) > 0, so CPR applies again:

P_¬C(A⇒C) = P_¬C(C | A).

But in P_¬C, C has probability 0, so P_¬C(C | A) = 0. Hence:

P_¬C(A⇒C) = 0.

Equivalently, P(A⇒C | ¬C) = 0.

3. By the law of total probability:

P(A⇒C)
= P(A⇒C | C) P(C) + P(A⇒C | ¬C) P(¬C)
= 1 · P(C) + 0 · P(¬C)
= P(C).

4. But CPR says:

P(A⇒C) = P(C | A).

5. Therefore:

P(C | A) = P(C).

Since A and C were arbitrary up to the nondegeneracy conditions, the rule seems to imply that no pair of such events is probabilistically relevant to the other. That is absurd: the probability that my service is down given that a deployment is in progress is different from the unconditional probability that my service is down.

My question is whether this derivation is valid. If it is not, which numbered step fails, and what is the correct restricted version of CPR? If it is valid, which premise is the most reasonable to give up: CPR itself, or the assumption that CPR survives conditionalization? I would like to keep some version of CPR, because it is the only clean interface I know for conditionals under uncertainty, but I want to understand exactly what I am paying for it.
