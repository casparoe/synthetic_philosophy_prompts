Consider the standard St. Petersburg game: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands tails, and if the first tails occurs on toss n, you are paid 2^n dollars. The familiar puzzle is that the expected monetary value is infinite, yet almost no one would pay more than a modest sum to play, and the usual resolutions appeal to diminishing marginal utility for money, to bounded utility, or to the finite wealth of whoever must make the payout. I want to think through a version in which the coin itself is not known to be fair. Suppose the coin has a fixed but unknown tails probability p, and before deciding what to pay you assign p a uniform prior on the open interval (0,1). You may then choose any finite number m and observe m preliminary tosses of that same coin — tosses that will not enter into your payoff — after which you must name a price for the game. For a known p, the expected payoff would be the sum over n of (1−p)^(n−1) p 2^n, which equals 2p/(2p−1) when p > 1/2 and is infinite whenever p ≤ 1/2.

My suspicion is that no finite number of preliminary tosses can settle the matter. After any finite sample, the posterior over p still assigns positive probability to the region where p ≤ 1/2; on that entire region the expected payoff is infinite, so the posterior-weighted expected payoff remains infinite no matter how reassuring the data look. If that is right, then a risk-neutral decision-maker who evaluates the game by expected monetary value can never reach a point, after any finite sample, where the game has a finite expected monetary value — and the paradox is not really a problem about missing information about the coin. Work through whether that suspicion is correct. Then explain what happens to the standard resolutions when the coin's bias is unknown rather than known to be fair: which resolutions still dissolve the paradox in this variant, which do not, and does the contrast show that an epistemic response (observe the coin first) and the classical utility-based responses are answers to different questions rather than rival answers to the same one?
