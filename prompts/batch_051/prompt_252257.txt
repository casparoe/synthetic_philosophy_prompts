I volunteer with the historical society of a small city that used to be a mill town. Our archive is large but lopsided: it is full of diaries, letters, ledgers, and photographs left by the town's prominent families — mill owners, merchants, clergy — while whole communities are nearly absent from the record: the mill hands, the domestic workers in those big houses, the immigrant neighborhoods, and a Black community that has been here since before the Civil War. Where working people do appear, it is mostly in payroll books, accident reports, and police-court dockets — records made about them by employers and public institutions, not records they made for themselves. Some of this absence reflects what people did not keep; some of it reflects what earlier curators decided was not worth keeping; some of it reflects what people were never in a position to write down.

We have received a digitization grant, and the board is divided about how to spend it. One group wants to use the money to conserve and digitize the fragile nineteenth-century family papers we already hold. Their argument is that this material is rare, verifiable, and historically significant, and that we should preserve what is already there before it decays. The other group — the one I lean toward — wants to run a community collecting drive: record oral histories, scan family photographs and letters, and deliberately seek out the communities missing from the archive. The first group replies that this would stuff the archive with trivia and unreliable memory. A diary, they say, can be checked against other documents; a family story can be misremembered, flattering, or invented, and in precisely the cases where the written record is silent there will be nothing against which to check it. They add that a digitization grant is for preserving existing holdings, not for creating new ones.

I need to make a recommendation to the board. I share some of the worry about memory, but I also suspect the standard of "historical significance" is doing the excluding: a group looks significant because it had the means and standing to leave records, and the groups now absent look unimportant precisely because they left so little. What should we do with the grant, and what principle should govern the choice? Please take a definite position and defend it, including the hard question of whether it is reasonable to fill gaps in the written record with oral history.
