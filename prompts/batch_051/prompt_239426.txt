I'm a retired experimental physicist and I've only recently gotten into philosophy of mind, and I definitly don't have the vocabulary for this. The zombie argument keeps bugging me because it feels like the mirror image of a habit I spent decades practicing. As I understand it: you conceive of someone molecule-for-molecule identical to me, behaving exactly the same way, but with no conscious experience at all; from the fact that this is conceivable, the argument concludes that the physical facts don't settle the phenomenal facts, so physicalism is false. In physics, we constantly dealt with models that made identical predictions but differed over some unobservable extra thing, and we were trained to treat that extra thing as idle bookkeeping unless it could be made to do some measurable work. The zombie scenario looks structurally similar: the presence or absence of consciousness is stipulated to make no difference to any behavior or measurement. But instead of dropping it as idle, philosophers use its conceivability to argue it's a real further fact. Is this a fair parallel or only a superficial one? If the two cases come apart, where exactly do they separate, and what would have to be true for the physics attitude to carry over? Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no introduction or conclusion.
