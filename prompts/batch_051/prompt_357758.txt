Construct a thought experiment in the philosophy of language in which a simple causal-historical theory of proper names and a cluster descriptivism, applied to the same name and the same facts, pick out different individuals as the name's referent. The divergence must arise from East Asian and Anglophone conventions for ordering given and family names in an ordinary bibliographic or administrative setting (a citation index, an author database, a conference record). No twins, impostors, deliberate renamings, or exotic name technology; use fictional people. The causal-historical theory should follow the original dubbing and the chain of communicative borrowing, while the descriptivist should follow the cluster of descriptions competently associated with the name at the time of use; arrange matters so the two criteria settle on different people. After the scenario, state the name, the relevant dubbings and chains, and the associated descriptions; show why each theory selects the person it selects; identify the structural feature responsible for the divergence; say how the case behaves if that feature is removed; and explain how a defender of the causal-historical theory would answer a descriptivist who insists that the name has shifted to the person the community is actually talking about. The case must not be something a conscientious librarian could fix by catching a typo—the systematic confusability should fall out of the naming conventions themselves.
