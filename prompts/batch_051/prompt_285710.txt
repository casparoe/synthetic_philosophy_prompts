I'm in a standoff with my roommate about the ants in our kitchen and I can't tell if I'm the one being unresonable. Every spring the ants come back, and every spring I put out those little poison bait stations: the workers carry the stuff back to the nest, and a few days later the whole colony is dead. This year my roommate asked me not to, and it's become a whole thing. His argument, reconstructed as charitably as I can: ants probably aren't completely insensate, the poison is a slow death that hits the queen and the larvae too, and if you multiply a large number of ants by even a modest probability that an ant's experience is something like suffering, then my exterminating a colony for the sake of a clean countertop might have expected costs that are wildly out of proportion to what I'm getting. He also did the thing where he pointed out that the number of insects killed for trivial human reasons dwarfs the number of farmed animals, so if insects count for anything at all, the math gets uncomfortable fast.

My side is that this is the kind of reasoning that sounds rigorous but has no principled place to stop. If ants matter, then what about the mites on my pillowcase or the bacteria in the yogurt in the fridge, and don't I end up either paralyzed or a monster by my own lights? Also I honestly can't tell whether he has a live moral point or whether he's just read too many blog posts about how much suffering exists in nature and has turned a kitchen nuisance into a philosophy seminar.

So I guess my question is: is there a defensible position in between "insects are outside the moral circle" and "me using ant bait is a serious wrong", and what would it actually rest on? What would I need to believe about ant minds, or about how to aggregate tiny probabilities of suffering across huge numbers, for the bait stations to come out clearly fine or clearly not fine? Steelman both of us and then tell me which steelman survives contact, because I've got ants on the counter and a roommate sulking in the living room.
