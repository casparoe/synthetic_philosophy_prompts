After a career in experimental physics, I have been spending my retirement working through the philosophy of probability, and I have become preoccupied by the Principal Principle: roughly, that when you know the objective chance of an outcome is x, and the rest of your information is of the right kind, your credence should simply be x — no matter how strongly a recent run of results or a hunch pulls you elsewhere. I have agreed to lead a seventy-five-minute session for a senior philosophy of science seminar (sixteen students), and I want the students to come away with a felt sense of why a known objective chance deserves that deference, and equally of where its authority stops — the boundary between ordinary admissible background information, which leaves the chance untouched, and information about the outcome itself, which does not.

Please turn this into a runnable classroom game, not a lecture. I have a rough idea, but I do not trust my first sketch: a physical randomizer with a publicly known bias, students posting probabilities round after round under a proper scoring rule, and a visible streak of surprising outcomes that tempts them off the posted chance, with the cumulative scores eventually exposing what the deviation costs. I would like you to commit to a definite version of this or replace it with something better. Specify the roles (a chancemaster, syndicates, a scorer, perhaps an information dealer in a later phase), the sequence of play, the scoring rule and why you have chosen it over alternatives, the number and pacing of rounds in the seventy-five minutes, and the exact way the tempting streak is produced. If you introduce a second phase involving leaks, peeks, or tips about the outcome, it should be there to dramatize the admissible–inadmissible boundary, not just to add a twist.

Then write the debrief. It should map particular moments of play onto the components of the principle — the published chance, the streak that felt informative, the difference between background information and information about the result — and it should say what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run (a syndicate that deviated and was rewarded by a lucky round, a student who insists the setup must be unfair because of the streak) and how a facilitator should handle those moments without simply announcing the moral. Be candid about which feature of the principle a classroom game cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply; I suspect the full normative force of deference is one such feature, but I will let you tell me. I want a single, committed design with reasons for each choice, not a list of options.
