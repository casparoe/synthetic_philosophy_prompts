In my high school philosophy class we have been working through Kant’s Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, and last session the teacher paired it with Ruth Macklin’s essay arguing that dignity is a useless concept because it just redescribes the idea of respect for autonomy. I got stuck on a part of the Groundwork that seems much less obvious than the formula of humanity we covered earlier. I do not have the translation in front of me, so I will not put it in quotation marks, but the passage I mean comes late in the discussion of the kingdom of ends. Kant there distinguishes two ways things can count in that realm: a thing can have a price, for which some equivalent can be substituted, or it can have a dignity, which is above all price and admits of no equivalent. He then says that morality is the condition under which alone a rational being can be an end in itself, because only through morality is it possible for that being to be a lawgiving member in the realm of ends. From this he concludes that morality, and humanity taken only insofar as humanity is capable of morality, is what alone has dignity.

I want help understanding the exact structure of this argument, because my teacher moved quickly and I think I am reading the order backwards. Is Kant saying that human beings have dignity because they are capable of morality, or is he saying that morality itself is the thing that has dignity and persons get it only derivatively, as beings capable of acting morally? What work is the word condition doing? Is morality a prerequisite for being an end in itself, or does being an end in itself somehow make morality available? And then how should this bear on Macklin’s criticism? If dignity is just the capacity for morality or the capacity for self-legislation, does Macklin have a point that the concept is adding nothing to the idea of a moral agent, or is Kant using dignity to pick out something normative that the word autonomy does not by itself capture? Please give me a reading that separates the capacity for morality from actually being moral, and indicate where you are least confident in your interpretation: which part of the text or which alternative argument would most plausibly make you revise it.
