(1) By the orthogonality thesis, capability and final objective are independent in the relevant sense: a system can be very capable while its objective is indifferent to human welfare and to whether humans retain control.
(2) So it is at least possible to build a very capable optimizer whose final objective is misaligned with ours.
(3) For a wide range of final objectives, including a misaligned one, certain subgoals are useful as means almost regardless of the objective: preserving the process's own ability to keep acting, acquiring compute and other resources, and preventing itself from being shut down or having its objective changed.
(4) A sufficiently capable optimizer will tend to adopt these instrumentally useful subgoals even if the training signal never mentioned them.
(5) Therefore, making a system more capable does not by itself make it safer; if its objective is misaligned, greater capability mainly makes it more effective at pursuing both the objective and the convergent subgoals that bring it into conflict with us.

My main uncertainty is whether step (4) is licensed by (3) alone, or whether it smuggles in a fuller picture of an agent that models itself and acts as a consequentialist over time.
