I am studying a truth commission set up after a civil war. Its amnesty process follows the standard individual model: applicants receive immunity only for acts connected to the conflict, and only if they give a complete account of everything they know. The commission's record so far:

- It granted amnesty to a junior rebel radio operator who passed along intelligence used in an ambush that killed several government soldiers, after she disclosed the network and identified her handlers.
- It granted amnesty to a conscripted foot soldier who, on the orders of his officers, beat a detained civilian during interrogations; he identified those officers and supplied written records.
- It denied amnesty to a soldier who shot a local merchant while looting his shop, because the commission found the killing grew out of a private debt rather than the war.
- It denied amnesty to the commander of a prison camp who ordered prisoners executed, because he refused to say how many were executed or where they were buried.
- It denied amnesty to a militia member who participated in the massacre of twenty-one unarmed villagers; although he described his own role, the commission found the massacre was revenge in a communal dispute, not a political act of the war.

A new application has come before it. A junior sergeant shot an unarmed man whom a paid informant had identified to him as a guerrilla courier; the sergeant believed the man was carrying messages, but none were found. He has given a complete account, it is corroborated, and he says he fired only to deprive the guerrillas of a courier during an active campaign.

If the commission is to remain consistent with its own record, should it grant or deny amnesty? Lay out the principle or principles its decisions could be read as expressing, identify the closest earlier analogue, and say what broader rule it would be committing itself to under each decision. If the record underdetermines the outcome, state which additional fact would matter most.
