Consider a pair of voting rules that many democracies maintain, in broad outline, at the same time. A citizen who left decades ago is still permitted to vote in her country of origin's national elections, even though she now holds no property there, owes it no taxes, and has no one in her household subject to its laws. A non-citizen who arrived on that same day decades ago and has ever since worked, paid full taxes, and raised children in that country's school system is barred from those same elections, and in many places from local elections too. The first exercises some power over a state whose ordinary coercion she never feels; the second feels the coercion of the state daily and gets no say. Yet most people treat both rules as plain common sense.

Your task is to decide whether this asymmetry is defensible, and if so on what principle, and if not which of the two rules should give way. Reason from first principles rather than from what particular theorists have said or from lists of countries.

Structure your answer into the following four sections, in this order, staying near the word counts given.

1. The asymmetry (~150 words). Restate the two rules precisely and present the two adults in a way that holds everything else equal: age, length of work history, law-abidingness, tax compliance, and family situation. Make the tension between them as sharp as you can.

2. Candidate justifications (~250 words). Set out no more than three principles that could jointly vindicate the pair — for example, a membership principle, an affected-interests principle, and a contribution principle. For each, say which of the two rules it supports comfortably and which it supports only with strain.

3. Stress tests (~250 words). Take whichever single principle you judge strongest and push it through these nearby cases: local-election voting for long-resident non-citizens; a naturalized citizen who emigrates a year after naturalizing; a resident who has lived in the country for twenty years and is one month from completing naturalization; and a non-resident citizen with no remaining family, property, or tax ties. State where your principle draws each line, and whether those lines look right.

4. Verdict (~200 words). Say whether the pair should be kept, revised, or given up, and specify the one change to voting law you would make first if you could make only one. Identify the intuition you are most willing to sacrifice, and state how confident you are.
