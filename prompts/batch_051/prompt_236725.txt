You are a professional decision theorist who works on normative models of choice under risk. I have drafted a short statement of my views for a seminar on the Allais paradox and expected utility theory, and I want a consistency audit before I circulate it. Please work through the material carefully rather than giving a quick reaction: first lay out the two Allais problems as tables or explicit distributions, show which axioms of expected utility theory they implicate, and then test my commitments against one another.

My commitments are these:

1. For well-defined monetary gambles with known probabilities, the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms—completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence—state the correct normative ideal, and an ideally rational agent maximizes expected utility.

2. In the canonical Allais pair, I side with the standard pattern. First problem: option A is a sure $1,000,000; option B gives $1,000,000 with probability 0.89, $5,000,000 with probability 0.10, and $0 with probability 0.01. I choose A. Second problem: option C gives $1,000,000 with probability 0.11 and $0 with probability 0.89; option D gives $5,000,000 with probability 0.10 and $0 with probability 0.90. I choose D. I do not regard either choice as a mistake or as evidence that I am irrational.

3. If these choices force a revision to the expected utility framework, I take the independence axiom to be the dispensable one. I would keep completeness, transitivity, and continuity, and I would also keep the core claim that rational preference consists in maximizing the expectation of a utility function; only the axiom that blocks the Allais pattern would go.

4. Rational preference must be dynamically consistent: a plan that it is rational for me to adopt now should not have to be reversed once the gamble has partially resolved into branches, provided no new information arrives and nothing about the options or the stakes has changed.

5. The rational pull of the sure thing in the first problem is the special value of certainty itself. It is not anticipated regret, not an independent aversion to the 1 percent chance of nothing, and not confusion produced by how the gambles are displayed.

6. Rational preference is invariant under redescription. If two formulations present the same underlying lottery, then switching from one formulation to the other should never change my choice.
