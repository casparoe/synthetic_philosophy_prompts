We have been reading Karl Marx’s Economic and Philosophical Manuscripts of 1844 in my modern European history class, and a sentence from the “Estranged Labour” section has been bothering me. Marx writes: “The devaluation of the world of men is in direct proportion to the increasing value of the world of things.” In class we applied this mainly to factory workers: the more physical commodities they produce, the cheaper their labor becomes relative to the wealth they create, and the product confronts them as an alien power.

I want to know whether the argument behind that sentence survives a change Marx did not anticipate. Consider someone who supports themselves by making videos, newsletters, or podcasts. They use their own laptop and camera, choose their own hours, and retain much of their personal reputation and creative control. But the platform they rely on owns the recommendation system, controls the feed, collects the engagement data, and can change payment terms or demonetize them without warning. The platform’s investors also become wealthier as the creator’s output accumulates, even though the creator is not selling a physical object to a factory owner. Does Marx’s sentence still describe this situation, or would applying it require stretching “the world of things” until the original argument loses its force? If it does apply, what is the contemporary equivalent of the commodity the worker produces and loses? If it does not, which premise of the estranged-labor analysis had to change?
