I recently sat on a jury in a case in which the defendant had a documented brain lesion, and the experience has left me unsettled about a question I had previously only considered in the abstract: what exactly are we doing when we punish someone, and when, if ever, is punishment deserved rather than merely inflicted? That question sits at the center of the exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso over just deserts and punishment, and I want to work it through a real case rather than leave it at the level of general principle.

As I understand the two positions: Dennett defends a compatibilist view on which the fact that our actions have causes does not dissolve responsibility; our standing practices of praise, blame, and punishment are not a conceptual error, but part of what makes us answerable agents, and in that sense punishment can be deserved. Caruso is a free-will skeptic: he argues that no one is ultimately deserving of retributive punishment, because what a person does is ultimately the product of luck and causes they did not author. He does not say dangerous people must go free; he says the criminal justice system should be rebuilt on a public-health or quarantine model that permits incapacitation and rehabilitation but forbids making people suffer because they supposedly deserve it.

The case I want analyzed through these lenses is Charles Whitman. In the early hours of August 1, 1966, Whitman, a former Marine and an engineering student at the University of Texas, stabbed his wife and his mother to death, then took rifles to the university's clock tower and shot at people below for more than an hour; by the time police killed him, more than a dozen people were dead and dozens more were wounded. He had sought psychiatric help in the preceding months, had reportedly expressed violent thoughts to a counselor, and had complained of severe headaches and episodes of rage that he himself found frightening. In notes he left behind he described himself as overtaken by violent impulses he did not fully understand. The autopsy found a glioblastoma pressing on his amygdala.

I would like the analysis to run in both directions. First, apply each position to Whitman. Under Dennett's compatibilism, does the tumor place Whitman outside the class of agents it is appropriate to hold answerable, and if so, what does that do to the desert he wants to preserve in ordinary cases? Under Caruso's quarantine model, should Whitman be treated differently from an offender with no such tumor, and if so, what does that difference amount to if not desert? Second, let the case test the positions: which features of Whitman does each account explain well, which does it blur or leave out, and where does each position have to strain? Finally, say what the episode shows about the debate itself: is the tumor an anomaly chiefly for Dennett, chiefly for Caruso, or for both, and does the fact that each could assimilate the case reveal that their disagreement is really about something other than these facts?
