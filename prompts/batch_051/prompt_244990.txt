I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm putting together a feature on priority disputes and the claim that major discoveries are largely inevitable — the thought that the calculus would have arrived without Newton because Leibniz was on the same track, and that Wallace's manuscript shows natural selection was, so to speak, already in the air. I'd like you to take on the role of a professional philosopher of science with a satirical streak, and to write a deadpan, Swiftian modest proposal of about 700 words. The voice should be a sincere, reform-minded research administrator addressing a national funding council. Their proposal: now that the historical record shows discoveries arise predictably from the state of a field rather than from singular genius, individual credit should be abolished. Future findings should be published as anonymous outputs of the research community, prizes should be awarded to the state of the field at the moment of discovery or to no one, and textbooks should stop telling heroic origin stories. The piece should follow a real line of argument about simultaneous discovery to a conclusion the reader recoils from, while staying straight-faced and internally consistent. It should be funny to a reader who knows the debates over discovery and priority, and still land for a general reader. Before drafting, take a few minutes to brainstorm the strongest real arguments for and against the inevitability of discovery, then let the proposal exaggerate the strongest version of the inevitability case without inventing features of the debate.
