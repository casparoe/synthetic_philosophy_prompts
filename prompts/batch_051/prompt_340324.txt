Last week in my school's law and society club, two members argued about an article we read concerning a shop owner who was fined for selling alcohol to a seventeen-year-old. The buyer had presented a strikingly realistic fake ID, and the owner had carded her and checked the ID against a scanner, so it was generally accepted in the discussion that he had no realistic way of discovering her age. The sale was nonetheless a strict-liability offense, meaning the law did not require the usual mens rea, or guilty mind, element, and he was fined. Two members, Sofia and Devin, got into a back-and-forth afterward, and I wrote down their exchange.

(1) Sofia said the fine struck her as unjust, because what makes punishment deserved is some kind of guilty mind: intent, knowledge, or at least carelessness. The owner had none of those. He checked the ID and used the scanner. He never chose to sell to a minor, and punishing him is punishing an accident.

(2) Devin replied that the law does not always say "be careful"; sometimes it says "do not let this result happen." For a rule like this, the point is to guarantee that minors do not get alcohol from licensed sellers, and the responsibility is put on the seller because the seller is the one best placed to set up procedures to catch fake IDs. If an honest mistake about age were a defense, every seller would claim one, and the rule would be unenforceable.

(3) Sofia answered that deterrence only works on conduct a person can control. This owner could not realistically have checked more effectively, so no threat of a fine would have changed what he did. The only remaining function of the fine, she said, is to make an example of someone who acted reasonably, which looks more like using him than punishing him. The fact that some sellers might lie to escape responsibility is a problem of enforcement, and courts could demand proof of a real age-check without convicting people who actually performed one.

(4) Devin came back by pointing to the broad class of public-welfare offenses. We fine businesses whose food is contaminated even when their safety procedures were excellent, and we fine companies for pollution even when a hidden equipment failure caused the discharge. Those rules are not about calling anyone evil; they attach a penalty to an activity the law permits only on certain conditions. Selling alcohol, he said, is a conditional privilege, and the fine is effectively part of the cost of holding that privilege.

(5) Sofia said this merely relabels punishment as a fee while leaving the experience of a conviction intact. When the state says you violated the law and takes your money, it is blaming you, whatever economists call it. And she thought the line between "regulatory" and "criminal" was doing too much work: some no-fault offenses carry records and even custody, and age-related offenses are the most obvious example. Once the machinery of guilt exists, she said, you cannot de-fang it by calling the penalty a licensing cost.

(6) Devin answered that the distinction he was drawing is precisely one of moral seriousness. Strict-liability offenses are usually minor and low in stigma, which is why the fine here was modest. The owner also was not faultless in the relevant sense, because he chose to enter a trade that the law defines by an outcome: if a minor ends up with alcohol from your shop, you are responsible. By operating under that rule, he accepted a duty of success, and a voluntarily accepted duty can be a duty to bear the loss when the outcome is bad even if he tried his best.

(7) Sofia ended by saying that "duty" was doing all the work. A duty someone can fail without any failure of effort or attention is not a moral duty but a liability rule. The legislature can write liability rules, but it should not call them crimes or attach to them the moral vocabulary of guilt, because punishment is the one state action that expresses blame. If the owner owes anything, it is compensation or a licensing fee, not a conviction.

I have been unable to sort out who is right, and they asked me to referee. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, saying for each numbered move whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and briefly explaining each rating. Then say who is more right overall and what the most important consideration is that both of them missed or underdeveloped. Please write your entire answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; even though I have labeled the moves with numbers, discuss them in ordinary paragraphs by referring to, for example, "the first move" or "Sofia's opening point."
