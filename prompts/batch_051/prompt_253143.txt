I am a PhD student in moral psychology, and I have built the following argument for a seminar; I now find it suspicious because it drives toward a conclusion I cannot accept, though I cannot see which move to abandon. (1) If psychopaths genuinely know that what they do is morally wrong, then moral knowledge does not require the characteristic affective responses—remorse, guilt, empathic distress—since psychopaths are standardly described as lacking those responses even while saying of their actions that they are wrong. (2) If moral knowledge does not require those affective responses, then a subject could know that an act is wrong while being entirely motivationally indifferent to that fact. (3) If a subject could know that an act is wrong while entirely motivationally indifferent to it, then moral knowledge is exhausted by a reliable capacity to sort acts in accordance with the community's moral classifications. (4) If moral knowledge is exhausted by that, then any system with such a reliable sorting capacity—a look-up table, a well-trained machine classifier—thereby counts as possessing moral knowledge. (5) That is absurd: a look-up table does not know that anything is wrong. (6) Therefore psychopaths do not genuinely know that what they do is wrong. I would like to keep (5) and the denial of (6), but I suspect that combination forces me to give up more than I can see. Where does the argument go wrong, and which premise or step is least costly to reject? Please answer in British English.
