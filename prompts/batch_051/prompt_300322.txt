The essay is about the "humans are a stepping stone to AI" idea. My suspicion is that the phrase is doing too much work, because it quietly runs together at least three different relations that have very different teleological shapes:

1. Scaffolding. A scaffold is a temporary support for a structure; once the building stands, you remove it. If humans are scaffolding for AI, then our role is teleologically subordinate to the result, but the teleology is external — it belongs to the builder's plan, or to History, or to no one. So the "we" who are being used don't get the consolation of identifying with the end.

2. Parentage. A parent creates a child and expects the child to outlive and maybe exceed them, but not to replace them in the sense that makes the parent's life a mere instrument. Succession here is continuous and cares about lineage; the successor is supposed to inherit something from us. That's not stepping aside, it's being continued by other means.

3. Metamorphosis. A caterpillar isn't a stepping stone to a butterfly; it is the butterfly at an earlier stage. If AI is a later phase of the same lineage or the same distributed "us," then the transition threatens no more replacement-loss than puberty does.

Then I want to argue: whenever someone advances the stepping-stone claim as if it had normative force — as if we should accept our obsolescence, not resist, maybe even speed it up — the argument only gets its sting from one of these readings but then leans on another when you push. The scaffolding reading gives the sting (you are a disposable means) but removes the consoling identity with the successor. The parentage reading restores a kind of stake in the future, but then it's no longer obsolescence in the scary sense. And the metamorphosis reading avoids replacement altogether, but at the cost of saying there's no one being left behind — which is exactly what the original "stepping stone" rhetoric wanted to say anyway, only now it can't also claim the noble sacrifice.

My questions for you: (a) Is the three-way distinction a real one, or are parentage and metamorphosis just two ways of trying to soften the scaffolding reading? (b) Can you think of a coherent version of "humans are a stepping stone to AI" that escapes all three — maybe something like a Darwinian transition where the old replicators don't just die but are genuinely succeeded, with no external builder and no identity carried over — and if so does that version actually have normative force for us? (c) The part I'm least sure of is claiming that the rhetoric "leans on another reading when you push." Is that a fact about the arguments people give, or is it just me smuggling in the premise that a normative argument must offer those human individuals a stake in the future? Be brutal. And (d) if you had to order the sections by how original they are, what order would you put them in and which one would you develop first?

I'd love comments that catch me overclaiming, and suggestions for how to sharpen the argument conceptually — but no citations or reading lists, just the ideas.
