I find myself pulled in opposite directions about petitionary prayer, and I want help deciding which of my commitments should be revised—or whether the apparent conflict dissolves under better framing.

On the theory side, I am convinced by a cluster of considerations about divine perfection. If God is omniscient, my requests cannot supply God with information. If God is immutable or at least perfectly wise and good, then God's will needs no correction, and no argument I can formulate should move God to a better course of action. If God is the creator and sustainer of everything, then it seems like a category mistake to think my asking could make a causal difference to what God does—as though God were a decision-maker who could be lobbied, persuaded, or bargained with. From this angle, petitionary prayer looks either incoherent or at best instrumentally useful for the one praying, with the content of the request (heal her, avert this, provide that) doing no work at all.

On the intuition side, I cannot shake the sense that petitionary prayer as it is actually practiced is not a mistake. In the traditions I take seriously, asking God for particular goods is treated as a commanded and relationship-constituting act rather than a tolerated error. The intuition is not that prayer is magic or that God is a vending machine; it is that the request itself belongs to a relationship, rather as a child's request to a parent is not made pointless by the parent's already knowing the need or even by the parent's already having decided how to respond. If a theory of prayer entails that the petitionary element is strictly inert, I suspect the theory has missed something, even if I cannot yet say what.

I am not asking which side wins. I want to know what would have to be true for the intuition to deserve to be surrendered: what would a debunking explanation of prayer's felt meaningfulness actually have to show, and what would it have to explain? I also want to know what would have to be true for the theory to be revised: which premise about omniscience, immutability, or divine action would have to be loosened, and at what doctrinal cost? Finally, I want to know whether there is a reading on which both sides can be preserved—for example, one on which petitionary prayer does not alter God's will yet is not causally irrelevant, because God eternally wills that certain goods come about through the request itself, or because the request changes the petitioner in a way that is itself part of God's plan. I want a negotiated verdict that makes the costs of each move explicit, rather than a mere choice between them.
