Air-accident investigation agencies routinely reconstruct, minute by minute, what a flight crew noticed, believed, and intended, and they treat those mental states as determinate facts recoverable from cockpit voice recordings and switch timings. If the transcript shows a pilot calling out the correct altitude just before acting correctly, the report credits a moment of situational awareness; if not, it records a lapse — and courts and regulators later assign blame on that basis. The practice appears to presuppose that at each instant there is a settled fact about what the crew consciously experienced, a single finished stream presented somewhere inside the head. What theory of consciousness does this practice presuppose? Are investigators implicitly committed to a Cartesian theater, or can their findings be fully discharged under the Multiple Drafts model, on which conscious content consists of many parallel drafts under continual revision with no master version? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely comports with, and say what would have to change — if anything — in the writing of accident reports if investigators explicitly adopted the Multiple Drafts model. Commit to a definite reconstruction and defend it rather than surveying the options.
