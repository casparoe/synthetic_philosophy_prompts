For the past year I have followed a policy in a small forecasting group, and I want to know whether it is defensible or a rationalized form of stubbornness. The group is non-hierarchical, works under time pressure, and consists of people I regard as roughly my equals in competence and honesty. We begin by pooling evidence: each of us states the key facts we are conditioning on, our base rates, our conditional probabilities, and our confidence intervals. We then record individual probability assignments for the same well-defined event. My policy is this: once that exchange is complete, if two of us remain materially far apart, I treat the residual gap as evidence that at least one of us has mishandled the information, not as evidence that the information itself underdetermines the question or that the other person holds some insight I lack. I therefore keep my own number and privately code the disagreement as noise or error.

I adopted the policy because I noticed that whenever I shifted toward the other estimate at the deadline, I usually regretted it: the movement was toward social convergence, not toward accuracy. I know the policy resembles Aumann's agreement theorem, which says that rational agents with common priors and common knowledge of one another's rationality cannot agree to disagree. But the theorem's conditions plainly do not hold in our room: we do not literally share priors, we cannot inspect one another's computations, and our disclosure is only partial. So I want to know whether the policy is still justified once those assumptions fail. When is it a reliable shortcut, and when is it exactly the sort of self-protective move that keeps me from updating on the disagreement itself? And how, in a given meeting, would I tell the difference from the inside? Is there a restated version that keeps the benefit — not surrendering a considered estimate for the sake of agreement — without the cost of discarding whatever information a live, post-exchange disagreement carries? Answer for my situation rather than giving a general essay on the theorem.
