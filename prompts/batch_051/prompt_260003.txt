I'm a philosophy professor, and I sit on the board of trustees at my college — specifically on the commitee that reviews major capital projects. We're looking at a $35 million proposal to replace our old steam-heat plant with a ground-source heat pump network that would cut emissions and, eventually, energy costs. The finance office's standard analysis applies a flat 5% discount rate to all future savings and maintenance costs, which makes the project look barely worth doing: most of the benefits arrive fifteen to forty years out, and at 5% a dollar saved in 2060 counts for very little today. Some trustees are perfectly comfortable with that — they say a dollar today is just worth more than a dollar later, full stop, and that the endowment could earn more elsewhere. A few of us think this confuses two different ideas: discounting because future benefits are uncertain or because the money could be invested elsewhere, versus discounting because the future itself matters less just because it's later (pure time preference), which feels like a moral position rather than a bookkeeping one. The project would also be a living lab for our environmental studies students, but the finance office says we shouldn't let that cloud the numbers. I've been asked to draft a short decision memo for the finance committee on whether we should use a different discount rate — or a different method entirely — for long-horizon projects whose main rationale is benefits to future students and the surrounding community.

I'd like you to write that memo. You're an expert on intertemporal ethics and social discounting; take a few minutes to think through the difference between pure time preference and discounting for opportunity cost or risk before you start writing. The memo should recommend something concrete the committee could actually vote on — a specific rate, a schedule of rates, a two-part test, whatever you think is defensible — and it shouldn't just survey the options. Also take the finance office's view seriously: there's a real argument that a below-market discount rate is a hidden subsidy, and that a college shouldn't pretend it can borrow or invest on special moral terms. I need a bottom line and the reasoning for it, including the strongest point on the losing side.
