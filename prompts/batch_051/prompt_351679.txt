Two people in my political theory reading group got into it last week and neither of them would budge. I mostly listened. They were working through a case: a legislator, call her M, is pushing a cap on payday-loan interest rates. She's privately moved by a religious argument (roughly, that usury is wrong), but she knows she can't offer that to citizens who don't share the premises. So in public she gives only secular economic arguments — debt traps, regressive effects — which she thinks are decent but, by her own lights, not decisive. Priya says M has complied with public reason: what matters is the reasons you offer, and she offered the right kind. Dan says that's hollow; she's legislating for reasons she won't state, and offering arguments she doesnt fully endorse is a kind of bad faith. Priya comes back: if the offered reasons have to be sincerely decisive, then a citizen whose deepest reasons are religious has to either lie or leave politics — and that's exactly what the proviso is supposed to prevent. Dan says the proviso only requires the public reasons to be sufficient in their own right, not that the agent find them sufficient, so Rawls's test lets M through — but his worry is with the test itself: a determined theocrat could always get someone to draft secular-sounding arguments for whatever conclusion, and nothing about the offer guarantees good faith. Priya concedes the hollowing-out worry but says fixing it by demanding sincerity makes public reason unenforceable and turns politics into a test of people's hearts; better to insist that the public reasons acutally be good, whatever the speaker privately thinks. Dan answers that public reason is meant to be an ethic for citizens, not just a speech code, so the agent's actual reasons can't drop out entirely. They went in circles after that. Adjudicate the dispute point by point: who's right about which specific claim, what are they each conflating, and is there a defensible fix to the hollowing-out problem?
