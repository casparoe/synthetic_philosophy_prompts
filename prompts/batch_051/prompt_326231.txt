I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I tripped over a parallel I can't shake. Albert's Münchhausen trilemma says that whenever you try to justify a claim you've got three moves: give another reason, and another, and another (infinite regress); loop back to something you already leaned on (circularity); or just stop and announce that some claim is simply accepted (dogmatism). The pessimistic take is that all three are bad, so justification never really bottoms out.

This is also, pretty much move-for-move, what happens when my editor or a fact-checker asks how I know a sentence in a draft is true. I cite a source, which cites a source, which cites a source — regress. Or I catch two sources leaning on each other, outlet A citing outlet B and B having cited A's earlier piece — circle. Or I stop at the primary document, the on-the-record interview, the reporter's notebook, and say: this is where we decided it's solid. That last move isn't exactly arbitrary — there are professional norms about which sources count as stopping points — but it still looks like dogmatism wearing a deadline.

I'd love to know whether this is a real structural identity or just me pattern-matching. In particular: is the journalist's privileged stopping point the same thing as foundationalism's basic belief, or is it just Albert's dogmatic third horn dressed up with professional norms? If the structures are genuinely the same, what transfers — would a coherentist account of justification, say, explain why editors like two independent sources that corroborate each other even when neither is a foundation? And where does the parallel come apart? I'd especially want a prediction the parallel makes that turn out false, because that's probably where the comparison gets interesting.
