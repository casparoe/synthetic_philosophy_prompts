I am a software engineer on a mid-size infrastructure team, and I have no formal background in philosophy, so a question of this kind is beyond me. Last week two of my coworkers argued at lunch about a third engineer, Ravi, who had quietly stopped reviewing teammates' code while still expecting his own code to be reviewed. I could not tell who was right, and I would like a professional philosopher to adjudicate the exchange move by move. Here is the core of it, reconstructed as faithfully as I can manage.

(1) Marta opened with "What if everyone did that?" If every engineer reasoned that their own reviews rarely change outcomes, code review would disappear, the codebase would degrade, and Ravi himself would eventually be worse off. He is free-riding on a shared practice he refuses to maintain.

(2) Dev replied that this changes the subject from our world to a different one. Not everyone will stop reviewing. The expected harm of one additional non-reviewer is negligible, so Ravi's choice is not wrong; "what if everyone did that?" is a vivid hypothetical, but if acts are judged by the consequences they actually bring about, it is beside the point.

(3) Marta came back that the question is not a prediction but a consistency test. In Kantian terms, Ravi cannot will his maxim as a universal law: he can will the end — a codebase kept honest by review — but the maxim, universalized, would undermine the means on which the end depends. There is also a generalization-of-consequences form: if everyone in his position followed "contribute only when my individual effort is unlikely to tip the outcome," the practice would collapse, so the policy is wrong even when one instance does no measurable damage.

(4) Dev said that this makes everything turn on how the maxim is described. Restate Ravi's maxim as "skip reviewing when my reviews add little and the practice is healthy enough to survive without me," and it universalizes without contradiction. The same problem haunts a Hare-style requirement that a moral judgment commits you to the same judgment in all relevantly similar cases: everything depends on what counts as relevantly similar. "Others are still reviewing" is part of Ravi's actual situation, so he is not making an arbitrary exception; anyone genuinely in that situation may do the same.

(5) Marta answered that relevance cannot be gerrymandered that liberally. If "others are covering for me" counts as relevant, then every would-be free-rider can cite it at the margin, and the policy becomes "take the benefits of a cooperative practice while leaving its costs to others whenever the practice will survive your doing so." That is a policy no one can genuinely will for all, because it works only if most people do not invoke the permission. The fact that no individual withdrawal seems to cross a line merely shows that the line is scattered along a gradual slope; it does not show that nobody is wrong.

They kept arguing after this, but those five moves are the ones I need sorted out. Please adjudicate them move by move.
