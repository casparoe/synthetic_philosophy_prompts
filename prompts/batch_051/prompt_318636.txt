A line of argument that I have seen people extract from Bertrand Russell's early paper *On the Notion of Cause* is meant to show that causation should be eliminated from our fundamental metaphysics. I half-suspect the argument is too quick, but I have not been able to locate the exact failure. I am not asking for a survey of the causation literature, and I do not need references. What I want is a step-by-step assessment of the following argument.

(1) In mature physics, the fundamental laws are not stated as one event causing another. They are, as Russell emphasized, statements of how a system's total state at one time is related, through differential equations or similar functional relations, to its total state at later times. The word cause does not appear in the formulation of these laws.

(2) The ordinary, and much of the philosophical, concept of causation is essentially local, asymmetric, and event-event: a particular event, such as a window being struck or a match being dragged, makes a distinct particular event happen.

(3) If the fundamental ontology of physics contains no event-event causal relation, then anyone who wants to keep causation in a scientifically respectable metaphysics must either reduce causation to something non-causal or eliminate it.

(4) There is no viable reduction of causation to non-causal structure. Reductions to regular succession, counterfactual dependence, intervention, transfer of conserved quantities, or probability-raising either fail to capture central cases or silently reintroduce causal notions on one side of the reduction.

(5) Therefore, causation should be eliminated from fundamental metaphysics. We should regard the notion of cause as a remnant of a pre-scientific way of managing the world, not as something that carves nature at the joints.

Is this argument valid? If it is valid, explain why the conclusion, however unwelcome, follows. If it is not valid, identify the particular step that fails, say exactly what the failure is, and indicate whether the failure blocks the conclusion outright or merely leaves it underevidenced. In doing so, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers who work on this, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
