I'm a high school student in a political philosophy course, and we've been reading Henry David Thoreau's "Resistance to Civil Government" alongside excerpts from Milgram on obedience and Arendt on administrative responsibility. A passage that seems to me to do a lot of the argumentative work, though it isn't one of the famous lines people usually quote, is this:

> The mass of men serve the state thus, not as men mainly, but as machines, with their bodies. They are the standing army, and the militia, jailers, constables, posse comitatus, etc. In most cases there is no free exercise whatever of the judgment or of the moral sense, but they put themselves on a level with wood and earth and stones; and wooden men can perhaps be manufactured that will serve the purpose as well.

I want to test how much of Thoreau's critique survives if it is taken seriously as a policy proposal rather than as a piece of rhetoric. Imagine a medium-sized democratic republic, roughly the United States around 1850 but with modern administrative agencies, passes the following law: any government employee, including soldiers, police officers, tax collectors, and prison guards, may refuse an order they judge unjust. The refusal must be made in writing and may be reviewed afterward by a civilian board, but the employee cannot be imprisoned or fined for a sincere refusal, though they also cannot be paid for work they refuse to perform. Give me your best reasoned prediction of how a decade under this law would actually go in at least three areas: military chain of command, street-level policing, and tax collection. I do not want a story; I want a chain of reasoning about incentives, coordination costs, selection effects, and the threat of selective or opportunistic refusal. For each link in the chain, tell me whether you are highly confident, moderately confident, or guessing, and say what would have to be true about the people or institutions involved for your prediction to be wrong.
