I'm on the graduate studies committee and we've been tasked with rewriting the feedback norms for our program. The department has become a lot more international over the last few years—a big cohort of Dutch students and postdocs, and an equally big cohort of Japanese and Korean students—and every spring we hear a version of the same fight. Some students complain that supervisors here are so blunt its demoralizing; others complain that feedback is so hedged and compliment-sandwiched that they honestly can't tell wether their chapter is good or bad. The familiar contrast between Dutch directness and Japanese indirectness gets brought up at every meeting, and I'm sure that's part of the story, but we can't just legislate a cultural stereotype. Last term a supervisor told a student, in front of the lab, that a draft was "not good" and then moved on. The student later said they'd have preferred a small lie, or at least a long buffer, because the bluntness made it impossible to take in the actual criticism.

The committee is now split. One faction wants a policy requiring direct, unsoftened feedback, on the grounds that treating someone as a competent adult means telling them the truth without embroidery. The other wants a policy that permits, maybe even encourages, white lies and heavy buffering when a student is struggling, on the grounds that keeping someone able to work matters more than the bare truth. Draft a short policy memo for our committee recommending one of these—or a third option if you think we're framing the choice badly—with criteria a supervisor can actually apply when deciding how to phrase a critical comment. Make the bottom line actionable, and engage the strongest consideration on the side you reject.
