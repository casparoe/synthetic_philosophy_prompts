Yesterday I ended up in a long, annoying argument with a friend about complicity and I can't stop turning it over. His whole thing was that you can only be complicit in a wrong if it would have gone differently without you: no difference, no responsibility. But then he had no problem saying a guard at a prison camp who spends his shift checking locks, eating in the mess, never laying a hand on anyone, is obviously complicit in what the camp does. And I pointed out that the guard is individually irrelevant too, right? The camp would run the same without him, there are dozens of interchangeable guards and he's just one shift. But my friend would never say that lets him off, while he got really squirmy when I said the same no-difference logic seems to cover an ordinary taxpayer in the regime, or a railway clerk who routes the trains, or a baker who sells bread to the camp kitchens. He started drawing lines—roles, intentions, proximity—that felt like he was reverse-engineering the verdicts he already wanted. So I want to know: is there actually a principle that puts the guard on one side and the taxpayer or the baker on the other, or is it just a line we draw to spare ourselves the conclusion that half a society is complicit? What's doing the real work: role, intention, proximity, the guard's voluntary choice to take the job, something about what his presence signals to the prisoners? And as you go, please separate what's genuinely settled among people who think about this from what's still live and contested—my friend talks like all of it is obvious, and I suspect some of it isn't, so don't dress up one side of a live dispute as the consensus. Test whatever principle you propose against nearby cases and tell me honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept, given up, or reversed.
