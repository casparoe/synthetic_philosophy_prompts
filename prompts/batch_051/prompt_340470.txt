I am a professor of philosophy, and I also direct a university food-recovery kitchen that turns food which would otherwise be discarded into meals for students who struggle to afford them. Our kitchen has one firm ethical commitment: we will not support the exploitation of animals. Recently, however, we have begun receiving offers of animal byproducts that are not straightforwardly the products of commercial farming, such as eggs from a campus sanctuary's rescued hens or wool from a rescued sheep that must be shorn for its own health. At present my volunteer shift leads decide these offers ad hoc, and I need to give them a reusable procedure.

The kitchen has adopted, as its working principle, a broadly freegan view of these cases. On this view, the serious wrong in using animal products lies not in mere contact with the product but in the demand that our use creates, the way accepting a product props up, funds, or normalizes arrangements that treat animals as resources. A byproduct that will exist whatever we decide, and whose use would neither create nor signal demand for further exploitation, may be used. I do not need you to defend this principle or cite anyone; I need you to translate it into a procedure that people without philosophical training can run.

Please answer as a professional moral philosopher working at the intersection of animal ethics and the ethics of consumption, and reason carefully rather than reaching for a familiar summary. Before you draft the procedure, think through several edge cases to yourself, for example honey from a rescued hive or manure from sanctuary animals, and let those cases test the procedure you build.

Now the task. Write a decision procedure that my undergraduate shift leads can run each time such an offer arrives. These decisions come up once or twice a week; the shift lead has about ten minutes and no background in philosophy. The stakes are real in both directions: accepting an animal product we should not have accepted compromises our stated commitment, while refusing an unproblematic byproduct wastes edible or usable material and can insult the sanctuary that offered it. The procedure should give the questions to ask in order, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, say where the procedure stops with a no, and say where it hands the decision to me or to the kitchen officer on duty because what remains is a judgment of values rather than a question of fact. It should tell me which distinctions in the underlying freegan view must be preserved for the procedure to stay faithful, which can be dropped, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as rules. It should also be explicit about the cases it cannot settle.

Then run the procedure on three concrete offers: accepting eggs from rescued hens; accepting wool from a rescued sheep whose fleece will cause skin problems if not shorn; and accepting milk from a rescued goat whose kid was removed and bottle-raised by a volunteer so that the sanctuary could offer us the milk. At least one of these should come out in a way my volunteers will not like, and I want you to say plainly why the procedure yields that result rather than softening it.

Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do, the kinds of offer it will inevitably mishandle, and when a shift lead should stop and consult someone.

Please write your entire response as continuous prose, as you would write an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold text, and no tables. The procedure and the three worked applications should all be stated in flowing prose.
