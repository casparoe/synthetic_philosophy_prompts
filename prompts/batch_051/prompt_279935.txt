Question: Explain the core disagreement between W. K. Clifford and William James over whether it is ever okay to believe something when your evidence does not settle the matter. Include Clifford's shipowner example and James's conditions from The Will to Believe for when believing without sufficient evidence might be permitted. Then answer: does James's view justify believing whatever you want for emotional reasons? Why or why not?

Student's answer: Clifford's position is that it is wrong always, everywhere, and for every person to believe anything on insufficient evidence. His main argument is the shipowner example: a shipowner has real doubts about whether his ship is seaworthy, but he wants to avoid the cost of repairs, so he talks himself into a sincere belief that the ship is safe, sends it out, and it sinks. Clifford says the owner is morally guilty even though the belief was sincere, because he had no right to believe it on the evidence he had. Clifford also argues that beliefs are not private: they shape how we act and how we influence other people, and sloppy believing corrupts the shared habit of trusting only what is supported. This is why he thinks we should withhold judgment until we are certain. James then says Clifford is being too demanding. For James, when a person faces an option that is live, forced, and momentous, and the evidence is exactly balanced, their passional nature may decide. He thinks religious belief is the most important case: if you insist on proof first, you may lose the chance to believe at all, so you are allowed to let your feelings tip the scale. James is careful to say this does not license believing whatever makes you happy; it only applies when the option is genuinely live, forced, and momentous and cannot be settled intellectually. So James is not anti-evidence, he just thinks evidence sometimes runs out and then practical or emotional grounds can decide.
