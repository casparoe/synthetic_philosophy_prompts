I serve on a regional environmental review committee deciding whether to permit the release of a genetically modified insect intended to suppress an invasive pest. The decision has forced me to think through the precautionary principle more carefully than I ever have, and I keep returning to this argument:

(1) Where a proposed course of action carries a credible risk of severe and irreversible ecological harm, it should not be approved.
(2) Releasing the modified insect carries that kind of risk: suppressing the target species could trigger cascading effects on other species, effects we cannot predict with confidence.
(3) Therefore, the release should not be approved.

The conclusion matches my instincts, but I am uneasy, because refusing the release is itself a course of action that carries a credible risk of severe and irreversible harm: the invasive species continues to spread, and the chemical controls currently in use have known long-term risks. Applied even-handedly, the same principle appears to forbid the refusal as well. Is the argument valid? If it is, which step is doing too much work or needs qualification, and what is the strongest repair that avoids both recklessness and paralysis — or is the apparent symmetry between releasing and refusing misleading? I care more about the structure of the argument than about the committee's actual verdict, so please be precise about the step and the reading on which a critic should press.
