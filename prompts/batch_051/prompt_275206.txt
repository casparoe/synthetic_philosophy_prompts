I'm a PhD student in behavioural economics, and there's a problem I keep going round in circles on. On the theory side, I'm persuaded by backward induction in finite perfect-information games: solve from the last node backwards, and you get the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium, which is just iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies once you assume common knowledge of rationality. Take this four-node centipede game. At the first node Player 1 can take, giving payoffs (4,1), or pass. At the second node Player 2 can take, giving (2,8), or pass. At the third node Player 1 can take, giving (16,4), or pass. At the fourth node Player 2 can take, giving (8,32), or pass. If both players pass the final node, the terminal payoffs are (64,16). Backward induction says Player 1 should take on the very first move for £4, because at the last node Player 2 would take £32 rather than pass for £16, so at the penultimate node Player 1 would take £16 rather than pass and get £8, and the whole thing unravels back to the start. I find that arguement hard to resist.

Yet I also cant shake the intuition that two sensible people actually sat across a table from each other would pass, at least a few times, and that passing isn't a mistake. If I were Player 1 I'd pass, and I'd expect Player 2 to pass back, because we'd both end up better off; grabbing £4 and leaving the other player £1 while the pot could grow feels like leaving money on the table, not like being rational. Experiments on the centipede game mostly show people continuing well past the first node, and I don't want to write that off as pure error or irrationality.

So which gives? Is the intuition tracking something the theory omits — uncertainty about the other's rationality, reciprocity, a chance to signal, or something odd about common knowledge itself — or is it an artefact of imagining a real social encounter when the game specifies something much thinner? What would it take for the intuition to be the thing I drop, and what would it take for backward induction to be the thing I amend or abandon? Or is there a reading of "rational" on which both can be right? I'd like the strongest case for each side and then a verdict on what should yield, not just a survey of the debate. Please write the answer in British English.
