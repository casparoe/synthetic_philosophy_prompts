I am a PhD student in moral philosophy with a side interest in animal law, and I want help making explicit the commitments implicit in a legal pattern I keep noticing. In many jurisdictions, deliberately beating a dog to death is treated as serious animal cruelty, even when the dog is a stray and the point is not protection of an owner's property. In the same legal systems, standard practices in intensive animal agriculture—sows confined in gestation crates, piglets castrated or tail-docked without analgesia, chickens bred to grow so quickly that their legs fail—are not treated as cruelty, either because the statutes exempt customary agricultural practices or because welfare standards are set low enough to permit them. The pain involved in the second kind of case can be as severe as, or more severe than, the pain involved in many acts the cruelty statutes do punish.

I am trying to reconstruct what theory of moral considerability—of which beings can be wronged and in virtue of what—would make this divergence coherent rather than merely a compromise among powerful interests. Does the legal pattern presuppose that sentience alone is not sufficient for protection: that species membership, an animal's relational status, its legal classification as property, or some aggregate human interest does the decisive moral work? Or is the pattern best explained not by any consistent theory of animal minds or moral status, but by a political settlement among competing human concerns?

Please reconstruct the most charitable version of the implicit theory that could rationalize the legal asymmetry. Distinguish between what the law strictly presupposes and what it is merely compatible with, and identify the points at which the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Then tell me what would likely have to change in animal cruelty law if the operative commitment were replaced by the view that sentience alone is sufficient for direct moral protection from serious pain.
