I'm the animal-welfare compliance officer at a neuroscience institute, and I've been asked to write up a decision procedure for something that comes up with every new grant. Our ethics committee has to decide which species go on the "heightened review" track—the one we currently use for monkeys and apes, with extra justification, enrichment, and individual tracking—and which go on the default track like mice, rats, and pigeons. Right now the decisions are ad hoc and the arguments are circling around whether the line should be set by absolute brain size, brain-to-body ratio, total neuron count, cognitive flexibility, or some combination. I need a procedure a committee of non-philosophers can actually run in about twenty minutes per protocol: the questions in order, what counts as a pass or fail at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands off to a human because a values judgment can't be hidden inside an anatomy fact. I'd especially like the places where a factual question and a moral stance get confused marked clearly, because I keep watching committee members smuggle their moral commitments into what they claim are brain measurements.

Run the procedure on two hard cases. First: a study using common marmosets—a tiny monkey with a brain around eight or ten grams, far smaller than a pig's, but unmistakably primate, with a cortex that's densly packed for its body size. Second: a study on cuttlefish learning—they have large brains for invertebrates and are shockingly good at some tasks, but their nervous system is organized nothing like a mammal's. I'm especially interested in any point where the procedure outputs an answer the committee won't like, and in what it genuinely cannot settle. If it helps, the committee is a neuroscientist, a vet, a statistician, and a lay member, and the chair signs off on the final category. Can you draft that procedure and then walk it through those two cases?
