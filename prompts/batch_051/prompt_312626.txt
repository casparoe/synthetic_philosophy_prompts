I'm teaching a graduate seminar on aesthetics, and next week we close the unit on Adorno and Horkheimer's culture industry — the claim, roughly, that mass culture converts difference into an administered repertoire of interchangeable parts, so that the choice, novelty, and individuality consumers experience are mostly prefabricated variations on the same elements: the ever-same marketed as the fresh. I want to end the session not with another summary but with a poem that makes the idea felt.

Please write a sestina in the standard thirty-nine-line form (six six-line stanzas and a three-line envoi that uses all six end-words) about the culture industry. The six end-words should be a small, deliberately ordinary vocabulary of consumer life — words that at first seem to promise freedom of choice but that, as the poem proceeds, reveal themselves as a closed set that can only be permuted. The form itself should carry the argument: across the stanzas the same six terms return in rotated order, so the apparent variety of each new stanza is purely mechanical — new arrangements of old material, with nothing genuinely added. In the envoi, where the six words must crowd into three lines, the poem should land the point that what felt like movement or escape was only compression, not an exit. Stay faithful to the actual diagnosis: no romantic contrast with an older, authentic popular culture, and no consoling wake-up moral, since on this account even the gesture of stepping outside the system can be absorbed back into it as a marketable stance. Someone who knows the theory should recognize it without having the concepts name-checked, and someone who does not should come away with the claustrophobic texture of a world in which infinitely many options are just permutations of the same six things.
