I have a standing policy about my own future choices, and I am not sure whether it is sound epistemic hygiene or a self-protective habit. When a decision is still open to me—whether to accept a job, whether to vote for a measure, whether to take on another graduate student—I refuse to assign a probability to what I will do. If a colleague asks how likely I am to accept a deanship, I will give reasons for and against, but I will not say that I am, say, seventy percent likely to accept. When pressed, I explain that taking up a predictive stance toward my own undecided action feels like trying to observe a choice that is mine to make, not a fact already laid down for me to estimate. I have no parallel reluctance about other people's choices: I am comfortable saying which way a colleague, a student, or an administrator is likely to decide. But when the question is put to me in the first person, the credence refuses to come into focus, and I report only intentions, tendencies, and considerations.

The policy has real costs. It makes me opaque to people who need to plan around me, and I have begun to notice that I invoke it selectively—rarely when I am proud of what I am likely to do, often when I suspect I might not like the answer. There is also a challenge I cannot shrug off: someone with access to my track record might reasonably think I am ninety percent likely to accept an offer that meets all my stated criteria, and it is unclear why I should claim less basis for that judgment than the observer has. So my question is whether this refusal is defensible as a response to the distinctive relation between deliberation and prediction, or whether it is a bias dressed up as a rule. How would I tell the difference from the inside, and is there a restated version of the policy that keeps whatever is valuable in it without the evasion?
