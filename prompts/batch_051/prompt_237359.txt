Before I read further defenses or criticisms of the individualist restriction in Scanlonian contractualism, I want to know what a theory of aggregation in that framework would actually have to do. Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of how contractualist reasoning may and may not combine different people's claims has to deliver. For each requirement, give the case or feature of contractualism that forces it, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable if the view is to remain recognizably contractualist as opposed to consequentialist. In particular, I need you to address the following pressure points: the contrast between aggregating complaints across persons and aggregating harms within one person's life; how to handle large numbers of small benefits or burdens that seem jointly relevant without allowing them to silently outweigh an individual's serious complaint; whether the view can distinguish between aggregating complaints to reject a principle and aggregating harms under an already justified principle; what, if anything, your desiderata require about risk and uncertainty, where each of many people has only a small probability of severe harm; and whether the resulting account must itself be one that no one could reasonably reject, or whether that reflexive requirement creates a problem. End by saying which two desiderata are most likely to be jointly unsatisfiable, and which one does the most work in separating a contractualist account from a straightforward utilitarian or prioritarian aggregation rule.
