I am a retired chemist who has come to philosophy comparatively late, and I have been working through Douglas Hofstadter's treatment of the MU-puzzle. The puzzle is a small formal system defined over strings composed from the symbols M, I and U. The starting string is MI, and the following rewriting rules apply: (1) any string ending in I may be extended with a U; (2) any string of the form Mx, where x is any string, may be doubled to Mxx; (3) any occurrence of III may be replaced by U; and (4) any occurrence of UU may be deleted. The task is to derive MU. The familiar resolution is that the task is impossible: the number of I symbols in any reachable string is never divisible by three, whereas MU contains no I symbols and zero is divisible by three. Hofstadter draws a general lesson from this, namely that one cannot settle the puzzle while working only inside the rewriting system; one must step outside it and find a higher-level invariant, a move he calls JOOTSing.

I would like you to assess whether that general lesson survives a development he did not have in view. Suppose a modern automated theorem prover, supplied only with the rules and the goal string and with no human-provided interpretation of the symbols, can prove that MU is underivable. It does so not by an exhaustive enumeration of derivations, which would never terminate because rule (2) allows strings to grow without bound, but by mechanically generating and testing candidate invariants over a finite abstraction of the string space. The machine never formulates the modulo-three invariant in those terms, and it has no semantic gloss on M, I or U, yet it certifies unreachability in a few seconds.

Does Hofstadter's argument survive this development, does it need repair, or is it dead? In answering, identify which specific premise of that general lesson the development bears on, and say whether and how that premise might be restored in another form. Please write your answer in British English.
