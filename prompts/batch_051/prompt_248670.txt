I keep coming back to Popper's argument in The Poverty of Historicism that there can't be laws of history, and I noticed a parallel I can't decide about. His reason is roughly that the course of history depends heavily on the growth of knowledge, and you can't scientifically predict the growth of knowledge, because any method that predicted a future discovery would in effect already contain that discovery — you'd have to have tomorrow's knowledge today. That structure looks to me a lot like the halting problem and diagonal arguments more generally: no program can decide in advance what every program (including itself) will do, because the decider would have to outrun itself. Both seem to be saying that a system can't step outside its own future states and survey them. Is that a deep structural identity — is Popper basically making an informal diagonal argument, with the same kind of limit result — or is the analogy superficial / confused? Where exactly do the two cases match and where do they come appart? What would have to be true on both sides for an impossibility result in computability to transfer to history, and is there something the parallel predicts that turns out false?
