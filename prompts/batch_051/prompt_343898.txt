Exam question:
In Division One of Being and Time, Heidegger claims that equipment is normally encountered as ready-to-hand, and that we see this most clearly when equipment breaks down. Explain what ready-to-hand means, what the breakdown example is meant to show, and whether a broken hammer becomes present-at-hand. Then explain one way this supports his broader claim that our primary relation to the world is not that of a detached observer.

Student answer:
Ready-to-hand is Heidegger's name for how tools show up when we are absorbed in using them. When im hammering, the hammer is not a separate object I'm inspecting; it's tied into the nails, the wood, and the project I'm working on. It only steps out when something goes wrong, like the hammer is too heavy or the head starts coming loose. At that point the hammer becomes present-at-hand: it turns into a mere thing with determinate properties, which is how science and ordinary looking-at-it both operate. But the disruption also does more than isolate the hammer. It makes the whole network of references go visible at once, the workbench, the materials, what I was trying to do. So breakdown shows that the ready-to-hand world is primary, because the fact that it has to be interrupted before we notice it proves that it was already there doing the real work. Practical absorbed coping comes first, detached staring at objects comes second.
