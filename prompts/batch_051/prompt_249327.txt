I am grading a take-home short-essay question for an upper-level undergraduate course in the philosophy of social action, and I would like a second opinion before I settle on a mark. The question asked students, in under 600 words, to contrast reductive and non-reductive accounts of shared intention, using Michael Bratman and Margaret Gilbert as the central figures (with John Searle optional), and to identify one phenomenon each side claims its own account better explains. I have reproduced the question and one student's answer below. Please assign the answer a grade, on a letter scale or out of 10, and provide the point-by-point comments that justify that grade: what the answer gets right, where it is precise or vague, and any places where a plausible-sounding sentence in fact misstates a philosopher's view. Grade the philosophical content rather than the prose, and do not award credit merely for the presence of the right terminology.

Question: Compare and contrast reductive and non-reductive accounts of shared intention, taking Bratman and Gilbert as your central examples. In your answer: (1) explain what it would be for a shared intention to be reducible to individual attitudes plus common knowledge; (2) state one core condition that each of Bratman's and Gilbert's accounts places on shared intention; and (3) identify at least one phenomenon that each side claims its own account better explains. Define any technical terms you use.

Student's answer: Reductive accounts, such as Bratman's, treat a shared intention as a complex of individual attitudes rather than an attitude of any group subject. On Bratman's view, our shared intention to paint the house together consists roughly in this: each of us intends that we paint the house, each intends her own contribution partly because it meshes with the other's subplans, the subplans in fact mesh, and these facts are common knowledge between us. Bratman's motivation, on this account, is to show how shared agency can be understood using only the planning capacities of individual agents, without positing an irreducible group mind. Non-reductive accounts deny that such a complex of interlocking individual attitudes is sufficient. Searle, for example, argues that mutual individual intentions and mutual knowledge could be exactly as described and yet the participants need not count as collectively intending the action; what is missing is a we-intention, a primitive form of intentionality individuals can possess and whose content presents the action as ours. Gilbert's joint-commitment account is standardly classified as another non-reductive view. According to Gilbert, each participant forms a personal commitment, conditional on the others doing the same, to carry through the joint project should the others hold up their ends. She emphasizes that such commitments explain the obligations and entitlements characteristic of shared activity: if we agree to walk together, each of us acquires the standing to rebuke the other for breaking off without excuse. Thus, reductive accounts advertise parsimony and continuity with individual psychology, whereas non-reductive accounts advertise fidelity to the normative ties and the sense of acting as a body. On reflection, however, Gilbert's position may be closer to Bratman's than this contrast suggests, since both can ultimately be cashed out in terms of conditional personal commitments plus common knowledge.
