I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology and I've been chewing on a weird asymmetry in how we treat certainty about mathematical claims, and I want someone to tell me wether it's principled or just a double standard we've all agreed not to notice.

Take two claims. First: 2 + 2 = 4. Second: every even integer greater than 2 is the sum of two primes (Goldbach's conjecture, which, as far as I know, is still open). If a very reliable colleague told me she was 99% confident Goldbach is true, I'd find that completely sensible — in fact I'd probably say something similar myself. But if she told me she was 99% confident that 2 + 2 = 4, I'd think something had gone wrong with her, or that she was making a strange point about Cartesian doubt. Why not just be certain?

The thing is, both claims are true-or-false in a way that doesn't depend on anything empirical. There's no possible observation that could split the difference between them; the only thing that could settle either one is a proof or a counterexample. So the only real difference I can locate is that one derivation is short enough to see all at once and the other is nowhere in sight — that is, the difference is about how much computation would have to happen between the axioms and the result, and whether anyone has actually done it. But why should that difference show up in your credences? An ideal reasoner with unlimited compute would just assign 1 to every theorem and 0 to every falsehood (what people call the logical omniscience problem), which would make my colleague's 99% on Goldbach as irrational as her 99% on 2 + 2 = 4. So either the idealization is doing too much work, or our ordinary practice of having intermediate logical uncertainty about hard math is a concession to being bounded that we then dress up as epistemic virtue.

That's the question: is this asymmetry justified? Is there a principled account on which being 99% on Goldbach is rational specifically because the derivation is long or unverified, while being 99% on 2 + 2 = 4 is not — or is the asymmetry just where we happen to draw the line between "math we've internalized" and "math we haven't"? I don't want a literature review and I don't want you to cite anyone; I know there's a logical-induction literature and I'm deliberately setting it aside so you reason it through from the tension itself.

Also, if your answer is that we should reject the idealization and accept logical uncertainty as genuine, tell me whether there's any residual sense in which someone is being irrational by assigning 99.999% but not 100% to 2 + 2 = 4, and if not, why that case feels so different to everyone.

Structure your answer in this order:

1. The asymmetry, restated — restate the tension in your own words and flag anything you think I've misdescribed (one paragraph).
2. Saving the distinction — the strongest case that something non-arbitrary separates the two cases, even though both are mathematical claims (2–3 paragraphs).
3. The cost of keeping it — the strongest case that this is really just a computational concession with no genuine epistemic basis, and that our norm against doubting simple arithmetic is just a heuristic (2–3 paragraphs).
4. My ruling — whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned, and what you'd replace it with as a standard for credences about logical or mathematical claims (2–3 paragraphs).
