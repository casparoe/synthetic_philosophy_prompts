I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and I've been trying to settle what Kant's universalizability test actually amounts to by testing my own verdicts on particular maxims against it, but the verdicts are now pulling against the test rather than being explained by it. I take the test to say that I may act on a maxim only if I could consistently will that everyone act on the same maxim, and I have been assuming there are two ways this can fail: the universalized maxim either destroys the practice or arrangement on which the original maxim depends, or it leaves me unable to will the resulting world without making an exception of myself. My cases do not line up neatly behind that distinction.

Here are the cases. (1) Borrowing money I know I will not repay by promising to repay it: clearly impermissible, and the failure is of the first kind, since a world in which everyone promised with no intention to repay would make promising itself unintelligible. (2) A comfortably well-off person refusing to help a stranger in an emergency at small cost to himself: impermissible, but only on the second kind of ground; I can conceive of the world, I just cannot will it, because in it my own needs would meet the same indifference. (3) Letting a rare talent for languages go completely undeveloped: impermissible, again only on the second ground, and only because someone generally cannot be an effective agent while letting every significant capacity rust. (4) Lying to the armed man who knocks at my door demanding to know where my friend is hiding so he can kill him: permissible, and I think the test should let it through. (5) Quietly keeping the extra change when a busy cashier hands me too much: wrong, but I cannot find either of the two failures; I can conceive of a world of people keeping accidental overpayments, and I am not obviously unable to will it, yet the verdict stays. (6) Breaking a trivial promise, to return a borrowed shirt on Tuesday, because a friend needs a last-minute ride to the airport: permissible, though it is still a broken promise.

The pair I cannot reconcile is (1) and (4): both involve saying something false to someone whose plans depend on my words, yet the first seems to be a paradigm case of what the test forbids and the fourth seems to be something the test would be monstrous to forbid. Is there a principle — perhaps a more careful version of the distinction between contradiction in conception and contradiction in willing — that captures all six verdicts, or nearly all of them? What would that principle say about a case I have not settled: submitting a paper written for me by someone else in a course I do not care about, where everyone's doing it would destroy the point of assessment? And which of my verdicts, if any, would the cleanest principle force me to give up? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the principle or set of candidate principles — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
