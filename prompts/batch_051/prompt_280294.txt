Having retired from a career in industrial chemistry, I am comparatively new to academic philosophy, and I keep being struck by the fact that the unilateralist's curse is usually discussed in the abstract rather than treated as a design problem. As I understand it, the curse is this: when any member of a group may act alone on a matter that affects everyone, the group is in effect governed by its most optimistic member, because the first person to become overconfident that the action is worthwhile can set it off even when the pooled judgment of the group would have blocked it. I would like you to analyze a mechanism I have sketched for our local laboratory collective, whose members work with engineered organisms and where a single reckless release could impose irreversible costs on everyone.

The proposed rule is as follows. Before a member may perform any procedure that is reasonably expected to be irreversible outside the laboratory — a release, a disposal into shared water, a shipment to an outside collaborator — she must obtain the signed assent of two reviewers drawn at random from a standing pool of members who have completed a safety-training course. To each reviewer she must show (a) her own written estimate of the probability and severity of the principal failure modes of the procedure, and (b) the corresponding estimate she would have made before she became interested in running the experiment, reconstructed from her laboratory notebook. Reviewers are paid a fixed fee regardless of whether they sign or refuse, and their decision, with reasons, is published to the membership after a one-year delay. A refusal does not veto the procedure; it merely moves the proposal to a vote of the full membership, where a two-thirds majority is required to proceed.

Walk through how this arrangement would actually behave. In particular: how do random selection and the flat fee shape who enters the reviewer pool, who is selected in practice, and what they sign? What incentives does the requirement of a pre-interest estimate create for notebook-keeping, self-presentation, and for members who deliberately avoid writing down early estimates? Does a refusal that triggers only a vote strengthen or weaken the protection against the curse? Where is the scheme gamed first, and what second-order effects follow once everyone understands that a refusal is not a veto? Close by saying whether its main failure modes can be repaired without making the procedure so burdensome that members begin doing riskier work in secret. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
