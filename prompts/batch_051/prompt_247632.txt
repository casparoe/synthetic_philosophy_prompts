I teach a seminar on self-sufficiency and autarky, and I've been re-reading Aristotle's Politics, Book VII, where he fixes a limit to the size of a state. He writes:

"Similarly a state consisting of too few people will not be self-sufficing （which is an essential quality of a state）, and one consisting of too many, though self-sufficing in the mere necessaries, will be so in the way in which a nation is, and not as a state, since it will not be easy for it to possess constitutional government—for who will command its over-swollen multitude in war? or who will serve as its herald, unless he have the lungs of a Stentor?"

What strikes me is how foreign the argument is to modern debates: his worry is not about dependence on foreign suppliers or access to food and energy, but about whether a political community can still be a constitutional community once it gets too big. Please describe the most important implications of this passage for how we should think about contemporary appeals to national self-sufficiency—food security, "strategic autonomy," reshoring manufacturing, energy independence—and maybe also for personal self-sufficiency as an ideal. I'm especially interested in whether Aristotle gives us any reason to distinguish kinds of self-sufficiency, say mere material self-sufficiency from the self-sufficiency of a community that can actually govern itself, which contemporary debates tend to run together. Please seperate what is fairly settled in Aristotle scholarship from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
