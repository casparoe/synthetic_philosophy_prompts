I've been turning over an idea about money and would like you to analyze it as a mechanism: what it would actually do, not whether it sounds sensible. Imagine a small, isolated country that uses no metal coin and where no foreign money circulates. It sets up a public bank that issues paper notes, and the bank may issue notes in only one way: by lending them to landowners, up to two-thirds of the value of their land as assessed by local officials, at a low fixed interest rate payable in the same notes, with the land as security. The notes can never be redeemed for gold or silver. The government accepts them for taxes; private parties may take or refuse them in trade as they please. If a borrower stops paying, the bank seizes the land and auctions it for its own notes, then retires the notes it receives. I later read that Bishop Berkeley defended something along these lines, which is why I keep worrying at it. Walk me through how the setup would actually behave: what it leads landowners, assessors, the bank's managers, and ordinary traders to do; where it is likely to get gamed first and what that does to the notes' value; and whether the failure modes can be patched without giving up the basic design, or whether one of them is fatal. Write in American English. End with a final line beginning 'Summary:' that gives your overall assessment in one sentence.
