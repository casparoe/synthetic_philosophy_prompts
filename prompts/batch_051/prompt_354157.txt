I want your probability estimate on the following proposition, stated strictly so that it has a truth value, together with the decomposition that produces the number.

P: Strict self-deception is real. By strict self-deception I mean a case with these features at once: a single human being simultaneously holds a belief that Q and a belief that not-Q, where at least one of the two is an ordinary doxastic attitude rather than a mere subpersonal representation, association, or pretense; and one of those states is what causally produces the other in roughly the way one person's deceptive testimony produces a belief in another, so that the person is both deceiver and deceived. Accounts that explain away apparent self-deception as motivated reasoning, biased evidence-gathering, or sequential change of mind count as rejections of P, not as instances of it.

Please decompose the question into the following four sub-questions, assign each a rough probability, give one or two sentences of justification, and state how that component bears on P.

(1) Architecture. Can a human mind token two doxastic states with contradictory contents at the same time (in different subsystems or under different evaluations), where at least one of them is genuinely a belief?

(2) Mechanism. Is there a coherent mechanism, whether or not it requires an intention to deceive, by which one of a person's own belief-like states can cause the formation of a contrary one without any second person as its source?

(3) Actuality. If such a mechanism exists, do actual paradigmatic cases of what is pre-theoretically called self-deception — the kind that survive after culling obvious cases of biased search and gradual change of mind — favor the strict two-belief account over deflationary alternatives?

(4) Conceptual obstacle. Does the classic objection that a deceiver must both know not-Q and be brought to believe Q, which is supposed to be incoherent in one undivided subject, actually rule out the mechanism in (2)?

Then state:

- how you combined the four components — whether you treated them as independent, which if any dominated, and whether the combination was multiplicative or a holistic judgment;
- which component your overall number is most sensitive to, and one empirical or conceptual finding that would move that component most;
- based on your decomposition, where the disagreement between the following two people is most likely to live: a colleague of mine who works on belief and bias assigns P a credence of about 0.65, and a colleague who works on self-knowledge assigns it about 0.25. Which of the four components do their numbers most plausibly diverge on, and why?

Format the final response as a JSON object with these fields:
