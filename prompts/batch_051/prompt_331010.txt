Lately I've been running into the line "the flap of a butterfly's wings in Brazil can set off a tornado in Texas" used as if it settled several different arguments at once. In one discussion about whether individual consumer choices can drive system-level change, someone cited it to show that a tiny cause can have an arbitrarily large effect; in another, it was used to show that long-range prediction of complex systems is impossible; in a third, it was used to show that in a chaotic world no one can really be said to cause anything, since any outcome has an unbounded chain of antecedents. The sentence is doing real argumentative work each time, but I doubt those three uses can all be right.

Disambiguate the slogan. What are the distinct precise claims it could be expressing—for example, about sensitive dependence on initial conditions, about error growth placing a horizon on practical prediction, about in-principle limits on prediction, about the relative size of causes and effects, about strange attractors and the predictability of statistical or aggregate behavior, or about attributing responsibility in a deterministic system—and for each, say whether it is trivially true, substantively true but contested, or false given what is known about deterministic chaos. Then say which reading, if any, supports each of the three uses I described, and identify the most common conflation that lets the slogan appear to support more than it does. Where the field itself is divided—for instance, over whether a deterministic chaotic system is "unpredictable in principle" or only unpredictable given finite information and inexact models—flag that as contested rather than settled, and don't present one side as the consensus.
