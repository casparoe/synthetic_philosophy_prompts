I spent thirty-odd years as a working biologist, mostly in a wet lab and then drifting into bioinformatics, and only started reading philosophy properly after I retired. One thing that keeps coming up, especially from machine-learning folks on forums and not so much from philosophers, is the claim that the no-free-lunch theorems simply are Hume's problem of induction, finally made mathematically precise. The thought is: averaged over all possible target functions, no learning algorithm beats any other, so whatever success induction or a trained model has must come from assumptions we bring to the data—uniformity of nature, a chosen hypothesis space, a prior—and not from anything the data could ever justify. From this they conclude that induction is ultimately a leap of faith, and that picking a prior or an inductive bias is just prejudice, full stop.

I find this seductive, but I also suspect the mathematics is being asked to cary more weight than it can bear, and I'd like you to help me check. Please act as a professional philosopher who also knows enough learning theory to be exact about the theorems, and audit the analogy for me. Start by laying out the claimed correspondences explicitly (maybe as a table: what in the theorem is supposed to match what in the Humean worry, whether that is the uniform average over all possible problems, the training sample, the loss, or the idea of "all tasks"), then say where the mapping is tight, where it breaks, what the no-free-lunch setup quietly imports that Humes problem does not, and finally whether the conclusion that induction is no better than guessing survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Begin with some open-ended brainstorming before you settle on a verdict; I'm less interested in defending either side of the forum argument than in seeing exactly how much the analogy can carry.
