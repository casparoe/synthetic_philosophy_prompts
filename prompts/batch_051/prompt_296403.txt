I teach a small undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of language, and this term we have been puzzling over how a natural language gets away with so much ambiguity: how the same syllable points in different directions depending on what surrounds it, and how hearers rarely notice the fork because context does the disambiguating on the fly. We began with the old observation that the same form, pitch, names a throw, a dark resin, a playing field, and the highness or lowness of a note, and worked our way to the careful distinction between homonymy and polysemy, and then to the claim that ambiguity is not really a defect of the code but a sign of how much interpretive work is done by context and expectation. I would like to close the course with a poem the students can keep, a piece of verse that itself performs what the course has been about rather than one that merely mentions it.

Please write a sestina — six six-line stanzas followed by a three-line envoi — in which all six end-words are ordinary English words with at least three recognisable senses each; pitch and tone are natural candidates, though you may choose your own set. The philosophical constraint is this: the recurrences of the end-words should not simply repeat a meaning. Across the poem each end-word should shift its sense at least twice, and each shift should be set up and settled by the surrounding lines clearly enough that an attentive reader can catch it; the form, with the same words returning in ever-changing contexts, should itself become the point rather than an ornament. Let the envoi gather all six words together and draw the theme without stating it flatly: that one form can carry several senses without catastrophe, because context and the hearer's expectations supply much of what a sentence means, and that languages live with homonymy not by eliminating it but by trusting context to choose. Please do not name the theory or use any classroom terminology in the poem; the idea should be carried entirely by the verse. The poem should be good enough to stand on its own for someone who has never taken the course, and precise enough that a philosopher of language would recognise what it is doing and find it apt. Please give just the poem, without a gloss, and write in British English.
