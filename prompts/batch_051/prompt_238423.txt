The question on the problem set was:

On the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov (BHK) interpretation, what is a proof of a disjunction A ∨ B, a proof of an existential statement ∃n∈ℕ P(n), and a proof of a negation ¬A? Using those clauses, explain why the schema A ∨ ¬A is not generally constructively acceptable even though A → ¬¬A is. Then consider the following claim: if P is a decidable predicate on the natural numbers and a constructive mathematician has proved ¬¬∃n∈ℕ P(n), they are entitled to assert ∃n∈ℕ P(n) and extract a witness. Does this claim follow from the BHK interpretation? If an additional principle would be needed to license it, name or describe that principle and explain its computational content.

The student's answer was:

Under BHK, a proof of A ∨ B is a proof of one of the disjuncts; a proof of ∃n P(n) is a specific natural number n together with a proof of P(n); and a proof of ¬A is a construction that turns any proof of A into a proof of a contradiction.

Since asserting A ∨ ¬A for arbitrary A would require a general method that, for any proposition A, either proves A or turns every proof of A into a contradiction, and no such universal method is available, A ∨ ¬A is not generally constructively acceptable. By contrast, A → ¬¬A is valid: from a proof of A, we build a proof of ¬¬A by taking any supposed proof of ¬A — that is, any construction from proofs of A to a contradiction — and applying it to our proof of A, yielding a contradiction.

For the claim about ¬¬∃n P(n): suppose, for contradiction, that ¬∃n P(n) holds. Then since ¬¬∃n P(n) asserts precisely that ¬∃n P(n) leads to a contradiction, our supposition yields a contradiction. By reductio ad absurdum, this proves ∃n P(n). Once the existential is proved, the witness can be extracted by the usual effective search: check P(0), P(1), P(2), and so on. Because P is decidable and we have proved that some n with P(n) exists, the search cannot fail to terminate, and the first n found is the required witness. Thus the claim follows from the BHK interpretation alone; no further principle is needed.
