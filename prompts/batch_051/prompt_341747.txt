Assignment. In his Letter to Menoeceus, Epicurus writes:

Accustom thyself to believe that death is nothing to us, for good and evil imply sentience, and death is the privation of all sentience; therefore a right understanding that death is nothing to us makes the mortality of life enjoyable, not by adding to life an illimitable time, but by taking away the yearning after immortality.

Explain the argument in this passage, and then assess whether it gives us a good reason to think that death is not bad for the person who dies. In your answer, distinguish the argument from the mere claim that being dead will not be painful, and say whether the argument depends on hedonism.

Student's answer:

Epicurus's argument is that something can be good or evil for me only if it can affect my sentience—that is, only if I can experience something pleasant or painful. Since death is the total cessation of sentience, there is no longer anyone who can be benefited or harmed. So death is not bad for the person who dies. This is different from the weaker claim that being dead isn't painful, because Epicurus isn't just saying the corpse feels nothing; he's saying the very conditions for something's being good or bad for a person disappear. The last sentence of the passage shows his real aim is therapeutic: by removing the desire for immortality, the correct view of death makes mortal life enjoyable. On the question of hedonism, Epicurus does not actually need to be a hedonist here. Even if one thinks value is plural, as long as one accepts that the subject of value must exist, the same conclusion follows. The argument is strong, but it fails in one respect: it only looks at the time after death. It says nothing about the deprivation that death involves. If I die tomorrow, I lose all the goods I would otherwise have had, and this loss can be bad for me even though I never experience it. Similarly, I can be harmed now by events I never experience, like being betrayed by a friend behind my back. So Epicurus's argument is too narrow to refute the deprivation view.
