Act as a professor of practical rationality who knows both the philosophical literature on planning agency and the empirical literature on goal pursuit and naturalistic decision-making. I am an undergraduate taking a seminar on rationality and agency, and next week I present a short paper defending this claim: for an individual agent facing a novel task in a fast-changing environment, detailed up-front planning is systematically overrated relative to improvisation built on trained capacities, and much of the apparent value of planning is a retrospective evaluation bias — we credit plans for successes that actually came from mid-course adjustment and blame improvisation for failures no plan would have avoided. The audience matters: three philosophy students are committed to a broadly Bratmanian planning theory of intention; two psychology students work on implementation intentions and the empirical goal-pursuit literature; the convenor works in naturalistic decision-making and is suspicious of laboratory findings. Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from this audience, roughly ordered by likelihood or damage, phrased in their vocabulary, each with a short reply, a note on what to concede and what to hold, and where my reply is weakest. Also tell me the one question I should be praying nobody asks. Begin by brainstorming likely objections before settling on your list, think carefully about the reasoning, and throughout separate what is empirically settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature — do not present a contested position as the consensus.
