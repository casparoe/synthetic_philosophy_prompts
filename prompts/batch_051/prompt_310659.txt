i'm giving a paper at a political theory workshop next month. the paper defends a reading of rousseau's forced to be free passage — the bit in book i, chapter 7 where he says anyone who refuses to obey the general will will be compelled by the whole body, and that this is nothing other than being forced to be free. my thesis is that this isn't a licence for the state to coerce dissenters, but a point about how a citizen stays free even when outvoted, because the law expresses the general will, which is their own will as a citizen rather than their particular will as a private person. the audience is mostly analytic political philosophers who work on democratic theory and public reason, plus a couple of historians of early modern political thought. they'll know The Social Contract, but they've also read Constant and Berlin and will come in treating Rousseau as the slippery slope to authoritarianism. prepare me for the q&a. what are the seven or so objections i'm actually going to get from this audience, phrased the way they'd put them, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are? for each give me a short reply, a note on where the reply is softest, and which bits i should concede up front rather than fight. include the question i'm hoping nobody asks. my argument in a nutshell: (1) the general will is not the sum of particular wills; (2) when i'm compelled to obey a law i voted against, i'm not being coerced by another private person, i'm being held to the general will, which is my own will qua citizen; (3) the force is the community preventing me from following my private interest in a way that would undermine the conditions of my own freedom. i know step (2) is the one they'll attack; that's fine, but show me exactly how they'll attack it. answer in British English.
