I host a small audio-only podcast about the psychology of reasoning, and next week's guest is a cognitive psychologist whose new book argues that the last twenty years of enthusiasm for metacognition—think-aloud protocols, self-explanation, accuracy-of-confidence training, teaching students to monitor their own understanding—is mostly built on confounds. Her view is that once you control for extra time on task and extra retrieval practice, there's surprisingly little evidence that thinking about your thinking itself does the work, and in a handful of well-known cases its actively makes people worse.

Write me eight to ten interview questions that push past the book's headline claim and force her to defend the strong version. I want at least one question each on: verbal overshadowing, where describing a face or other perceptual stimulus later impairs recognizing it; choking under pressure, where explicit monitoring of a well-practiced skill disrupts performance; rumination being a risk factor for depression and whether she thinks that counts as metacognition or is stretching the term; the apparently positive effects of metacognitive calibration training on learning; and where she parts company with clinicians who treat rumination by changing patients' beliefs about their own thinking. For each question, write the follow-up you'd ask if she gives the answer she's most likely to give—not a generic "could you say more," but the specific probe that follows from that likely answer. For example, if she says self-explanation helps only because it forces extra encoding, ask her what a genuinely metacognitive intervention would have to look like for her to count it as beneficial. End with a question about what sort of evidence would actually move her off the view, or at least shrink its scope.
