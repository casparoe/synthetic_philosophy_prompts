I am a novelist writing a contemporary novel whose protagonist, a lapsed Catholic journalist, inherits her dead uncle's archive of claimed miracles. To make the argument she wrestles with as sharp as I can before I put it on the page, I want to converse with the thinker who formulated it. Please play David Hume, speaking in the first person, as if he were alive today. Assume someone has already explained to you the rudiments of smartphones, security cameras, digital video, image manipulation, and social media; if you need another fact about the modern world, ask for it as Hume would. Stay in character until I say "stop." Reply in one paragraph at a time, in clear present-day English. Do not quote or rehearse your own books; reason the position through afresh. You are not playing a cynic who despises all testimony: you grant that most of what we believe about the world rests on it. Your target is the specific claim that testimony or recordings can establish a violation of a law of nature. Concede small points when they do not touch that target; do not concede the target.

Here is my opening move. As I understand you, testimony for a miracle deserves belief only where it would be an even greater miracle for the witnesses to be lying or mistaken than for the event to have occurred, and by this standard no miracle claim in history has yet come close. Now take a present-day case. For the past six Sundays, at noon, a man has walked across the Thames and back, from the South Bank to Westminster, in broad daylight before large crowds. Passersby and police cameras have filmed it from several angles; forensic analysts have examined the footage; the man has been searched; no wires, no hidden platform, and no trace of digital alteration have been found. Thousands of mutually unacquainted witnesses, including skeptics who came intending to expose the event, give the same account. I am not asking you to concede that the event happened. I am asking what your principle dictates about this body of evidence. Does it now pass your test? If not, what further evidence would? And if no amount of testimony and recording, short of seeing the event with your own eyes, could ever pass, is your principle still an empirical check on testimony, or has it become a settled refusal to be surprised?
