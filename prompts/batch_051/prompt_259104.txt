I've been going back and forth on something after watching a documentary about the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission. A former security policeman describes, in flat detail, how he helped abduct and kill a political activist, and because he makes a full disclosure and shows the acts were tied to his work for the apartheid state, he is granted amnesty and leaves the hearing a free man. But if the same man did the same thing to a shopkeeper in a parking garage five years after apartheid—to collect a private debt or settle a grudge—no one would accept his truthful confession as a reason to drop the charges. We'd call that deal corrupt. The underlying acts are the same: abduction, torture, murder. What changes is that in one case the violence served a political cause and the confession was made to a commission rather than a prosecutor.

So I'm trying to figure out wether anything actually justifies that difference. Is politically motivated violence less culpable, or is it just that there was too much of it to prosecute? Is truth somehow more valuable than punishment when the crime is political, but not when it is ordinary? Or is the commission just a way of paying for information with impunity because the alternative—mass trials, maybe renewed conflict—was worse? I want the strongest principled case you can build for the asymmetry, tested against the parking-garage version of the same act, and an honest verdict on wether to keep it or call it an inconsistency we live with.
