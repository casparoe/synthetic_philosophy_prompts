Question: State van Fraassen's Reflection principle as precisely as you can. Explain the main argument van Fraassen gives for it, and say how the principle relates to — and differs from — both Bayesian conditionalization and Lewis's Principal Principle. Finally, say whether Reflection should be treated as a universal requirement of rationality.

Student answer:

Reflection is a diachronic coherence constraint on an agent's credences over time. Let P_t be my credence at time t and P_{t+1} my credence at a later time. The principle says that, for any proposition A and any value x, P_t(A | P_{t+1}(A) = x) = x, whenever the conditional probability is defined. In words: if I learn that I will assign x to A later, I should assign x to A now. Equivalently, my current credence in A should equal the expected value of my future credence in A.

Van Fraassen's main argument is a diachronic Dutch book. If I violate Reflection, a bookie who knows my current and future credences can offer me a series of bets, some accepted now and some accepted later, such that I suffer a sure net loss no matter how the world turns out. For instance, if I now assign 0.6 to A while being certain that tomorrow I will assign 0.3 to A, the bookie can sell me a bet on A at my current 0.6 price and then buy it back from me tomorrow at my new 0.3 price; I'm out 0.3 either way. Violating Reflection is therefore a kind of incoherence, the diachronic analogue of violating the probability axioms.

Reflection is closely related to conditionalization. In fact, they are essentially the same norm viewed from two directions: conditionalization tells you how your credences should move when new evidence arrives, and Reflection just restates that same requirement as a constraint on your current opinion about your own future opinion. So an agent who satisfies Reflection will update by conditionalization, and vice versa.

Reflection is also the diachronic cousin of Lewis's Principal Principle. The Principal Principle tells you to defer to objective chance, whereas Reflection tells you to defer to your own future self, who functions as an expert relative to your present self.

Because the Dutch book argument goes through for any violation whatever, Reflection is a universal requirement of rationality: any agent who fails to satisfy it is incoherent across the board.
