so in class we learned about stigler's law, the idea that basically no scientific discovery gets named after the person who actualy discovered it. and the law is kind of a joke because stigler himself says he got it from the sociologist merton, so the law applies to itself. anyway it seems to capture something real about how credit works in science, and i want to stress-test it on a case i know a bit about: halley's comet.

halley didn't discover the comet. people had been recording it for ages — like it shows up on the bayeux tapestry from 1066, centuries before him. what he did was notice that the comet he saw in 1682 looked like the same object recorded in 1531 and 1607, and he predicted it would come back in 1758, which it did, after he was dead. so he got the name not for discovering it but for predicting it.

analyze this case through stigler's law. does the law actually explain why the comet is named after halley, or does it kinda fail here because the name is arguably earned, just for a different achievement than 'discovery'? and then run it the other way: what does this case show about the law itself — is stigler's law a real claim about how credit gets assigned in science, or is it just a pattern that only looks true because we notice the outrageous cases and ignore all the boring ones where the name is basically right? i want the reasoning to be visible, not just a verdict.
