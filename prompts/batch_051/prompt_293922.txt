I'm doing a linguistics unit on sign languages, and the lecturer has been building a running puzzle all term. She keeps giving us examples and her verdict on whether each one is a 'full human language' or 'just a communication system', but she refuses to state her criterion — she says we should reverse-engineer it and she'll confirm or deny at the end. She's also a bit idiosynchratic, so I wouldn't trust the textbook definition to predict her. A couple of us in the seminar have a small bet on whether she'll surprise us next week. Her record so far, as best I've noted them:

- American Sign Language: language.
- British Sign Language: language, and clearly distinct from ASL.
- Nicaraguan Sign Language: language, even though it only emerged once deaf children were brought together in a school a few decades ago.
- A single deaf child's home sign, worked out with hearing parents and used only by that household: not a language.
- A pidgin used in a market, no native speakers, simplified grammar, adults using it for limited trade: not a language.
- Haitian Creole, which grew out of a pidgin but now has native speakers and a full grammar: language.
- Written English: not a language, just a way of recording one.
- Silbo Gomero, the whistled form of Spanish used on La Gomera: not a language, a mode of Spanish.
- Esperanto: language, despite being invented.
- Koko the gorilla's signing: not a language.

Next week she's going to give us a new case and make us commit to a prediction before she announces her own verdict. The case is a signed lingua franca used across a large area, mostly by hearing people, for trade, diplomacy and storytelling between communities whose spoken languages are mutually unintelligible. It is not derived from any surrounding spoken language; it has its own word order and spatial grammar; some children in the region learned it from birth, though alongside a spoken language rather than instead of one; and it was passed down over several generations.

Act as an expert on sign language linguistics and on the theoretical question of what separates a langauge from other communication systems. Think carefully, maybe start by brainstorming the candidate rules that fit her record so far. Then give me: your best prediction of her verdict on the signed lingua franca and why; which earlier case is the closest analogue; and which two or three candidate rules genuinely diverge on this new case. Finally, suggest one further case, real or invented, that would most cleanly tell the most plausible rules apart, and say what each would predict. I'm less interested in the label she'd choose than in the rule she seems to be running, and in where her track record stops determining an answer.
