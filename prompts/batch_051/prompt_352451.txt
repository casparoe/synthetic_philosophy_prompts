I'm drafting a paper on an idea I keep returning to: whether people or groups with deeply different value systems can make something like gains from trade through moral compromise—where each side ends up, judged by its own lights, better off than under the status quo, even though nobody has changed their mind about the underlying values. The concrete case I keep describing to friends is a school curriculum negotiated between families who disagree about what children should be taught: neither family gives up its convictions, each yields on something it cares about, and both prefer the resulting arrangement to no agreement at all. The trouble is that I cannot get the argument off the ground without a working definition of the compromise itself, because it is silently doing many different jobs in my draft.

Define 'compromise' in its moral-political sense so that it handles these seven cases: two neighbors who accept a fence line neither exactly asked for; a minority party that votes for a bill it dislikes in exchange for one it values more; a scientist who signs a consensus statement much weaker than her actual view; a person who does something she regards as morally wrong to prevent a greater wrong; a dissident who publicly recants under threat while privately keeping her beliefs; a union and an employer splitting the difference on wages; and a judge who imposes an equal division of a child's time without regard to either parent's proposal. My rough sense: the fence line and the wage split are clear compromises, the judge's ruling clearly isn't because no one conceded anything, and the dissident looks like coercion rather than compromise; the scientist and the conscientious wrongdoer are the two I keep reversing myself on.

Propose the definition—or, if the word does two jobs, the definitions—and run every case through it. Say plainly where the definition gives the wrong answer, where it gives no answer, and whether the surprising verdicts should be defended or the definition revised. Then close the loop: given your definition, does the gains-from-trade picture of moral compromise rest on a procedural notion of mutual concession, on an evaluative notion of a desirable achievement in pluralistic life, or on something else? I need that last part because I want to make sure the paper's central argument and its key term are not secretly pulling apart from each other.
