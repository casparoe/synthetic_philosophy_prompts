Something a bit different from my usual stuff. I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the ethics of memory, and I keep swinging between Avishai Margalit and David Rieff on whether forgetting is ever the right response to a painful past. The way I'd summarise the divide: Margalit holds that there are real obligations to remember—above all to remember particular people and the victims of great evil—because remembering is bound up with care, respect, and the 'thick' relations that make a life moral. Forgetting a person isn't just losing data; it's a failure of care. Rieff thinks the modern obsession with remembrance is overdone, that memory can be narcissistic or can feed grievance and cycles of violence, and that forgetting is sometimes exactly what lets individuals and communities live on. I'm flattening both, I know. Its probably unfair to Rieff especially.

Anyway, a friend suggested I rewatch Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind through this argument, and now I want a proper critical reading rather than my own reactions. In case the film isn't fresh for you: a couple have the memory of their relationship surgically erased after a breakup, and midway through the procedure Joel decides he doesn't want to let Clementine go. Can you read the film through the Margalit/Rieff debate? Specifically: where does the Lacuna procedure look like a violation of a duty to remember, and where does it look like defensible or even healing forgetting? Does the film side with either position, or does it use the relationship—the early days, Joel trying to hide Clementine inside his other memories, the beach house, and especially the ending with the tapes—to complicate both? I'm open to the idea that the ending is deliberately ambivalent, and maybe that's the point. I'd like the reading to be faithful in both directions: don't reduce the film to an illustration of the philosophy, and don't force Margalit or Rieff into shapes the film happens to fit; say outright where the film resists each of them. And tell me what the film adds to the philosophical debate that a straight essay doesn't. I'm thinking of using this in a postgrad seminar or maybe a conference paper, so feel free to be a bit in the weeds.
