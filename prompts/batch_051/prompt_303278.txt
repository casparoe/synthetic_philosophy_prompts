I'm TA-ing an undergrad elective that's basically philosophy of language and trust, and I got roped into helping grade the final while the professor is away. I wrote this short-answer question:

In "On Bullshit," Frankfurt distinguishes bullshit from lying in terms of the speaker's relationship to the truth. Explain that distinction, and why he takes bullshit to be a greater enemy of truth than lying. Then apply the framework to the fluent but fabricated statements produced by large language models (often called "hallucinations"). On Frankfurt's view, are they best classified as lies, bullshit, or simple error? Be explicit about whose speech act is being classified: the model, the developer, or the user.

A student submitted this answer:

Frankfurt's central move is that both the liar and the truthful speaker are oriented toward the truth: the truthful speaker tries to state it, the liar tries to hide it, and that's why lying requires the liar to know (or take themself to know) the truth and to intend to mislead. The bullshitter is different. Their utterances are cut loose from any concern about whether things are as they say; the bullshitter doesn't try to tell the truth or to conceal it, but says whatever suits the goal of shaping how the audience sees them, or just filling the conversational space. That's why Frankfurt thinks bullshit is a bigger enemy of truth than lying: the liar still has to track the truth to avoid it, but the bullshitter ignores it altogether. Error sits beside these categories, not inside them: a person who forms a false belief while genuinely aiming at the truth is simply mistaken.

On this picture, an LLM cannot be lying, since it has no beliefs and no intention to deceive. The right classification is bullshit. The model produces strings of text that are indifferent to whether their content is true or false, because it is optimized for plausible continuation rather than accuracy, and hallucinations are just a downstream consequence. The model can also produce errors; when it does it is simply making a mistake, but the broader phenomenon is still bullshit, since the model is not even trying. If we want to assign responsibility, it falls on the developers or users who deploy the system in contexts where its guesses are presented as findings.
