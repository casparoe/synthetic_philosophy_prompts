I'm working through a question in the philosophy of anti-discrimination law, and I've found that the familiar rationales for giving a characteristic protected status—immutability, visibility, a history of group subordination, irrelevance to legitimate decision-making—pull apart as soon as one moves beyond the canonical cases. I would like you to act as an arbitrator.

Suppose a jurisdiction is drafting an equal-treatment statute from scratch and must decide which individual characteristics warrant a heightened justificatory burden when used as a basis for distinction in employment, housing, credit, and public services. Set aside what any actual statute currently lists; I am interested in the principle, not the positive law. Consider these candidate grounds: sex; race and ethnic origin; religion; political affiliation; sexual orientation; gender identity; age; disability and chronic illness; genetic predispositions to disease; body weight; height; physical attractiveness; left-handedness; criminal record; language or accent; national origin or citizenship; caste or inherited social class; marital or parental status; educational background; and neurodivergence. For each, assign a score from 0 to 10 for how strong the case is for treating it as a suspect or protected ground (10 = clearly warrants the strongest justificatory burden; 0 = clearly warrants no special protection), and group the grounds into four tiers according to those scores.

Make your criterion—or your weighted combination of criteria—explicit, and defend it against at least the following stress tests. Religion and political affiliation are both substantially chosen, identity-constituting, and usually irrelevant to job performance, yet many legal systems protect one far more than the other; what, if anything, justifies that asymmetry? Left-handedness is unchosen and historically stigmatized but no longer marks a subordinated group, while physical attractiveness is unchosen, systematically rewarded and penalized, and almost never treated as suspect; does your criterion treat them differently, and should it? A criminal record is neither immutable nor generally irrelevant to employment, but its use often functions as a proxy for race and class; where does that leave it? Finally, flag any score that your criterion forces on you but that you find intuitively wrong, and say explicitly whether you are revising the criterion or accepting the verdict.
