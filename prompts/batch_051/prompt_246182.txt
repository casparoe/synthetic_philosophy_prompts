I'm two drafts into a novel set in a port town during a drawn-out insurgency, and the word terrorism has become a problem in the manuscript itself. My narrator is close to a character who, depending on which chapter you're in, gets called a freedom fighter, a militant, or a terrorist, and I keep catching myself reaching for the word and then pulling back. I want the book to dramatize why the label sticks and slides the way it does rather than just have the narrator hand-wring, so I'd like the underlying philosophy made explicit.

Take the actual practice, in law and in ordinary talk, of treating terrorism as a special category of forbidden violence. It's mostly applied to non-state actors: when a state bombs a market or a wedding and kills civilians, we reach for collateral damage or war crime but almost never terrorism, even when terrifying the population is plainly part of the point. It's also applied according to the target — attacking soldiers in uniform tends to read as combat while attacking civilians reads as terrorism, even when the non-state group has no other way to fight. And motive does a lot of work: the same attack can get called terrorism if a political cause is found and something like madness or revenge if not. I want to know what philosophy of political violence this practice presupposes. Does it presuppose that states have a standing moral right to use force that non-state actors lack? That some category of person, the unarmed civilian, is categorically off limits as a target in a way a conscripted soldier is not? That the decisive moral fact is the actor, or the target, or the motive behind the act? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with or inherits from whoever writes the laws, and name which rival views of political violence the practice quietly rules out. Then tell me what would have to change in the practice if we swapped its presuppositions for a straightforwardly consequentialist view — or for the view that any violence against the unarmed is equally wrong no matter who does it. And flag the places where the practice is underdetermined, where more then one moral theory would ship the same differential use of the word.
