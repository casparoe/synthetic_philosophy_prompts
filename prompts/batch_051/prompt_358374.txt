After a career in the physical sciences, I have lately been reading philosophy of science, and I find that my own evidential instincts about fine-tuning are less coherent than I would like. I would like to report my considered verdicts on a range of cases and ask what principle, if any, captures them, which of my verdicts the principle forces me to abandon, and what it says about the further case at the end.

1. The fact that the cosmological constant is vastly smaller than naive physical reasoning would lead one to expect, and that a value only modestly different would have made structure formation impossible, seems to me strong evidence against its simply being brute chance, and evidence for either a designer or a mechanism that produces many universes with different constants.

2. When a particular person wins a national lottery against odds of one in ten million, I do not take that win, by itself, to support the hypothesis that the drawing was rigged in that person's favor. I would need independent evidence of rigging.

3. If a colleague specifies in advance a particular two-hundred-digit string and a random generator produces exactly that string, I treat the result as overwhelming confirmation of whatever hypothesis led her to the prediction. But if a colleague first sees the string and then points out that it encodes her date of birth, telephone number, and a phrase in ASCII, I am almost unmoved, even though the second description is far more improbable than "whatever string happened to come up." Pre-specification matters to me here.

4. The fact that the Earth occupies a narrow habitable zone around a stable star, with a large moon, plate tectonics, and other local conditions conducive to life, does not seem to me to be evidence that the Earth was arranged for life. The obvious selection effect, that we could find ourselves only where observers are possible, seems sufficient.

5. And yet, if the constants had been fine-tuned not for life but for some other remarkable feature, such as black-hole production, I suspect the argument would have struck me just as forcefully. This makes me worry that in (1) I treat life as a privileged target only because I happen to be alive, and I cannot state the difference that is supposed to do the work.

6. If I become convinced that a physical mechanism generates an enormous number of universes with different constants, the design conclusion in (1) is considerably weakened for me; so, too, if the constants turned out to be fixed by a fundamental law. But I cannot articulate the principle on which these alternatives weaken the inference, or why they differ from the advance specification in (3).

The further case I keep going back and forth on is this. A dart thrown at a dartboard lands on some exact point; before the throw, that point had probability zero, or on any realistic treatment vanishingly small, yet no reasonable person thinks its landing there requires a special explanation. Is that different from fine-tuning? If it is, exactly how? If it is not, which of my verdicts must go?

I would like a coherent account of evidence, explanation, or probability that underlies as many of these verdicts as possible, an honest statement of where it forces revisions, and a statement of what the fine-tuning argument amounts to if it survives at all.
