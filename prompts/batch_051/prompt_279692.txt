While I was reporting a piece on how algorithmic systems ration scarce attention, I kept coming back to a statistical idea that refused to stay in its lane: the bias–variance tradeoff. An unbiased estimator gets the whole population right on average but can be wildly unstable in any particular sample, whereas a biased estimator that shrinks or regularizes toward a narrower set of cases often achieves lower expected error by trading coverage for reliability. Then I read a moral philosopher defending loyalty to family, friends, and political allies against the demand for impartial concern, and the two literatures sounded as if they were describing the same curve. Impartiality demands something like unbiasedness: treat every person as equally important, and give no one more weight ex ante. Partiality is deliberately biased allocative attention: overweight the people near you, underweight everyone else, and do so as a matter of principle. In statistics, that kind of bias is not a defect to be removed; it is a tool for being less wrong on average when information and processing power are finite. So I cannot decide whether this is a genuine structural equivalence or just a pun on the word bias.

I am asking as a reporter who covers ideas rather than as an engineer, so what I want is a way to check the analogy, not a survey of either field. What exactly would the mapping be between the statistical concepts and the ethical ones—estimator, true parameter, loss function, variance—and can that mapping be made precise enough to say anything non-vague? What would have to be true of the moral psychology, or of the normative standing of special obligations, for the formal results about regularization and shrinkage to transfer? And where does the parallel come apart? For example, unbiasedness is defined against a true value and a loss function, and it is far from clear what the ethical analogue of expected squared error would be, or whether caring is the kind of thing that can be deployed as an estimator at all. Could the analogy make a concrete prediction—say, that the narrower a moral agent's resources, the more justified partiality becomes—and does that prediction survive contact with how we actually reason about loyalty, betrayal, and fairness to strangers?
