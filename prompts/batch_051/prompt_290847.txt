You are a faculty-level moral epistemologist working on the methodology of normative theorizing. Please think through the problem in stages before writing: first map the logical space generated by the questions below, then place the named positions within that space, and only then draft the instrument. Do not start from definitions of the positions and then reverse-engineer the nodes.

I am a PhD candidate in philosophy preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on reflective equilibrium as a method in ethics. The seminar has a recurring failure mode: students collapse the topic into Rawls versus intuitionism and pick teams. I want a sorting instrument that forces them to answer diagnostic questions and discover where they actually stand, rather than choose a label.

The deliverable is a taxonomy of positions on the epistemic status of reflective equilibrium, built as a decision tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Please construct it around the following four binary branch questions.

1. Revisability. Does the method permit every commitment brought into reflection — case verdicts, everyday rules, high-level principles, and the method's own standards — to be revised in light of the rest, or does it treat some privileged class of commitments, such as considered judgments formed under favorable conditions, as fixed data that constrains the rest?

2. Scope of materials. Does the balancing include only first-order moral materials — particular judgments, rules, and principles — or do background theories about persons, agency, moral psychology, social structure, or human nature enter the balancing as materials on a par with the first-order judgments?

3. Aim. Is the endpoint of the method truth-claiming, such that an acceptable equilibrium must track independent moral facts, with coherence serving at most as evidence of or a constraint on that tracking? Or is correctness constituted by the procedure itself — by coherence or by what is practically acceptable — with no independent standard applied from outside the set of commitments?

4. Orientation. Is the method proposed as normative, saying which moral commitments we are justified in holding, or as descriptive and explicative, making explicit the structure of a moral competence or sensibility we already have without itself supplying the justification?

Work these four questions into a two-by-two-by-two-by-two space of sixteen cells, and present the result both as a decision tree and as a table of the sixteen cells. Then do the following.

(a) Locate at least these positions in the space: narrow reflective equilibrium, wide reflective equilibrium, foundationalist intuitionism, a constructivist reading of reflective equilibrium (with Rawlsian political constructivism as its best-known relative), and the descriptive reading of reflective equilibrium.

(b) For each occupied cell, give the position's standard name, or call it unnamed if no standard label exists, and add one sentence on the commitment its defenders find hardest to defend.

(c) For each unoccupied cell, say in one sentence whether the combination is empty because it is incoherent, possible but unattractive, or merely unexplored.

(d) If a named position occupies a region across several cells rather than a single leaf — I suspect the descriptive reading will behave this way — represent it as a region and explain which question fails to pin it down. Likewise, flag any two positions the tree cannot cleanly separate.

Phrase every node as a question a graduate student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and ground at least two of the four questions in a concrete diagnostic case. One case you must use is this: a student has the firm considered judgment that it would be wrong to punish an innocent person in order to prevent a riot, but also finds the principle that one should minimize expected harm simple and powerful enough that its simplicity and power might themselves be reasons. The question is whether, and in virtue of what, the firm judgment could be revised. You may introduce other cases for the remaining nodes.

Throughout, separate what is settled about the architecture of reflective equilibrium from what is genuinely contested among people who work on the method, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. In particular, do not assume that wide reflective equilibrium has a single determinate epistemic reading; if its status as coherentist, realist-anchored, or constructivist is itself disputed, say so explicitly rather than building the tree around one reading.

Do not assign authors to positions beyond what is built into the standard names, and do not include citations or suggestions for further reading; I will add those myself. The handout is for advanced graduate students, so precision matters more than accessibility, but the nodes should not presuppose the very classification the tree is meant to teach.
