My high school's philosophy club meets weekly, and I have agreed to lead the next session, on the hole argument in the philosophy of spacetime. The audience is about a dozen students who have done a little philosophy but whose physics stops at what we cover in school; I will have forty minutes and a whiteboard, and there will be no assigned reading beforehand, so the session has to be self-contained. The club recently discussed the Leibniz–Newton dispute over whether space is a substance or a set of relations, and several members now expect the hole argument to be merely that debate dressed up in harder mathematics, so part of the session must make clear what the argument adds. I would like you to plan the session rather than write it out for me. In what order must the ideas be introduced so that the argument arrives as a genuine dilemma rather than a technical result—for example, what has to be secured about spacetime, and about determinism, before the hole construction can do its work? What single example or analogy should carry the session, and what features must it have and lack? Which misconceptions should I expect—for instance, that the argument concerns empty regions of space, or that determinism is just predictability, or that the argument's conclusion is that spacetime does not exist—and at what point in the sequence should each be headed off? What question or exercise at the end would show me that the students have grasped the dilemma rather than memorized a slogan? And what should I openly say we are leaving out at this level? Finally, tell me how confident you are in your recommendations, and single out the point you are least sure of.
