Last night I had a long argument with a friend about unenforced criminal laws. His position was that a statute almost no one is prosecuted under is a harmless dead letter; mine was that such statutes are corrosive to the rule of law, because they remain available for sudden, selective enforcement against disfavored people even though the legislature has never had to defend keeping them. We kept circling back to the same example, and I would like a disciplined analysis of it.

Take as the case the history of American sodomy statutes up through Lawrence v. Texas (2003). To supply the salient facts: Texas retained a law criminalizing same-sex but not opposite-sex sodomy long after most states had repealed such laws; in 1998 Houston police arrested two men in a private apartment under it; and in 2003 the Supreme Court struck the law down in Lawrence v. Texas, with the majority resting on due process and overruling Bowers v. Hardwick (1986), while Justice O'Connor concurred on equal-protection grounds and would not have overruled Bowers.

Analyze this history through the lens of desuetude — the doctrine that a law can lose its binding force through long and settled non-enforcement. Work in both directions. First, what would a desuetude theorist say about the status of the Texas statute in the decades before 2003: was it still law, and what should a judge have done if asked to apply it? Second, how well does the doctrine capture what was actually wrong with the statute — better than, worse than, or just differently from the majority's due-process reasoning and O'Connor's equal-protection concurrence? Third, what does the episode show about the relation between desuetude and the rule of law: is allowing a law to lapse by non-use a repair to a dysfunctional legal order, or is it itself a violation of legality? Be scrupulous about the mapping: distinguish desuetude from mere prosecutorial dormancy, from selective enforcement, from constitutional invalidation, and from the legislature's failure to repeal, and say plainly where the theory stops explaining the case.
