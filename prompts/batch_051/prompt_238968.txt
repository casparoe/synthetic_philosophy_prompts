I keep going back and forth on this and want you to actually adjudicate it instead of just listing tradeoffs. I work on risk scores for a lending product (not recidivism, but the math is identical), and every fairness review I end up at the same wall. The impossibility results in algorithmic fairness strike me as airtight: a score that predicts the probability someone defaults cannot be calibrated across groups, have the same false positive rate across groups, and have the same false negative rate across groups, all at once — not unless you're in a degenerate case like identical base rates or an essentially perfect predictor. I've worked through the arithmetic enough that I'm convinced its not just a quirk of our data. So the theory side is: fairness is not one property; it's a bundle of statistical conditions that are partly incompatible, and picking among them is a value choice the math cant make for you.

But I also have an intuition I can't shake. If the model hands the same default probability to a Black applicant and a white applicant, it seems straightforwardly unjust when the resulting errors land systematically harder on the Black applicants — that isn't a "different conception of fairness," it's the score being wrong in a way that tracks group membership. The idea that mathematics itself could force a just system to choose between "treat similar people similarly" and "don't let errors pile up on one group" feels, to me, like a reductio of the formalism rather than an insight about justice. When I try to explain this to our compliance people, they just hear me rationalizing a discriminatory model.

So: give me the strongest version of each position and then tell me which should yield. Should I debunk and drop the intuition, amend or abandon the statistical-criteria framing that the impossibility theorem presupposes, or is there a reading on which the two are actually compatible? I want to know what would justify each move, not just which side you'd pick. Please answer in American English.
