At a dinner party last month I watched two friends of mine spend an hour going in circles about how to do good in the world, and I keep turning it over. Priya works at a charity evaluator, and her whole thing was that the only question that matters is the marginal impact of your next dollar. Marcus works in policy advocacy, and he kept saying that's exactly the mindset that keeps the world broken. The back-and-forth went roughly like this.

(1) Priya: If I have a hundred dollars, I should give it to whichever program does the most measurable good per dollar at the margin. Bednets and deworming are good because the evidence says the next dollar does more there than it does anywhere else. "Changing the system" is a slogan. If you can't even state the counterfactual effect of your dollar, you don't have a theory of impact, you have a vibe.

(2) Marcus: That only works if problems are independent little widgets. Bednets work because there's a distribution network, a health ministry, and a market, and those are upstream of the bednet. If you always optimize at the margin inside the current system, you fund the symptoms and leave the causes untouched. Worse, you can end up subsidizing the very system that should be changed, making it less likely to change.

(3) Priya: Maybe, but the systems view licenses anything. It says the best intervention is the one you can't measure, can't bound, and won't know failed until twenty years too late. Lots of money has gone to governance reform and political advocacy with no evidence it worked. The marginalist can at least be wrong in a way she can see. What's the falsifiable prediction your view makes?

(4) Marcus: It isn't that nothing is measurable; it's that marginal measurement breaks exactly where the highest-leverage effects live. Some effects are non-linear: one new campaign does nothing, twenty of them change the equilibrium once a threshold is crossed. A marginal dollar looks worthless because, on its own, it is worthless — but the strategy isn't. If you define "impact" as "what one more dollar moves," you've ruled out threshold effects by definition, not by evidence.

(5) Priya: And if you define impact as "what might move once dozens of other coordinated actors also move," then every failed systems-change project can claim it would have worked with a bit more coordination. That's just as unfalsifiable. Also, you've slid from "some systems matter" to "always fund the system," which doesn't follow. I can accept that bednets depend on supply chains and still think my next dollar is better spent on bednets than on a democracy-promotion grant.

(6) Marcus: But you've made the mirror-image slide: from "this is the most measurable piece" to "this is the best place to put the dollar." The sum of individually optimal choices isn't automatically collectively optimal. If everyone follows your rule, nobody pays for the unmeasurable upstream conditions, the distribution network rots, and even your bednet intervention stops being optimal. Your whole approach free-rides on a system it refuses to fund.

I couldn't tell by the end whether they were really disagreeing about facts, about what "doing good" means, or about how make decisions under uncertainty. Can you go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move? For each numbered move, say which claims are defensible, which are overstated, and exactly where each person is talking past the other. Then tell me what would have to be conceded on each side for their views to be compatible, and whether the dispute is even resolvable without first answering some deeper philosophical question.
