Give me a working definition of "a statistical life" that handles the following cases. Policymakers use the phrase with confidence—a safety rule is said to save thirty statistical lives a year, and benefits are priced with a "value of a statistical life"—but the referent is strange: no identifiable person is the life that was saved, and the saving consists of many small risk reductions spread across a population. I want a definition that can do real work in cost-benefit analysis and triage, not merely restate the jargon.

Start with two anchors:

1. A passerby pulls a drowning child from a river. This is plainly saving a life, but it would be odd to call the child a statistical life.
2. A regulation lowers annual traffic fatality risk for each of thirty million drivers by one in a million. The expected number of deaths prevented is thirty, but no one can say which thirty drivers are alive because of the rule. This is the paradigm case of a statistical life.

Now the hard cases:

3. A mine-safety improvement changes risk only for two hundred identifiable miners, reducing each one's annual mortality risk by one in one thousand. The expected number of deaths prevented is 0.2 per year. Is this a fraction of a statistical life, and is it coherent to say so?
4. In a pandemic, a triage protocol gives a ventilator to three patients with good survival odds rather than to one patient with poor odds. All four are identifiable; the decision runs on expected survival and quality-adjusted life-years. Has the statistical-life framework dropped out here, or does it still apply?
5. An aid program must choose between spending its budget on road safety in a rich country and spending the same amount on malaria prevention in a poor country, where it would prevent many more deaths per dollar. If a statistical life has a single value, the poor-country program dominates; if it has a local value derived from willingness to pay, it may not. Which way should the definition cut?
6. A cancer screening program catches treatable tumors earlier. Because most screened people would have survived without screening, no identifiable person can be told afterward that the program saved their life, even though the population-level risk reduction is well estimated. Is "saving a statistical life" the right description here at all?
7. A cost-effectiveness model counts one year of life lived with severe disability as worth some fraction of a year without disability. Is that consistent with a definition of a statistical life as equal in value, or does the equality belong to one job of the term while the weighing belongs to another?

I suspect the term does two jobs at once. One job is actuarial: a statistical life is a unit for converting tiny, anonymous mortality risk reductions into an expected number of deaths prevented—a weighted sum, not a thing in the world. The other job is moral: the phrase borrows the idea that each person's life has equal and nonsubstitutable worth. The trouble is that the actuarial job requires treating lives as divisible and fungible, while the moral job resists precisely that. If you agree, give me two definitions—one actuarial, one moral—and trace which cases each one handles and which cases shift when you move between them.

After you have proposed a definition or definitions, run them against all seven cases, including the anchors. Where a definition yields a verdict you think is wrong, either revise it or defend the surprising verdict. If no single definition earns its keep, say whether "statistical life" should be retained as an acknowledged bookkeeping fiction, replaced by something like expected life-years gained or cost per quality-adjusted life-year, or abandoned as a misleading metaphor.
