I teach an evening course for non-specialists on theories of the self, and we are closing a unit on Douglas Hofstadter's account of the first-person pronoun as a self-referential symbol. The claim we keep circling is that the word I does not name a little observer seated somewhere behind the eyes; it is an abstract pattern that emerges once a system of symbols grows rich enough to refer to itself, so that its levels of description form a tangled hierarchy rather than a pyramid with a supervisor at the summit. On this view there is no privileged bottom level and no final homunculus, and yet the loop is real enough to act back on the very symbols that give rise to it, even while it can feel like an illusion generated from below. My students find the idea vivid but hard to keep in view, and I would like a poem to read aloud before the discussion.

Please write a villanelle — five tercets and a closing quatrain, nineteen lines — in which the form enacts the strange loop instead of merely illustrating it. The two refrain lines should carry the philosophical structure: let one refrain speak from the level of the observing self, and the other from the level of the symbol system that constitutes it, so that each time a refrain returns the surrounding context has shifted and the line means something slightly different, the way a loop re-enters itself. The closing couplet should bring the two voices together without resolving them. The poem should be faithful enough that a reader who knows Hofstadter recognizes the account — self-reference with no bedrock, a hierarchy that folds back on itself, the self as both genuine pattern and seeming illusion — but it should also stand on its own as a good poem for someone who has never encountered these ideas. Avoid naming the concepts; let the refrain structure and the imagery do the work.
