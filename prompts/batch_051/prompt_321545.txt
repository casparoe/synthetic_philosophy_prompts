As a journalist covering the politics of international justice, I have noticed that arguments over whether an atrocity should be named genocide often run through a physiological metaphor: a national or ethnic group is imagined as a living body, attacks on its institutions are imagined as injuries to organs, and genocide is imagined as the death of the organism rather than harm to individual cells. The metaphor is made to work in two directions. One side says that destroying a group’s cultural, educational, or economic foundations is genocidal because it attacks what keeps the social body alive. The other side says such destruction is not genocide because it merely reshapes or disables the body without killing it. Raphael Lemkin’s formulation in Axis Rule in Occupied Europe seems to supply the source of this picture:

"Generally speaking, genocide does not necessarily mean the immediate destruction of a nation, except when accomplished by mass killings of all members of a nation. It is intended rather to signify a coordinated plan of different actions aiming at the destruction of essential foundations of the life of national groups, with the aim of annihilating the groups themselves. Genocide is directed against the national group as an entity, and the actions involved are directed against individuals, not in their individual capacity, but as members of the national group."

I would like you to audit how far the body/organism analogy actually holds for interpreting this passage and the legal category that grew out of it. Give me the correspondence table—what plays the role of the cells, the organs, the immune system, the metabolic processes, the death of the organism—and then identify where the analogy breaks down. In particular, separate disanalogies that matter for the legal and moral question from those that are merely rhetorical. Then tell me whether either of the two conclusions above survives the disanalogies, or whether the analogy was doing the argumentative work all along and a better comparison would be needed.
