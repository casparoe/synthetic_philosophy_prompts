I have a rule for using language models that I would like you to evaluate as a piece of epistemic engineering rather than as a confession. I am a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and I read five to ten papers a week in fields adjacent to my own specialty. When a paper or long technical argument lies outside that specialty, I let a capable model explain it to me until I can do three things unaided: state the position in a paragraph, identify the single claim on which the argument turns, and give one observation that would make the position more attractive and one that would make it less attractive. If I pass all three, I mark the position as understood and usually never open the original paper unless I later need to rely on it in my own work. Inside my specialty I do the opposite, reading originals carefully and using the model only as a memory aid.

I am unsure whether this is defensible. It is not ordinary deferral, since I am testing my ability to operate on an explanation rather than checking the model's facts. But I am grading my own test, and the model may have compressed away exactly the detail that would change my mind. I worry that the fluent reconstruction I am left with is a kind of credit laundering: a borrowed compression that passes for understanding because it survives my own questions.

Is this rule defensible as it stands? If it needs amendment, what is the strongest repaired version? I would particularly like you to address whether being able to reconstruct a position from a compressed account can be separated from being able to notice when the compression is misleading, and whether my three tests track the second or only the first.

Answer in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
