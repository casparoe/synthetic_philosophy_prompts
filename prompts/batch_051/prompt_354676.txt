I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm drafting a courtroom thriller set in London. The hinge of the trial is that the only real evidence against my defendent is the output of a facial-comparison algorithm: trained on millions of labelled images, it returns an 87% probability that the figure in a grainy CCTV still is him. I've been reading around the legal-theory debate about naked statistical evidence — the blue bus hypothetical, the rodeo gatecrasher, that lot — and I want to get the underlying argument right before I put it in a barrister's mouth.

As I understand it, the standard view is that you can't convict somebody on bare statistics, however high the probability, because a statistic only tells you something about a class the defendant belongs to, not about whether he actually did this particular act. The blue bus case: if four out of five buses in town are run by the Blue Bus Company and somebody is forced off the road by a bus, that doesn't get you to a verdict against the company, because the evidence never touches this accident as opposed to any other accident. The gatecrasher example is the same idea: you might know that a good slice of the crowd at a rodeo snuck in without paying, but that won't let you sue any particular person for the price of a ticket. The percentage attaches to the group, not to the individual.

But here's what's bugging me. The evidence in my novel isn't the old-fashioned kind of statistic, like a market share or a base rate. It's the output of a trained model that looked at a specific image from this specific night, checked it against everything it has absorbed, and produced a probability about this particular incident. It feels individualised; it's not obviously a generalisation about a class. So my question is weather the old argument against naked statistical evidence actually survives that development. Does the distinction between merely statistical and individualised evidence collapse once the statistic is generated by an opaque model aiming at the particular case, or is that just the old problem wearing a new coat? Which premise of the original argument is doing the real work, and is it under pressure? And if the argument does survive, give me a repaired version: what exactly would the prosecution need to add before a jury could reasonably treat that 87% as proof beyond reasonable doubt, and can a facial-comparison algorithm ever supply it, or is there something about its output that can never do that job?

And two practical points. I don't want citations, reading lists, or anything like that — just the reasoning, worked out from scratch. And please write it as continuous prose, like a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just paragraphs.
