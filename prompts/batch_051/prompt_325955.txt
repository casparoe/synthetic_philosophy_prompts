In a conversation about whether it is sensible to choose one’s activities by asking which will make one happiest, a colleague dismissed the idea with what he treated as a decisive piece of folk wisdom: happiness is not the sort of thing one can obtain by pursuing it, because the very effort to pursue it is part of what makes it recede. I have since noticed the same thought being used in several quite different ways. Sometimes it is offered as practical advice about how to live. Sometimes it is a psychological claim about attention, pleasure, and enjoyment. Sometimes it is a normative claim that only non-hedonic goals are worth having. I want to understand what this piece of wisdom could actually be asserting.

Please lay out the distinct precise claims the saying could be making, from the trivial to the radical, and say for each whether it is plausible, whether it is false or only conditionally true, and which reading, if any, would actually support my colleague’s dismissal of choosing activities for their expected happiness.

Please structure your response as follows:

1. The slogan in context (brief): what work the saying was being made to do, and why an unanalysed appeal to it is unsatisfactory.

2. Candidate readings (numbered list, one or two sentences each): at least five distinct propositions the saying might express. Distinguish, for example, claims about the effects of deliberate pursuit, claims about the objects of attention, claims about what happiness consists in, and claims about what one ought to aim at.

3. Assessment (one short paragraph per reading): for each candidate, state whether it is a platitude, a credible but limited psychological generalisation, a substantive normative claim, or a falsehood, and note the circumstances under which it would fail.

4. Application to the original exchange (brief): identify which reading my colleague’s dismissal depends on, and say whether that reading is strong enough to carry the conclusion.
