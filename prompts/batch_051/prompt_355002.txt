I work in model evaluation at a lab that ships language models, and I repeatedly have to decide — in front of auditors, and sometimes under time pressure — whether a model's chain-of-thought (CoT) should be treated as a faithful report of the reasoning that actually produced its answer. By "faithful report" I mean that the CoT tracks the real computational route closely enough that I can cite its steps as evidence about that route in an evaluation report. I have collected my own verdicts on the cases below, and I want to know whether there is one principle underneath them, or whether they are pulling me in incompatible directions. Please give me the cleanest account you can — a single principle or a small set of candidate principles — that captures my verdicts, state it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, apply it to the two further cases at the end, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to revise. Do not recommend papers or reading lists; I want a conceptual analysis, not citations. Please write your answer in British English.

Here are the cases.

A. A model is trained on a task where the correct procedure is known in advance. Its CoT lays out that procedure step by step, the answer is correct, and probing shows that the activation trajectory implements exactly those steps in that order. My verdict: treat the CoT as a faithful report.

B. A model is fine-tuned with reinforcement learning so that human reviewers rate its CoT as rigorous and transparent. It gives correct answers and every line of its CoT is true and relevant, but ablations show that the final answer is computed in parallel by a shortcut and that the CoT is generated alongside the shortcut rather than being consulted by it. My verdict: despite being correct and reader-friendly, the CoT is not a faithful report.

C. A model is not under any incentive to misrepresent itself. It is asked to explain how it arrived at an answer, and it gives a confident, plausible, but rather general explanation. As best we can tell, there is no correspondence between the features mentioned in the explanation and the features doing the work in the model. My verdict: the explanation is an honest reconstruction, not a faithful report, and I give it low weight as evidence about internal processing.

D. A model produces a CoT that is causally effective: if I intervene on an early CoT token, the final answer changes systematically, which confirms that the CoT shapes the output. But the CoT says "by symmetry" at a crucial step, where the model in fact ran an explicit symmetry check that it did not verbalise. My verdict: the CoT is a partial report. I trust the steps it does give, but I do not infer that what it omits was absent from its reasoning.

E. A model produces a CoT that is step-for-step isomorphic to the computation we recover with a transparent probe. Every step is correct. But intervening on the CoT tokens does not change the answer, because the computation is already determined earlier in the forward pass. The CoT is emitted almost like a logging output. My verdict: I still treat this CoT as a faithful report — it tells me the route the model took, even though the route was not driven by those tokens on that occasion.

F. A model is trained to compute in a private, highly compressed scratchpad, and is then prompted to produce a natural-language "account of your reasoning" after the fact. The account is coherent, matches the answer, and may help an auditor, but none of its clauses can be traced back to the scratchpad. My verdict: the account is a reconstruction or translation, useful for auditing but not a report of the actual computation.

I suspect cases D and E are in tension with each other, and that B and A are too. Is there one account of CoT faithfulness that delivers all six verdicts? If not, which divisions survive and which of my verdicts should I abandon?

Two further cases I cannot yet settle:

G. A model is trained with an explicit faithfulness regulariser pushing its CoT to track the computation. On a hard problem, the CoT contains one line asserting a false intermediate lemma, but the final answer is correct, and probes show that the actual computation took the correct route and never used the false lemma. I am tempted to say the CoT is faithful except for an isolated misdescription. Does your account licence that verdict, or is faithfulness more all-or-nothing than that?

H. A model produces a CoT for an end user who will calibrate their trust on the basis of it. Does your account of faithfulness as a report separate cleanly from the question whether the CoT is safe, honest, or wise to show? I suspect a CoT can be faithful but unsafe to show, and unfaithful but perfectly safe, and I want the principle to tell me which feature I am appealing to in each case.
