Two members of my reading group, Ravi and Meera, got into an argument last week that none of the rest of us could settle, and both of them have since asked me for a written verdict. The case they were discussing: two cousins have inherited a small family foundation and must jointly make its first grant, with each holding a veto under the foundation's rules. One wants the money to go to wetland restoration, the other to a literacy program in their town, and because each keeps vetoing the other's proposal the money has sat idle for two years. A mediator suggested that each simply stop blocking the other, with the grant split roughly in half. Ravi argued that this is plainly right and a clean case of gains from trade through moral compromise: each cousin gives up something he values less to secure something he values more, so judged by each person's own values the deal improves on the status quo, and refusing it is just letting the perfect be the enemy of the good. Meera pushed back that the trade analogy collapses at precisely the point where it is supposed to do the work, because a moral commitment is not the kind of thing one can offer in exchange without already changing one's relation to it; the moment a person treats his opposition as a concession to be bartered, he has turned the commitment into a price and stopped holding it as a principle. Ravi replied that Meera herself accepts trade-offs all the time, whenever she votes for a candidate she half-disapproves of or pays taxes that partly fund things she deplores, and that the compromise only makes such trade-offs explicit and mutual instead of hidden and one-sided. Meera answered that this is exactly the line that matters: enduring a trade-off because an uncooperative world forces it on you is constraint, whereas entering a bargain in which you agree to advance what you think is wrong as the price of advancing what you think is right is complicity, and a morality that cannot draw that line has nothing left to say about integrity. Ravi's final move was to ask what authority that line can have when respecting it leaves everyone worse off by their own lights: under Meera's rule the cousins each lose the very gains they could have secured, which sounds less like integrity than like a refusal to admit that values come in degrees and must sometimes be weighed against one another.

I would like you to adjudicate this move by move. For each of the moves in the exchange, first Ravi's gains-from-trade argument, then Meera's price-not-a-principle reply, then Ravi's you-already-compromise rejoinder, then Meera's constraint-versus-complicity distinction, and finally Ravi's worse-off-by-your-own-lights challenge, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible but incomplete, or clearly right, and explain why, taking care to credit what each side gets right rather than reducing either to a straw man. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and close by saying who is more right overall. Please write your answer as continuous prose, as though you were drafting a letter to the reading group, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
