I am a PhD student in archaeology, and each summer I direct the finds-processing unit for a settlement excavation. My team examines several hundred small finds and assemblages a season; each catalog record is written in a few minutes by graduate and undergraduate students with no shared theoretical vocabulary, yet those records feed the site report and later get cited as fact. The permanent staff default to one of two readings for any puzzling object — a practical tool or a ritual object — and the "ritual" label in particular tends to become a residual category for anything we cannot explain. I would like a procedure that breaks this habit.

Please write a decision procedure my team could actually operate when a find or small assemblage must be classified as: (a) an object of practical or technological function; (b) a trace of an institution; (c) a trace of belief or ritual practice; or (d) insufficient evidence to choose among these. The deliverable is a reusable, ordered sequence of questions or tests, with, for each step, what counts as a sufficient answer to move on, where the procedure stops with "insufficient evidence," and where it hands the decision to the project director as a judgment call rather than a factual inference. The theoretical content should be translated into steps a student without archaeological theory can run: in particular, the problem of equifinality — different behaviors leaving materially similar traces — and the middle-range project of linking static remains to the behaviors that produced them through experimental and actualistic analogy. Tell me which distinctions from the theory you kept, which you simplified away, and where you expect the procedure to misfire.

The procedure should separate two inference tasks we currently run together. The first is institutional inference: deciding that a pattern of objects reflects standardized production, coordinated deposition, or some other supra-household arrangement. The second is belief inference: deciding that the objects reflect what people thought, intended, or valued. Please design the steps so that a claim about an institution does not smuggle in a claim about belief, and so that "ritual" is not treated as the default explanation when practical function fails.

Then run the procedure on these three cases from this season:

1. A cache of ceramic vessels deliberately broken and placed inverted beneath a courtyard floor. Our director is preparing to publish this as evidence of ancestor veneration.
2. A polished bone tube with little use-wear, recovered from a domestic midden.
3. Several dozen standardized miniature clay vessels, scattered across ordinary households rather than concentrated in one structure.

For each case, state the verdict the procedure yields, the step at which it becomes decisive, and what further evidence would change it. At least one of the three verdicts should be one we will not like. Tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do: in particular, how far it can establish patterned practice, and where the content of beliefs, or the difference between ritual, play, craft, and fashion, remains beyond the objects alone. Conclude with the final line \answer{...} containing a one-sentence summary of the decision rule.
