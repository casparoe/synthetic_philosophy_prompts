I need a careful diagnosis of an argument I built for a seminar on intergenerational justice. I now distrust it because the conclusion is absurd, but I cannot see where the pressure should fall.

(1) A modest pure rate of time preference is rationally permissible for an individual. Caring about your own utility one year from now roughly 1% less than you care about your utility now, merely because it is later, is within the bounds of ordinary rationality.

(2) If a person discounts at a constant pure rate of 1% per year, then in that person's choices utility t years from now gets weight 0.99^t, up to the difference between continuous and annual compounding. That is just what constant-rate discounting means.

(3) Social decisions about how much present people should sacrifice for benefits or harms that fall on future people are ultimately decisions by present people. So if each member of the present generation is rationally entitled to use the 0.99^t weighting for future utility, the corresponding social decisions may properly use that same weighting.

(4) The weighting compounds. At t = 1,000, 0.99^t is about 0.00004: an identical benefit to a person a thousand years from now counts as less than one twenty-thousandth of a benefit to a person now.

(5) Therefore, by (1)–(4), accepting the mild-sounding 1% rate commits us to being close to indifferent between a policy that prevents one death now and a policy that prevents twenty thousand or more deaths a thousand years from now.

(6) But we should not be indifferent between those policies. That conclusion is morally intolerable, and I take it to be a fixed point.

So at least one of (1)–(5) must go — either a premise is false, or the move to (5) fails. Where exactly does the argument go wrong? I would most like to keep (1), because giving up the rational permissibility of modest impatience toward one's own future feels like overreacting, and I would rather not give up the arithmetic in (4), because it is arithmetic. But I want to know the price of whichever rejection I am forced into, including what happens to standard economic practice if the individual rate cannot carry over to social evaluation.

Please separate, in your answer, what is settled among people who work on discounting and intergenerational justice from what is genuinely contested. I do not want a contested position about the social discount rate presented as if it were the field's consensus.
