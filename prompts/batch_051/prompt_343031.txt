I can't stop seeing a parallel between a really old philosophical move and a really modern software rule, and I can't decide if its deep or if Im just pattern-matching. The old move is the ascetic one, the way I understand it from people like Epicurus and the Cynics and from a lot of voluntary simplicity writing today: treat desires as liabilities, not assets. Each desire you let yourself keep—for a nicer apartment, for praise, for the coffee machine—is a new way to end up unsatisfied, and it keeps generating a low-level craving even while it's being met. So the ideal is to trim desires down to the ones you actually need, not just to get better at satisfying a lot of desires. The software rule is YAGNI, short for you aren't gonna need it: don't build a feature until you genuinely need it, because every extra feature is ongoing maintenance cost, a new place for bugs to hide, and something that constrains future changes. It seems to me the structure is the same: desires are features, frustration is a bug, self-sufficiency is a lean codebase with no dead code. But I also see places where the mapping should come apart: you can't just delete a built-in desire the way you delete dead code, and a codebase doesn't suffer from the feature it never had, whereas an ascetic treats the mere presence of an extra desire as a cost even before it goes unmet. So: is this a real identity of structure about optional complexity and expected future costs, or a loose metaphor that's mostly leaning on the word need? If it is real, what should transfer between the two sides—say, from refactoring to self-cultivation or from Epicurean classification of desires to software architecture—and what's the clearest thing the analogy would predict about one side that turns out false?
