I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. I work on a team that builds targeting-support software, and through that work I keep running into a just war theory principle called the moral equality of combatants. I'd like to check whether the principle can survive the conditions my own work helps create.

As I understand it, the principle says that, regardless of the justice of the war as a whole, combatants on both sides have the same permission to kill enemy combatants, as long as they respect the rules governing how they fight. The standard case for it seems to rest on several claims at once. First, ordinary soldiers usually cannot know whether their side's cause is just; they get a selective picture from their own state and are not in a position to override it. Second, the decision to go to war is made far above them, and it would be unjust to strip someone of the right to defend themselves because of a choice they did not make. Third, on the battlefield the two sides stand in a rough symmetry: each has made themselves a legitimate target by being dangerous, and each is exposed to the other's violence. Fourth, the rules of war can restrain anyone only if they apply identically to both sides; if permissions depended on the justice of the cause, each side would declare itself just and the rules would dissolve.

Now consider a different kind of combatant, the kind my team's systems are designed to support. An operator sits safely outside the combat zone, follows an individual through persistent surveillance, builds up a detailed picture of his activities over days, and then directs lethal force at a person who has no ability to shoot back at the operator or even reach them. Assume the operator is still a combatant in the relevant sense — a legitimate target and a participant in hostilities — so the only things that have changed are exposure to risk and availability of information. Assume also that the operator is not making a split-second decision in the fog of war, and that the basic public facts about the campaign make its justification at least assessable, in a way it may not be for a drafted infantryman.

My question is whether the moral equality of combatants survives this development. It seems to threaten the symmetry claim and the ignorance claim simultaneously, but I cannot tell whether that means the principle is dead, needs repair, or was never doing the moral work I took it to be doing. So: does a risk-free, comparatively well-informed remote operator still have the same permission to kill as the opposing combatant they kill? If yes, show which premise actually absorbs the change and why the principle survives it. If no, identify exactly which premise fails, state the weaker or revised principle that should replace the original one for these cases, and say whether the replacement is an update to the old principle or a genuinely different idea.
