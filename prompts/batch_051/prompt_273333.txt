I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been building a sense inventory for a lexical database. The part I keep getting stuck on is deciding when a form is one word with several senses and when it's two words that happen to sound alike. My gut keeps delivering the following verdicts:

- 'bank' (river's edge) and 'bank' (financial institution) are two different words.
- 'school' (the institution) and 'school' (the building) are one word with two senses; 'school' in the sense of a shoal of fish is a different word.
- 'chicken' (the animal) and 'chicken' (the meat) are one word.
- 'crane' (the bird) and 'crane' (the machine) are one word, the machine being a settled metaphorical extension.
- 'mole' (the animal) and 'mole' (the spy) are two different words, even though the spy sense looks like a metaphor from the animal; 'mole' as a skin mark and 'mole' as the chemical unit are two more different words to me.
- 'run' is one word across programs, noses, rivers, candidates, and motors, even though I cannot say what all these uses share.
- 'healthy' in 'healthy diet', 'healthy child', and 'healthy complexion' is one word contributing causing health, having health, and indicating health respectively.
- 'book' in 'this book is heavy' and 'this book is available as an audiobook' is one word, though one occurrence picks out the object and the other the content.

My working hunch is that we have one word with several senses when the senses are linked by a recoverable relation, like part for whole, source for product, or metaphorical extension, and two words otherwise. But my own cases seem to break this: 'crane' and 'mole-spy' look structurally parallel yet I treat them differently, and 'run' feels like one word with no single link at all.

I'd like you to state a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts. Please say which clause each case motivates, apply the principle to cases I haven't settled ('window' in a wall versus a GUI window, 'mouse' the animal versus the input device, 'table' the furniture versus a data table, 'hard' of surfaces versus problems), and be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle forces me to abandon and why. If the best account ends up denying that a word has a meaning at all, as opposed to a family of more or less related uses, I'd also like to hear that.
