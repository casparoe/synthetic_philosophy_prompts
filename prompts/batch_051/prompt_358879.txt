I have a colleague who works on comparative anti-corruption policy, and I have been keeping a running record of how she classifies particular transactions. Her calls are firm, but I cannot tell what rule is generating them. The record so far:

1. A procurement officer takes a cash envelope from a contractor and, in exchange, steers a public works contract to the contractor's firm. She calls this corrupt.

2. A mayor approves a neighbor's building permit after it has moved through the ordinary queue; the permit meets every requirement and receives no special handling. The neighbor, who gives a loaf of homemade bread to everyone on the street at the holidays, left one at the mayor's door as usual. She calls this not corrupt.

3. A political machine gives voters grocery vouchers conditional on their voting for the party's slate. She calls this corrupt.

4. In a rural district where it is a longstanding practice for anyone seeking a service from the land office to bring the registrar a customary basket of produce as a mark of respect, a man requests a title transfer, brings the usual basket, and the transfer is processed in the normal order and satisfies every formal condition. She calls this not corrupt.

5. In the same district, the same registrar receives the customary basket from a man whose file lacks a required document, and the registrar registers the transfer anyway. She calls this corrupt.

I now want to know how she would most likely classify three new cases:

A. A minister appoints her clearly unqualified nephew to a paid post in her ministry. Nothing passes from the nephew to her; it is a matter of family obligation to look after kin.

B. At a passport office in a country where speed money is the entrenched norm, a traveler pays a small unrecorded sum to a clerk to have a passport issued in one day rather than the usual three weeks. The passport would be issued eventually either way; the payment changes only the timing.

C. At a campaign rally in a country where distributing cash and small gifts to supporters is the standard way to mobilize a base, a politician hands out money to attendees. Nothing is said about how anyone must vote, and the money is not conditional on any vote; the party relies on the recipients' loyalty and expects most to turn out and vote for her.

The verdicts themselves matter less to me than the structure. What rule or rules could she be running such that all five of the earlier calls come out as they did? Which candidate rules fit the whole record equally well, what does each candidate predict for A, B, and C, and which one of the three cases — or what single further case — would do the most to separate the candidates? Finally, if I had to recommend the classification of C that best coheres with the track record she has already built, what should it be, which earlier case is the closest analog, and what principle would she be committing herself to either way?
