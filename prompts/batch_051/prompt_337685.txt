I'm TAing an applied-ethics course and I've been going back and forth on how to grade one student's final-exam answer on the doctrine of double effect. I want a second read before I write up the comments. The exam question was:

Explain the doctrine of double effect and its standard four conditions. Then apply it to this pair of cases. In both cases a terminally ill patient is in severe pain that morphine is the only thing that touches. The doctor gives the same high dose, which will definitely relieve the pain and will also depress respiration enough to hasten death. In case A the doctor gives the dose in order to relieve the pain, and she accepts the earlier death as a price she is willing to pay. In case B the doctor gives the dose in order to end the patient's life, because she thinks that is the only remaining way the suffering will end. Does the doctrine judge the two cases differently, and if so on exactly what grounds?

Here is the student's answer:

The doctrine of double effect distinguishes between consequences an agent intends and consequences she merely foresees but does not aim at. The standard conditions are: (1) the act itself must not be intrinsically wrong; (2) the bad effect must not be intended, either as an end or as a means to the good effect; (3) the good effect must not be brought about through the bad effect; and (4) there must be a proportionately serious reason to permit the bad effect. In case A, the doctor intends only pain relief. The earlier death is a foreseen side effect, not a means to the pain relief, because the pain relief would still occur if death did not. So conditions (2) and (3) are satisfied, and since the pain is severe and the patient is already dying, condition (4) is satisfied too. Thus DDE permits case A. In case B, the doctor intends the patient's death, treating it as the means by which the suffering is ended. That violates condition (2), and arguably condition (1) as well, so DDE condemns case B. Finally, because the two cases are identical from the outside, the only thing the doctrine can appeal to to distinguish them is the doctor's own report of what she intended. But then any doctor could make a lethal dose permissible just by describing what she is doing as pain relief while accepting a side effect of death. So the doctrine depends on a distinction that cannot be applied in practice.

Grade the answer and give me the specific comments that justify the grade — a point-by-point breakdown, not just a overall impression. I'd like a concrete grade out of 10, and an explanation of where the student earns marks, where she loses them, and why, including whether the final objection is well-founded.
