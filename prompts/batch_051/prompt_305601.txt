I serve on the ethics committee of a teaching hospital, and I need to circulate a decision memo before Thursday's meeting. Our palliative and bereavement care service has asked us to approve a new opt-in program. Under the proposal, adults who have just experienced the sudden or traumatic death of a spouse, child, or parent would be offered a brief pharmacological intervention that substantially reduces the intensity of acute grief over the following six to twelve weeks. It would work not by general sedation or by numbing emotion in the moment, but by dampening the consolidation of intensely painful memories; ordinary recollection and day-to-day functioning would be left largely intact. Each patient would be screened, would receive a preparatory counseling session, and could decline. The service chief frames it as continuous with what we already do: we treat pain, panic, and acute insomnia after bereavement, and a minority of patients go on to develop what clinicians now call prolonged grief disorder. She proposes offering this as a standard option for six months, with data collection and a review at the end.

The committee is split. Some members read the proposal as straightforwardly compassionate and autonomy-respecting. Others think it quietly medicalizes a human response that is not a disease and, by making grief optional, commits the hospital to the view that grief has no value beyond what it costs. I argued about exactly this with a friend last night, and he pushed a stronger version: even if acute grief were, for the bereaved person, bad on balance, the hospital should still hesitate, because once an institution offers to remove grief it alters what kind of response to loss a community treats as normal and admirable. I did not have a crisp reply, and I do not want the memo to dodge it.

Please draft the memo. It should recommend one of three courses — approve the program as proposed, reject it, or approve it with conditions — with a concrete time frame and review triggers. It should give reasons our clinical service chiefs could actually respond to, not unsupported assertions. In particular, it should engage the strongest version of the losing side: the claim that grief, and sadness more generally, might have value that a grief-dampening offer would fail to respect or would gradually erode. And please separate what is empirically settled about the psychology of grief from what remains genuinely contested among people who know the philosophy of emotion; do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
