I am a retired physical chemist, and since leaving the laboratory I have been working through some epistemology on my own. A certain style of argument about knowledge and practical stakes keeps occupying me, and I would like it checked step by step rather than merely summarized. The concrete case I keep returning to is this. A technician reads a calibrated temperature logger. In the low-stakes version, the question is whether a box of stable reagents must be re-tested after a borderline reading; if she is wrong, the cost is a few hours of delay. In the high-stakes version, the same display, on the same logger, with the same calibration history, decides whether a batch of vaccines in a mass immunization campaign is released; if she is wrong, thousands of people could receive spoiled doses. The technician's evidence, training, and felt confidence are identical in the two versions; only the practical cost of error differs.

The argument for the conclusion that practical stakes can by themselves affect whether someone knows runs as follows:

(1) If a subject knows that p, then the subject is in a position to rely on p in practical reasoning; that is, the subject's epistemic standing with respect to p is adequate for acting on p.

(2) In the low-stakes case, the technician knows that the logger reading is within tolerance.

(3) In the high-stakes case, the technician is not in a position to rely on that same proposition in practical reasoning; the responsible course is to seek further confirmation before releasing the batch.

(4) The two cases differ only in what is at stake if the belief is false. The proposition believed, its truth value, the technician's evidence, the reliability of the logger, the technician's level of confidence, and the absence of Gettier-type luck are all held fixed.

(5) Therefore, from (1) and (3), the technician does not know in the high-stakes case; from (2), she does know in the low-stakes case; and from (4), the only factor that can account for the difference is the practical stakes. So practical stakes can make the difference between knowing and not knowing.

Please tell me whether this argument is valid, and if not, exactly which step fails and why. I want to know in particular whether (1) is false because someone can know something while practical caution still forbids acting on it, whether (3) trades on a shift in what "in a position to rely" means, whether (4) conceals a difference in which possibilities are relevant in the two cases, or whether the argument actually goes through. I can also hear a contextualist objecting that the word 'knows' changes its content between step (2) and the conclusion, in which case the argument would be valid only on a reading that already assumes an invariantist view of knowledge. Address that possibility as well.
