I'm putting together a quiz for my reading group—a mostly LessWrong/ACX-adjacent crowd—and I'd like you to act as a philosopher of aesthetics who writes for exactly that kind of reader. The group has been circling the question of whether beauty is objective, and I keep noticing that people use "taste is subjective" as an applause light while also, ten minutes later, arguing with total confidence that one novel is better than another. I want a "which theory of aesthetic judgment do you actually hold?" quiz that sorts people by how they answer concrete cases, not by which label they like; no item should ask "do you think beauty is objective?" or "do you agree with Hume?"

The instrument should be built from the logical space rather than from a list of names. First work out the separating questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the positions in that space, and note any coherent-sounding combination of commitments that none of the standard positions occupies. The leaves must include at least these five: objectivist realism, on which beauty is a genuine property of objects and verdicts about it can be straightforwardly correct or incorrect; the Humean ideal-critic position, on which correctness is grounded in the convergence of practiced, comparison-rich, unprejudiced critics; the Kantian position, on which aesthetic judgment is subjective but claims universal validity through disinterested pleasure and the free play of understanding and imagination rather than through any rule or concept; the contextualist/practice position, on which standards are achievements of local historical communities and correct only relative to a practice; and plain subjectivism, on which there is no correctness and judgments merely report or express individual liking. Please include at least these five items, paraphrased in your own voice: someone who cannot enjoy a novel but, after a course on it, says they can now see it is excellent though they still do not enjoy it; two tasters in a group who alone detect leather and iron notes in a cask and are vindicated when a key on a leather thong is found at the bottom; a building judged beautiful both by its anxious owner and by a disinterested tourist, and the question whether one of those judgments is more genuinely aesthetic; a film the respondent enjoys while insisting it is terrible; and a disagreement about a ceramic cup between a seventeenth-century Japanese tea master and a twenty-first-century Scandinavian industrial designer. Add five more cases of your own that separate the five positions, drawn from food, music, writing, games, fashion, or similar everyday domains. Every case must be answerable by an intelligent reader with no formal aesthetics background and without knowing any of the five labels, and no item may presuppose familiarity with actual artworks.

For scoring, do not simply add up points toward each leaf; classify respondents on underlying axes—something like whether aesthetic verdicts admit of error at all, what makes one verdict better than another when they do, and whether the standard is practice-internal or practice-transcendent—and then map answer patterns onto positions. Be explicit about mixed profiles and uncodable patterns, and flag which pairs of leaves the quiz cannot reliably separate or where the questions themselves cannot bear the weight. I would like you to begin by brainstorming candidate cases and axes and stress-testing each item against the five positions before committing to the final instrument.

Please structure the response exactly as follows:

1. Design notes (roughly 200 words): the axes you chose, why they cut, and a short justification of the item set.
2. The quiz (roughly 1200–1500 words): ten items, each a concrete case with two to four unlabeled answer options, written so that respondents cannot see which option feeds which position.
3. Scoring key (roughly 500 words): precise rules for transforming answer patterns into positions, including tie-breaking and mixed profiles.
4. Taxonomy table (roughly 400 words): each leaf with its diagnostic commitments and the answer pattern typical of it.
5. Unoccupied and unstable combinations (roughly 150 words): combinations of your axes that are logically possible but that no sane position occupies, and whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
6. Separation failures and caveats (roughly 150 words): which positions the quiz cannot tease apart and what a respondent with that profile should do.
