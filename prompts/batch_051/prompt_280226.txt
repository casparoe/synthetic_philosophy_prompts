In De Rerum Natura Lucretius makes a big deal of the idea that atoms are like the letters of the alphabet: the same letters in different positions make different words, and different arrangements can turn the same set of letters into words with totally different meanings. He leans on this analogy to convince us that the world is made of a finite stock of indivisible bits that combine by arrangement in the void, rather than of continuous stuff that can be divided forever. I want you to audit how far that letter analogy actually holds for ancient atomism. Map the correspondences: what in the analogy is the atom, what's the void, what's a word or body, and what would correspond to letters being written in different orders? Then press it: letters aren't solid or impenetrable in the atomist sense, the blank space in a word isn't really void because the page and air are still there, and the same letters can form both meaningful words and meaningless strings—so where does the meaningfulness or function of a body come from on the atomist picture, and is that disanalogy damaging? Also flag which parts of the analogy quietly import features atomism denies or just ignores (for example, letters are conventional symbols someone has to read, while atoms are supposed to be mind-independent with no reader). Finally say whether the analogy can actually support the specific conclusions Lucretius wants—that matter is discrete, that all change is rearrangement, and that void is necessary—or whether it breaks at exactly the point where he most needs it.
