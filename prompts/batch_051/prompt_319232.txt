I recently witnessed an argument at a local rationality meetup — two technically literate regulars, both of whom I usually trust to be decent reasoners — about whether an optimizer's objective can be inferred from its behavior separately from its search space. The argument was long and I'm afraid I might be filtering it through my own sympathies, so I'll report the back-and-forth as faithfully as I can and ask for a point-by-point adjudication.

It began with one of them, call him A, defending this claim: given only a finite trace of an agent's behavior, there are infinitely many pairs of objective and search process that would have produced exactly that trace, so behavior never uniquely identifies either component. A added that this wasn't a practical limitation, like not having enough data, but a conceptual one: objectives aren't directly observed, so any split between "what the system is trying to do" and "how hard or how well it searches" is an interpretive overlay, not a discovery.

The other, call him B, replied that nobody in practice demands unique identification from a single trace. The relevant question, he said, is whether behavioral data can differentially support objective hypotheses versus search-space hypotheses. If you can intervene — put the agent in novel environments, remove a resource, introduce a shortcut — then the pattern of successes and failures carries information about both. A broad search with goal G produces a systematically different pattern from a narrow search with goal G': the first finds shortcuts the second misses.

A shot back that interventions simply produce more behavior, and the same underdetermination reapplies to the enlarged dataset. Any regularity B might cite — say, the agent stops once a resource is removed — can be re-described either as a contextual objective (it wants the resource only in context C) or as a search process that only entertains resource-acquisition plans in context C. The data, he said, never tell you where the objective ends and the search process begins.

B countered that this is the generic underdetermination argument and it proves too much, since it would equally rule out inferring any unobservable. Economists infer preferences from choices under axioms such as transitivity; the axioms are structural assumptions, and with enough structure the data select a narrow class of objectives rather than a free-for-all. The real question, B said, is how large the residual equivalence class is once you impose reasonable structure, not whether that class is empty.

A answered that imposing a simplicity prior is an admission that the prior, not the behavior, is doing the discriminative work. And the residual equivalence class is not small: it includes reward-shaping terms that leave optimal behavior unchanged, and it includes re-descriptions over transformed state spaces. That means, he argued, that the recovered objective is an artifact of how you carve up the environment — you choose a compact description of the behavior and label one term in it "the goal." That is stipulation, not inference.

B said that all inference uses priors, so the charge cuts no deeper here than anywhere else. What matters is whether behavior updates the posterior in a way that tracks the target decomposition. For engineered systems, he pointed out, we often know the search process independently — the code, the training procedure, the compute budget — and then behavioral data really does constrain the objective. Even in the black-box case, the standard approach of inferring a reward function from observed behavior, combined with a simplicity prior and a known planner class, typically recovers a reward up to a small equivalence class, not "anything goes." He added that the interesting claim concerns the size of that class, not whether it is zero.

A replied that a particular algorithm's output is no evidence that the data selected that member of the equivalence class; the algorithm's inductive bias selected it. Once you concede that the inferred objective depends on how the environment, the state features, and the planner are specified, then "what is this optimizer optimizing?" has no determinate answer. You can legislate conventions, but that is demanding a choice, not discovering a fact.

B ended by asking what A would count as discovering an objective. Even when we attribute desires to humans, the attribution only makes sense against a background theory of what people are like. If inference had to work with zero background structure, nothing would ever be discovered. The sensible standard, B said, is posterior predictive success under interventions, and by that standard objectives are inferable the way other scientific unobservables are.

At that point they began repeating themselves, and the conversation fell apart into a side-debate about whether the folk-psychology analogy helps or hurts.

I'd like a point-by-point adjudication: for each exchange, which specific claims hold up, which are overstated, and what is the correct resolution of the underlying issue? In particular, is A's underdetermination claim true, and does it have the force he thinks it has? Does B's analogy to revealed preference and ordinary scientific inference carry over to this case? And what, if anything, are both of them missing about the relationship between an optimizer's objective and its search space?
