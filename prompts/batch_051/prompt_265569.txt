For the past few years I have been trying to work out what it means to know how to do something rather than merely to know that something is the case, and I have talked myself into a position that is tremendously appealing on paper but keeps colliding with an intuition I cannot shake. I want help figuring out which of the two should give way, or whether both can be kept.

The position is this. There is only one kind of knowledge, and it is knowledge of facts. To know how to do something is to know a great many true facts about that activity: facts about what follows from what, about what to expect, about what the body should do under what circumstances. A skilled baker, on this view, is someone who has accumulated an enormous store of such facts and can act on them fluently. The fact that she cannot put her knowledge into words does not show that it is not factual; it only shows that the facts are too many, too fine-grained, and too deeply tied to the feel of the dough to be retrieved into speech. I like this view for three reasons. It makes the world simpler, because there is one kind of knowledge rather than two. It explains why both instruction and practice improve skill: each adds true information, instruction in words and practice in feel. And it refuses to make skill mysterious; a skill is not an extra ingredient attached to the facts but simply a large body of correct information about how things go.

But here is the intuition that will not leave me alone. An aunt of mine has kneaded bread by hand for longer than I have been alive. She can press a single finger into a batch of dough and say it is ready, yet she cannot produce the rule she is following; she just knows. When she makes that judgment, it does not feel like the fast retrieval of many stored facts. It arrives whole, the way a face is recognized. And a person who had read every book on bread-making but never once kneaded dough would know more true facts about fermentation and gluten than my aunt does, and would still not know how to bake. There seems to be a difference here that sheer quantity of facts cannot close: the difference between having information about a practice and having the practice itself. If I call my aunt's readiness-judgment a collection of facts, I place a factual structure behind what she does, but I cannot point to a single fact she would recognize as her own.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a way to keep both. In particular: what would need to be true about skill, about introspection, and about the difference between my aunt and the well-read novice for us to accept that knowing how just is knowing a very large number of facts, and that the sense of something more is an illusion produced by fluency? Conversely, what would a decisive demonstration that skill cannot be reduced to facts have to look like; what would it have to rule out? And is there a possible reading on which both claims are true: a sense in which the facts my aunt knows are tied to her body and her situation so tightly that they cannot be lifted out into a rule, so that she both knows a great many facts and knows something that outruns any report of them?
