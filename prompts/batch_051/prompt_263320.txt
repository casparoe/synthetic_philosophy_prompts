I keep tripping over a tension in how my own university justifies itself, and I want someone to write me the implicit theory of value that would make the whole package coherent, if any exists.

Here's the practice, concretely. The same institution that will fight to protect a tiny program in, say, Assyriology or historical musicology -- two faculty, six majors -- and will defend it on the grounds that knowledge is worth having for its own sake and that a university is not a trade school. And yet the same administration, in the same budget cycle, demands that nearly every new hire in that very division include an "impact statement," that every major publish career outcomes and starting salaries, and that the strategic plan talk almost entirely about workforce development, innovation pipelines, and regional competitiveness. So we fund the useless as internally valuable and then justify the invoice in terms of usefulness somewhere downstream. The question isnt just whether that's hypocrisy; it's which theory (or theories) of the value of scholarship the institution is actually committed to.

So reconstruct the implicit theory of value -- or theories, plural, if different parts of the university are committed to different ones -- that this pattern of funding and defending pretty much useless scholarship presupposes. Concretely:

1. Does the practice strictly presuppose that some knowledge is valuable for its own sake? Or is the whole thing compatible with a purely instrumental story once you add the right epicycles -- option value, the chance that ancient languages or pure math turn out to be useful later, the role of obscure fields as an intellectual ecosystem or seed bank, or the thought that the university is really selling prestige and credentials rather than knowledge?

2. Are the parts in tension? Can you defend an Assyriology department as intrinsically valuable while also making its faculty prove impact, or are those two justifications inconsistent unless you smuggle in something like intrinsic value still imposes a duty to be instrumentally useful?

3. Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined? That is, what rival theories of value would all license roughly this institutional behavior, and what's the cheapest observable change in behavior that would tell them apart?

4. What would a university built on a thoroughly instrumental theory of the value of scholarship actually have to change? Would it kill the field with no foreseeable downstream payoff, or just fund it on a lottery-ticket basis? And what would a university built on wholehearted intrinsic-value grounds have to change about how it talks to legislatures and donors?

Don't give me a bibliography. But do tell me what would change your mind about the reconstruction: which argument or observation would flip you between the intrinsic-value reading and the indirect-instrumental reading, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
