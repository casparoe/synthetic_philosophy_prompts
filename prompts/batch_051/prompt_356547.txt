I'm giving a short talk in two weeks at my local rationalist meetup, and I want to do a pre-mortem on the Q&A rather than walk into it blind. The talk is on voting ethics, and I'm defending what I've been calling a two-frames view. Roughly: there are two ways people think about an individual vote, and most of the online fights happen because each side pretends the other frame doesn't exist. Frame one is the vote as a tiny intervention—technically positive expected value, but probably dominated by the chance of being the decisive vote, which is basically nil in any electorate above a few hundred thousand. Frame two is the vote as a cheap expressive act: it declares something about what you value, and it can be good or bad for reasons that have nothing to do with the margin. My claim is that both frames are legitimate but they do work at different places. In a plausibly tight race, the intervention frame is the honest one, and lesser-evil reasoning is not just allowed but pretty close to what you should do if you care about consequences. In a blowout, the expressive frame is doing all the work, and framing a vote as lesser-evil is usually a way to launder a purity preference into consequentialist vocabulary. The part I know is weakest is the boundary: there is no sharp threshold where one frame turns into the other, and most real elections sit in a murky middle where I suspect we're partly reasoning and partly rationalizing in real time.

The crowd will be maybe a dozen people. Three or four are committed consequentialists, mostly EA-adjacent, who have said before that voting is a rounding error and if you happen to enjoy it, fine, just don't pretend it's a duty. Two are Kantian-ish deontologists who think vote-your-conscience survives this whole debate and that universalizability matters more than marginal impact. One is a self-described epistocrat who thinks most people shouldn't vote at all because they're not informed enough to exercise power over others. The rest are agnostics who will pounce on any move that looks like a motte-and-bailey.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defense. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually arise, phrased the way those specific people would phrase them—the consequentialists will talk expected value and opportunity cost, the Kantians will talk about willing the maxim, the epistocrat will talk about information and competence. For each objection: what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. Then tell me the objection I'm most hoping nobody asks, the one that threatens the two-frames picture itself rather than one of it's applications.
