I'm a software engineer and I keep tripping over the same tangle when I read postmortems after big outages, so I'm going to try to pin it down.

Here's the contrast. Case one: a company makes a medical device. The firmware team knows a sensor can fail in a rare low-battery state, the complience team knows the company promised regulators the device had no single-point sensor failure, and the marketing team knows they advertised "zero unexpected shutdowns." No one person holds all three facts, and if you asked each of them individually whether the company had lied to anyone, each would honestly say no. The device fails, someone dies. Regulators fine the company for knowingly selling a defective device, and everyone—including me—is comfortable saying "the company knew, and it lied."

Case two: a stock message board with no moderators and no membership rules. Over a week, thousands of strangers each contribute one scrap about a pharma company's failed trial—one person posts a shipping manifest, another notices a domain registration, another recognizes a doctor in a photo at an airport. No single poster has the full picture, but the aggregate of their posts tips off enough traders that the stock crashes before the company announces. A pension fund loses a lot of money. Almost nobody would say "the message board knew the trial failed and lied about it." We'd say the market just processed scattered information.

So we treat the company like a thing that can know and lie, but we treat the message-board-plus-traders like weather, even though in both cases the "knowledge" is distributed across people who never compare notes and there's no single brain that has the whole picture. I want to know whether that asymmetry is actually justified, or whether it's just personification plus legal convenience.

What's the strongest principle you can find that makes the company a blameworthy knower/liar but not the message board? Test it—I especially want to know how it handles variations like: what if the company is a small startup where the same three people do talk, but each still doesn't connect the dots seperately? What if the message board has moderators and a shared mission? What if the company's decision procedure is literally "the VP signs whatever pops out of the ticket queue without reading it"? At the end, tell me straight whether you'd keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or drop it somewhere in the middle.
