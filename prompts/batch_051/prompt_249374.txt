One of my housemates is doing a PhD on Nietzsche and she's developed a rule I can't decide whether it's deep or just bias with a reading list. When she's deciding how seriously to take a philosopher's views about value, the good life, the passions, she first digs into how the person actually lived in their body — what they ate, how they slept, whether they walked, their digestion, their migraines, whether they spent their life in a chair or out in weather. If the philosopher seems to have been a physical wreck (bad stomach, insomnia, chronic pains, self-neglect) she heavily discounts anything they say about how to live, and a bit about their metaphysics too; if they were sturdy and ate decently she treats their views as more trustworthy, even before she's read the arguments. She says this is just Nietzsche taken seriously — stuff from Ecce Homo about diet and climate and how a philosopher's gut is not incidental to their philosophy. She paraphrases; when I asked her for the exact line she waved her hands and said something about the spirit being a kind of stomach, but I don't trust her memory. I pushed back that this is textbook ad hominem, and she said: it isn't an argument from someone's digestion to the falsity of their conclusions; it's a policy for allocating scarce attention, and for deciding who gets to be your guide to living. She applies it mostly when she's deciding what to read and who to take as a model, not when she's evaluating a particular argument. Still, she admits her information about how these people lived is mostly memoir, anecdote, and gossip — she can't exactly run a dietary trial on Kant or Nietzsche. Is this a defensible epistemic policy, or just a bias dressed up as Nietzsche? If it's not defensible as she states it, can you restate it so that it keeps whatever is good in it — or should she drop it entirely?
