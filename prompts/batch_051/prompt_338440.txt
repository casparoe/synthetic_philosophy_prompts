In the philosophy club at my high school, the same argument about epiphenomenalism has been running for months, and from what I've read it is a version of an old, unresolved dispute among philosophers. One side holds that conscious experience is real but causally inert: brain processes produce qualia, the felt properties of experience, but qualia never feed back into the physical world, and a complete physical explanation of a person's behavior could in principle be given without mentioning them. The other side keeps pressing the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If qualia cause nothing, then they do not cause us to notice them, remember them, or say anything about them. Yet people talk about the felt character of their experience constantly, and their reports systematically track what that character is actually like. The epiphenomenalist answers that the reports are caused by the neural states that accompany the qualia, with the qualia along for the ride; the opponent answers that this turns our introspective reports into blind outputs that merely happen to correspond to the facts, and that a physical duplicate with no qualia would make the very same reports, which seems to leave out why we in fact make them. Both sides accept the same broad picture of the brain, so the impasse is not obviously about the neuroscience. I suspect the real disagreement concerns what it takes for a mental state to explain a judgment about that state: one side wants a physical causal story, while the other wants an account in which the judgment is held rationally and reliably because of what it is about. Diagnose this dispute. Is it verbal or substantive? Are the two sides differing about facts, about standards of explanation, or about bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a conceptual distinction, an empirical result, a formal argument, a better-posed question — or should we expect the impasse to be permanent?
