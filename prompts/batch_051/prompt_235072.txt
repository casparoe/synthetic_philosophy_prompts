I've been chewing on something and want your actual ranking, not a survey. In the deepfake/photo-epistemology stuff, most of the attention goes to whether a particular video is fake and how good the generators will get. But I've started to think of recordings less as a primary source and more as a tiebreaker: they matter most in the exact cases where people already disagree and institutions are inclined to side with whoever has more power. For the person claiming police abuse, or a whistleblower, or someone in a custody fight, a video is often the only thing that can force belief from a hostile audience. Cheap synthetic media seems to attack precisely that tiebreaker role, because it gives the powerful side a respectable way to dismiss the recording without having to supply an alternative account. That's the liar's dividend, and I'd say its worse than the more familiar problem of people getting duped by fakes, because it doesn't require any particular fake to be good — it just makes doubt cheap and deniable.

A friend says I've got this backwards. On their view the real damage is ambient: a world flooded with synthetic media slowly trains everyone to stop treating recordings as decisive evidence at all, without anyone ever challenging a specific video. The backstop doesn't get beaten in a particular case; it just quietly stops doing work across all cases. If they're right, the priority should be detection or provenance, not the epistemology of skeptical challenges.

Say I've got about a year of research time and a funder who will listen to a philosophical pitch for one direction in this area. Rank something like these four for me: (1) the liar's dividend and the epistemology and ethics of dismissing genuine recordings; (2) what it takes for a recording to function as a tiebreaker or backstop in the first place, and whether cheap fakes destroy exactly that; (3) whether the ambient trust-erosion picture is right and how to tell it apart from the dividend picture; (4) institutional or technical backstops like provenance, detection, or legal presumptions, and whether they address the underlying problem or just paper over it. Which one or two would you actually put the year into, and which would you tell the funder is overrated or doesn't follow from the real issue? I want the criteria you're ranking with — importance, tractability, how many other questions depend on it — and I want you to defend the order, not just describe the options.
