Here's something that's been bugging me. So much of how we buy and sign up for things now runs on the same two quirks of valuation. Online stores make returns almost frictionless because they know that once the jacket is in your house you value it more than you did on the store page — the endowment effect. And employers and governments increasingly set up retirement plans and organ donation as opt-out rather than opt-in, because they know people stick with the default — loss aversion, status quo bias, whatever you want to call it. What I can't figure out is what picture of preference and welfare these practices are actually committed to. Either these quirks are biases, mistakes in how we value things, in which case the practices are deliberatly exploiting a glitch (or, more charitably, nudging us for our own good). Or they're legitimate preferences, in which case there's nothing to exploit: the person who keeps the jacket or stays enrolled really does want it more than the person who never had it. But the practices seem to want it both ways. Retailers talk about returns as giving you freedom while pricing in the fact that you mostly won't use them; policymakers pick the default carefully because they know it changes outcomes, and then treat everyone who doesn't opt out as having made a choice. So: what theory of the endowment effect and loss aversion does this whole setup presuppose — bias, legitimate preference, or something more mixed? Does the practice strictly need one of these theories, or is it compatible with several? And what would have to change in how defaults and returns are designed and described if we came down firmly on one side?
