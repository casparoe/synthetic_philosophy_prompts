For a high-school essay on the history of scientific ideas I have been reading about the standard objection to Malthus: he argued that population tends to increase faster than the food supply, so that some check, such as famine or disease, must eventually hold numbers down. In the countries he knew best, the predicted crisis did not arrive on his timetable, largely because agricultural productivity rose far more than he expected. A common defence is that Malthus was not wrong, only early: the check was postponed, not cancelled.

That defence attracts me, but it seems to lead to a conclusion I cannot accept. Consider this argument.

(1) To call a prediction 'right but early' is to say that the predicted outcome was postponed rather than refuted by what actually happened.
(2) If a prediction may be saved by saying that the outcome was postponed, then it may be saved again and again in the same way, however many times it fails.
(3) So a defender who accepts (1) is committed to saying that a long-run prediction could fail every year forever and still be 'right but early'.
(4) A prediction that no possible course of events could refute is not an empirical prediction.
(5) Therefore, if Malthus's defenders may say he was right but early, they are committed to saying that no long-run prediction about population and resources could ever be refuted.
(6) But that cannot be right: we do sometimes take long-run predictions to be refuted by events, and learning from failed predictions would be impossible if every failure could be postponed indefinitely.
(7) So the 'right but early' defence must be rejected, and Malthus was simply wrong about his central claim.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which premise or step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (6), but I suspect keeping it forces the conclusion. Please answer in British English.
