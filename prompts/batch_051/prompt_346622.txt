Ever since encountering the Pasadena game, I have been stuck on what it shows about rational choice. In the standard game, the expected utility sum is conditionally convergent: rearranging the terms can make it converge to any real number or diverge to either infinity, so it has no well-defined expected value. I am inclined to reason as follows: expected utility theory supplies no verdict for the game, and if a theory supplies no verdict, then no price is ruled out. From there I conclude that paying £1,000, £1,000,000, or £0.01 to play the Pasadena game are all equally rationally permissible, and that there is no rational basis for choosing between the game and a certain £100. But I also feel that this conclusion cannot be right, because it makes the difference between accepting and rejecting the game arbitrary rather than merely uncertain.

Where exactly does this reasoning go wrong? Does the absence of a defined expected value really leave rational choice unconstrained, or do other principles—dominance, stochastic dominance, risk attitudes, or something else—still apply?
