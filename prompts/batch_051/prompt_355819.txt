I'm a retired physicist — spent forty years in spectroscopy, of all things — and I've only recently started reading philosophy seriously instead of through pop-science name-drops. Lately that means Huizinga's Homo Ludens and a fair bit of Pieper, mostly because I got elected to the committee of a very old games society and keep arguing with the other old-timers about one of our by-laws. So here's a practical question, but I want the philosophical machinery, not just the committee-room answer.

The by-law dates from 1907 and reads, in full: "No member shall play for a wager, a purse, or any other payment, nor accept any reward or emolument on account of their play; for play pursued for gain ceases to be play and becomes work."

Everyone agrees it was aimed at professionals and hustlers — paid exhibition players, people betting on their own games, that sort. The case that's split us: one of our best members, call her M, has always played the same games in the same style and never for an audience. A year ago she started streaming her home sessions on Twitch, and it took off. She now earns meaningful money from ads and subscriptions. She doesn't change what she plays or how, she ignores the chat while playing, and she insists she'd play identically if the income vanished. The money is caused by the play but doesn't enter into the playing.

So my question is whether the by-law actually reaches her, and wether there's a coherent reading of "play pursued for gain" under which it doesn't. I'm less interested in how the committee vote lands than in the interpretive problem underneath it. A flat textualist says "any other payment... on account of their play" is satisfied at the bank. A purposivist says the point was to stop gain from corrupting play, and hers isn't corrupted; it's more like someone paying to watch a garden she was going to tend anyway. But that might be too comfortable — if a musician plays exactly the same with or without a paying audience, is she still playing for the sake of the playing, or has the situation around the playing changed in a way that matters? And what should we make of the fact that the 1907 drafters couldn't have imagined broadcasting at all: if we asked them about pay-from-spectators, would their imagined answer control us, or is that just a way of smuggling our own views in?

Put on your best philosopher-of-law hat, but with a feel for Huizinga's idea that genuine play is done for its own sake. Start by brainstorming the two or three genuinely different readings of the clause — strict text, original purpose, reasonable member in 1907, reasonable member today — before you commit, and think out loud a bit. I can take a technical answer; just keep it in plain words, I'm new to this side of the library.
