After a comedy night last Thursday, two people from my writing group, Miriam and Saul, got into a proper row about what's actually going on when something strikes us as funny, and I've been turning it over since because I couldn't referee it on the spot. Miriam was defending the benign violation view: a thing is funny when it breaks a norm or expectation, but only if it lands as harmless or distant enough. That's why a bloke slipping on ice is funny and a bloke slipping and cracking his skull isn't, she said, and why the same joke dies at a funeral. Saul said that's not a theory of humour in its own right, it's the old incongruity theory with a safety valve bolted on: the incongruity does the cognitive work—expectation, suprise—and "benign" just tells you when the surprise isn't swamped by pity or fear. He added that superiority has been written off too quickly: we laugh at the pratfall partly because we're glad it wasn't us, and at wordplay because we're pleased to be in on it; relief theory captures the exhale but not the joke. Miriam shot back that superiority only survives by going metaphorical—"superior to language" can't be made to predict anything—whereas benign violation makes testable predictions: ratchet up the threat and funniness drops, add distance and it rises up to a point. Saul answered that "benign" is then doing all the work as a subjective judgement rather than a mechanism, and that pure incongruity cases like a surreal non-sequitur or a victimless pun show "violation" is either being stretched until it just means surprise, or the view doesn't cover half of what makes us laugh. Neither convinced the other. Can you adjudicate the argument they actually made, point by point: who is right about which bits, what if anything are they both missing, and who is more right overall? And could you write your answer in British English, please.
