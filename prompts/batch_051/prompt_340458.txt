Two friends of mine recently argued about a giving decision, and I have not been able to decide who has the better case.

Daniel plans to give most of his annual giving to an organization working on what he calls a trajectory-change project. The group's model assigns its intervention a one-in-ten-thousand chance of making the difference between a flourishing long-run future and a catastrophically bad one, with billions of lives at stake. Multiplying, Daniel concluded that the donation's expected value is thousands of lives saved from catastrophe, far beyond what any near-term charity he could fund would reliably produce.

Iris pushed back that the calculation does all the work while the numbers do none. The one-in-ten-thousand figure is not a measured frequency but a judgment from a model with no track record, and the estimate of billions of lives depends on a future no one can predict. Because the payoff is so large, she said, the conclusion would survive nearly any shrinkage of the probability or inflation of the stakes; insensitivity like that is a warning sign, not a virtue.

Daniel replied that refusing to multiply is refusing to reason about tradeoffs. If small probabilities are allowed to matter anywhere—and she accepts that a one-in-ten-thousand chance of a fatal drug reaction matters—they must be allowed to matter in philanthropy. To carve out an exception for large speculative payoffs is to impose an arbitrary cutoff.

Iris answered that her point is not a cutoff but an epistemic distinction. A drug-risk probability is grounded in observed frequencies across a population. A probability of steering the long-run future is not grounded in any comparable track record; it is a model output whose inputs could be moved an order of magnitude without contradicting anything observable. The appearance of rigor, she said, comes from the multiplication, not from the evidence.

Daniel came back that every expected-value calculation carries model risk, and the remedy is updating and sensitivity analysis, not abandoning the principle. If a vague worry that the model might be wrong could override the central estimate, any donor could say that about any number, and the result would be paralysis.

Iris replied that the long-term case is different in kind. Once the payoffs are allowed to be arbitrarily large for the purposes of the calculation, any tiny probability can be made to dominate any finite benefit, so the recommendation is controlled by whichever corner of possibility space one allows to carry the largest product. She called this the structure of Pascal's mugging: a payoff too large to be refused on any ordinary grounds, offered at a probability too small to have been earned by evidence.

Daniel answered that the far future is not a mugger. A mugger's promise comes from a stranger with no prior credibility, whereas the existence of a vast number of possible future lives is among the most robust claims we can make, even if our influence on them is uncertain. If the stakes really are enormous, then a small probability of influencing them can properly outweigh a near-certainty of helping a few people now; otherwise the size of the stakes would, absurdly, stop mattering beyond some point.

Iris said that this locates the problem without solving it. If the stakes are unbounded, there will always be a still more remote but still conceivable project that claims priority over the present one, and another after that, so the procedure never yields a stable ranking. Daniel replied that this shows only that the probabilities must be revised as evidence improves, not that the original calculation was wrong.

They asked me to referee, and I could not. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point: which claims are right and wrong, where each side overreaches, and what both of them are missing.
