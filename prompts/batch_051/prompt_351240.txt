I'm a novelist, so I wasn't much use when two friends of mine locked horns over this a while back, and its been nagging at me since. Callum used to write novels too but now writes for a big-budget war shooter, and Iris is a historian who works mostly on the First World War. We got onto Callums new project, which his studio keeps describing as a serious anti-war game, and Iris said that's basically a contradiction in terms.

Iris: A game has to ask you to enjoy getting better at it. The war becomes objectives, rewards, a skill tree, a kill feed. If the shooting feels satisfying you're glorifying it; if it doesn't, nobody plays. So the form is against you either way, and a soldier's death can't be a tragedy one minute and a failure state the next.

Callum: That's the argument people made about the novel — that it was light entertainment for idle people and couldn't be a serious form. Greek tragedy was performed as entertainment at festivals, with competitions and prizes. A medium isn't its worst examples. And interactivity has an advantage: in a film you watch a bad decision and judge it, but in a game you make it and live inside the consequences.

Iris: Sure, but in a novel or film nobody is having fun being good at the Somme. The pleasure of mastery is built into the mechanics themselves. You can put sombre music over the menus, but the loop is still compulsion and achievement. You aren't feeling the horror; you're enjoying your own competence and calling it seriousness afterwards.

Callum: No artwork controls what its audience feels. People watch a Holocaust film, pat themselves on the back for crying at the right moment, then go for pizza. The real question is what the systems make true. My favourite war game — not the one I work on — models supply lines and morale so well that the optimal strategy reproduces the historical stalemate. It doesn't just tell you the generals were trapped; it makes you feel the trap.

Iris: That's cognition, not grief. War is about bodies, and in a game no body is ever really lost. You respawn, reload, restart. Nothing is finally at stake, and that safety is the obscenity. A serious depiction of war has to include that the cost cannot be undone.

Callum: Then a documentary watched from a warm cinema is also unserious, because the audience is perfectly safe. And what about re-enactors, who put on the uniforms and drill on weekends? Is re-enactment categorically incapable of being serious, or does it depend on how it's done?

Iris: Honestly, most re-enactment is a hobby about kit and nostalgia, which rather proves my point — even people physically there mostly aren't serious about it. But the bigger issue is that the re-enactor isn't being scored. A game has to flatter the player to keep them, and flattery is the opposite of what war deserves.
