Give me a working definition of "semantic similarity" — the relation we're trying to point at when we say two words are close in meaning — that gets these cases right. For context, I work with word embeddings, so this isn't idle: the standard operationalization is cosine similarity between vectors, and it's famously weird on exactly the cases I care about. The cases:

- big/large (obviously similar)
- buy/purchase (obviously similar)
- hot/warm (similar, but not the same — is it just a degree thing?)
- hot/cold (opposites, so not similar)
- good/bad (same: opposites, not similar)
- king/queen (related, but I wouldn't call them similar in meaning)
- bank in "river bank" vs bank in "the bank foreclosed" (same string, two unrelated meanings — definitely not similar)

The embedding wrinkle is that cosine similarity thinks hot/cold and good/bad are extremely close, because opposites show up in the same sentence slots ("the water is __", "she's a __ person"), it tends to return "king" as one of the nearest neighbors of "queen" even though they're not synonyms, and a single vector for "bank" smushes its two senses together so it sits moderately near both "river" and "mortgage" at once. So I'm not asking for a definition of the measurement; I'm asking for a definition of the concept the measurement was supposed to be a proxy for. Propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, and tell me where it gives the wrong answer, forces you to bite a bullet, or makes me give up one of my own verdicts.
