ok so here's roughly where I've landed on Moloch and multipolar traps, and I'd like someone to check the whole set for consistency rather then re-explain the concepts to me. I'm after an audit, because I suspect I've been fudging something but I genuinely can't see which bit. I'll number the positions to make it easy.

(1) I'm an institutional optimist. The world is mostly improving, and markets, science, and open institutions do most of the heavy lifting. Almost every everyday coordination problem is solved by repetition, reputation, exit, prices, or social norms, and I'm suspicious of people who see a prisoner's dilemma in every queue.

(2) At the same time, I think Moloch — multipolar traps, races to the bottom, situations where individually rational moves sum to a collectively terrible equilibrium — is the best single lens on the bad stuff that remains. Arms races, status competition, credential inflation, performative work, antibiotic resistance, AI race dynamics, metrics becoming targets: these strike me as basically everywhere, and they're the main reason rich societies still do self-inflicted damage.

(3) I think the participants in a genuine multipolar trap are usually acting rationally. They aren't making an epistemic mistake: conditional on what everyone else is doing, each move is a best reply. The badness lives at the level of the equilibrium, not in any individual's reasoning.

(4) I think the practical error in most claims that a given situation is a Moloch trap is misdiagnosing the game. In a one-shot anonymous prisoner's dilemma, with no reputational carry-over and no enforcement, defection is the rational play, full stop, and any view that tells you to cooperate anyway is smuggling in iteration or selection effects. In an iterated game, cooperation and tit-for-tat-ish strategies are rational because the payoffs differ.

(5) But I also think that in some high-stakes, genuinely one-shot-ish races — say several labs or states independently deciding whether to publish or build something dangerous — the right move is often unilateral restraint even when continuing has higher local expected value. You don't want to feed the race, and your restraint keeps a slightly less awful equilibrium available a bit longer.

(6) And I think when we identify a Moloch trap, the right response is almost never to moralise at the individual defectors: it's to change the game, build institutions, move the default, alter the payoffs. But I don't think calling a situation a trap works as a blanket excuse for participating in it either; some agents have obligations not to pour fuel on the thing.

What I'd like from you: go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look that way on a surface reading. Order the genuine tensions by severity rather than tarring everything with the same brush. For each genuine tension, say what it would force me to give up and what the cheapest repair is, and then be decisive: recommend the single revision you'd actually make and tell me what it costs.
