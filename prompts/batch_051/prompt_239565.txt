A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about the color phi phenomenon and the so-called Orwellian and Stalinesque ways of interpreting it. After we stopped, I wrote up the argument I had been defending, and I now find it suspicious because its conclusion seems far too strong. I would like help locating the mistake: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?

First, the setup. In a color phi display, a red spot is flashed briefly at one location, and after a short interval a green spot is flashed briefly at a nearby location. With appropriate timing, subjects do not report two separate flashes. They report one spot that appears to move from the first position to the second and to change from red to green roughly halfway along the path. The puzzle is that the brain can only incorporate the green flash into the episode after that flash has occurred, yet the subject reports the color change at a point in the apparent sequence before the second flash appears.

Two standard stories are available. According to the Orwellian story, the subject first has an experience of a red spot and then an experience of a green spot; after the second flash arrives, the brain rewrites the memory of the episode, and it is the edited memory that the subject later reports. On this story, the revision happens after experience, to memory rather than to experience itself. According to the Stalinesque story, the conscious experience is delayed until after the second flash has arrived, so the first and only conscious experience is already the integrated moving spot; there is no later rewriting because the delay itself does the work. On this story, nothing is revised after the fact.

Now the argument:

(1) All evidence about what a subject experiences in the color phi display reaches us either through the subject's later reports or through neural measurements made around the time of the flashes.

(2) Any report the subject could give is consistent with both stories. A report of seeing a moving spot that changes color halfway can be treated by the Orwellian as a report of an edited memory and by the Stalinesque as a report of a delayed experience.

(3) Neural measurements are likewise consistent with both stories. Whatever pattern is found, the Orwellian can classify it as part of the later editing process, and the Stalinesque can classify it as part of the delayed construction of experience, so long as each story is allowed to adjust its auxiliary assumptions.

(4) Therefore, no evidence can discriminate between the Orwellian and the Stalinesque stories.

(5) If no evidence can discriminate between two hypotheses, then the choice between them is not an empirical or rational matter; it is merely a choice of description or taste.

(6) So the dispute between the Orwellian and the Stalinesque interpretations is not empirically or rationally decidable.

(7) But the distinction between having an experience and later misremembering or confabulating an experience is the same kind of distinction: it is the distinction between what enters experience itself and what is inserted afterwards into memory.

(8) By parity of reasoning, then, the distinction between genuinely having an experience and merely misremembering one is also not empirically decidable.

(9) That conclusion cannot be right. In an ordinary case—say, a witness who claims to have seen a face during a crime—there can be external evidence, such as what was recorded on video or what other witnesses observed, that helps determine whether she really saw the face or merely confabulated the memory.

(10) Since the conclusion is unacceptable, at least one of the earlier steps must be wrong.

I would prefer not to give up (9), and more generally I want to keep the everyday distinction between seeing something and only misremembering it. But I cannot locate the failure. Did the argument equivocate, or does one of the innocent-looking premises carry more weight than it can bear? Which step should I reject, and what do I lose—about the color phi case, about evidence, or about the epistemology of experience—by rejecting it?
