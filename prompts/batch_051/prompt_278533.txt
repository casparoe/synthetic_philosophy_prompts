In a reading group on vagueness, one of the participants set out an argument that none of us could find the exit from, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a survey of the terrain. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Consider a Sorites series for "heap." Stage 1 contains a single grain of sand, which is clearly not a heap. Stage 200,000 contains 200,000 grains, which clearly is a heap. Each stage adds exactly one grain, and the predicate is monotonic: adding a grain cannot turn a heap into a non-heap.

(2) Classical bivalence has no exceptions: for each stage k, the sentence "the grains at stage k form a heap" is either true or false, with no third status and no merely "borderline" truth-value.

(3) If every stage receives one of the two truth-values and the predicate is monotonic, then all the false stages form an initial block and all the true stages form a final block; the series cannot run false–true–false.

(4) A finite series whose false stages all precede its true stages has a unique cut: there is a least n such that n grains form a heap and n − 1 grains do not.

(5) Therefore "heap" has a sharp, unique boundary in this series — a single precise number, not a blurry region — fixed by nothing more than the truth-values of the stages.

(6) But no competent speaker can produce n. Any candidate is arbitrary, and no further observation of the sand, reflection on the word, or experiment would distinguish n from n + 1.

(7) So every competent speaker is necessarily and systematically ignorant of the exact point at which their own word "heap" switches on.

(8) That consequence cannot be accepted by a sane theory of meaning: a word whose precise extension is in principle unknowable could not be a word we teach, learn, and successfully apply. Its content would float free of everything we do with it.

We were left with the uncomfortable thought that at least one of (2), (6), and (8) must go, and each option looks expensive. Where does the argument actually go wrong, if it does? Please identify the precise premise or transition you would reject, explain why that premise seemed unassailable before it met this argument, and set out the price of each live option. In particular, what would a defender of classical logic have to say in order to make (7) and (8) consistent, and what exactly does the nonclassical route forfeit in ordinary reasoning with vague words?
