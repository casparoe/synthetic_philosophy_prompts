Please act as a professional philosopher of cognitive science with a good working knowledge of the history of automata theory in the mid-twentieth century. I am a retired natural scientist — my career was in experimental physics, so the technical material is familiar, but I am comparatively new to the philosophical literature — and I am drafting a short essay on von Neumann's views about the brain. Below I give you my notes and the central argument. I would like pointed comments on them. Please do not give me a reading list or citations; I want your critical judgment, not bibliography. Think carefully before you answer: begin by brainstorming several possible objections and alternative interpretations, discard the weak ones, and organize your response around the strongest.

The essay's target is a widespread shorthand that reduces von Neumann's interest in the brain to the observation that neurons fire in all-or-none pulses, and that the brain is therefore, in some important sense, a digital computer. I want to argue that this misses what he actually found puzzling. In the unfinished reflections on the brain published after his death, the emphasis falls less on the all-or-none character of the nerve impulse than on a contrast between two ways of achieving reliable function. The artificial automata he knew kept error rates at each elementary step very low and maintained sharp, nearly discontinuous distinctions between signal states. The brain seemed instead to compute reliably with components that were slow, cheap, crowded, and individually noisy, by means of enormous parallelism and a statistical organization rather than the clean step-by-step logic of circuits. My thesis is that the question he was really posing was not "Is the brain digital or analog?" but something like: "What kind of logical organization can support reliable, meaningful transformations when the elementary operations are not individually trustworthy?" I propose to call this a system's reliability architecture — the pattern of trade-offs among precision, parallelism, energy, redundancy, and error correction that allows a physical system to carry out organized transformations. The claimed payoff is that this notion cuts across the usual digital/analog divide, and to some extent across the hardware/software divide, and that it reframes recent debates about whether the brain literally performs probabilistic inference or merely approximates it. On this reading, the more illuminating question is not which formal idiom the brain "really" speaks, but which reliability architecture a proposed model attributes to the brain, and what that architecture costs.
