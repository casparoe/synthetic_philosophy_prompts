I keep going back and forth on weather mathematics actually believes its own rhetoric about what a proof is, and I'd like you to help me sort it out. I'm a grad student, and I've noticed that the four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture are treated as settled mathematics: they get taught, cited, and built on without much fuss. But their proofs are enormous computer searches that no single person has ever read in full. The four-colour proof works through nearly two thousand cases with a program, and the Kepler proof depended on a huge computation and was only made airtight years later by formalizing the whole thing in a proof assistant. In any human sense, the proof is unsurveyable: a referee who signs off is really vouching for the code, the description of the search, and the formalization, not for having followed the whole argument. Yet the same community talks as if a proof is supposed to be an argument a trained mathematician can hold in their head and see is valid, and we all still sneer at proof by checking lots of cases when it is done by hand.

So what view of proof and mathematical knowledge does this practice presuppose? Does accepting these theorems commit us to saying that a proof is a reliable procedure whose correctness could in principle be independently checked, even if no human ever will check it? Does it turn the computer into a witness, so that proof becomes partly testimony? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology as best you can, say whether it is internally consistent (for instance across how we treat huge finite case-checks versus probabilistic or heuristic evidence), and tell me what the field would have to change if it went back to the view that only humanly surveyable proofs count. I care less about the history than about what the practice is presupposing right now.
