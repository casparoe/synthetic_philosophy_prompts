Question: Explain Nozick's tracking analysis of knowledge, making clear how its two subjunctive conditions are relativized to methods. Then use a concrete example to show how the tracking theorist argues that knowledge is not closed under known entailment. Finally, state the strongest objection a defender of epistemic closure could press against this rejection of closure, and sketch how the tracking theorist might begin to answer it.

Student answer: Nozick's idea is that knowledge requires not just true belief but belief that tracks the truth. Tracking is spelled out by two conditions. Sensitivity says: if p had been false, the subject would not have believed p. Adherence says: if p had been true, the subject would have believed p. Both conditions must be evaluated relative to the method the subject actually used, not some other method, because otherwise the account penalizes subjects for using a generally reliable method in an unusual world. The standard illustration is the brain-in-a-vat. I know I have hands, and I know that having hands entails that I am not a brain in a vat. Yet I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat, because my belief in that conclusion fails adherence: in the nearest worlds where I am not a brain in a vat, my hand-like experiences are still caused by the vat, so I still believe I have hands. Since the conclusion fails to track, knowledge is not closed under known entailment. This shows that knowledge is not closed under multi-premise entailment, though the tracking theorist can leave single-premise closure intact. The strongest objection to this is that rejecting closure makes it mysterious how deduction from known premises could ever yield new knowledge: if I know the premises and know they entail Q, how could I fail to know Q merely by deducing it? The tracking theorist's reply is to stress that deduction changes the method in play, and that the deduced conclusion must track the truth under the deductive method, which skeptical conclusions will not.
