I teach a philosophy seminar on the foundations of human rights, and one of my better students has been pushing an argument I can't shake. He says the concept of human dignity is either paternalistic or redundant, so it can't possibly be doing the foundational work people assign to it. It goes like this, roughly as he wrote it on the board:

(1) A value can justify a right-conferring norm only if either it adds prohibitions that ordinary respect-for-autonomy morality doesn't already contain, or it adds none, in which case it's redundant and can't ground anything. (2) Dignity either adds a prohibition or it doesn't; there is no third option. (3) If it adds a prohibition, the only place it can add one is where the person consents to the treatment, since nonconsensual treatment is already ruled out by autonomy-and-harm norms. So dignity's distinctive work would have to be the paternalistic work of overriding someone's choice for her own good. (4) But dignity theories, at least the broadly Kantian ones, present themselves as anti-paternalistic: dignity is supposed to underwrite respect for the person as a self-governing agent, not to act as a brake on her choices. (5) So dignity can't be doing paternalistic work without ceasing to be dignity in the relevant sense. (6) Therefore dignity adds no prohibitions to autonomy-based morality, and "human dignity" is ceremonial language pasted over rights that are actually grounded in autonomy and harm alone.

I've tried the obvious counterexamples on him. The patient with advanced dementia, he says, is protected by her prior or would-be autonomous wishes; the dead body or the permanently unconscious person is not someone there is a duty to at all, only a duty about, owed to the living. And the person who voluntarily sells herself into degrading conditions is making an autonomous choice, so by premise (3) any dignity-based intervention would have to be paternalistic. So none of the easy cases help.

I want to reject this, but every premise looks plausible, and when I try to deny one I find myself giving up something I'd rather keep. I'd most like to keep two claims: first, that we wrong a person with advanced dementia when we treat her degradingly even when there are no prior wishes to go on, and that dignity, not autonomy, is what explains the wrong; second, that a person can degrade herself in a way that genuinely wrongs herself, without my judgment thereby being paternalism toward her. I'd really like to here where the argument goes wrong, which premise to give up, and what the least costly replacement is. And if you think the argument is basically sound and I should just accept that dignity is ceremonial, say why that's the better result rather than pretending it has an easy flaw.
