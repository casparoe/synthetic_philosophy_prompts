I'm stuck on an argument a friend made, and the conclusion seems way too strong but I can't locate the slip. Here are my actual preferences in the Allais setup (small stakes). Call the options A, B, C, D in order: A = a sure $3000; B = an 80% chance of $4000, otherwise nothing; C = a 20% chance of $4000, otherwise nothing; D = a 25% chance of $3000, otherwise nothing. I take A over B, no hestitation — B's expected value is a bit higher, but the sure thing wins for me. And I take C over D, again with no hesitation.

My friend says A over B plus C over D is the common-ratio version of the Allais paradox, and he runs this argument:

(1) If both preferences are rational, the independence axiom is false. C and D are just B and A scaled down by a factor of 0.25, with a common 75% chance of nothing added. Independence says adding that shared 75% chance of nothing shouldn't flip my preference, but it does.

(2) Now consider a two-stage version. Stage one: 25% chance you reach stage two, 75% chance the game ends with nothing. At stage two, if reached, you choose between A and B. Choosing now, from stage one, to take the sure thing at stage two is exactly D; choosing now to take the gamble at stage two is exactly C. Since I prefer C to D, I should choose now to take the gamble if stage two is reached.

(3) But if I actually land at stage two, I'll take A, the sure $3000, because that was my preference when A and B were right in front of me. So the same underlying choice — A versus B — gives one answer when I choose it directly and another when I choose it from behind the 25% chance of reaching it.

(4) And that's exploitable. Suppose I start out holding the "sure thing at stage two" plan. Since I prefer C to D, I'd pay a little to swap it for the "gamble at stage two" plan. Then, if the 25% event hits and I reach stage two, I'll be holding the gamble; since I prefer the sure thing once I'm there, I'd pay a little more to swap back. Run the setup repeatedly and I reliably pay to end up exactly where I started.

(5) A money pump like that is basicly the strongest operational sign of irrationality decision theory has. So my two preferences can't both be rational.

(6) But they both feel rational — that's the whole point of the paradox. So either the independence axiom is non-negotiable, in which case nearly everyone's ordinary choices are irrational, or money pumps don't certify irrationality, which sounds absurd. Something in (1)-(4) has to give, but I can't see wich step, and I don't know what it costs.

I'd rather keep my two preferences and ditch the independence axiom than call the sure-$3000 choice a mistake. Give me a committed diagnosis: which step do I reject, why did that step look fine, and what exactly am I on the hook for if I reject it — do I have to own the dynamic inconsistency and the pump, or is there a real way out I can actually use? Don't lay out options and leave me to pick. Take a stance and defend it.
