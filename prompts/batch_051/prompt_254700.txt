I'm a novelist, not a philosophy person, so fair warning that this is probably a third- or fourth-hand thought. But I've been working out a little theory for a few years, and I want a real verdict on it, not a survey.

I call it the Third-Person Trap. It started as a craft observation. When I write a character, there are two modes that won't mix. In one mode I decide for her: her next move is genuinely open until I settle it, and I discover it from the inside, almost like it costs me something. In the other mode I already know what she'll do because I outlined it, and then I'm just narrating a settled fact. What I noticed is that the move from the first mode to the second isn't just a difference in information; it changes what the character is. Once her action is something I predict, it stops being something she decides. I can write the words "she chose to lie," but from inside the draft the choosing is already done. The character has become a fact about the book rather than someone with a live decision in front of them.

Then I started catching the same machinery in my own head. Right now I'm chewing on a real choice: whether to move across the country for a teaching job. There are plenty of things I can say about myself in predictive mode — I'm very confident I won't burn down my house, won't enroll in dental school. But those aren't live options. The job is live. And I've found I cannot honestly both deliberate about it and assign a probability to my doing it. If I tell my wife, "seventy percent chance I take it," I've half-stepped outside myself; now deliberation feels like either wiggling to match the forecast or defying it, which isn't deciding at all. So here's the principle in its grand form: no one can hold a genuine subjective probability about their own currently live choice without turning that choice into a settled event and poisoning the deliberation. Prediction of yourself, the moment the thing is still up to you, is the third-person trap. You can't write yourself in first person and third person at the same time.

I know this sounds like free-will stuff, but I'm explicitly not saying choices are uncaused or that determinism is false. I'm making a smaller, meaner claim: prediction and decision are two different activities with different operating requirements, and running both at once on the same object is an unstable posture — closer to trying to sign your own name while shaking your own hand than to a metaphysical impossibility. A craft observation, blown up into a claim about what an agent can rationally do.

What I want is a committed assessment. Tell me whether this has legs or is a dressed-up confusion, and defend that yes or no hard enough to survive cross-examination. Am I just re-covering something the philosophers have under a cleaner name? Does the novelist/character analogy carry real weight, or does it smuggle in the thing it's meant to prove? And what's the strongest objection to the modest version — the one that attacks it on its own terms, not the free-will objection — and is it fatal?
