I'm running a small film-and-philosophy group this spring, and we're doing a unit on Merleau-Ponty's idea that the lived body has its own kind of intentionality, what he calls motor intentionality. The rough idea: before any explicit representation of the world or of my own body, I'm already bodily oriented toward tasks; my posture and hands respond to the practical meaning of a situation in a way that isn't a proposition I entertain. Movement isn't just the execution of a mental plan, it has its own directedness and its own kind of understanding. I wanted to use the original Karate Kid as a way in, because the whole montage where Mr. Miyagi has Daniel waxing cars, sanding the floor, and painting the fence and the house seems like almost a textbook dramatization of this: Daniel's body is being formed into a karate repertoire while his conscious mind is convinced he's just a kid doing chores for an old man.

I'd like a careful reading of the film through that lens. What does it get right about the difference between a movement known as an explicit, nameable technique and a movement the body can deploy as spontaneous, situation-responsive skill? Where does it oversimplify or invert the phenomenology—for instance, by treating Daniel's training as the mere ingraining of fixed routines, or by staging his big realization as the moment bodily know-how finally gets translated back into explicit self-knowledge? Does the final tournament, with its rules and the crane kick as a single decisive move, fit the picture of skill as a flexible bodily style, or does it quietly pull against it? I'd like an honest reading that says where the metaphor holds and where it breaks, not a pure vindication, and it would help if you kept the Merleau-Ponty side concrete and anchored in scenes rather than jargon, since the group has more film people than philosophy people.
