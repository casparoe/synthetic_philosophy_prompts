You're an expert on legal epistemology and evidence law, especially the literature on naked statistical evidence (the blue bus and gatecrasher examples) and the question why bare frequencies so often dont count as proof. I'm a PhD student in philosophy of law, and I volunteer as an adviser to my university's academic honesty tribunal. The tribunal is a panel of three faculty and two students, none of them lawyers, and it meets once a month to hear a handful of cases under a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard; a finding of responsibility can end in suspension or expulsion, and the chair (a professor of history) signs off on every decision. Lately we keep getting cases where the only real evidence is statistical: for instance, a student's wrong answers on a multiple-choice exam line up with the wrong answers of the student next to them at a rate that is wildly unlikely under independent answering, or a student's essay matches a hard-to-find online essay on 90 percent of its sentences, but no one can establish the student ever saw that source. The university's lawyers sometimes say bare statistical evidence just isn't proof, but they never tell the panel what to actually do with that. Please turn whatever legal epistemology actually knows about naked statistical evidence into a decision procedure the panel can follow. It should list the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather then facts. It has to be usable by non-lawyers with no statistical training in a single sitting, and it should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as rules. Then run it on three cases: the exam-copying case, the essay-match case, and a case where a fraternity is accused of stealing an exam and the only evidence is that its members' scores on that exam are suspiciously higher than the rest of the class. I especially want to see a case where the procedure gives an answer the panel won't like. Also flag, for at least one case, whether the answer would change under a clear-and-convincing standard. Start by brainstorming the main reasons philosophers have offered for treating naked statistical evidence differently from individualized evidence, then use that to build the procedure, but keep the final procedure short enough to hand to the panel.
