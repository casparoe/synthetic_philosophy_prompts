I keep getting stuck on one fact in the solar geoengineering stuff I've been reading, and I want it as a poem rather than another explainer. The fact is this: the sulfate particles you'd inject into the stratosphere to reflect a couple percent of sunlight only stay up there for about a year or two before they rain out. That's the reason the whole enterprise gets described as reversible—if something goes wrong, you just turn it off and the sky clears. But the exact same fact is what makes termination shock so bad: if you ever do turn it off while CO2 is still elevated, the warming you were masking comes back at a pace that ecosystems and agriculture can't adapt to. So the "off switch" is simultaneously the main safety feature and the main hazard. I can't find a prose way to say that without it becoming a policy memo, hence the poem.

So: please write a strict villanelle from the point of view of a shift operator at a stratospheric aerosol injection facility, maybe explaining to their kid or a visiting regulator why the program is safe, and slowly realizing they're not able to finish the reassurance. The two refrains should carry the two readings of that one fact—something like "it washes out, so we can stop anytime" and "it washes out, which is why we must never stop." Let the refrains alternate through the tercets and land next to each other in the closing couplet, so the reader sees they're really the same sentance with the valence flipped. It should be accurate enough that someone in solar geoengineering governance would recognize you understand termination shock and not just the word "geoengineering," but avoid the jargon. If the operator's pitch brushes against the moral-hazard worry, let it; don't force it. And leave the bind open at the end—no escape hatch, no resolution of who should decide.

Format it as six numbered stanzas: the five tercets numbered 1–5 and the closing quatrain numbered 6. No title, no epigraph, no introduction, and no gloss after the last line.
