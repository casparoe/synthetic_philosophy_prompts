I am a novelist, and over the years I have settled into an epistemic rule that I treat as ordinary intellectual hygiene. When two sources of advice about craft pull in opposite directions and I cannot settle the disagreement on its merits, I defer to the source whose judgments are subject to the tighter feedback loop. For example, I weight a television writer's claims about narrative structure above a literary critic's, because the television writer's choices meet production constraints and a quick ratings-and-notes cycle, whereas the critic's judgments pass through slow, prestige-mediated gatekeeping and rarely encounter an unambiguous check. I am effectively treating the speed and clarity of a field's feedback as a proxy for how much that field has been forced to learn. Is this shortcut defensible, or am I dressing up a bias toward commercially legible taste as an epistemic principle? More precisely: under what conditions does tight feedback select for knowledge rather than for mere adaptation to local incentives, and can I reasonably assume those conditions hold in the cases where I apply the rule? Please separate, in your answer, what is settled among people who work on verifiability and progress from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
