I keep coming back to the old experience machine thought experiment: you can plug into a machine that gives you whatever experiences you want, and once you're inside you won't know it isn't real. Most people say they wouldn't plug in because they want to actually do things and be in touch with reality, not just have the experience of doing them. I've been trying to live simply for a while — buy less, grow some of my own food, slow down — and Ive been trying to work out whether that changes my answer.

Here's the version I keep chewing on. The machine works exactly like the usual one, except the life it feeds you isn't non-stop pleasure. It's a life of voluntary simplicity. You grow food and sometimes the slugs get it. You fix a leaky roof with your neighbor. You spend an afternoon chopping wood and mostly feel bored and sore. You eat plain meals and catch yourself feeling a little smug for not buying anything. It all feels completely real, and inside you don't know it's simulated. But before you plug in, outside the machine, you know what you're signing up for, and you pick it anyway.

Would someone who actually values voluntary simplicity plug in? The usual anti-machine argument is that you want real accomplishments and real contact with the world, not just their felt copies. But in this version the life inside is full of restraint, discomfort, and small failures — the person is giving things up and sticking with it, or at least having the experience of doing that. So is that genuine ascetic practice, or is it just another way to buy the feeling of having given something up while nothing is actually forfeited? I'm trying to figure out whether the simulation splits the usual motives people have for simple living. If you're after the internal state — wanting less, paying attention, patience — maybe the machine works as well or better. If you're after the actual footprint — using fewer resources, not propping up a wasteful system — the machine gives you none of that. Does the usual reality-matters answer survive this version, or does it turn out to have been leaning on the fact that the original machine was selling pleasure, so the verdict shifts once its selling asceticism instead?
