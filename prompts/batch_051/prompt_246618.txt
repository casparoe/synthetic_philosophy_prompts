I am a retired natural scientist—geochemistry, for what it is worth—and have only recently begun reading philosophy systematically. I find that folk maxims do more argumentative work in everyday moral reasoning than I used to notice, and I would like one of them taken apart with the care I would once have given a disputed assay.

On a recent trip I was travelling in a country whose tipping practices are very different from those I grew up with. I asked a fellow traveller, an economist, whether I was under any obligation to adopt local practice or merely well advised to do so. He answered with the proverb “When in Rome, do as the Romans do,” and plainly took it to settle the question. It did not settle it for me, but I found I could articulate only fragments of why.

My sense is that the proverb folds together several quite different claims. It might be a piece of prudential advice: you will get better service, or avoid confrontation, if you comply. It might be a claim about respect or recognition: by conforming to a practice that matters locally, one acknowledges the standing of locals to set the terms of that interaction. It might be an epistemic claim: locals know how the practice works and what its violation signals, so their conventions are the best available guide. It might be a stronger normative claim: local conventions generate genuine obligations for visitors, at least when the conventions are not themselves wrongful. Or it might be something close to a claim about authority or standing: whatever the merits of the convention, a visitor has no business reforming or flouting it.

I would like you to do three things. First, lay out the distinct precise theses the proverb could be expressing, from the most deflationary to the most demanding, keeping them genuinely distinct rather than restating one idea in different words. For each, say whether it is plausible on its own and whether it actually gets the economist his conclusion in the tipping case. Second, test those readings against at least two neighboring cases that make me uneasy: queueing, where a local norm can be strong yet invisible to a visitor until it is violated; and jaywalking, where a widely followed local practice conflicts with the letter of the local law. A reading of the proverb should say something sensible about all three, or explain why the proverb only governs some of them. Third, say which reading the economist most likely needed, and whether it survives.

Finally, I ask you to be explicit about the fragility of your verdict. Tell me which argument or counterexample would lead you to revise your ranking of the readings—or, if you prefer, where your reasoning about at least one of the readings is most likely to fail. I would rather know the weak joint in the analysis than receive a confident list.
