In Montaigne's essay Of Cannibals (Book I, Chapter 30 of the Essays), he first says, in substance, that people have no criterion of truth and reason other than the opinions and customs of their own country: everyone calls barbarian whatever differs from his own practice. Later in the same essay he turns around and says that the Europeans' habits of burning people alive, feeding them to dogs, and torturing them under a pretext of piety are more barbarous than the Brazilians' habit of killing prisoners and eating them after they are dead. Are these two moves in tension? Does the second claim require a non-relative standard of cruelty even though the first seems to rule one out, or can he use barbarous in two different senses? Give exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. In the five points, lay out the most plausible reconciliations—different senses of a term, a shift in target, a hidden natural standard, an outright inconsistency, etc.—and say which one needs the least special pleading.
