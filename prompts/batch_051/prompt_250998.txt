I teach a first-year undergraduate module on political philosophy, and next week I'm doing Rawls on religion and public reason. About 18 students, 75 minutes. I want an activity where they actually feel the force of the duty of civility and the proviso, rather than me just telling them what Rawls says. The group is pretty mixed: some religious students, some very secular, and I don't want anyone to have to defend their own beleifs, so role cards are essential.

The idea I want them to run into themselves is this: in a pluralist society you often can't justify laws to people who don't share your faith unless you translate your reasons into terms they could accept. And there's a real cost to that — it can feel like being told your deepest reasons don't count. Then I want them to feel the difference the proviso makes: Rawls doesn't say you can never bring religious reasons into public debate, only that they have to be backed up in due course by proper political reasons.

Could you design a structured deliberation or game along those lines? Something like a town council deciding a genuinely divisive policy — say whether to keep funding faith schools, or whether the council should open meetings with a prayer; pick whatever you think will work best, or use one per round. Two rounds with different speaking rules: one strict round where comprehensive doctrines are off the table, and one proviso round where religious reasons are allowed but have to be backed by political reasons. Then a debrief. I want the full kit: rules, role cards or a way to generate them quickly, a list of public values they're allowed to lean on, and a way to enforce the rules that doesn't just become me nagging. In the debrief, map what actually happened to Rawls: reasonable pluralism, the duty of civility, the proviso, overlapping consensus. And be honest about what the activity can't capture — there's no real stakes, and Rawls's public reason is narrower than all public speech. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, especially people adding a fig-leaf public reason in the proviso round and students feeling silenced in the strict round.

Oh, and write the answer in British English please, not too formal.
