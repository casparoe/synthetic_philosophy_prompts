I'm in a philosophy of cognitive science seminar and I'm thinking about writing my final paper on the symbol grounding problem, but before I commit to it I'd like some comments on the basic argument, because my professor tends to hate papers that just complain a problem is badly posed. The essay I have in mind is aimed at the Harnad-style worry that a system manipulating formal symbols can't be "about" anything because its symbols are ungrounded. My one-line thesis is that grounding doesn't stop the regress it's supposed to solve; it just pushes it down a level, so the standard framing is either trivial or unsolvable. The argument goes like this. The problem starts from the thought that a purely syntactic system--say, a lookup table that translates Chinese symbols into other Chinese symbols--has no way to hook its symbols onto the world. The standard fix is to ground at least some symbols in nonsymbolic capacities: perception, action, analog transduction, sensory icons, something like that. But here's the move I'm less sure about. Those capacities only help if the system can use their outputs, and as soon as it uses an output, that output has to take on a representational role. If the output stays genuinely nonsymbolic, it's just a physical quantity, and a physical quantity no more refers to the thing that caused it than a symbol does. If instead the output is allowed to carry information about its cause, then we've already conceded that symbols (or symbol-like states) can carry information too, and the original problem collapses. So either the "grounding relation" is just more symbol processing all the way down, in which case the challenge is self-defeating, or grounding doesn't add anything, in which case the original problem was overstated. The bit I'm most worried about is the middle option: maybe iconic or analog representations are nonarbitrary in a way that breaks the regress, and I haven't figured out exactly why that fails.

So what I'd like from you: (1) tell me whether the central dilemma--grounding either is just more symbol manipulation or doesn't do any work--is fair to the way the problem is usually set up, or whether I'm attacking a strawman; (2) say where the argument is weakest and what the strongest Harnad-style reply would be; and (3) suggest one concrete amendment that would make the paper's conclusion more constructive than "the problem is ill-posed," since I need an original positive claim. Dont suggest readings or citations, I just want argument-level comments. American English, please.
