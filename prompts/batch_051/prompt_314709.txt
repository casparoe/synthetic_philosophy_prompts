I've been thinking about Parfit's hitchhiker — the one where somebody stranded in the desert is rescued only after promising the driver money, and then once they're safe the narrowly self-interested reason to pay has evaporated — and I want a clean case that gets at the "rational irrationality" side of it. The basic idea I'm chasing is that an agent might do better overall by making themselves into someone who predictably follows a locally bad rule, even when the rule fires in a case where nothing causally enforces it. Every version I write ends up cheating, usually by letting reputation leak in, or by giving the agent such bad guilt that paying is actually the less unpleasant option, which collapses the whole thing back into ordinary self-interest.

So: construct a thought experiment to specification. The case should be in the hitchhiker genre (a promise extracted under real pressure, a later moment where reneging is the prudent local choice) but rigged so that a disposition to honor the promise is ex ante better than a policy of always renegging when it becomes prudent. Constraints: one ordinary-life scenario, no reputation, no legal enforcement, no informants, no perfect predictors or exotic tech, no afterlife, and the agent's unpleasant feelings about breaking a promise can be present but cannot do the work. After the scenario, do the check: say what a local expected-utility maximizer does at each node and why; what a dispositional or commitment-based view says (and why it calls this rational rather than merely moral); and what an enforcement-skeptic says (someone who holds that a promise with no causal teeth has no rational grip at all). Then identify the single structural feature that creates the gap between the ex ante and ex post evaluations, say what the case does if that feature is removed, and give the strongest objection from someone who thinks the "useful disposition" is really just a self-imposed illusion. Hard cap: 700 words.
