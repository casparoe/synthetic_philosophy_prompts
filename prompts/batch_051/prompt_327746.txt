I write about the science of decision-making, and I keep coming back to a problem in the theory of legal proof that leaves me genuinely torn. On one side is a theory I find hard to resist: legal proof is, at bottom, probability. The civil standard—the balance of probabilities—is just a posterior probability above one half; the criminal standard of beyond reasonable doubt is a much higher threshold; and once the evidence has been weighed, the fact-finder's only question is whether the probability that this defendant did this act crosses the relevant line. The picture is unified, treats all evidence in one currency, and replaces vague talk of proof with an explicit decision rule. On the other side is an intuition I cannot shake. Suppose a bus hits a pedestrian and the victim sues the Blue Bus Company; the only material fact established is that the company operates 80 per cent of the buses on that road, so the probability that its bus caused the injury is 0.8—comfortably above the civil threshold. I, like many people, recoil from finding the company liable on that bare statistic. Or take a rodeo at which 980 of the 1,000 people in the stands climbed over the fence without paying; a man is charged with gatecrashing on the strength of nothing except that he was one of those 1,000. The probability that he gatecrashed is 0.98, yet I would not want him convicted on that alone, and I doubt a jury would either. The puzzle sharpens when I notice that forensic DNA evidence is now often presented in something close to this form—a match probability, a frequency in some reference population—and yet courts treat it as paradigmatic proof rather than as a bare statistic. So which should yield? Is the intuition merely a bias: a failure to see that a probability is a probability whatever its source, or a confused demand for evidence that feels 'individual' when individualisation adds nothing to the numbers? Or does it track something the probabilistic theory leaves out, such as a requirement that a verdict explain or causally engage with what this particular defendant did, or that convicting and holding someone accountable is an act with evidential preconditions that bare frequencies cannot satisfy? Or is there a reading on which both survive, because the probabilistic account captures when evidence is relevant while the stricter account captures when it is sufficient? Do not simply announce which side wins. Give me the strongest version of each move, then say what would change your mind: which argument, or which empirical finding about how fact-finders actually reason, would lead you to abandon the probabilistic theory, and which would lead you to abandon the intuition—and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
