I am a novelist, not a philosopher of science. I am drafting a novel whose protagonist is an economist who has built a labor-market model, and the hinge of the book is a scene in which he must answer, in public, for the fact that his model assumes things he knows to be false: that every worker knows the full distribution of wage offers before searching, that firms can revise posted wages continuously and at zero cost, that all agents discount the future at one fixed rate, and that no one enters or exits the market over the model's horizon. He is convinced that some of these false assumptions are legitimate idealizations and that others are distortions which quietly determine the results, and that the difference is not settled by the familiar line that what matters is predictive success. I need to understand the real difficulty before I put words in his mouth.

What I want from you is the specification a theory of idealization and unrealistic assumptions in scientific modeling would have to meet before I would count it as adequate. Adequacy here is fixed by a purpose: the theory must be good enough to let a practitioner decide, for a particular false assumption in a particular model, whether it is a permissible idealization or a defect, and to explain how one could tell without merely waiting to see whether the model's predictions happen to succeed.

Give me the requirements list. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it and say why it is a requirement rather than a prejudice of one side of the debate. Include the distinctions the theory may not collapse — for example, between a harmless idealization and a result-driving distortion, between predictive and explanatory adequacy, and between an assumption that can in principle be removed without loss and one whose removal would dissolve the model — and the facts about scientific practice it may not contradict. The cases should be ones a working scientist or economist would recognize without specialist reading: the frictionless plane, the ideal gas, point masses, perfect competition, infinite populations, rational agents, and the like. Then tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are negotiable, and which single requirement excludes the most commonly held positions, including the view associated with Milton Friedman, sometimes called the F-twist, that the realism of assumptions is irrelevant so long as predictions succeed; say what that requirement rules in as well as out. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring of named theories. A table is welcome.
