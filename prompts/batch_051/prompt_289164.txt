Spinoza draws the line between forms of government like this. In a monarchy, everyone hands their natural right over to one person. In an aristocracy, they hand it over to a small council. In a democracy, nobody hands their right over to another person: each person transfers it to the majority of the society they remain a part of, and that's why everyone stays equal. That equality is the whole reason he thinks democracy is the most natural form of government, the one closest to how people where before any government existed.

Okay, now take a case. A large online community is governed by liquid democracy. Every member starts with one vote on every proposal. You can vote yourself, or you can delegate your vote on a single issue to any other member, and you can take the delegation back whenever you want. After a few years, the same hundred or so respected delegates end up casting most votes, because people keep assigning votes to them. Formally, anyone can recall or override at any time; in practice, almost nobody does.

Does this count as a democracy in Spinoza's sense, or is it an aristocracy with extra steps and better PR? Go criterion by criterion: equality, whether right has been transferred to another person, whether the majority still governs, and whether revocability changes things. Then say whether the case shows Spinoza's distinction is too coarse, or whether it shows formal equality is what matters. And flag any criterion the case leaves genuinely underdetermined.
