I got roped into the grants committee of our local mutual aid network, and at the last meeting someone's cousin, who did a philosophy MPhil and will not let you forget it, pushed through a scoring rule she called lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. The way it works in practice is this: every application gets sorted into one of two tiers. Tier one is anything whose main measurable effect is reducing extreme suffering, defined as distress above an 8 on a self-reported ten-point scale, even if only one person is affected. Any tier-one project automatically outranks every tier-two project, no matter how many people the tier-two project would give small enjoyments to. Only after all the tier-one projects are funded or rejected on feasibility grounds do we compare the rest, and in that second tier we are told to score one hour of mild pleasure as exactly commensurate with one hour of mild pain, so a street festival and a queue-shortening project get ranked by a single cost-benefit arithmetic.

Leaving aside whether this is morally right, I am trying to figure out how the rule actually behaves over a year. What does it pressure applicants to say or genuinely do? Where does it get gamed first: pain-score inflation, relabelling projects as extreme-suffering reduction, splitting one project into many small "extreme" cases, disputing where the threshold sits, or something else? What happens to mild diffuse goods and to long-term systemic projects that do not describe themselves easily as pleasure or pain? Does the committee itself start quietly moving the threshold around because it cannot stand to refuse a hundred children's painting classes in a row? Are the failure modes fixable without turning the whole rule into mush? Also, I know there is real philosophical disagreement about whether one unit of mild pleasure can ever be weighed against one unit of severe pain, and about where the line for extreme gets drawn. When you answer, please separate the parts that are settled mechanism-design lessons from the parts that are genuinely contested among people who work on negative utilitarianism, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
