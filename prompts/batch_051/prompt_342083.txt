I've been going back and forth on Feyerabend's whole anything goes thing, and I've come up with an argument that seems to show way more than even he wanted, so I'm convinced I'm making a mistake somewhere but I can't find it. Here's the argument:

(1) Feyerabend's main historical claim is that every methodological norm philosophers have proposed as essential to science—falsifiability, replicability, no ad hoc moves, whatever—has been violated by some episode we now regard as good science. Galileo pushed a telescope-based case that his contemporaries couldn't fully validate; Copernicanism hung around for decades while its predictive track record was worse than the Ptolemaic system; plenty of successful research programmes have kept themselves alive with moves that look ad hoc after the fact.

(2) If every norm proposed so far has been violated by at least one piece of good science, then no proposed norm is a necessary condition on good science.

(3) If no proposed norm is a necessary condition on good science, then there is no norm whose violation can by itself rule a practice out as non-science.

(4) If there is no norm whose violation can rule a practice out as non-science, then the distinction between science and pseudoscience has no content: astrology, creation science, homeopathy, flat-earth theory, plate tectonics—all science in exactly the same sense.

(5) But that's false. Whatever science is, it is not just the same kind of thing as astrology.

(6) So either Feyerabend's history is cherry-picked, or I've slipped somewhere between (2) and (4), or the whole picture of science as norm-governed is what needs to go.

I honestly can't find the slip. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I had to choose, I'd rather keep (1) than (5), but I don't know what that forces me into.
