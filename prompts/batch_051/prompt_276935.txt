You are a professional philosopher of economics and decision theory with an unusually good command of the empirical literature on reference-dependent preferences. I am a PhD candidate preparing a job talk for a department whose faculty is split between empirically minded behavioral economists and philosophers of action and practical reason. I want you to think carefully before writing: first brainstorm the likely objections separately for each bloc in the audience, then estimate how likely and how damaging each objection would be, and only then draft a polished Q&A prep sheet.

My defended thesis is that the patterns usually called loss aversion and the endowment effect — the fact that people typically demand more to give up a good than they would pay to acquire it, and weigh losses more heavily than equal-sized gains — should not be treated as biases to be corrected. They are, I argue, the signature of a defensible, context-dependent structure of practical reasons: what a good is worth to an agent can properly depend on whether the good is already hers, because ownership changes the baseline against which plans, expectations, and entitlements are assessed, not merely a psychological reference point that distorts judgment.

I defend this in three moves. First, the standard debunking evidence — for example, that the gap between willingness-to-accept and willingness-to-pay shrinks with market experience — shows only that these valuations are learned and context-sensitive, which is compatible with their being responsive to reasons. Second, the charge that the gap is inconsistent presumes a reference-independent notion of preference, and that is exactly the notion the evidence calls into question. Third, the idea that a loss and an equal-sized gain should be valued symmetrically misdescribes the practical situation of an agent who already holds the good and has organized plans, expectations, and entitlements around it.

The economists in the audience were trained in revealed-preference theory and experimental methods and are broadly sympathetic to the heuristics-and-biases program; several of them build reference-dependent models of choice. The philosophers are mostly Humeans about practical reason, who think preferences ground normative reasons rather than track them, but there are also two Kantian constructivists who will be suspicious of any view that allows contingent possession to shape what an agent should value. One senior person works on welfare economics and cost–benefit analysis.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, not a generic list. For each objection, indicate which bloc would raise it, phrase it the way they would put it, state how likely and how damaging it is, give a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, and flag where that reply is soft. Order the sheet by likely impact, and end with the question I should be most hoping nobody asks, with a note on why it is dangerous.
