I have been trying to reconstruct an argument that would explain why Henry Sidgwick, a philosopher whose ethics rests on a rigorous ideal of impartial utilitarian duty, spent so much of his later life investigating mediums, telepathy, and apparitions. Here is the reconstruction, and I half-suspect it is too quick. (1) Sidgwick held that practical reason is dual: it addresses the agent from two standpoints, that of her own happiness and that of everyone's happiness, and it gives no principle for settling cases in which they conflict. (2) If empirical evidence could establish that persons survive bodily death, then the demand to pursue one's own happiness would not be cut short at death. (3) The two standpoints conflict only when the general happiness asks of a person a sacrifice that cannot be recovered from within her own happiness. (4) Once a person's own happiness has an indefinitely extended horizon, no such sacrifice is in principle unrecoverable, provided the post-mortem world contains further goods for the person. (5) Therefore successful evidence of survival would resolve the dualism of practical reason by showing that the two standpoints need not finally conflict. I find step (4) suspicious but cannot articulate the flaw. Is the argument valid? If not, identify the first step that fails, and say whether adding a premise about the moral character of the post-mortem world would repair it, and what that premise would have to assert.
