I'm in an undergrad seminar on the moral psychology of inequality, and we just spent two classes on Rawls on envy (the excusable envy idea) and on a couple of articles about resentment as a political emotion. The room is pretty split: half the people think envy is always just a vice that poisons egalitarian politics, and the other half think feelings of envy and resentment are often the only signal you have that a distribution is actually unjust. I got volunteered to make a discussion activity for our study group, and I'd like it to be one of those "which position on envy and resentment do you actually hold?" sorting quizzes — the kind where the questions are all concrete cases and the scoring key sorts people by what they said rather than by what label they already like.

So here's what I'm hoping you can write: a 7-to-9 question quiz with a scoring key. Each question should be a short scenario — a coworker gets a raise you don't think they earned, a friend receives a big inheritance, a tenant resents their landlord during a housing crisis, a teammate feels bitter when a less skilled person gets an award, that sort of thing — with two or three answer options that map onto different underlying commitments. Please keep the cases mostly about distributive goods rather than romantic competition, because the envy/jealousy boundary is part of what I want to surface, but I don't want the whole quiz to collapse into relationship jealousy. The scoring key should sort answer patterns onto at least five positions: (1) the traditional vice view, where both envy and resentment are character defects to be rooted out; (2) a Rawlsian view, where envy proper is still something to regret but becomes excusable when it is directed at unjust inequalities and pushes toward reform; (3) a signal-of-injustice view, where resentment in particular is a fitting kind of moral anger that carries information about having been wronged; (4) a benign/malicious envy split, where envy divides into an emulative kind that can be healthy and a hostile kind that cannot; and (5) a data/epistemic view, where the feeling is neither virtuous nor vicious in itself but tells you something about what you actually value. For each position, add one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Also flag any positions the questions can't really seperate, like wether the signal view and the Rawlsian view about resentment actually come apart on these cases.

The questions should be answerable by people who haven't done the reading — so no technical vocabulary inside the cases themselves. The audience is a dozen undergrads with mixed majors. Oh, and please write the whole thing in American English: we're an international group but we default to US spelling.
