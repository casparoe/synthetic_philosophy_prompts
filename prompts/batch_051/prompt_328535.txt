I have been trying to arrive at a working definition of manipulation, because I find that people use the word with great confidence but cannot easily say how manipulation differs from persuasion, bargaining, or even well-meaning pressure. I would like a definition that separates manipulation from these nearby things, and that can actually be applied to the cases below.

Here are the cases I keep testing my definitions against, with the verdicts I am currently inclined to give:

1. A friend, concerned for your health, tells you — truthfully and because it is true — that your smoking is harming your young children. I would call this pressure or persuasion, but not manipulation.
2. A salesperson tells a customer that a used car has never been in an accident, knowing this to be false. I would call this deception and, I think, manipulation.
3. A university sends a fundraising letter featuring a particular scholarship student's photograph and story, saying that without donations students like her will have to leave. I cannot decide.
4. A political campaign runs an advertisement that uses accurate crime statistics but selects them carefully, sets them against dark imagery and a harsh voiceover, and omits context. I incline toward calling this manipulation, but I am not sure whether the selection of facts, the imagery, or the intent is doing the work.
5. A therapist deliberately asks "what happened next?" instead of "why did you do that?" because she knows the first wording tends to make patients less defensive and more forthcoming. I would not call this manipulation, even though the therapist is choosing words for their predictable effect on someone.
6. A parent tells an adult child that if the child does not enroll in medical school, the parent will stop paying tuition. The parent presents this as an attempt to steer the child toward a more secure future. I do not know whether this is manipulation or hard bargaining.
7. A video-sharing platform keeps recommending content it knows tends to provoke outrage, because outraged users watch longer and stay on the platform. No individual human is choosing to provoke you at the moment you scroll. I go back and forth on this one.

Propose a definition of manipulation that classifies these seven cases, and run the definition through each one. Then tell me where the definition disagrees with my tentative judgments, and whether you would revise the definition or stand by the verdict. Along the way, answer three sub-questions. Must manipulation be intentional, or can a system manipulate a person through its design even when no one is intending the manipulation at the moment of use? Must manipulation involve deception or concealment, or can it consist in the exploitation of a known weakness without any lying? And is the word "manipulation" doing two different jobs — one descriptive and one condemnatory — and, if so, which of the cases above fall under one job but not the other?
