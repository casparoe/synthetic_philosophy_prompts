I am in my final year of sixth form and am writing an extended essay on the claim that race is socially constructed. I understand the rough idea — that racial categories would not exist without continuing human practices of classification, even though the physical markers people use are real — but I want to move past the slogan by working through a controlled counterfactual.

Here is the scenario. In a medium-sized, ethnically diverse democracy, a new government enacts a de-racialisation law that takes full effect immediately. From that day, no organ of the state may collect, publish or use racial categories: birth certificates and passports are reissued without any racial or ethnic descriptor, the census drops the question, schools and hospitals stop recording it, equality law is rewritten so that a complainant must show they were treated worse than a similarly situated person without invoking a protected group label, and ethnic monitoring by employers becomes illegal. Nothing else changes at the start. Private individuals still notice skin colour, hair and facial features and still gossip, joke, date, hire and sell or rent housing exactly as they did before; neighbourhoods, places of worship, surnames, languages and social networks are unchanged; and private discrimination is no more policed than it had been.

I would like a reasoned prediction of how racial categories themselves change in that country over the next fifty years. I care less about whether the policy is good than about the mechanics: which parts of a social kind are carried by the state and which by ordinary face-to-face interaction; whether removing the official layer causes the kind to weaken, freeze in place, or simply migrate into unofficial proxies such as surname, religion, neighbourhood or accent; and where you would expect race to behave differently from money as a social kind, given that both are supposed to depend on collective recognition. Please structure your answer exactly as follows:

1. "Best prediction" (about 250 words): a concrete description of how people in this country classify themselves and others after fifty years.
2. "The mechanisms" (about 400 words): the main causal paths, written as explicit claims about how state categories and everyday categories feed each other, and which path you think dominates.
3. "What I am most confident about" (about 150 words): the links in your chain you would defend most strongly, and why.
4. "What could overturn this" (about 200 words): the assumptions or background conditions that, if different, would most change your prediction.
