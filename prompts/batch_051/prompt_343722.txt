In my twentieth-century history course we have been covering two things in the same unit, and I can't get the tension between them out of my head. We learned that a ban on ex post facto criminal law — the rule that you cannot punish someone under a standard that did not exist when the act was committed — is one of the markers separating a decent legal system from a tyrannical one. But we also learned that the postwar trials at Nuremberg and Tokyo are usually treated as a high point of international justice, even though they prosecuted people for categories such as "crimes against peace" and "crimes against humanity" that were, at the time of the underlying conduct, at best only loosely established in international law and plainly not part of the criminal codes the defendants lived under. When I mention this to the people around me, most seem to hold both judgments at once: retroactive criminal punishment is an abuse of state power, and the trials were plainly legitimate.

I want to know whether there is a principle that generates both judgments, or whether the real explanation is just that the Allies won and could write the rules. Give me the strongest possible justification for treating the international trials as legitimate while continuing to condemn retroactive criminal law in ordinary domestic settings. Then test it: would the same justification license a future victorious power to invent a new international crime after a war and prosecute the losers for acts that preceded the rule? Would it let a domestic court retroactively criminalize atrocities committed under an old regime, or should the two cases come apart? End with an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or given up. If my framing rests on a mistake — for instance, if you think the acts were already crimes under pre-existing international law so that there is no objectionable retroactivity in the first place — please say so plainly rather than working around it, because I would rather have the premise corrected than defended.
