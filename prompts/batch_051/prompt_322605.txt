I've been going back and forth on Mandeville since a rationalist Discord argument about whether "greed is good" is one of those true ideas that's socially dangerous to say in public. The thing that's bugging me is a specific moment in Bernard Mandeville's The Fable of the Bees, in the Grumbling Hive section, after Jove grants the bees the honesty they've been asking for. In the passage I'm thinking of, fraud disappears from the courts, but so do the luxury trades, demand for skillful work collapses, and the hive shrinks until the remaining bees move into a hollow tree. I used to read this as an early invisible-hand move: individual self-interest plus markets turns private vice into public benefit. But now I think that might be a modern projection. Mandeville seems to be using a really demanding moral psychology where virtue means having few wants, so once the bees stop wanting nice things there's nothing left for the economy to do. The self-interest doing the work before looked more like emulation, luxury, and status-seeking than plain accumulation.

What are the most important implications of reading the passage that way for contemporary defenses of greed as an engine of prosperity? I'm particularly interested in which parts of the modern greed-is-good story survive if the actual driver is status competition rather than acquisitiveness per se, and which parts were just leaning on Mandeville being read as a straightforward utilitarian apologist for greed. Also, because I'm trying to update rather than win an argument: say how confident you are in the reading, and single out the point you're least sure about.
