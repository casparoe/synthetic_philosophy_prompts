I am a novelist. A secondary character in my current manuscript—a semiretired logician who interrupts town meetings—argues that the tidy scientific generalizations on the health department's posters are not literally true at all, and that anyone who uses them in everyday reasoning is making a systematic mistake. I wrote the argument for him because I thought it would enliven the novel, but now I suspect the reasoning proves too much: if it worked, it would condemn half of ordinary thought, not just the bureaucrat I originally meant to mock. Here is the argument, as the character gives it at a meeting about mosquito spraying.

(1) A declarative sentence is literally true only if some state of affairs makes it true. (2) For a generic sentence of the form "Ks F," the only available truthmakers are statistical: either all Ks F, or most Ks F, or at least some Ks F. (3) "Ducks lay eggs" is true. (4) But neither all nor most ducks lay eggs—drakes and ducklings never do, and a given mature female is not laying during much of the year. (5) Nor can the truthmaker be "at least some ducks lay eggs," since under that reading the sentence would amount to a bare existential, and "Ducks are female" would come out true just as readily, which it is not. (6) So no statistical state of affairs makes "Ducks lay eggs" true. (7) Therefore "Ducks lay eggs" is not literally true. (8) The same reasoning applies to "Mosquitoes carry malaria," because only infected females of certain Anopheles species transmit the disease. (9) Yet sober adults rely on "Mosquitoes carry malaria" as a factual premise when they move from "there is a mosquito on the child's arm" to "there is reason to brush it away," and a sentence that is not literally true cannot serve as a factual premise. (10) So either a great deal of ordinary practical reasoning is invalid, or something in steps (1) through (9) must go.

I would most like to preserve (3) and (9), but I can be talked out of that. Which step should a philosophically careful interlocutor reject first, and why? What would that character have to give up in rejecting it, and what shape would an alternative account of sentences like "ducks lay eggs" need to take in order to keep the rest of the argument from re-forming? Please answer in American English.
