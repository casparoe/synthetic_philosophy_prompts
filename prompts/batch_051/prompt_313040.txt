One of the most discussed features of John Stuart Mill's On Liberty is that the principle limiting society's interference with individuals is not, on careful reading, meant to apply to everyone everywhere. Mill claims that coercing a person against their will is justifiable only to prevent that person from harming others, but he also holds that this principle applies only to persons in societies that have reached a certain stage of development; for peoples he judges not to have reached that stage, he accepts that a more advanced government may rule them without their consent, provided the rule genuinely improves them. This second strand of thought runs through his long service with the East India Company and his public defenses of British rule in India, where he blends an empirical estimation of local conditions with a general theory of civilizational progress. Compare these two strands as distinct ideas rather than as a contradiction to be resolved at all costs. Specifically, is the developmental threshold that appears to justify colonial paternalism already implicit in the harm principle's own conception of the person as a mature, self-improving agent, or is it supplied by an external theory of history that the harm principle does not itself contain? In other words, does Mill's case for governing India lean on his liberal principle at all, or does it lean on a separate doctrine of progress with its own justificatory logic? I would like an account that distinguishes principled developmentalism from ad hoc exceptions, and that states plainly which of the two ideas does the main work in his colonial writings.
