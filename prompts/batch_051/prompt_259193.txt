I have been working through the algorithmic-information-theory side of the philosophy of probability lately — Kolmogorov complexity, Martin-Löf tests, and the idea that randomness is a property of infinite sequences rather than of individual finite outcomes — and I have ended up with a tidy theoretical position and an intuition I cannot shake, and I want help deciding which should give way.

The theoretical position runs like this. No finite string is intrinsically random. A binary string can look disorderly, but 'looks disorderly' is a fact about my pattern-finding habits, not about the string itself; for any finite string there is some universal machine relative to which it has a short program, and under a fair coin every string of a given length has exactly the same probability. On this view, a string like 0110100111 that looks like a typical fair-coin output is no more and no less random than 0000000000, and to call the first random while calling the second non-random is either a category mistake or a disguised remark about the process that produced them. The serious notion of randomness — the one that tolerates a mathematical definition, as in Martin-Löf randomness — applies to infinite sequences or to sources, never to a single finite string in isolation.

But the intuition will not die. If I am shown a finite string that passes every standard statistical test of disorder, I want to say the string itself is random, and if I am shown an alternating or all-zero string, I want to say it is not. I do not think I am merely reporting that one would surprise me more than the other, and I do not think I am merely making an inference about the process that produced it, since I form the judgment even when I am told nothing reliable about the provenance of either string. This is not idle: talk of 'random samples', 'random passwords', and 'randomized controlled trials' all seems to predicate randomness of finite things, and statistical practice would be hard to do without some finite, task-relative notion of it.

I can see three exits, and I do not trust myself to choose among them. The first is to debunk the intuition: treat 'random' as shorthand for 'there is no sign of the mechanism of interest', with the apparent property of the finite string explained away. The second is to amend the theory: admit a legitimate but relative sense in which a finite sequence is random relative to a fixed description method, a significance level, or an intended use. The third is to insist that the intuition and the theory were compatible all along, because my intuition tracks compressibility under the specific encoding I bring to the table, or coherence with a probability model, rather than absolute randomness. Give me the strongest statement of each side, then tell me which exit you would take, what each exit would cost, and in particular whether a shallow, task-relative notion of finite-sequence randomness counts as real randomness or only as a convenient fiction. If you think the question itself is badly posed, say what the better-posed question would be.
