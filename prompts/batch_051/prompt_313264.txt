I am co-running a two-hour seminar for working literary translators — mostly prose translators, with no formal training in poetry — on a single claim drawn from Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot: that the formal choices of a source text are not a disposable casing around its propositional content, and that a translation which preserves the sense while letting the form go has not thereby been faithful. The session is built around the kind of case Hofstadter uses: a very short sixteenth-century poem by Clément Marot in which the content is a get-well message so slender that almost everything the poem does is carried by its metre, its short lines, its tight rhyme scheme and its refrain-like address. I do not want the explanation itself; I want a plan for teaching it. The participants will mostly arrive believing that a translator translates meaning first and that rhyme and metre are 'style' to be kept only when convenient.

The plan should tell me what has to be put in place before that claim can seem intelligible rather than obviously false, and in what order the pieces must be introduced, because what depends on what; what one carrying example should anchor the session and what features it must have and lack, so that the point does not seem contrived or hostage to a single special case; which misconceptions to expect — I suspect at least that form is ornament, that a facing-page crib or footnote preserves what matters, that constraint-following translators are playing games, and that because form can never be perfectly transferred the attempt is a distraction — and at which point in the sequence each should be headed off; how I can check at the end that they have grasped the claim as a claim about translation rather than merely acquired a taste for poetry; and what I should explicitly decline to settle in this session, such as general typologies of equivalence or the ultimate nature of meaning, so that the central idea stays in focus.
