I retired a few years ago from a career in the physical sciences and have since been working through moral and political philosophy more systematically than my earlier reading allowed. Next month I have agreed to lead a ninety-minute discussion for a small group of working scientists — mostly physicists and engineers, with one biostatistician — on the claim that some values are incommensurable and that hard choices are not merely poorly modeled or insufficiently informed ones. The group is not hostile; its members are simply trained to regard any problem as ripe for formalization, and they will arrive expecting that any genuine trade-off can be captured by a utility function if one is disciplined enough — and unlikely to notice that the demand for a single scale is itself a substantive assumption rather than a neutral methodological virtue.

I do not want an explanation of incommensurability; I have been reading this material and understand it. I want a teaching plan for the session. The deliverable should be a plan, not a lecture. Tell me:

- What must be in place before the core claim can do any work, and why. What conceptual distinction does the audience need before it can see how incommensurability differs from incomparability, and in what order should the pieces be introduced so that each step feels inevitable rather than asserted?

- The single example or case that should carry the whole session. It must involve a genuine choice between options each favored on a dimension that does not obviously yield to the other, with no hidden empirical uncertainty to explain the difficulty away, and it must not be a familiar culture-war or life-decision cliché that lets people retreat to prepared positions. It should be simple enough that everyone in the room can hold every part of it in mind at once. Say what the example must have and what it must lack, and why the obvious alternatives fail.

- The misconceptions this specific audience will bring or form, and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. I expect at least these: that incommensurability just means the options cannot be compared at all; that it is the same as their being equal in value, or as our being indifferent between them; that it is merely unresolved uncertainty about one's own preferences; and that if values are incommensurable, any choice between them must be arbitrary. Add others if you foresee them, and for each say where the plan should intercept it and how.

- What the session should openly refuse to do at this level — for instance, formal utility theory, the metaphysics of whether values are objective, or the recent discussions of parity — and how to say so without making the audience feel the session has withheld the real subject.

- One final check by the end of the ninety minutes that would distinguish someone who has genuinely acquired the distinction between commensuration and comparison from someone who has merely learned to say that trade-offs are complicated. The check should require the audience to produce or diagnose something themselves rather than recognize a definition.
