Consider the Pasadena game, the relative of the St. Petersburg game used to probe the limits of expected-value reasoning. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads; if the first head occurs on toss n, the player receives (2^n)/n dollars when n is odd and pays (2^n)/n dollars when n is even, so the sequence of possible payoffs runs +2, −2, +8/3, −4, +32/5, …. The probability of ending on toss n is 2^(−n), so the expected value is the alternating sum 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ⋯. This series converges conditionally but not absolutely; by rearranging its terms one can make it sum to any real number, to +∞, to −∞, or fail to settle. The standard moral drawn from the game is that it has no well-defined expected value, so expected-value reasoning gives no determinate guidance even though every payoff is finite and the probabilities are perfectly ordinary.

Now change exactly one thing: bias the coin. On each toss, independently, the coin lands heads with probability p, where 0 < p < 1, and the game ends on the first head as before; the magnitudes and signs of the payoffs are untouched.

Work through the biased game. In particular:

(a) Write the expected value as a series, giving each term as a function of p and n. Introducing r = 2(1 − p) will simplify the analysis.
(b) Analyze convergence. Determine, as p ranges over (0, 1), where the series converges absolutely, where it converges only conditionally, and where it diverges. Where it converges, give a closed-form expression for the expectation.
(c) Examine both sides of p = 1/2. What is the limit of the expectation as p approaches 1/2 from above? What happens for p just below 1/2?
(d) Draw the philosophical conclusion. In the original Pasadena debate there are at least two live verdicts: one, that the game has no well-defined expectation and expected-value theory is thereby in trouble; two, that the natural ordering of the outcomes privileges the value ln 2. Say which verdict the biased-coin family supports. Does the Pasadena-style failure survive the variation, or does it turn out to be an artifact of an exactly fair coin? If it is a knife-edge artifact, is that reassuring for expected-value theory, or does the existence of an undefined expectation at a single parameter value still matter? End by identifying the assumption or premise of the original paradox that changing the coin bias bears on.

2. The biased version (about 200 words). State the variant and derive its expected value as a series.
3. Convergence analysis (about 350 words). Work through the absolute, conditional, and divergent cases and give the closed form where one exists.
