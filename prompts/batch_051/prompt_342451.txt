I am the newly promoted supervisor of four trainee counselors at a small community mental health clinic. I meet each trainee for an hour of individual supervision every week, and every six months I write a formal evaluation of each trainee that goes both to the trainee's graduate program and to the clinic director, who signs off. Those evaluations help decide whether a trainee passes the placement. I have no philosophy background, and in a typical week I have about thirty minutes total to prepare for supervision.

I've been working out something on my own, and I need it turned into something I can actually use. People usually speak of respect as a way of behaving. But I've come to believe that, with my supervisees, correct behavior is not enough. I can ask open-ended questions, restate what the trainee has said, offer reassuring feedback, and document everything fairly — while privately having already written someone off, or performing interest in a case presentation I have stopped listening to, or offering kind-sounding feedback mainly to avoid an uncomfortable conversation. I have also done the reverse: given warm praise while not believing a word of it, because I thought warmth was what the trainee needed. I have a growing conviction that I owe these trainees something in my actual way of taking them seriously — call it recognition — and not only in my outward actions. But I know I cannot make myself admire people on command, and I suspect some of what I call recognition is really just my comfort with people I happen to like.

I keep confusing three things, and I need a procedure that keeps them apart. One is a basic taking-seriously that I might owe every trainee equally, simply because they are a person and my supervisee. Another is honest evaluative recognition that tracks what the trainee has actually shown, which cannot be spread around equally without becoming meaningless. The third is something more personal — liking, admiration, warmth — which I doubt can be owed at all. I want a reusable procedure, something I can run every week with every trainee rather than a one-time judgment about my current four, so that I catch the places where my actions are fine but my attitude toward a trainee is a lie, and also catch the places where I use honesty as an excuse for hardness toward someone I have already written off.

The procedure has to handle at least these hard cases: a trainee I like personally but who is not meeting standards; a trainee I find irritating but who is doing competent work; a trainee whose deferential style may be leading me to underrate their initiative; and an evaluation in which the truthful negative statement will hurt the trainee while the kind statement would be false.

Please write the response in the following sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths:

1. "What I am actually claiming" (about 300 words). Restate my half-formed principle as separate, precise claims, and tell me which of those claims I would actually be signing up for and which are overstated.
2. "The procedure" (about 900 words). A reusable checklist, with steps in the order I should run them, usable by someone who will never read the theory behind it. For each step, say what I am testing for and what I should do with the answer.
3. "Hard cases and judgment calls" (about 400 words). Show where the steps conflict or run out, and mark explicitly which points are genuine judgment and cannot be made mechanical.
4. "Worked applications" (about 600 words). Run the procedure on three of the hard cases above, including at least one case where the procedure tells me to do something I will not like doing.
5. "What this procedure cannot do" (about 250 words). State the procedure's limits and when I should set it aside.
6. "Settled and contested" (about 250 words). Separate what people who have studied this seriously generally agree on from what is genuinely disputed, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus.

Do not give me citations or a reading list. I need something usable, not references.
