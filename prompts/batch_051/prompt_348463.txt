I run a small poetry workshop and we've been going back and forth on whether effort is valuable in itself, or only because of what it gets you. I want a poem to close the session that doesn't lecture, so here is the assignment: write a sestina about the claim that effort can have value in itself. The six end-words should be the actual vocabulary of the debate, something like cost, worth, ease, struggle, outcome, and meaning; you can tweak them, but each one should earn its place by doing conceptual work rather than just filling a slot. Let the form do the arguing. The sestina's grind — the same six words forced through their rotations until the envoi gathers all six — should make the instrumentalist's case (this is just pointless toil, the repetition adds nothing) and the other side's case (the toil is what makes it a sestina instead of six loose paragraphs, so the form is part of the value) at the same time. Get the distinction right: the instrumentalist side isn't saying effort is bad, it's saying effort's value is exhausted by what it produces or signals; the other side is saying some value remains even when the result is the same or missing. Dont end on the thought that effort builds character. Sit in the tension, and let the envoi bring the six words together without pretending the argument is settled. Roughly ten syllables a line is fine but don't wreck the end-word scheme for the sake of meter. It should work as a poem for someone who hasn't read any of the philosophy, and someone who has should be able to see you know the actual positions.
