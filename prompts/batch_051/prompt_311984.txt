I'm a novelist, and in the book I'm drafting two characters argue about a bit of music: a slow cello theme that recurs at the end after the person its associated with has died. One character insists the theme is 'sad in itself,' not sad because of the story. The other gives him this argument:

(1) To be sad is to be in the emotional state of sadness, and only creatures with minds can be in emotional states.
(2) A melody is just an arrangement of sounds; it has no mind, no feelings, no inner life.
(3) So a melody can't literally be sad.
(4) If that's right, then when we call music sad we must mean one of two other things: that it expresses the composer's sadness, or that it makes the listener sad.
(5) But the first option fails: composers write convincing sad music when their perfectly cheerful, and audiences have no access to the composer's mood anyway.
(6) And the second fails too: the same passage can wreck one listener and leave another cold, and I can hear something as deeply sad without feeling sad at all — sometimes it's even exhilarating.
(7) So if the only alternatives are the two in (4), and both fail, our whole way of talking about sad music is just a mistake, a habit of make-believe.

I'm sure there's a slide here — I just can't point at it without sounding muddled. Is the argument valid? If not, where exactly does it go wrong, and what would the corrected version of that step be?
