Exam question:

"Set out the identity of indiscernibles, and explain why Max Black's two-sphere universe is thought to be a counterexample to it. Then reconstruct the strongest reply a defender of the principle could give, and say whether it succeeds."

Student answer:

"The identity of indiscernibles (PII) says that if two objects have exactly the same properties, then they are the same object. Equivalently, two distinct things must differ in at least one property. It's the converse of the indiscernibility of identicals, which says that if a and b are identical, then whatever is true of one is true of the other. Only the former is really contested.

Black asks us to imagine a universe containing two iron spheres, exactly alike in size, shape, mass, colour, and temperature, two miles apart in otherwise empty space. There's no observer and no other object around. Since the spheres share every intrinsic feature and the universe is perfectly symmetric, it looks like they satisfy the antecedent of PII while failing the consequent: there are plainly two spheres, not one. So Black refutes PII—at least the interesting qualitative version of it.

The strongest reply is that the spheres differ in their relational properties after all. The sphere on the left has the property of being two miles from a sphere to its right, and the sphere on the right has the property of being two miles from a sphere to its left. Relational properties are properties, so the spheres don't share all their properties and PII's antecedent isn't satisfied. Black's example only works if we perversly restrict 'property' to intrinsic, monadic properties. Once relations are allowed in, the two-sphere universe isn't a counterexample."
