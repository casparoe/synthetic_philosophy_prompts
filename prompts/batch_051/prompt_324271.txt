I teach a graduate seminar on decision theory, and I want to get a distinction straight before I present it. My current understanding is that orthodox expected utility theory already has a fully adequate representation of risk aversion: a concave utility function over money makes the expected utility of a gamble lower than the utility of its expected monetary value, and that, it seems, is all risk aversion amounts to. From this perspective, risk-weighted expected utility theory puzzles me. As I understand it, the theory says that a rational agent need not weight outcomes by their probabilities; instead, a risk function can transform those weights, allowing an agent to be sensitive to risk in a way expected utility theory allegedly cannot capture. But if probabilities are the best information we have about how likely the outcomes are, reweighting them looks less like a new theory of rational choice and more like a way of misdescribing the gamble itself. I would like to know where the confusion in this picture lies. What is the difference between the kind of risk aversion that a concave utility function captures and the kind of risk sensitivity that risk-weighted expected utility theory is meant to capture, such that the latter is not already available within orthodox expected utility theory?
