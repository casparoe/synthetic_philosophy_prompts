I keep going back and forth on epiphenomenalism, and before I read any more defenses of it I want to fix what a theory in this neighborhood actually has to deliver. So here's the assignment: write the desiderata for any theory of the causal status of phenomenal consciousness -- that is, any view about wether and how the felt hurt of pain or the redness of red makes a difference to what we do, say, or believe. I want the list of requirements such a theory has to meet to count as adequate, with the consideration behind each. And I most want to know which requirements are non-negotiable, which are just one camp's preferences, and whether some of them can't be jointly satisfied.

The one that worries me most is this: a theory has to explain why our reports about our own experience track the experience. If someone's pain is sharp, they say 'sharp'; if dull, they say 'dull'; the match is fine-grained and not a miracle. A theory that leaves that match as a standing coincidence has failed. For epiphenomenalism this is exactly the sore spot, because if the pain itself causes nothing, then my saying 'sharp' is caused by whatever physical state underlies the sharpness, and the pain itself is just along for the ride. So the theory owes an account of why the report and the quale line up so well without the quale doing any of the lining up. Is that a non-negotiable, or is it a demand only a physicalist would insist on?

Second, the theory has to say something explicit about physical causal closure, and I don't just mean take a position. It has to absorb the consequences of that position. If it rejects closure, it inherits the problem of saying where and how physical goings-on get pushed around by something nonphysical. If it accepts closure, it inherits the self-defeat worry: my belief that I have qualia is itself a physical event with a physical cause, so it would be just the same even if the qualia were absent or different -- which makes the belief look unjustified, which undermines the very introspection that motivates the theory. A theory must either block that inference, or explain why the resulting skepticism about introspection is survivable.

Third, any adequate theory has to explain why it introspectively seems to us that pain causes wincing, that the sharpness is why I jerk my hand back. Maybe that seeming is an illusion, but the illusion itself is a datum: a theory that says the seeming is false still owes a story about why introspection would produce it. Same goes for the first-person/third-person asymmetry: I know my pain in a way you can't, and the theory had better not make that asymmetry inexplicable or make it quietly vanish.

There are probably more requirements I'm missing -- for instance, something about why we talk about qualia at all if they do no causal work, and why evolution or learning would build a reporting practice for something inert. Add whatever else you think belongs on the list, with the case behind each.

Then the part I actually need: mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and say which subsets you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable. I have a vague sense that causal closure, phenomenal realism, and the reliability of introspection can't all be held at once without some violence, but I want that made precise. And which single requirement does the most work to exclude the candidate views? Finally, very briefly, score classical epiphenomenalism (qualia are caused but cause nothing), type-B physicalism (identity without an explanatory bridge), interactionist dualism (qualia push the physical around), and illusionism (there's nothing real to push) against the list -- but keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard.
