David Hume argued in his economic essays that money is best understood as a representation of labour and commodities rather than as wealth itself. He held that an increase in a country's money supply tends, over time, to raise its prices, so that a trade surplus is partly self-correcting: the inflow of money bids up domestic prices and gradually erodes the price advantage that produced the surplus. At the same time, he allowed that such an inflow can enliven economic activity for a while before prices fully adjust. He also located the durable sources of prosperity in productive labour, commerce, and sound laws rather than in the stock of precious metals, and he expressed deep unease about the long-run consequences of heavy public borrowing. He died in 1776 and never saw a monetary union of independent states sharing one fiat currency and one central monetary authority.

On that basis, predict as concretely as you can what Hume would have thought of a modern currency union such as the euro area, in which some member states persistently run large trade surpluses while others persistently run large trade deficits, and in which no exchange rates between the members can adjust. Would he have regarded those imbalances as broadly self-correcting and therefore tolerable, or as a sign that the arrangement was malfunctioning? What measures, if any, would he have been inclined to support, and what reasons drawn from his own views on money and trade would he have given for them?

Please state how confident you are in the overall prediction, and single out the point you are least sure of.
