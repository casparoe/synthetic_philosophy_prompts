Maxwell in his Theory of Heat sketches the thought experiment: a clever being sits at a partition between two chambers of gas and opens a small door so that swifter molecules pass to one side and slower ones to the other, making one side hotter and the other colder without doing work. I'm putting together a handout on why this doesn't actually beat the second law, and I've collected what I think are the four standard resolutions:

(1) Szilard's version: reduce the demon to a one-molecule engine. The demon needs one bit of information about which side of a partition the molecule is on, and the maximum work extractable from that bit is kT ln 2. So information acts like a currency with an exchange rate, not a loophole.

(2) Brillouin's point: the demon has to see the molecules, and seeing means illuminating the gas. The scattered photons heat the gas by more than the sorting cools it, so the measuring apparatus is itself the sink.

(3) Landauer's principle: even if measurement is reversible, the demon has finite memory. To keep operating it has to erase old records, and erasure dissipates at least kT ln 2 per bit into the environment. That's where the entropy bill is paid.

(4) Bennett's twist: measurement can be done reversibly; the irreversibility is all in erasure. So a demon with infinite memory could in principle put off the payment forever, but a finite, cyclic demon must pay.

Are these four basically the whole menu, or is there another kind of resolution I'm missing? I don't want a restatement of one of them, and I don't want a combination like 'Landauer plus Brillouin.' I want a physically or conceptually distinct point — maybe about fluctuations, the demon's own body, observer status, coarse-graining, whatever. If you can think of one, tell me what it is, whether it actually works or has a known flaw, and how it relates to the four. And write your answer in American English.
