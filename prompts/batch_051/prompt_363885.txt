For a piece I am working on about how later mathematics forced a reassessment of early modern attempts to make politics a demonstrative science, I want to work through Hobbes's case in detail. His position, briefly, was that geometry is the model of genuine knowledge because we construct its objects ourselves — we define the figures and generate them by our own motions — and therefore can know their causes and deduce their properties with certainty. He then applied this lesson to politics: the commonwealth is also a human artifact, so a science of civil life ought to be able to reason from precise definitions to necessary conclusions, with no less certainty than geometry, and without falling back on experience or contested authority.

The received view is that this program was later undermined from two directions. The discovery of consistent non-Euclidean geometries showed that the axioms of geometry are not uniquely forced on us by intuition or the nature of space; they are one choice among several workable systems. And the incompleteness theorems showed that a sufficiently strong axiom system cannot establish its own consistency, which casts doubt on the idea that a deductive system can certify its own foundations.

What I would like is a careful verdict on whether Hobbes's argument survives these developments, and in exactly what form. Which premise of the analogy carries the damage: the claim that geometry offers uniquely certain foundations, or the claim that political institutions are artifacts in the special sense needed to make them knowable through their causes? Is it plausible to argue that the non-Euclidean turn actually strengthens the constructivist core of Hobbes's view, while the incompleteness point lands elsewhere, or does that rescue miss something essential? And if a repaired version is possible, what weaker claim could still be defended once both mathematical developments are taken seriously, and what would it have to sacrifice from the original program? I want the parts of the analogy that are dead distinguished from the parts that might still be alive.
