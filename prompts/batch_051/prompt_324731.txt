I cover science and ideas type stuff for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a piece about whether inequality that comes about through freely made choices can still be a fair target of criticism. I've gone down a rabbit hole on the distributive-justice side — specifically the Nozick entitlement-theory argument with Wilt Chamberlain — and I've got an argument in my notes that feels airtight but ends somewhere I dont think anyone would want to defend. Tell me where it breaks.

Here it is, written out the way I'd put it in a column:

(1) Pick any distribution you consider totally fair, for whatever reason: equal shares, shares by need, shares by merit, whatever.

(2) If that distribution really is just, then each person's share is theirs to use as they want. So its not stealing or exploiting for someone to pay a little of their share for a ticket to watch Wilt Chamberlain play, and its not wrong for Wilt to keep the money.

(3) After a million people each freely do that, Wilt has a huge pile of money and the original pattern — equality, need, merit, whatever made the starting point just — is gone. But every single step was, by hypothesis, just. So the final distribution has to be just too.

(4) Which means any principle that requires the final distribution to keep matching the pattern is not protecting justice; its interfering with people using what is rightfully theirs. A patterned principle can only be a starting point, and it gets washed away as soon as people start doing things.

(5) And this is where I get nervous, because the move seems to prove too much. If the Chamberlain reasoning is sound, it looks like it rules out objecting to any outcome that accumulates through consensual transactions, as long as no individual transfer was forced or fraudulent: a labor market where people freely take dangerous jobs at low pay because the realistic alternatives are even worse, a family passing a fortune down five generations through ordinary gifts, a company buying up all the land around a town until it is de facto the boss of everyone who lives there. The end state ends up untouchable.

(6) But that can't be right. Even people who are pretty hostile to equality-of-outcome think at least some of those outcomes — the company town, the dynastic pile, the labor market with no real alternatives — are legitimate objects of criticism or political response. So the Chamberlain point, taken at face value, defends a lot more than anyone wants to defend.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than give them up, because I think there's something right about the idea that a fair starting point plus free transfers shouldn't become unjust just because a pattern shifts. But past that I'm open to being told any of the later steps is the weak one. I want a precise diagnosis of which step to reject or qualify, and an honest accounting of what I'd be paying for it.

- Step: which numbered step, or which move between steps, is doing the damage.
- Why it looks safe: the natural reading that makes it plausible.
- The catch: what it quietly assumes, equivocates on, or glosses over.
- Cost of rejecting/qualifying it: what you can no longer say, or what part of the Chamberlain point survives and what part you lose.
