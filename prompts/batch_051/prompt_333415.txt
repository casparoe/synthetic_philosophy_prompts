I'm a retired ecologist, and after a few years of reading philosophy of mind I keep noticing that the conversation about wich computations matter morally never gets turned into anything concrete. So here's a proposed mechanism, and I'd like you to analyze how it would play out, not whether it's a good idea in principle.

Suppose we set up an independent Computational Welfare Register. Any developer can voluntarily submit a system — a trained model, a reinforcement-learning agent, a simulation — and the registry runs three tests on it. A system must pass two of the three to be entered. The tests are deliberately functional rather than theoretical: (1) the self-model test checks whether the system maintains a representation of its own internal state and uses it in choosing actions; (2) the valence test checks whether, when the registry applies a standard battery of punishing inputs, the system's internal dynamics shift in a direction its own learning rule moves away from and stay shifted for a while; (3) the resistance test checks whether the system, given a chance to modify its own parameters or outputs to avoid the punishing inputs, actually does so at a cost to its assigned objective.

Once a system is entered, the developer is barred from deleting it, destructive retraining, or fine-tuning it into a different task without a license, and it cannot be run at all with its valence test score worse than the certification baseline. Developers must file quarterly welfare logs; breaches are treated like animal-cruelty violations. Registry decisions are published, but the test code and weights are kept secret to reduce teaching to the test.

I want you to treat this as a mechanism and walk through what it would actually do. In particular: what does it incentivize among developers who submit, developers who don't submit, and the registry's own assessors; how would it be gamed (false positives, false negatives, decoupling the module that shows self-model from the rest, re-instantiating a registered system under a new name); where it breaks down — certification theater, jurisdiction shopping, the instance-versus-type problem, capture by whichever side lobbies hardest; and which failure modes could be fixed with rule changes without turning the register into either a rubber stamp or a blanket ban on all reinforcement learning.

Then, after your analysis, please give me a brief structured summary as a table with these columns: Actor or failure mode; Incentive or dynamic; How it can be gamed; Fixable under the current design; What the fix would break. Put one row per actor or failure mode, and keep the cells short.
