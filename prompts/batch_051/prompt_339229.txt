I've been trying to figure out what makes a backward-looking explanation real vs. just a story we impose after we already know how things turned out. This came up at work (postmortems) but also in how I read business history and actual history. I have gut reactions on a bunch of cases but I cant find the rule. Here they are:

1. A startup makes a big risky bet, it pays off, and afterwards every interview says the bet was "obviously right." I don't buy it. At the time it looked like a coin flip, and if it had failed the same people would have called it "obviously reckless." The explanation feels built after the fact.

2. Historians explaining the outbreak of World War I point to alliance systems, mobilization schedules, and great-power rivalry. I find that convincing, even though nobody in 1910 could have predicted the exact war. Those factors were real before the war and seem to explain why it happened when and where it did.

3. A bridge collapses, and the investigation shows an engineer spotted a crack in an inspection photo a year earlier and never escalated it. I accept that as a real explanation: it says why this bridge collapsed while others didn't, and I can say what would have been different if he'd escalated it.

4. A team misses a deadline after their best engineer quits, and the postmortem says management failed to maintain a "bus factor." I don't like it. Tons of teams have no bus factor and still ship on time. It feels like a story that happens to fit the outcome.

5. A big product launch fails, and the retrospective says it failed because the company ignored early user feedback. I'm genuinely torn. The feedback was there and was ignored, but I also feel like every failed launch can be retold that way after the fact. It's probably partly real and partly hindsight, and I don't know where the line is.

6. A market crashes and people explain it by saying "it was a bubble." I think that's close to empty: it says almost nothing beyond prices went up and then down, unless you specify some independent feature that made the bubble identifiable before it burst.

My rough principle would be: a backward-looking explanation is okay to the extent it points to factors that were identifiable before the outcome and would have made me expect that outcome rather than its opposite. But I immediately see problems — sometimes an explanation only becomes visible after the fact (records get released, or the outcome reveals what mattered), and that doesn't automatically make it fake.

Can you actually work this out? I'm looking for: the principle (or a couple of candidate principles) that best captures these verdicts, stated precisely; which of my verdicts the principle forces me to drop or tweak; and what the principle says about a case I keep going back and forth on — a company that succeeds and is then described in the press as having succeeded because of its healthy engineering culture. Then tell me honestly which of my gut reactions can't be kept without making the principle ad hoc.

One more thing: when you answer, please seperate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who seriously study explanation and history, and don't present a contested side as if it were the consensus.
