I'm co-teaching a two-week unit on empire in a combined Shakespeare-and-political-thought seminar, and I've been struck by how rarely Adam Smith's long treatment of colonies in The Wealth of Nations is brought into contact with the literary texts alongside him. Our students know The Tempest well enough to describe its plot, but they tend to read Prospero either as a wise magician renouncing power or as flatly villainous; I want a reading that forces them to keep the economic and the moral registers distinct, because Smith himself does. As I understand him, Smith argues that Britain's American colonies prospered not because of the mercantile system but in large part despite it: they had abundant land and unusually free institutions, and the colonial monopoly mostly served a narrow merchant interest while distorting trade and burdening everyone else. At the same time, Smith does not mince words about the injustice done to indigenous peoples, distinguishes settling land that is thinly peopled or uncultivated from conquering an already cultivated society, and nevertheless treats the discovery of the Americas as one of the great events in history, even contemplating a transatlantic political union in which the colonies would be represented rather than treating independence as the only alternative to the status quo. I want the students to use that complicated Smith — not the cartoon free-trader — to read the play.

Specifically, I'd like a reading of The Tempest through this Smithian lens: which scenes and relationships does it illuminate most sharply, and where does the play refuse to fit Smith's categories? I'm thinking of Prospero's claim to the island in the face of Caliban's insistence on prior possession and inheritance through Sycorax; the forced labor of Caliban and the bound service of Ariel; the idea of the island as something to be organized, improved, and made fruitful; and the play's insistent language of gratitude, ingratitude, and desert. I suspect something particularly interesting happens around Smith's distinction between thinly peopled and already cultivated lands: Prospero seems always to treat the island as if it were the former, while Caliban insists it was already the latter — a society with its own claim. Does the play expose that assumption rather than share it? I'd also like you to say, as the honest critic, where the reading fails or would have to twist the play to make it work. Please work by scene and situation rather than by quotation, since I'll use this as a class plan, not a textual commentary.
