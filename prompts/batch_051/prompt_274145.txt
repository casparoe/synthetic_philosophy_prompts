I revise the welfare standard for a mid-sized aquaculture certification scheme, and I need a decision procedure I can put in front of our auditors. Our inspectors are not philosophers and not welfare scientists: they are experienced aquaculture people with a two-day refresher, they have at most a day and a half on a given farm, and their written findings determine whether the farm keeps certification. A senior certifier and a standards committee must be able to defend the decision if it is appealed. We can state the principle behind the standard, but we do not yet know how to make it usable in the field.

The principle is this. The moral weight of an animal's suffering should not depend on its species, its distance from humans on the tree of life, or its cognitive sophistication, but on its capacities; and when we are genuinely uncertain whether a kind of animal has the relevant capacities, that uncertainty should be resolved by considering how much welfare is at stake, how strong the evidence is, and how reversible or avoidable the practice is — not by defaulting to the assumption that the animal cannot suffer.

Please turn that into a reusable procedure for assessing any production practice — slaughter, transport, stocking density, handling, invasive husbandry procedures, and so on — involving finfish or decapod crustaceans. I want an ordered checklist: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure stops with a "no" or flags the case for a human decision, and the points at which the decision is genuinely one of values rather than fact. Keep the theory out of the auditors' line of sight: the deliverable should read like an audit tool, with the philosophical scaffolding made explicit in a short appendix rather than in the field instructions.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study animal minds and welfare, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. In particular, be explicit about which steps depend on claims such as "nociception is sufficient evidence of conscious pain in fishes" or "decapod crustaceans are sentient," and show, where it matters, how the procedure would change if the dispute were resolved the other way.

Then run the procedure on three practices:

1. Electrical stunning of Atlantic salmon immediately before slaughter, where the stun is reversible and the fish are bled out before they can recover.
2. Eyestalk ablation of female prawns to induce spawning.
3. Holding rainbow trout in a recirculating system at a density that measurably raises fin damage and stress indicators, where lowering the density is feasible but more expensive per kilogram.

Make sure at least one of these is a case where the procedure gives an answer our industry would rather not hear, and say which one that is and why the procedure cannot be adjusted to avoid it without changing the underlying principle. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do: the places where judgment cannot be eliminated, the cases where it will misfire or generate false precision, and the questions it cannot answer because they are not empirical.
