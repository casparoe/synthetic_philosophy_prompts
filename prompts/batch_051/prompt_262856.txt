You are marking an undergraduate assignment in a course on ancient philosophy and the concept of obligation. The question and the student's answer are reproduced below.

Question. In the Theogony, trans. H. G. Evelyn-White (1914), lines 782–806, Hesiod describes the oath by which the gods swear:

But when strife and quarrel arise among the deathless gods, and when any one of them who live in the house of Olympus lies, then Zeus sends Iris to bring in a golden jug the great oath of the gods from far away, the famous cold water which trickles down from a high and beetling rock. Far under the wide-pathed earth a branch of Oceanus flows through the dark night out of the holy stream, and a tenth part of his water is allotted to her. With nine silver-swirling streams he winds about the earth and the sea's wide back, and then falls into the main; but the tenth flows out from a rock, a sore trouble to the gods. For whoever of the deathless gods that hold the peaks of snowy Olympus pours a libation of her water and is forsworn, must lie breathless until a full year is completed, and never come near to taste ambrosia and nectar, but lie spiritless and voiceless on a strewn bed: and a heavy trance overshadows him. But when he has spent a long year in his sickness, another penance more hard follows after the first. For nine years he is cut off from the eternal gods and never joins their councils or their feasts, nine full years. But in the tenth year he comes again to join the assemblies of the deathless gods who live in the house of Olympus. Such an oath, then, did the gods appoint the eternal and primeval water of Styx to be: and it spouts through a rugged place.

Explain what this passage implies about what binds an oath-taker in Hesiod's theology. In particular: does the binding force lie in the expected penalty, in the authority of the god who procures the oath, or in something about the oath itself? Attend to the details of the passage.

Student answer. "Hesiod's picture is that an oath binds a god because the god has something to lose. Anyone who pours the libation and is forsworn 'must lie breathless until a full year is completed' and is then excluded from the gods' assemblies for nine further years. Binding force, on this reading, is nothing over and above the expected cost of breach: to be bound is just to risk a penalty greater than the gains of perjury. The passage supports this because the penalty attaches to the act of swearing automatically, and even the deathless gods cannot escape it. It also shows that the oath is ultimately enforced by Zeus: he sends Iris for the water, and Styx received her office only after siding with Zeus against the Titans, so what really backs the oath is royal authority rather than anything intrinsic to the water itself. The fact that the water is called 'a sore trouble to the gods' merely shows that it is the instrument Zeus uses. A consequence of this reading is that an oath taken by someone who believes no penalty will follow is not binding, whereas a promise backed by a reliable penalty is binding even if it was never sworn."
