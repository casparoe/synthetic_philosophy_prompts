My philosophy of language course uses a study guide written by a teaching assistant, and its section on Kripke offers the following gloss (set off below):

Kripke's central lesson is that necessity and apriority come apart: 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' and 'water is H2O' are necessary truths, yet we can know them only a posteriori, through astronomy and chemistry respectively. The key notion is a rigid designator. A term is a rigid designator if it refers to the same object in every possible world in which it refers at all; proper names and natural-kind terms are rigid, while ordinary definite descriptions are not. A description such as 'the teacher of Alexander,' however, becomes rigid once we use it to fix on Aristotle, since in the worlds that matter Aristotle is the one who taught Alexander. Because 'water' rigidly designates H2O, the sentence 'water is H2O' is true in every possible world even though its truth was an empirical discovery; the necessity comes from what our language has settled, not from what anyone knows. The same reasoning generalizes: if a thing is picked out by a rigid name, then any property we discover that thing to have is a property it has in every possible world, so the apparent contingency of ordinary discoveries is largely an artifact of incomplete information.
