I am a senior legislative drafter in a small common-law jurisdiction. Every bill and delegated regulation that leaves my office must first clear a rule-of-law review, and my team currently runs that review off a checklist that is, in substance, Fuller's eight canons of legality: generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, consistency, possibility of compliance, stability through time, and congruence between announced rule and official action. Last night I spent several hours arguing with a friend who teaches administrative law, and he convinced me the checklist is doing very little of what we claim it does. His challenge was roughly this: the canons are either too weak, because a wicked regime can satisfy them while using law as an instrument of injustice, or too demanding, because the modern administrative state cannot function without open-ended discretion and occasional retroactive correction. He pressed the Raz-style point that these canons are virtues of a tool, like sharpness in a knife, and can be put to bad ends; and he pressed the Waldron-style point that the live question is whether the people affected by an official decision can contest it before a body that is required to answer with reasons. I ended the evening agreeing that the checklist should be replaced, but I could not say what should replace it when I walk in on Monday with forty drafts waiting.

Please write me a decision procedure my drafting team could actually run on every proposed instrument. Fix the role as follows: the reviewer is an experienced drafter, not a legal philosopher; the review must be completed in under half a day per instrument; and the output must be one of three recommendations: proceed, return for redrafting, or flag for the minister as a decision that cannot be settled by drafting. The stakes are high: our instruments bind thousands of people, and courts here most often strike down delegated legislation on rule-of-law grounds.

The procedure should take the Fuller–Raz–Waldron disagreement seriously rather than pretending it away. It should distinguish at least three kinds of defect: formal defects that redrafting can cure; structural defects, such as a grant of discretion that is effectively immune from challenge or a rule that closes off any forum in which reasons must be given, which can survive a formally clean text; and objections that are really policy disagreements and should not be smuggled into a rule-of-law review. It should say what to do when the canons conflict—for example, when a short retroactive curative amendment restores stability at the price of non-retroactivity, or when a rule is clear and stable but demands what many people cannot do. It should also mark, honestly, the steps at which judgment cannot be eliminated and a drafter is making a value call under the cover of procedure.

Then run the procedure on these three instruments: a tax-avoidance reporting rule that operates retroactively for six months to overturn a recent court decision and restore the understanding on which everyone had been acting; a public-health regulation empowering a single official to declare an emergency, suspend specified rules, and act without giving reasons for up to ninety days; and a licensing scheme for a new industry whose standards are framed in broad terms and left to a code of practice issued by the industry association itself. I expect the procedure to return flag-for-the-minister or return-for-redrafting on at least one instrument that is politically popular. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, and whether it can answer my friend's deeper worry that the whole exercise is still a virtue of an instrument rather than a moral ideal.
