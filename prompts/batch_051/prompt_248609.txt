I've been arguing with a colleague about a style of reasoning we keep encountering in informal discussions, where Aumann's agreement theorem gets invoked to suggest that any residual disagreement between rational people must be due to a mistake. My colleague insists that the result works even when the "common prior" requirement is weakened, and he has offered the following reconstruction. (1) Two agents, A and B, both assign probability one-half to some proposition E before either examines any evidence. (2) Each then observes different private evidence and conditions on it correctly, arriving at posterior probabilities p_A and p_B for E. (3) They publicly announce these posterior probabilities to one another, in circumstances where the announced values, and the fact that both heard them, are common knowledge. (4) When A learns that B's posterior is p_B, A thereby acquires the information that B's private evidence led B to assign p_B to E; likewise for B. As a rational Bayesian, each must now condition on this new information. (5) Since both agents are perfect Bayesians by hypothesis, each performs this additional conditioning correctly. (6) Therefore, after the exchange, they cannot rationally remain in disagreement about E, and any divergence persisting after the exchange would show that at least one of them had updated improperly.

He wants to draw the further moral that the following three things—an identical initial probability for the disputed proposition, rational updating on private evidence, and mutual disclosure of the resulting probabilities—suffice to force consensus. I suspect this is exactly where Aumann's result gets overextended in casual discussion, but I cannot localize the error cleanly: each step looks individually plausible. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and on which reading of its key terms? In particular, state what Aumann's theorem actually requires that this argument does not supply, and explain whether the failure lies in a specific step or in a suppressed premise needed to move between them.
