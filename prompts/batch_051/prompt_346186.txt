I am drafting an essay on Mill's late views on socialism and would like your comments on the following draft. The essay's central claim is that the unfinished Chapters on Socialism (1879) is not a retreat from his Principles of Political Economy but a sharpening of one of its underlying commitments. The pivot is a passage early in Chapters on Socialism. In the passage I have in mind, Mill grants that the socialist critics of the existing property order have a powerful indictment: under current arrangements, the distribution of the produce of labor is governed less by labor or merit than by accident of birth. He then says that if the comparison were between communism, with all its chances and uncertainties, and the present institutions, with all their known miseries and injustices, the balance of the argument would deserve to go to the socialists. But he immediately shifts the terms: the relevant comparison, he argues, is not between communism and private property as it now exists, but between communism and a private-property system reformed so as to distribute the fruits of labor more in accordance with individual effort and contribution. I want to claim that this move, and not the concession itself, is the most important part of his late socialism, because it shows that his qualified defense of private property rests on a normative ideal of distributive justice that he thinks private ownership could approximate but currently does not.
