I am not a scholar, just someone who reads seriously in the evenings, and a passage from W. E. B. Du Bois's *The Souls of Black Folk* has been turning over in my mind. He is describing the way white acquaintances talked to him, and he writes:

"They approach me in a half-hesitant sort of way, eye me curiously or compassionately, and then, instead of saying directly, How does it feel to be a problem? they say, I know an excellent colored man in my town; or, I fought at Mechanicsville; or, Do not these Southern outrages make your blood boil? At these I smile, or am interested, or reduce the boiling to a simmer, as the occasion may require. To the real question, How does it feel to be a problem? I answer seldom a word."

What surprises me is that the sentences he actually reports are not slurs, and some of them might even be intended as friendly. Yet Du Bois experiences the whole exchange as being handled rather than met as a person. My hunch, after sitting with this, is that a racist statement cannot be defined just by the speaker's intentions or just by a list of forbidden words; it seems to have something to do with what the words do to someone in a particular social position. But I keep wavering.

I would like you to give me a working definition of a racist statement, and then run that definition through the following cases:

- a white man who shouts a racial slur at a Black teenager on a train;
- a history teacher who reads aloud the text of a Jim Crow-era segregation sign, word for word, so that students understand what the period was like;
- the Du Bois acquaintance's remark, "I know an excellent colored man in my town;"
- the question Du Bois says is never asked directly — whether it can count as a racist statement at all, and whether the polite substitutes for it count in the same way or differently;
- a politician who, arguing against a new bus route into a white suburb, says, "This has nothing to do with race; it is about property values and keeping class sizes down;"
- an analyst who tells a school board, without apparent malice, "Hispanic students at this school are suspended at a higher rate than white students;"
- a Black comedian who, in front of a mostly Black audience, uses a slur that would be racist in nearly any other mouth as part of a joke about the police.

After you have worked through all seven, tell me which verdict you are least confident in, whether the definition still fits Du Bois's examples, and whether it is ultimately a definition about intent, effect, something in the words, or something in the background history the words draw on.
