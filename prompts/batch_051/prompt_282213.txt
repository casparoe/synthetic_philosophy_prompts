I find myself persuaded by a simple thesis about the moral relevance of time: the passage of time has no intrinsic moral significance. If an act was wrong and the agent remains the same person, then the mere fact that years have elapsed changes neither the wrongness of the act nor, it seems, what the agent owes in response. The view is attractive because it is uniform and simple, refusing to let moral judgment track the calendar and insisting that otherwise identical wrongs be treated alike. At the same time, I firmly believe that statutes of limitations are not merely practical compromises; in many cases it would be positively unjust to prosecute someone for a decades-old offense, even where the evidence is strong and the offender is identifiable. I am aware of the standard reconciliation: the theory governs the wrongdoer's obligations, while limitation periods govern what the state may do, for reasons of evidence, deterrence, and social repose. But this strikes me as possibly a merely verbal solution that leaves the pressure untouched. Does the theory actually contradict the intuition, or only a philosophical gloss on it? And if they do conflict, should the intuition be debunked, should the theory be amended or abandoned, or is there a reading on which both can be preserved? Please address what would justify each of those moves rather than simply choosing a side.
