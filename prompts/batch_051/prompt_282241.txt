I first got puzzled by a move in Descartes's Sixth Meditation, and I want help locating where it goes wrong. Descartes seems to reason — I am paraphrasing, not quoting — that since I can clearly and distinctly conceive the mind existing apart from the body, it follows that the mind is genuinely distinct from the body and can exist independently. The principle behind this looks like:

(1) If I can clearly and distinctly conceive of something F existing without something G, then F is not identical with G and F can exist without G.

Now consider the philosophical zombie: a being exactly like me physically — same brain, same behavior, same functional organization — but with no consciousness. I find myself able to conceive of this being. So by (1), a zombie is possible. And if a zombie is possible, consciousness cannot be physical, since the physical facts are held fixed while the phenomenal facts vary. That is the familiar anti-physicalist conclusion.

Here is the part that bothers me. The very same principle seems to prove too much. I can also clearly and distinctly conceive of a world with exactly the same physical particles, forces, and positions as this one, but with no tables in it — only particles arranged tablewise. By (1), the absence of tables while all those physical facts remain is possible, so tables cannot be physical objects either. But if the principle entails that tables are non-physical, something has gone wrong, or else we have accepted a bizarre kind of dualism about ordinary artifacts.

So here is my question: where exactly did the argument go wrong? Is the flaw in (1), in the move from conceivability to possibility, in the idea that we can truly conceive the zombie, or in the inference from zombie possibility to the failure of physicalism? And if I reject (1) to save tables, what does that cost me in the zombie argument? If instead I keep (1) and accept that tables are non-physical, what else do I have to swallow? Please answer in American English.
