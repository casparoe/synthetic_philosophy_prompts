I lead the policy team at a social platform where the rules forbid racist speech, and my job is to write the operational rule that front-line moderators actually apply. The moderators are contract workers with a few weeks of training and high turnover; each handles several hundred items a day and has only minutes per decision. Every removal must be defensible in an appeal, which means the rule has to record which question was answered which way and why, not merely return a verdict. The stakes are asymmetric: too loose a rule lets coordinated bigotry travel under plausible deniability, while too strict a rule chills legitimate discussion and converts clumsy phrasing into a ban.

I am a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, so I have absorbed the standing debate about whether a statement is racist in virtue of the speaker's intent, in virtue of its effects on hearers, or in virtue of functioning as a dog whistle—an utterance whose surface reading stays innocent while one audience reliably receives it as an attack or a call to action. What I lack is not another gloss on those positions but a translation of one of them into a procedure a moderator can run without a philosophy degree.

Write me that procedure: a reusable decision tree or checklist for deciding whether a piece of text should be treated as racist speech under the platform's rule. The questions should come in a fixed order; at each question, say what a sufficient answer looks like, what evidence the moderator can consult in the time available, and when the procedure stops with a no, stops with a removal, or escalates to a human reviewer. It should be teachable in a day to someone who will never read the underlying theory, and it should make its theoretical commitments visible rather than hiding them inside an unstated judgment. The rule also has to be signed off by our legal and public-policy leadership, who will reject anything that cannot be explained without jargon. Before the procedure, state in plain language the view of what makes a statement racist that the procedure encodes, and flag the points where that view is being simplified and what the simplification sacrifices.

Then run the procedure on four cases. (1) A post that uses an unambiguous racial slur. (2) A post that asserts, without slurs, a familiar racist thesis about a racial group and crime. (3) A post that cites an officially race-neutral statistic about crime, framed in a way that one online community reliably reads as an attack on a racial group while the text retains an innocent reading for outsiders. (4) A post by a newcomer discussing the same statistic in apparent good faith, with no sign of awareness that the framing carries a second meaning. For each case, show the step-by-step path through the rule and the outcome. Make sure at least one case yields an outcome that a well-intentioned writer of hate-speech policy will instinctively reject; resist the urge to adjust the rule afterward so that it flatters the intuition. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot settle in principle—which cases no rule can decide—and what a human reviewer is left doing at those points.
