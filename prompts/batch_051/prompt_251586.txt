i keep coming back to a tension and i want you to hold me to the strong version of both sides instead of resolving it fast. On the one hand I'm nearly persuaded by the welfarist picture: the only thing with final value is the goodness of lives — pleasure, preference-satisfaction, capabilities, however you cash it — and everything else is a means. Wealth, infrastructure, technology, GDP: they matter only through what they do for someone. On that view, if you offered me two societies with identical well-being outcomes, and one was materially abundant while the other was materially threadbare, the abundant one is not better in any deep sense. Better insured, maybe; but once the outcomes are stipulated equal, the insurance has already been counted.

But I have a firm intuition I can't shake that the abundant society is better anyway. Not as an investment, not through anyone's later experience, just that prosperity has worth beyond what it buys. I imagine one society as productive, inventive, full of accumulated capacity, not precarious, standing in a relation of abundance rather than scarcity toward the world — and the other somehow matching it on measured health, happiness, life satisfaction, anxiety, everything, while remaining materially poor and low-capacity. I still think the first is better. When I try this out on people I get told the intuition is either (a) an artifact of my not truly being able to imagine the stipulation, (b) a status or relative-position thing I'm sneaking back in, or (c) the hedonic treadmill confusing me about adaptation. None of that feels like enough.

What exactly would it take for the welfare-only account to be amended or abandoned?
