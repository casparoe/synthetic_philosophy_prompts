I keep returning to a parallel between the mental imagery debate in cognitive science and a design choice in computer graphics, and I want your help deciding whether it is deep or superficial. You are a philosopher of cognitive science who also has a working knowledge of how graphics systems are built. On the cognitive science side: depictive accounts of imagery, associated with Kosslyn, hold that imagery runs on a spatial medium — a visual buffer — in which a scene is laid out, inspected, and transformed. Propositional accounts, associated with Pylyshyn, hold that what underlies imagery is an abstract, language-like description of the scene, and that the picture-like phenomenology is a byproduct rather than evidence of a filled-in spatial display.

On the engineering side: a framebuffer stores a complete image pixel by pixel, which is costly in memory but cheap to inspect or transform later because the answer is already there. A scene graph stores a compact symbolic description of objects and their relations, and computes pixels only when some interface needs them, which is cheap to store but costly to render each time.

The proposed analogy is this: the visual buffer is a materialized, already-computed image, while the propositional representation is a symbolic program that renders visual properties on demand. And the tradeoff seems to transfer: materialize when the same visual information will be inspected or transformed repeatedly; keep it symbolic when storage is expensive and queries are rare. On this reading, the imagery dispute is not about what images are "really" like, but about which costs dominate in the mind.
