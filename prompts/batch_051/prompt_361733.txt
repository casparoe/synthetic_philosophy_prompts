I sit on my city's charter-review commitee and we're stuck on a clause we inherited from the 1880s. The charter says: "No money of the city shall be applied, directly or indirectly, to the building, improvement, or repair of any house of worship, nor to the support of public worship therein." Now a congregation that has met entirely online since 2020 and doesn't intend to get a building has applied for a "neighborhood digital access" grant. The money would cover the streaming server, cameras, and internet subsidies for their elderly members that they use to run Sunday services; the same equipment also hosts a food-bank calender and a tenants' rights workshop, but worship is what they most need it for.

How does the clause apply read as its drafters would have read it? How does it apply read as a general principle that public money shouldn't underwrite public worship? And what would a reasonable reader of the charter today take "support of public worship" to cover? If these come apart, which should the committee follow, and does the mixed-use detail change the result?

Answer in American English. And tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what would change your mind — say, if the equipment were mostly used for the food bank, or if "directly or indirectly" turns out to be doing the real work.
