I'm putting together a session for my department's "work in progress" lunch, and I want to use Keynes's *A Treatise on Probability* — specifically this passage from Chapter VI:

"The probability, that a proposition is true, is one thing; the weight of the argument in its favour is another. It is a matter of common experience that the degree of our rational belief in a proposition is not determined solely by the probability of the proposition in relation to the evidence, but also by the amount of the evidence. The probability of a proposition may be small, but, if the evidence is extensive, our rational belief in it may be great; and the probability may be large, but, if the evidence is slight, our rational belief may be small. The question of the weight of the argument is, therefore, distinct from the question of the probability."

My plan is to argue that this distinction between probability and weight does real work that most economists who only know Keynes from *The General Theory* never see: it gives us a way to think about confidence in a decision without collapsing it into the odds. But I'm worried I'm about to get myself into trouble with the decision-theory people in the room.

If the distinction is less tidy than it looks, tell me where it breaks and what the person defending it should concede right away.
