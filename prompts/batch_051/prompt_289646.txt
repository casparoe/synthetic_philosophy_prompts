For the past few years I have been trying to sort out what I think about obedience and deference, and I have ended up with a list of commitments. I want to know whether they actually hang together. Please give me a committed diagnosis rather than a survey of options: tell me which pairs are in real tension, how severe each tension is, which apparent conflicts dissolve once they are stated carefully, and which single revision would leave the most defensible overall set.

Here is where I have landed:

1. In hierarchical settings such as the military, hospitals, and aviation, people should generally obey instructions even when they personally disagree, because a system in which everyone second-guesses every order cannot coordinate anything.
2. The excuse that one was just following orders is never by itself an excuse: when an order is wrong, the person who carries it out is still to blame.
3. The participants in the Milgram obedience experiments acted wrongly when they kept administering shocks; I want to call it a moral failure rather than mere bad luck. Yet I also suspect I would probably have done the same, and I do not think that suspicion excuses them.
4. On technical questions, such as vaccines or climate, people should defer to expert consensus even when their own reading of the evidence tilts the other way, because their own reading is likely worse.
5. Whistleblowers are right to break ranks when they see serious wrongdoing, even though doing so means betraying a chain of command and their professional obligations.
6. Ordinary adults should not treat rules as binding merely because they are rules; a pointless or harmful rule should be questioned and often ignored, and the insistence that that is just the policy is not a virtue.

Something feels off among these, but I cannot locate the problem, and I also suspect that some combinations that look contradictory are actually compatible.
