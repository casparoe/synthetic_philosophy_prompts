I help run a small secular mutual-aid collective that has been debating whether to host a monthly social evening with wine. One of the older members has been pressing for it on grounds I found surprising: he says that when people drink together in a controlled setting, the alcohol exposes their true dispositions—who becomes aggressive, who becomes maudlin, who stays steady—and that over time, with the right expectations, the experience can train self-restraint. Others think this is just rationalizing a bad idea, and that alcohol is more likely to produce exactly the disorder we want to avoid. I had been reading Plato’s Laws, Book I, around the same time, and I was struck by how close the Athenian’s defence of wine is to my friend’s argument. As I read it, the Athenian claims that a well-run drinking party can serve as a relatively safe way to reveal what a soul would otherwise hide, and that an older, sober supervisor can turn it into an exercise in mastering shame, fear, and desire rather than a licence for excess.

I am now unsure whether my friend has given me a genuinely useful Platonic framework for our decision, or whether he has cherry-picked an ancient justification for something that would be bad for us in practice. Our group meets in a community centre basement, and our first two dry events were polite but a little stiff; his view is that wine would loosen people up and make honest conversation possible. I would like advice: if we were to take the Platonic argument seriously, what would a modern equivalent of his supervised symposium actually have to include to count as a test or training of character rather than just a party? And is my friend’s position about using alcohol to evaluate people—and perhaps to improve them—defensible on its own terms, or is he asking the wine to do work that only the surrounding structure and supervision could do?
