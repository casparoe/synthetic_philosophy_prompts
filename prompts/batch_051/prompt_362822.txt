Me and my friend got into a long argument about whether the wierd posting rules in an old game lobby we both used to play are a "convention" in Lewis's sense, or just a habit that spread. I've only read bits of Lewis, so my grasp of the definiton is secondhand, but the way it was put to me was roughly this. A regularity R in a population P in a recurring situation S counts as a convention iff it is common knowledge in P that: nearly everyone follows R; nearly everyone expects nearly everyone else to follow R; nearly everyone has approximately the same preferences about the possible combinations of actions; nearly everyone prefers that any one more person follow R, on condition that nearly everyone follows R; and there is some alternative regularity R* (incompatible with R) such that nearly everyone would prefer that any one more person follow R*, on condition that nearly everyone follows R*. The last condition is meant to capture the arbitrariness: people aren't attached to R itself, they just don't want to be the odd one out. I might be mangling some of this, but that's the version we were arguing with.

The case is this. The lobby is for a cooperative puzzle game with no real matchmaking, so hosts post room codes in a shared text channel. Over about ten years the community converged on a pattern: the code in lowercase letters, one room per line, and a trailing period, like abcd.efgh. Nobody can quite say why. Early on there were several competing formats, and the current one mostly won through copying. Now every regular follows it and expects every other regular to follow it; if you break the pattern, people assume you're new, but not that the pattern itself is bad. The thing is, when you ask people in private, a lot of them say they don't prefer the current format and would switch to a cleaner one in a week if they believed everyone else would switch. But each of them also believes they're in the minority here, that most others like the current format and would ignore or mock any change. So everyone keeps posting the old way, and the norm stays locked in place.

Is this a Lewis convention? I keep going back and forth. My main sticking point is whether the common-knowledge clause covers just the behavioral regularity and the mutual expectations, or whether it also covers the shared preferences and the availability of the alternative. If it covers the latter, this case seems to fail, even though it functions like a convention from the outside. Does that mean Lewis's criteria are too strict for half-forgotten, evolved norms, or is this something else entirely (a social norm, a pluralistic-ignorance equilibrium, a mere regularity)? Argue it out, going condition by condition.
