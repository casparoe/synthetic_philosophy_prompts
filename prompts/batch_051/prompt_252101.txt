Just got back from a three hour argument with a friend about whether philosophy has ever actually helped science. He kept bringing up Weinberg, Hawking, and Feynman, you know, the usual line that philosophers have contributed nothing to working physicists and that scientists don't need them; I kept pushing back with the usual examples of conceptual clarification in the foundations of quantum mechanics and relativity. It ended with him proposing this scheme, which he thinks would settle the question empirically, and I cant stop thinking about how it would actually play out.

Here's the proposal. Every physics department that takes public funding has to hire one philosopher of physics. The philosopher's salary comes from a central conceptual review fund, not the department's budget, so the department doesn't bear the cost directly. The philosopher reviews the department's preprints before they go out to journals. If the philosopher's objection leads to a paper being substantively revised before submission, or to a submitted or published paper being corrected or retracted, the philosopher gets a bonus and a short anonymized case note goes into a public log. The physicists face no penalty, and the philosopher has no power to block publication on their own. The bonus is decided by a joint committee of three physicists and two philosophers who compare the before and after versions. After three years, someone evaluates the log to see what philosophy actually contributed.

He thinks this is a clean way to test whether philosophy is useful to science. I have a strong hunch that it's an incentive machine that would collapse into gaming pretty fast, but I couldn't articulate exactly where it would break first. Walk me through how it would behave: what would the philosophers start doing, what would the physicists start doing, how would the idea of an error the philosopher caught get corrupted, and would the three year log end up measuring anything real. Don't take a side on whether philosophy is useful in the abstract; treat the proposal as a mechanism and show me its failure modes, second-order effects, and whether any of it is fixable. And please, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just continuous prose like you're writing me an email.
