I am a novelist drafting a novel in which one character, a contemporary of Spinoza, claims to have achieved what Spinoza calls the third kind of knowledge. I want to think through a variation of a thought experiment Spinoza himself uses in a letter to Schuler. In the original, Spinoza asks us to imagine a stone set in motion: if the stone were conscious of its motion but not of the causes determining it, it would believe itself free. Now vary the case. Give the stone not ordinary consciousness but the third kind of knowledge as characterized near the end of the Ethics, Part V, Proposition 32 and its corollary. I am paraphrasing the Elwes translation rather than quoting from it directly: Spinoza says there that from the third kind of knowledge there arises an intellectual love of God, and that this love is eternal; and in Proposition 36 he says that the mind's intellectual love of God is part of the infinite love with which God loves himself. My question is whether the stone in this modified thought experiment still suffers the illusion of freedom, or whether it now escapes that illusion and participates in the intellectual love of God. I want the reasoning to track Spinoza's own distinctions: the three kinds of knowledge, adequate and inadequate ideas, and the difference between passive love arising from the affections and the active intellectual love that arises from clear understanding. Also consider whether the stone, as a very simple finite mode, can possess the self-understanding and the kind of eternity of mind required for that love, or whether Spinoza's account implies some minimum complexity or bodily history for a mode to be capable of the third kind of knowledge. If the stone's understanding arrives all at once rather than through the gradual passage from imagination to reason to intuition, does that change whether the resulting state counts as adequate knowledge or merely as a different inadequate idea? I am less interested in a yes or no than in the argument: which parts of Spinoza's analysis survive the variation, which parts break, and what the breaking point shows about what the intellectual love of God actually requires.
