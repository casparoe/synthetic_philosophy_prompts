Over the last few years I've accumulated a set of commitments about loss aversion and the endowment effect, and I've never had to defend them all as a single package. I'd like an audit of whether they actually hang together before I commit to the full set. Here they are:

1. I accept expected utility theory over final outcomes as the normative theory of rational preference. Rational preferences are complete and transitive, satisfy independence, and are invariant under redescriptions of the same final states. Whether a choice is rational should supervene on the probability distribution over final outcomes, not on the route, the frame, or the reference point used to describe the options.

2. Under that standard, loss aversion is a defect. A loss-averse choice lets gains and losses measured from a reference point do decisive work even though the reference point need not correspond to any difference in final outcomes. That is exactly the sort of influence the invariance requirement rules out, so I count loss aversion as a bias rather than a legitimate preference.

3. The endowment effect looks to me like loss aversion applied to an arbitrary initial assignment of property, so it inherits that verdict: it is a bias, not a separate legitimate phenomenon. I also suspect that the classic mug-experiment gap between willingness to accept and willingness to pay is substantially inflated by transaction costs, experimenter demand, unfamiliar tasks, and similar confounds, though I accept that the directed gap is robust and probably not entirely noise.

4. At the same time, I accept the standard hedonic psychology: losing a good typically feels worse than gaining an otherwise equivalent good feels good; adaptation to losses is slower and less complete than adaptation to gains; and people's forecasts of these asymmetries are not, on the whole, badly wrong.

5. I also accept experienced-utility welfarism about welfare: a person's well-being consists in the quality of their subjective experience. If a pattern of choice reliably leaves someone feeling worse on balance, then it really is worse for them, whatever its formal properties.

6. Finally, I accept that preferences grounded in long personal history, such as an heirloom, a home lived in for decades, or an instrument known for years, can legitimately resist trade even at above-market prices, and I do not want those lumped in with mere endowment effects.

Please audit this set. Tell me which commitments are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and then say what the cheapest revision is and what it would cost me elsewhere. Please answer in American English.
