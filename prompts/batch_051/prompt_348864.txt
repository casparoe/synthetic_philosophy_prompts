I help moderate a large online neighborhood group, and one of my jobs is deciding what happens to videos that members post as proof of something serious. This comes up every week or two: someone posts a clip that seems to show a local official, landlord, or business owner doing something damaging — taking an envelope of cash, saying something bigoted, threatening someone — and the comments immediately split between "the video proves it" and "it's a deepfake, take it down." I'm a volunteer with no technical training and about fifteen minutes per decision. I can consult one other moderator when a case is close, and anything involving a threat of violence has to be escalated to the platform and the police, but there is no expert I can call and no legal team behind me. Our policy says that leaving up a video that accuses a named private person requires a second moderator to agree, so the procedure should make clear what that second sign-off has to amount to beyond a shared hunch.

The way I've been thinking about this on my own is that a recording used to be what ended an argument. When two people told different stories and no one could settle it, you went to the tape or the photograph, and the recording was supposed to be the last word — the backstop that kept a dispute from going on forever. Deepfakes seem to remove that backstop, because the recording itself can now be one more manufactured thing rather than a trace of the event. I take it that some videos can probably still serve as a backstop under the right conditions, and that when they can't, something else has to stand in for it, but I can't articulate what those conditions are or what the substitute should be.

Please write me a decision procedure I can actually run in the time I have: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure should stop with "leave up as evidence," "take down as likely fake," or "leave up but label disputed," and where it should hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Mark the judgment calls that can't be eliminated, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do — for example, what it can't determine about a technically perfect deepfake posted as an ordinary clip.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a grainy, anonymous video of a council member apparently accepting an envelope of cash, with no other source; (2) a clear, well-lit video of a restaurant owner making a bigoted remark, posted by a long-standing member who has publicly feuded with the owner; and (3) a video of a landlord threatening a tenant, posted by that tenant, plus a second, lower-quality recording of the same incident made by a neighbor from across the street. Make sure at least one of these comes out in a way I won't like, and say why the procedure should still be followed. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which step in the procedure is least secure, what argument or evidence would most plausibly make you rewrite it, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
