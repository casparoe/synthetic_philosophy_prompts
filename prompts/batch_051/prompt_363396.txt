I have been collecting a colleague's snap verdicts on a series of moral cases. She teaches ethics and is plainly not a simple utilitarian, but I cannot pin down the principle behind her judgments. Here is the record so far.

1. A man's wife and a stranger are trapped in a burning building; he can carry out only one person. He carries out his wife and does not deliberate about it. My colleague's verdict: the act was right, and the absence of deliberation was no flaw.

2. A woman spends a modest inheritance on her daughter's music education rather than donating it to a charity that would do a roughly comparable amount of good for strangers. She does not run any comparison. Verdict: she acted rightly, and a person who insisted that she first establish that this was the optimal use of the money would be misunderstanding what it is to be a parent.

3. A man is asked why he saved his wife rather than a stranger; he answers that she is his wife and offers nothing further. Verdict: that answer was complete as it stood, and someone who dismissed it as unprincipled would be the one making the mistake.

4. A foundation officer must choose between two poverty-relief grant proposals of similar quality; one is run by her closest friend and is slightly less cost-effective than the other. She awards the grant to the more effective organization. Verdict: she acted rightly; in that professional role, the friendship should carry no weight.

5. A judge assigned to a matter involving her son's school recuses herself rather than decide it. Verdict: she acted rightly; the impartiality a judicial role requires is different in kind from a demand that someone justify a personal commitment, and there is nothing alienating about it.

6. A composer uses his own savings to complete an opera he has worked on for many years, although the same money could instead have made a modest but real improvement to a school. Verdict: he acted rightly; the opera is the work that gives his life its shape, and requiring him to justify completing it as the best possible contribution to the general good would misdescribe what makes a life his own.

Next week I will put this case to her. A physician has spent fifteen years building a small clinic for a neglected disease; the clinic is the project that gives her life its direction. A global health agency offers her the directorship of a program that would prevent several hundred otherwise certain deaths each year, but only if she closes the clinic and moves abroad. Her patients would continue to receive standard care elsewhere, though less conveniently. She declines even to look at a cost-effectiveness comparison, saying that the clinic is who she is and that running the comparison would already amount to abandoning it. She holds no professional role that requires her to be impartial between her own project and the agency's.

What should I expect her to say, and why? I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the structure behind it: (a) identify the principle she seems to be running; (b) identify the rival principles that fit her earlier verdicts equally well and say what each predicts about the physician case; (c) give your best prediction with the reasons for preferring one rule over the others, and say how much confidence the record actually warrants; (d) tell me which further case would most decisively separate the leading rules. If the exercise rests on a confused premise — for example, if a finite list of verdicts cannot determine the rule someone is running, or if any of my case descriptions smuggles in an answer — say so plainly rather than giving me a forced prediction.
