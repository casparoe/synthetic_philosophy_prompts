I help run a monthly discussion circle for working software engineers interested in conceptual questions in the life sciences. We have no formal philosophy background, which means we are comfortable with random variables, expected values, and sampling, but we have not read the philosophy-of-biology literature. Next month I want to put the group inside a familiar-looking worry: if fitness is defined as whatever survives and reproduces, then the slogan that evolution favors the survival of the fittest seems to say only that survivors survive. I know there is a standard reply — the propensity interpretation of fitness — but I understand it only at the level of a slogan myself: fitness is not actual reproductive output, but a probabilistic disposition, like a probability distribution over possible numbers of offspring, from which the actual outcome is a single draw. I would like the group to feel the difference between a distribution and a drawn outcome before anyone says the word propensity.

Design a self-contained 75-minute activity for about 18 participants that can be run in one room with printed sheets, index cards, small tokens, and dice or a phone RNG. No real money, no computers required. The participants are capable with probability but have no formal biology. The activity should make three things happen in this order: (1) participants watch two lineages with the same underlying fitness produce very different realized numbers of descendants, including one going extinct while the other spreads; (2) they feel the circularity of defining fitness by actual success, by being nudged into making that definition themselves and then noticing it explains nothing; and (3) they become genuinely uncertain about how a hidden propensity could ever be measured except by sampling outcomes, and about how much work the notion of the environment is doing. Include one later round with two types that have roughly equal expected offspring counts but different variance, and do not resolve what that means for the concept of fitness in the rules; save it for the debrief.

Structure your response with these sections, in this order and these approximate lengths:

1. Setup and materials (about 120 words). Everything to print or prepare, including the probability tables for each round.
2. Rules and play sequence (about 350 words). The exact mechanics of each round, and the one rule or scoring moment that deliberately lets participants define fitness by observed success so that the circularity shows up in their own reasoning.
3. Timeline for 75 minutes (about 100 words). How to divide setup, play, and debrief, with rounding up at each transition.
4. Debrief script (about 250 words). The exact questions to ask, in order, before the terms are introduced; then a mapping from specific moments in the play onto the propensity interpretation, the tautology objection, expected value versus realized outcome, and the equal-mean-different-variance case.
5. Likely failure modes and fixes (about 150 words). What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and what to do when each happens.
6. What the activity cannot show (about 100 words). Which feature of the propensity interpretation the game is too crude to capture, and what I should supply in discussion instead.

No opening lecture: do not have the facilitator introduce the terms fitness, propensity, or tautology objection before the debrief, and do not name the philosophical issue until the participants have already run into it themselves.
