I am a retired biochemist who has spent the past few years reading philosophy of mathematics, and next month I am leading a single two-hour session for a reading group of retired scientists and engineers on Benacerraf's dilemma for mathematical platonism—roughly, the tension between the standard truth-conditional semantics for mathematical statements and the demand that knowledge require some kind of access to what those statements are about. Most of the group is mathematically literate at the undergraduate level but has no training in philosophy, and this will be their first encounter with the dilemma. Do not explain the dilemma to me. Plan how I should run the session.

I want the ordering that does real work: which components must be in place before the dilemma can even be stated cleanly, and what depends on what earlier piece. Identify the one example, or running case, that should carry the whole session; say why it is the right carrier, what properties it must have and must not have, and—if you set aside the first example a scientist would reach for, an arithmetic truth such as 7 + 5 = 12—say why you set it aside. Then give me the misconception map for this particular audience: the predictable mistakes or temptations (I suspect at least these three: thinking the dilemma is a skeptical argument that mathematics is not knowledge; thinking it refutes platonism outright rather than exposing a tension between semantics and epistemology; and thinking that rejecting the causal theory of knowledge was always an obvious, cost-free escape). For each misconception, say exactly where in the sequence I should head it off rather than correct it afterward. End with a check for understanding—a short question or exercise to pose at the end that would show whether someone has grasped that the dilemma is a dilemma, not a one-sided argument. Finally, list what I should deliberately leave out at this level, even though it is relevant, and say why, so the session does not collapse into technicality.
