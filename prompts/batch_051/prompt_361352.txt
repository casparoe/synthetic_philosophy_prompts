In my seminar on political authority, a student pressed the following argument against consent-and-exit theories of state legitimacy, and I would like a careful diagnosis. (1) A state's authority over a person is legitimate only if it rests on that person's consent, and for someone born into a state that consent can only be supplied by the fact that one may leave. (2) A charter city or seastead is the limiting case of this idea: residents positively enter a jurisdiction chosen among rival providers of legal rules and may leave it under specified terms. (3) But entry into any such jurisdiction is negotiated against a background of property, enforcement costs, and risk fixed by existing states, so no act of joining is unconstrained. (4) Therefore joining a private city cannot supply the uncoerced choice that consent requires. (5) If constrained choice is nonetheless good enough for joining a charter city, then parity requires that it is good enough for staying in the state one was born into, since emigration is simply another constrained choice among alternatives. (6) Hence the residence-plus-exit criterion either legitimises every state however coercive, so long as some exit is conceivable, or refuses legitimacy to every private city however voluntarily it was joined. (7) The second horn then generalises: marriage, employment, and ordinary association are constrained choices too, so the criterion would condemn all voluntary association. That conclusion seems absurd, yet I cannot find a principled place to break the chain. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep the idea that exit matters for legitimacy, but I want an honest account of the alternatives. Please answer in British English.
