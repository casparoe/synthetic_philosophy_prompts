Two friends of mine, Dario and Mei, got into it over the surprise exam paradox, and I wrote down the main moves because I couldn't referee it myself. Standard setup: on a Friday, a professor announces there will be an exam on one of the next five weekdays, and the students won't know in advance which day it will be. The students reason backwards: it can't be Friday, because after no exam Monday–Thursday they'd know on Thursday night; it can't be Thursday, because with Friday ruled out they'd know Wednesday night; and so on until they conclude there can be no exam. Then the professor gives it on Wednesday and they're surprised.

Dario started. He said the whole paradox is an artifact of the students' certainty in the teacher. If the students allow even a small chance that the announcement is false — or that the professor cancels — the backward induction can't get off the ground: on Thursday night, having seen no exam Monday–Thursday, they'd face two live possibilities, exam Friday or no exam at all, so they couldn't know it's Friday, and the professor could still surprise them. Real students are never certain, so the paradox only appears because the puzzle stipulates an idealized, perfectly trusted announcement that doesn't describe any real situation.

Mei replied that this changes the problem instead of solving it. The interesting case is exactly the one where the students do know the announcement is true; that's part of the stipulation. In that case, on Thursday night with no earlier exam, they'd know it's Friday, so Friday can't be the surprise day — and the chain runs back until the two parts of the announcement contradict each other. Adding doubt dodges the contradiction rather than locating a false step in the original reasoning. A real solution has to say which premise is false or which inference is invalid in the strict setup, and "real students aren't sure" does neither.

Dario came back: the original reasoning has to contain a false step, because the professor may have actually chosen Wednesday, and the students' argument would run identically no matter which day she choose. If it were sound, its conclusion — no exam — would be sound even though the exam is in fact going to happen on Wednesday. The bad step, he said, is the very first elimination. The students reason "if the exam is Friday, then on Thursday night we'd know it's Friday." But that conditional silently assumes their Thursday-night information state would still include full trust in the announcement. If they've already derived that the announcement is inconsistent, their Thursday-night state wouldn't look like that at all. The surprise condition is a constraint on the students' epistemic state after their own reasoning, and their reasoning changes that state. So the induction is what he called an unstable fixed point: each elimination appeals to a future information state that the elimination itself destroys.

Mei shot back that this is a psychological redescription, not a counterexample. The argument doesn't require the students to be logically perfect; it just tracks what follows from the premises. If their own reasoning makes the announcement look impossible, that's exactly the paradox: apparently sound deduction from known premises led to a contradiction. Calling it an "unstable fixed point" doesn't say which premise is false or which step is invalid. It just says no real student would keep believing the teacher — which is Dario's uncertainty point again, wearing extra vocabulary.

They went in circles after that.
