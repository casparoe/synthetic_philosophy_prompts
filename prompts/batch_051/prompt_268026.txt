I keep going back and forth on how much to trust the climate model ensembles, and I can't settle it, so I want someone to actually make the two sides of my head fight it out.

On one side, the ensemble approach seems like the only sane way to handle climate uncertainty. No single model is right, but if you run a bunch of them, built by different groups, with different grids and different ways of handling clouds and ocean mixing, and they still roughly agree that doubling CO2 gives you a few degrees of warming rather than half a degree or ten, that agreement feels like it should count for something. If they disagreed all over the place, that would be a sign we didn't understand the system. And the spread across the ensemble is supposed to be the uncertainty to use for actual decisions, like how high to build a sea wall or what emissions target to set. Picking one favorite model would be arbitrary, so the spread seems like the most honest thing to hand a policymaker.

On the other side, I have this persistent intuition that the whole thing is less independent than it looks. These models are not independant witnesses. A lot of them share code, or share the same basic architecture, the same big equations, the same textbooks for the small-scale stuff. And all of them have been tuned against the same observed record in one way or another. So if there's some deep mistake they all share, some process every model gets wrong in the same direction, it won't show up as scatter in the ensemble at all. In that case, agreement among forty models is more like forty near-copies of the same model than forty separate lines of evidence, and the spread might be measuring the group blind spot, not the real uncertainty.

So the clash is pretty clean. The ensemble method is systematic, non-arbitrary, and gives a definite range to act on, but it seems to assume the models are more independent than they are. My intuition says robustness across near-copies isn't much evidence, and the real uncertainty is probably wider than the spread. But I also know that "probably wider" by itself gives me nothing to work with.

Which one should give? Is my intuition just the sort of objection that would leave us with no way to make any decision, and so should be discounted? Or should I give up the idea that the raw ensemble spread is a decent estimate of decision-relevant uncertainty? Or is there a middle position where the ensemble is still useful but has to be corrected for shared assumptions, and what would that correction look like in practice? I want to know what would justify each move, not just which side wins. And can you write the answer in American English.
