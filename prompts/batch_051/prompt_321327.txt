Ok so I've been chewing on the idea that the self is a center of narrative gravity — not a little boss in the head but an abstraction we posit to keep the story of a life coherent, the way a center of gravity is a useful fiction that still does real work. I want to test it against Stranger than Fiction. In the film Harold Crick starts hearing a narrator describe his life in real time; the narrator turns out to be a novelist who thinks she's writing fiction and is famous for killing off her protagonists, and the boundary between the story she's telling and the life he's living gets dangerously blurry. On the face of it, that's the theory's dream movie. But I'm not convinced the film actually vindicates it. Harold has fear, taste, a crush, a wish not to die; a lot of that seems to outrun anything the narrator says about him, and the movie gets much of its force from treating him as a first-person subject rather than a convenient posit. Yet even his attempt to escape being narrated arrives already narrated, and the ending practically conflates living a good story with living a good life. I'd like a reading that goes both ways: where the film stages the self-as-center-of-narrative-gravity idea, where is pushes back, and whether it leaves us with something the idea can't absorb. I'm thinking of using this in an intro class, and I don't want a reducitive reading that turns the movie into a diagram of the theory; I want to know where each one exposes the other's limits, and what that leaves us with about the self.
