I run a philosophy of mind seminar for second-year undergrads, 18 to 22 people, and we get one 90-minute session a week. I've found that when I explain the hard problem of consciousness they all nod and even use "explanatory gap" back to me, but a week later they talk as if the only question left is which neural mechanism to point at. I want an activity, not a lecture, that makes them feel the gap before I name it — something where they run into the fact that a complete-sounding physical and functional description of an experience still didn't prepare them for what the experience is like.

My rough idea is this: give them a dense, scientific-sounding description of a taste they've almost certainly never had, get them to write down predictions about what it will be like, then let them actually taste it and compare. The choice of stimulus should be cheap, safe, and handle allergies, consent, and people who refuse to eat things on principle. Could you design the full session — stimulus options, timing, a prediction sheet or handout, and a debrief — so the problem shows up in what happens rather than in me explaining it afterwards? They shouldn't hear the phrase "hard problem" until the end. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the idea, say what predictably goes wrong (people refusing, allergies, someone claiming they predicted it perfectly fine, the person who says taste is just brain states), and be frank about which feature of the hard problem the activity cannot reproduce. In particular I'm worried about the objection that a particular description failing to prepare you doesn't show that no description could, and I'd like the debrief to address that head-on rather than dodge it, namly by explaining what the activity shows and what it merely gestures at. And please write the answer in British English.
