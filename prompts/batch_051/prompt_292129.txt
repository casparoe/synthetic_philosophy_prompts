What is your actual credence that strict self-deception is psychologically real? To make the target precise: the proposition is that at least one actual human being has, at some time, knowingly and intentionally brought it about that they hold a belief they knew to be false, and the resulting false belief then functioned in their reasoning and action for a nontrivial period. I want a number between 0 and 1, not "it depends on how you define self-deception." If you think this intentionalist description misdescribes ordinary self-deception rather than capturing a central part of it, fold that judgment into the decomposition instead of refusing the question.

Decompose your number into at least these components: (a) the probability that this intentionalist analysis captures a central part of what people ordinarily call self-deception, rather than being a philosopher's invention or an over-literal reading; (b) the probability, given (a), that the apparent paradox — a single agent knowingly causing herself to believe what she knows is false — can be dissolved in a way that leaves the phenomenon real rather than reducing it to ordinary motivated bias, forgetting, or a change of mind; and (c) the probability, given (a) and (b), that actual human psychology contains confirmed or confirmable cases of it. Show how you combined them, identify which component your final number is most sensitive to, and say what evidence or argument would move it most.

Structure your answer in these sections, in this order:

1. Credence — state your number and, in one sentence, exactly what it ranges over. (1–2 sentences)

3. Sensitivity — the single largest lever on your number and the evidence or argument that would move it most. (50–100 words)
