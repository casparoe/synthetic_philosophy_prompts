I am taking a morphology course this term, and I would like help with a conceptual issue that the lectures keep circling without quite settling. The textbooks present the inflection–derivation distinction through a familiar set of criteria: inflectional morphology is said to be highly productive, paradigm-based, obligatory or syntactically triggered, category-preserving, and to produce forms of the same lexeme; derivational morphology is said to be less productive, optional, often category-changing, and to produce new lexemes. But I can produce a number of cases in which these criteria point in different directions, and I want to know what the distinction is ultimately a distinction between, and what to say when the criteria part company.

To make the problem concrete, here are the cases that trouble me. English comparative and superlative -er/-est look paradigm-like and are tied to the syntax, through than-phrases and definite superlative descriptions, yet many adjectives take more/most instead, and some comparative forms such as elder and inner have drifted into lexically distinct adjectives. The English possessive 's attaches to the final word of a phrase, as in the Queen of England's title, which makes it look like a phrasal clitic rather than a word-internal case ending. Regular-looking plurals such as damages and customs carry non-compositional, lexeme-like meanings rather than a straightforward compositional plural reading. Participles such as interesting or drunk can serve as adjectives, with some fully lexicalized and others still transparently verbal. Diminutives such as kitchenette or Spanish -ito are optional and category-preserving, quite productive without being grammatically required, and therefore sit uneasily with the textbook picture of both inflection and derivation.

I am not looking for a survey of analyses or for citations, only for a conceptual explanation. In particular: is the underlying issue best framed as one about lexeme identity, that is, deciding whether two surface forms count as the same word, or as one about syntactic automaticity, productivity, or something else? Do the criteria form a family-resemblance cluster, a cline, or would it be better to say that inflection and derivation name two explanatory jobs that do not sort observed morphology into two clean classes? And what, if anything, does the messiness show about where one word ends and another begins?

One further complication I have encountered: some accounts distinguish between inflection that realizes features the syntax demands, such as agreement, and inflection that realizes a semantic category the speaker chooses, such as number on a noun, implying that only the first is inflectional in the crucial syntactic sense. Does this move dissolve the problem, sharpen it, or merely relocate it?

Where there is genuine disagreement in the literature, I would rather see the live options separated cleanly than have one analysis presented as settled. And if the way I have set this up is itself confused, please say so plainly rather than defending the framing; for instance, I may be treating diagnostics as rival definitions, or the boundary question may turn out to be a pseudo-problem. I would rather hear that than receive a tidy answer to a badly posed question.
