I've been reading about how NICE decides what the NHS will fund, and the method is basically to measure a treatment's benefit in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) and approve the things that give the most QALYs for the money. What's bugging me is that this seems to assume you can add up benefits across different people like one big pot. A treatment that gives one person an extra thirty years of decent health can lose out to a treatment that gives a hundred people one extra year each, if the total QALYs come out higher. So one person's huge loss gets outweighed by lots of other people's small gains, and that feels like treating people as containers of utility rather than as separate lives that each matter on their own.

I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but I've heard people say utilitarianism doesn't respect something called the separateness of persons. How exactly does that objection apply to a QALY-based system like NICE's? What view of persons and fairness does this sort of cost-effectiveness policy presuppose? Could you run the same policy while still respecting separateness, or would a serious separateness-of-persons view force NHS priority-setting to work differently? Also, is the practice internally consistent — the NHS still has strong consent rules and refuses to treat people merely as a means in, say, organ donation — or is it quietly combining two incompatible philosophies?
