In the canonical Ship of Theseus, each plank of an oceangoing ship is replaced one at a time over several years. The removed planks are stored, and after the replacement is complete they are reassembled into a ship of the same design. Call the continuously repaired vessel the sea ship and the vessel built from the removed planks the warehouse ship. The standard question is which, if either, is Theseus's ship. Three analyses pull apart here. On a continuity analysis, Theseus's ship is the sea ship, because identity follows an unbroken spatiotemporal and functional career. On a material-composition analysis, Theseus's ship is the warehouse ship, because the warehouse ship contains the original planks arranged shipwise. On a mereological-essentialist analysis, the original ship ceases to exist at the first replacement, and neither later vessel is identical with it.

Now modify the case in one respect. When a plank is removed, it is not stored whole. It is resawn along its length so that only a fraction f of its thickness is kept; the rest is discarded. The kept layer is immediately laminated onto new timber of the same dimensions, producing a composite plank that is fraction f original material by volume. These composite planks are stored for later reassembly, with each original layer restored to its original position and orientation in the hull. The sea ship, as before, is repaired with wholly new planks and remains in service throughout, so its spatiotemporal career is unbroken. At the end of the process, the sea ship contains none of the original material, while the warehouse ship is assembled from composite planks each of which is only fraction f original material.

Consider first the case f = 1/2. Work through what the continuity analysis, the material-composition analysis, and the mereological-essentialist analysis each say about this modified case, and whether their verdicts diverge as sharply as they do in the original. Then consider allowing f to take any value between 0 and 1. Is there a principled value of f at which the material-composition analysis changes its answer, or is it forced to choose between imposing an unearned threshold and abandoning the claim that identity tracks the original material? Does the force of the original puzzle survive once the warehouse ship is only partially composed of original material, and which of the competing analyses best withstands the modification?
