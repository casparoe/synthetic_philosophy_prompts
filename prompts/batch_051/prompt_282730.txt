I've been asked to lead a discussion on how much we can actually know about the historical Jesus, and I've started by writing down my gut verdicts on individual gospel passages rather than reasoning from a method. I want help extracting the principle behind the pattern.

My verdicts: I'm confident Jesus was baptized by John, because a movement that called him sinless would hardly have invented a scene in which he submits to a baptism of repentance. I accept the saying that some standing there would not taste death before the kingdom came, since no later church would have manufactured a timetable that had already failed. By contrast, I doubt the detailed passion predictions that spell out rejection, execution, and rising after three days; they read like the outcome written back into the story. I count the saying about giving Caesar his coin as authentic, partly because it is too evasive to serve any later agenda cleanly. I accept the command to love one's enemies and pray for one's persecutors, even though the early church treasured that teaching, which should make me suspicious under the principle I keep using. I lean toward accepting the Good Samaritan, though only Luke preserves it and it fits Luke's sympathy for Samaritans, because the ethnic reversal feels more abrasive than Luke's use of it. And I cannot hear Jesus in John's long symbolic discourses; they sound like later theology wearing his name.

The principle I keep reaching for is roughly: trust a passage when the early movement would have been embarrassed by it or had no reason to invent it, and distrust it when it conveniently serves post-Easter needs. But my own verdicts don't all obey this rule, and I can't tell whether the rule is too crude or the disobedient verdicts are wrong. The rule also gives no guidance on cases I care about: the saying about leaving the dead to bury their own dead sounds authentic in just the way an abrasive, embarrassing saying should, but when I find the same hard demand softened elsewhere into something more livable, the rule doesn't tell me which version to pick, or whether it's just following my gut rather than correcting it.

So: what principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures these verdicts? Which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? What does it say about the case I go back and forth on — the saying about making oneself a eunuch for the kingdom, which only Matthew has and which fits later asceticism but sounds to me like the same hard voice? And if such a principle can be stated, does it actually settle who the historical Jesus was, or does it mostly rationalize intuitions we already bring to the evidence?
