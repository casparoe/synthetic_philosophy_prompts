I want a working definition of 'betrayal' that gets this list of cases right, and I want to know where the definition begins to strain. The cases: (1) a friend who divulges a secret you confided in him solely to impress other people; (2) a stranger who watches you drop your groceries and offers no help; (3) an employee who reports her firm's illegal dumping to regulators; (4) an employee who leaves for a competitor and persuades three colleagues to follow, while breaching no contract term; (5) a spouse who has a secret affair while fulfilling every explicit promise to the family; (6) a friend who breaks a confidence because he judges that keeping it would let you seriously harm a third party; (7) a legislator who votes against a bill that would rescue a close friend's business, on the merits. I'm confident that (1) is a betrayal and (2) is not; I am genuinely torn about (3) through (7). My hunch is that the core of betrayal is violating a specific commitment on which the other side reasonably relied, but cases like (3) and (6) make me suspect that 'reasonably relied' quietly imports a moral judgment, and that the word does two jobs—one structural, one condemnatory. Propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, and tell me which of the seven verdicts you are least confident in and whether that case should push you to revise the definition or to accept a surprising verdict.
