Design a reinforcement-learning thought experiment that forces two live views about whether RL agents can suffer to come apart in the following direction: the agent clearly satisfies a reward-based or valence-based criterion for suffering, while it just as clearly fails a plausible architectural criterion. Use only architectures and methods that are routine in present-day deep RL (e.g., a policy network plus a learned value function or critic, recurrence, standard gradient-based updates, reward shaping). No exotic hardware, no dualism, no stipulation that the agent does or does not have a felt quality. The negative reward events should play the causal role pain plays in avoidance learning: the agent learns to anticipate them, generalizes avoidance to novel situations, and reliably alters its policy to escape, forestall, or shorten them.

The two positions to engineer around:

1. Valence sufficiency. For an RL agent, negative reward is negative valence in the relevant sense, and a state whose learned value is strongly negative is a state of negative affect. On this view, whenever the agent is receiving negative reward, or is in a state its value function represents as deeply bad, the agent suffers; no further architectural story is required, because the reward signal already is the valenced teaching signal.

2. Architectural requirement. Suffering requires negative valence to be integrated in a certain way: the system must possess some self-model that represents its own present state as bad, and the negative evaluation must be globally available, meaning it is broadcast to and consumed by multiple downstream systems (such as goal-ranking, attention, memory, or report-like output), rather than remaining a scalar used only to update a value head or drive a narrow avoidance reflex. On this view, a penalty that merely changes weights is an error signal, not suffering.

Construct the scenario so that the first position gives a clear yes and the second gives a clear no. Then do the check: specify the agent architecture, the environment, and the reward schedule; trace an episode in which the agent is penalized; and show, step by step, why each position gives the verdict it does. Finish by identifying the single structural feature that does most of the work in making the two positions diverge, saying what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and giving the strongest reply a defender of valence sufficiency could make to stop the architectural view from dismissing the case. The purpose of the case is to isolate whether a reward signal that functions exactly like pain is sufficient for suffering, or whether a self-model and global availability are further necessary conditions; a case that merely stipulates the desired verdicts rather than generating them from the architecture and reward structure fails the brief.
