A peculiar impasse in the literature on collective memory has been occupying me lately, and I would like a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a ruling on which side is right. The question is what should happen when a community's collective memory conflicts with the documentary or archival record. One camp — call it memory-first — holds that collective memory is not a degraded approximation of professional history but a different kind of relation to the past, one through which a community constitutes its identity and its standing as a moral agent. On this view, when the archive contradicts what a community remembers about, say, a dispossession, an atrocity, or a founding episode, the demand that memory "give way" is not a neutral appeal to truth but an exercise of epistemic authority by professional historians and state institutions; communities are entitled to privilege their own transmission of the past, because what hangs on the memory is not merely a proposition to be verified but a form of collective self-respect. The other camp — call it record-first — holds that the evidentiary discipline of professional history exists precisely to correct the ways memory can flatter, console, or harden into legend. On this view, sources, cross-checking, and the public scrutiny of evidence have no intrinsic loyalty to present identities, and that is exactly why they are the right instrument when memory and record part ways; letting memory override the record, however painful, buys present cohesion at the cost of self-deception, and the cost is ultimately paid by the same community. The debate has run for decades across historiography, social theory, and the study of collective memory, and, as far as I can tell, the two sides have barely moved. It has recently become personal for me: a commemorative dispute in my own community turned on a remembered injustice that conflicts with the surviving documents in one important detail, and I watched the same standoff replay itself in miniature.

My working hypothesis is that the two camps are not really answering one question. The memory-first side, as I read it, is making a claim about identity and normative continuity, while the record-first side is making a claim about truth and justification, and the impression of a single question is manufactured by using the single word "history" for both an account of what happened and a community's practice of understanding itself. But I doubt this captures everything; there may also be a substantive disagreement about whether inherited communal beliefs deserve special epistemic standing, or about whether the goods of identity and self-respect can ever legitimately override evidentially warranted beliefs. I want you to state the strongest version of each position, then diagnose where the disagreement actually lies — a verbal conflation, a hidden normative dispute, a difference in standards of evidence or in which cases count as data, bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, or some combination — and say what would count as progress given that diagnosis. If the honest conclusion is that no progress should be expected, say why, and say what responding well to the impasse would amount to. Please do not settle the underlying question of which claims should give way; the object is the structure of the impasse itself. At the very end, after the full discussion, give your diagnosis in a single sentence on its own line, enclosed as \answer{...}.
