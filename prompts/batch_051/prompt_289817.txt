I am a graduate student in comparative cognition and have been arguing with a colleague about what Morgan's canon actually does. As I understand it, the canon says that if an animal action can be explained by a lower psychological faculty, we ought not to attribute it to a higher one. My colleague treats this as a purely methodological rule of thumb: it tells us which explanation to prefer until that explanation breaks, but it does not by itself tell us what the animal is actually doing. I suspect this is too deflationary, but I am not sure how one would show that.

Take a concrete and common behavior: a dog that appears to watch its owner and to wait until the owner closes her eyes or turns away before taking forbidden food from a counter. This looks like a case in which the dog is sensitive to something like the human's visual attention. But it may also be a case in which the dog is merely reading simpler cues, such as the direction of the head, the visibility of the eyes, bodily posture, or a history of being punished under certain configurations. How would one design an experiment that could genuinely separate the richer interpretation, on which the dog is attending to what the owner can see, from the leaner interpretation, on which the dog is using salient observable cues without representing anything about the owner's perceptual state?

I would like a concrete experimental design, a statement of which result would favor each reading, and then an honest account of what a clean result would still leave unresolved. In particular, I want to know whether Morgan's canon is doing real empirical work in such a design, or whether it merely instructs us to prefer the leaner story even when the evidence is ambiguous. What exactly would a positive result, on either side, establish about the dog's psychological faculties? And what part of the dispute cannot be settled by any such experiment, not merely because the experiment is difficult, but because the concepts themselves leave a residue that evidence cannot reach?
