I'm putting together a unit on the politics and ethics of memory, and I keep running into the same puzzle. Four societies all say they are remembering a violent past, but the practices they call remembering appear to rest on quite different commitments. In Germany, Holocaust denial is a crime, Nazi symbols are banned, and public money supports a dense memorial landscape: museums, national monuments, and tens of thousands of small brass Stolpersteine embedded in sidewalks, each marking an individual deported person by name. Sustained engagement with the Nazi past is built into school curricula and civic ritual as a permanent national obligation. In Japan, the Yasukuni Shrine in Tokyo enshrines the nation's war dead, including men convicted as Class-A war criminals by the postwar Tokyo tribunal; official visits to the shrine are typically defended as honoring the dead rather than endorsing the war, and teaching about Japanese atrocities such as the Nanjing Massacre and the system of military sexual slavery is politically contested rather than legally required. Turkey has a criminal provision against public denigration of the Turkish nation, and it has long treated describing the 1915 destruction of the Ottoman Armenians as genocide as an attack on national identity itself, preferring to speak of shared wartime suffering. Meanwhile, in the American South, most monuments to the Confederacy were erected not in the immediate aftermath of the Civil War but in later waves, during the Jim Crow and mid-twentieth-century Civil Rights eras, and they are usually defended today as honoring heritage, ancestors, and regional identity rather than slavery or secession.

What I want to know is what each practice presupposes about memory, collective identity, and responsibility — and whether those presuppositions form consistent rival theories or are using the word "remembering" in different senses. The German practice, for instance, seems to presuppose that a nation is a transgenerational agent that can inherit guilt and owe acknowledgment to the victims of its past. Does the Japanese practice presuppose that commemorating the dead can be cleanly separated from endorsing what they died for? Does the Turkish practice presuppose a performative view of national identity, on which the nation is something that must be continually affirmed in public speech and can be wounded by words? Does the Southern defense of monuments presuppose that honoring ancestors is separable from endorsing their cause, or does it secretly depend on a substantive and contested historical claim about what that cause was? Finally, what would actually have to change in any one of these practices if we replaced its underlying theory of memory with one of the others?

Please work through this as a professional philosopher of collective memory and social ontology, and think carefully rather than reaching for labels. Start by brainstorming the candidate commitments each practice might presuppose; then, for each case, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, including places where the same surface practice is compatible with several rival theories. The goal is not a history paper but a reconstruction of the implicit philosophies — and a verdict on whether the four societies actually disagree about how to remember, or about what remembering even is.
