Lately I've been trying to figure out how to respond to my mom, who's convinced a healing at her church was a miracle, and I keep coming back to Hume. In the Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section X, "Of Miracles," he writes:

All Probability, then, supposes an Opposition of Experiments and Observations; where the one Side is found to over-balance the other, and to produce a Degree of Evidence, proportion'd to the Superiority. A hundred Instances or Experiments on one Side, and fifty on another, afford a very doubtful Expectation of any Event; tho' a hundred uniform Experiments, with only one contradictory one, does reasonably beget a very strong Degree of Assurance. In all Cases, we must balance the opposite Experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the lesser Number from the greater, in order to know the exact Force of the superior Evidence.

He seems to be giving a kind of bookkeeping rule for evidence: subtract the smaller side from the bigger, and the remainder is your degree of assurance. But then later in the same section he argues that a miracle, as a violation of a law of nature, has uniform experience against it, so testimony for it can only be accepted if the witnesses' being mistaken or lying would itself be contrary to uniform experience in an even stronger way. I'm confused about how these two parts fit together. If you apply the bookkeeping rule literally, then even a massive body of uniform experience is just one side of the ledger; enough testimonial evidence should be able to outweigh it, no matter how many past instances are piled up. But if uniform experience against miracles is treated as a full proof, it seems like no finite amount of counter-evidence could ever tip the balance. Which interpretation does Hume actually need for the argument to work, and is it consistent with the bookkeeping picture? Or is the bookkeeping picture not meant to be applied to testimonial evidence—and if not, why not?
