I am currently taking a course on Hellenistic philosophy with a substantial unit on the modern revival of Stoicism, and I keep noticing a pattern in that revival that I do not yet have a precise term for.

The first instance concerns the dichotomy of control. The ancient line, as we have read it in the Handbook attributed to Epictetus, is considerably more austere than the version that circulates in popular presentations. What is up to us, on the ancient account, is roughly the governing use of our impressions: judgment, assent, desire, and aversion. What is not up to us includes body, property, reputation, public standing, and even the success of the actions we attempt. In the contemporary practical-Stoicism material we have been set alongside the primary texts, however, the boundary is drawn elsewhere: effort, intention, decision, habits, and the actions one takes are counted as things within our control, while only outcomes and other people's responses remain outside it. I understand why the modern version is easier to live by, but it seems to me that the line has been moved, not just simplified.

The second instance is the treatment of the emotions. In the primary texts the passions are presented as the product of false assent, and the ideal state looks like freedom from them rather than better management of them. Several contemporary retellings, by contrast, describe Stoicism as the art of feeling an emotion without being ruled by it, of regulating anger or grief rather than extinguishing it. Again the revival keeps the school's name and its authority and presents itself as a faithful continuation, but one of the school's hardest commitments has been softened into something much closer to common-sense emotional self-control.

I would like to know whether this specific phenomenon has an established name in the history of philosophy, in reception studies, or in the intellectual history of ideas. The nearest categories I can think of all seem to miss it: popularization can be faithful; bowdlerization concerns the removal of offensive material, not the quiet rewiring of a concept's boundary; presentism is about imposing present categories on the past, whereas this is more like adjusting the past to fit present needs while claiming its authority. If no established term captures it, I would value a precise definition and delimitation of a candidate term, including how to tell when a revival has crossed from vigorous rephrasing into this kind of boundary-redrawing, and when it has gone far enough that the revived view is no longer the same view. A bare label would not help much; I am trying to learn how to carve the distinction.
