I keep running into the same pattern in maths and I'd like to know whether it has a name, or whether I'm conflating two or three different things. The clearest instance: when you solve x² − 2 = 0 you get two roots, and if you want to write them down you have to put them in some order — '√2 and −√2' — but which one comes first is entirely your choice, and nobody thinks anything hangs on it. Now take x² + 1 = 0. The roots are i and −i, and this time there is no property either root has that the other lacks; complex conjugation swaps them and fixes every true statement about the field they generate. I can point at one of them only by saying 'the one I'm calling i.' Yet the moment I've labelled it, I can write things like 'i is the root with positive imaginary part,' which sounds like a description but is really just a way of redrawing the same arbitrary line. It bothers me more that some constructions quietly depend on the choice. I once wrote a function that returned the 'larger' root of a cubic, and only later realised that 'larger' does real work only when the roots happen to be real; over the complex numbers it's meaningless until I've imposed an ordering that isn't there.

I'm not really asking about quadratics, though. I'm asking about the general phenomenon: a piece of mathematics is perfectly well-behaved, but to state it you must choose among equivalent-looking options, and sometimes that choice is harmless (an ordering) and sometimes it's corrosive (a definition that claims to be canonical but secretly depends on your labelling). Is there an established distinction here — and if it's roughly the difference between 'well-defined' and 'canonical', I'd like more than those two words, because I want to know exactly where the boundary sits between an arbitary-but-harmless labelling and a choice that genuinely undermines the construction, and what you should do in the murky middle. If no single term captures it, what would you call it, and where would you draw the line? Also, please say what would change your mind about where the line falls — what sort of example would push you to treat a case I've called harmful as merely conventional, or the reverse?
