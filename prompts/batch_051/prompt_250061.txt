In A Theory of Justice, Rawls distinguishes envy from resentment. Resentment, as he describes it, is a moral feeling: it responds to a perceived violation of a principle of justice. Envy, by contrast, is said not to be a moral feeling. It is a propensity to regard another person’s greater good with hostility even when that person’s advantage does not diminish one’s own, and it can include a willingness to deprive the other of that good even at some cost to oneself. Yet Rawls also allows that envy may be excusable, in particular when the inequalities in a society are large enough to damage the self-respect of those who are worse off; their envy is then directed at advantages enjoyed by others in conditions where their own standing is already precarious.

Please argue that this concession is less stable than Rawls presents it. Specifically, I want you to defend the following position: once excusable envy is grounded in an injury to self-respect produced by large inequalities, the sharp distinction between envy and resentment begins to collapse, because such envy implicitly tracks a judgement about the justice of those inequalities even though it does not take the full form of resentment. Develop the argument that excusable envy should be understood as an inchoate or misdirected moral response rather than a merely non-moral disposition, and then explain what this implies for how we should assess expressions of envy in public life: when should we treat them as symptoms of injustice that call for investigation, and when should we treat them as vices to be criticised? In doing so, be careful to distinguish your position from the stronger claim that every episode of envy is a moral criticism.
