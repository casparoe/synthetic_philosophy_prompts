I am a PhD student working on the foundations of statistical mechanics, and I keep running into a disagreement about Maxwell's demon that I would like you to help me think through.

The standard resolution runs as follows. A Maxwellian demon can sort fast and slow molecules so as to lower the entropy of a gas, but it can do so only by acquiring information about the molecules and storing it in some memory; when that memory is reset, Landauer's principle imposes a minimum cost of k_B T ln 2 of dissipated heat per bit erased, and this is held to save the second law. Some take this to be a substantive discovery about the world: information is physical, the bit is a genuine unit of entropy, and the Landauer bound is a law of nature constraining any possible information-processing device. Others maintain that the bound is bookkeeping — a theorem within our particular statistical-mechanical idealization, true of the devices we happen to build under the entropy definitions we happen to use, but no proof that information is physical in any deeper sense.

I want to know whether evidence can separate these readings. Design the strongest experiment or sequence of experiments you can — it may be an idealized design, but it must be concrete — that would distinguish the law-of-nature reading from the bookkeeping reading. Specify the system, the quantity measured, the conditions, and the numerical result that would count for each side. Then say exactly what a sharply positive result would and would not settle. In particular, if a device is shown to dissipate at least k_B T ln 2 per erased bit across a wide range of implementations, does that force the strong reading that information is itself physical, or does it leave open that we have merely confirmed an artifact of our accounting and our choice of idealization?
