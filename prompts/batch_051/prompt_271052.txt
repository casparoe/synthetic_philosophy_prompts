Use the Senate fight over ratification of the Treaty of Versailles and American entry into the League of Nations, from the summer of 1919 through the final defeat of the treaty in 1920, as the material for testing "American exceptionalism," where the phrase is divided into three distinct things: (a) a historical thesis, namely that the United States developed along a path sufficiently unlike Europe's that European institutions, alliances, and modes of power politics cannot be transplanted onto it; (b) a normative claim, namely that the United States bears a special role or responsibility in world affairs, whether as an uncorrupted example held apart from the world or as a redeemer nation that enters and transforms it; and (c) an ideological register, namely a legitimating vocabulary through which factions present their preferred policies as the working-out of a national destiny.

Reconstruct the main positions in the debate — Wilson's demand for ratification without reservations, Lodge's reservationist bloc with its objections centered on Article X's collective-security commitment, and the irreconcilables who opposed membership on any terms — and identify what each strand of exceptionalism, applied consistently, would have prescribed for the Senate vote. Show that exceptionalism cuts in both directions here: the exemplarist version counsels standing apart from the Old World so as to remain a model, while the missionary version counsels joining the League so as to transform it. Map the actual arguments of the debate onto these strands, and do not reduce any side to a single strand.

Then run the analysis in the other direction. Ask whether the threefold distinction survives contact with the episode: which features of the debate and of the treaty's defeat are genuinely explained by exceptionalist ideas acting as constraints or motives, and which are better explained by partisan discipline, senatorial prerogative, regional and ethnic politics, or hard postwar calculations of power? Conclude by saying what the League fight shows about American exceptionalism as an analytical concept — whether it counts as a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the concept was never designed to handle — and mark explicitly where the concept falls silent rather than stretching it to cover what it cannot explain.
