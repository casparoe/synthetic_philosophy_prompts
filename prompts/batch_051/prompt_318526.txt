Set aside the equal-basic-liberties and fair-equality-of-opportunity requirements; I am interested only in how the veil of ignorance is supposed to deliver the economic part of the second principle. I have an argument that seems to derive the difference principle with almost no appeal to risk aversion, and I cannot tell whether it goes through.

(1) A principle of justice is just only if rational parties would accept it behind a veil of ignorance, where they do not know their place in society, their natural endowments, or their conception of the good.

(2) Behind the veil, a party must regard each social position in the resulting basic structure as one they might come to occupy.

(3) So a party cannot rationally accept an arrangement if there is an alternative arrangement under which the position that is worst for them is better, and no other position is worse.

(4) Thus the decisive test for any arrangement is simply whether the position of the least advantaged could be improved; if it could, the arrangement fails the test.

(5) The just arrangement, then, is the one that makes the least advantaged position as good as possible, whatever the consequences for other positions.

(6) That is the difference principle: economic inequalities are permitted only under a basic structure arranged so that they work maximally to the advantage of the least advantaged.

I half-suspect something goes wrong around steps (4) and (5), but I cannot say exactly what. Is the argument valid? If not, identify the exact step that fails, spell out the weaker and stronger readings of that step, and say what further premise would be needed for the conclusion to follow.
