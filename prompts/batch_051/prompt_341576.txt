I'm a novelist, and one of my characters is an AI researcher who became wierdly obsessed with the Lucas-Penrose argument. I picked up a copy of John Lucas's Minds, Machines and Gödel so he could refer to the actual argument rather than the usual bloodless online summaries, and there is one passage early on that I'm stuck on. My best paraphrase of the passage is this. Lucas asks us to consider any given machine that is supposed to mimic a mind by proving arithmetical truths. He says, roughly, that for any such machine that is consistent, there will be a formula of arithmetic that the machine cannot prove but that we can see is true. From this he concludes that no such machine, however much we refine its program, can be the whole story about the mind, because the mind can always recognize the truth of the machine's Gödel sentence from outside. What I can't get straight is whether Lucas is entitled to this outside step. Seeing that the Gödel sentence is true seems to require knowing the machine is consistent, which is not something the machine itself has shown and not something we can just read off the sentence. So is the argument actually showing a non-mechanical ability, or is it smuggling in an unproved assumption about consistency through the phrase we can see it is true? Please walk me through what Lucas actually establishes in that passage, and whether the argument survives if we refuse to grant the consistency claim outright. My character's beliefs need to be a more faithfull version than the usual caricature.
