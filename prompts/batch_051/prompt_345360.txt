I am taking a seminar on collective decision-making under uncertainty, and the argument that now has the most grip on me is the unilateralist's curse. As I understand it, the argument runs as follows. Suppose some action is all-or-nothing, very hard to reverse, and will bind an entire group, and any one of many roughly equally informed members can take it unilaterally. If each person acts whenever her own evidence makes it more likely than not that the action would be good, then even if every person is unbiased and follows the evidence scrupulously, the probability that someone takes the action when the action is in fact bad grows toward certainty as the group gets large. The chance of being that mistaken person does not need to be high for any individual; it only needs to be greater than zero for each of many people. From this I am drawn to the conclusion that the decision rule should not be "act at just over fifty percent confidence" but something much more conservative: a much higher threshold, a requirement of consensus, or delegation to a single accountable body.

Yet I have a firm intuition that refuses to go away when I consider a different sort of case. A field engineer notices what she honestly estimates is a thirty-five percent chance that a dam will fail overnight and flood a town. Alerting the town would force a costly evacuation and, if she is wrong, would damage confidence in future warnings. She also knows that several other equally competent people are in a position to notice the same signs, each of whom might independently raise the alarm on weak evidence. The curse-style reasoning seems to tell her to stay silent, because false alarms are exactly the kind of unilateral harm the higher threshold is meant to prevent. But my intuition is that she should raise the alarm at once: the expected harm of waiting, including the chance that everyone else will also wait or miss it, is enormous, and a rule that treats doing nothing as neutral rather than as itself a risky unilateral choice seems to be stacking the deck.

I want to know whether there is a genuine conflict here, or whether the curse, properly specified, applies only when inaction is the safe baseline, so that the dam case is not a counterexample but a different decision problem with asymmetric costs. If the conflict is genuine, should I give up the intuition as an artefact of status-quo bias, or amend the theory so that its threshold reflects the cost of false negatives as well as false positives? What, concretely, would justify each of those moves, and what would the rule look like once it stops assuming that the status quo is cost-free?
