I'm in a philosophy of law course and we just spent a week on contracts of adhesion, mostly on the standard argument for why clicking "I agree" binds you even though almost nobody actually reads the terms. The version we got in lecture runs something like this: contract law doesn't care about your private mental state, it cares about the objective manifestation of assent; clicking the box is an outward act that any reasonable person would read as agreeing, and since you had the opportunity to inspect the terms and decline, enforcing the unread boilerplate respects your autonomy and keeps mass transactions cheap. Our professor also gestured at Llewellyn's backstop, the idea that consumers don't assent to each clause one by one but to a blanket of reasonable terms, with surprising or indecent clauses getting extra scrutiny from courts.

But I keep feeling the argument is quietly assuming a choice situation the internet has mostly dismantled. Terms now show up after purchase, inside nested scroll boxes, or behind a button that doesnt even say "I agree"; the layout gets A/B-tested so that more people click without reading (dark patterns), and the terms can be changed later through unilateral modification clauses sent to an unread email. The opportunity to read isn't just something people squander anymore; it's engineered to be inert. So here is what I actually want to work through: does the objective-manifestation / duty-to-read argument survive this development, does it need repair, or is it dead as a justification for most online boilerplate? Which premise is exactly under pressure, and is there a repaired version that still justifies enforcing some of this without pretending anybody chose it? I care about the argument itself, not about which consumer-protection law we should pass.
