I'm a PhD student in archaeology, and this term I'm in a seminar on collapse and resilience—the usual Bronze Age, Classic Maya, and Easter Island material. My advisor keeps wincing whenever I use the word "collapse." Her line is that "collapse" is a narrative category we impose rather than a process we observe: populations did drop in some places, sure, and people stopped building monumental stuff, but that's reorganization, not a society failing or dying. I see the point, but as stated it feels unfalsifiable—like an objection you could make about almost any scientific category.

So I want to pin down what the claim could actually amount to. If someone says "collapse is not a real kind of event, its an artifact of our sources and our taste for dramatic endings," how would you test that? What concretely would you operationalize—settlement counts, household occupation histories, paleodemography, agricultural intensity, the survival of craft traditions, the rate and scale of monumental construction—and what pattern would count as evidence that something rightly called collapse occurred, rather than just elite turnover plus demographic shifting? Design the strongest version of the test you can, be specific about which result favors which reading, and then be honest about the residue: if the test came out positive, what would it still leave open about whether the word "collapse" is doing real conceptual work rather than bundling together changes that happen to co-occur in some periods but not others.
