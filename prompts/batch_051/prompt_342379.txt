I teach political philosophy, and about three years ago I made the mistake of using the phrase tragedy of the commons one too many times at a department party, and now I'm the token academic on our town's Shellfish Advisory Committee. We manage a small municipal clam flat, maybe eight acres of bay bottom, and every spring we get three or four proposals: someone wants the daily bushel limit raised, someone wants to seed the flat and take a cut later, someone wants commercial digging banned outright, someone wants to put in a dock or a public access path. We're seven volunteers, we meet once a month in the harbormaster's office, none of us is an economist or a marine biologist, and whatever we decide has to be defended at town meeting against people who will absolutely play to the crowd. We also cant gather much data: the harbormaster counts licenses and weighs a few buckets, and that's about it. Right now we mostly vote on vibe and whoever talks longest wins. Half the committee thinks the flat should earn the town money; the other half thinks it's basically a public park with clams in it. I keep muttering that a clam flat isnt exactly a public good because the clams are subtractable, and then I lose the room.

So here's what I'd like. Build me a decision procedure we can actually run in one sitting per proposal, grounded in whatever the theory of common-pool resources and public goods really supports -- excludability, rivalry, free-riding, monitoring, graduated sanctions, boundary rules, all of that -- but translated into questions a lay committee can use without the jargon. For each question tell me what counts as a good enough answer, where the procedure should stop and say no, and where the question is really a values call that we should own as appointed officials and not dress up as a fact. Then run the procedure on two real-style proposals we'd actually see: (i) a commercial digger wants the daily bushel limit doubled for one season because he says abundance is up, and (ii) a local aquaculture outfit offers to seed the flat and harvest a third of the clams each year for five years, and in exchange will build a public access path and keep the flat open to residents. Be honest about where the procedure pushes us to reject something most of the town clearly wants, if that's where it goes, and tell me what the procedure cannot settle and why.
