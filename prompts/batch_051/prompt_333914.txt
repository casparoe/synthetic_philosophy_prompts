I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am drafting a long-form feature about the way popular cinema keeps alive the suspicion that there is no world apart from the minds that perceive it. I would like a single piece of sustained criticism that reads one film through one philosophical argument.

Read The Truman Show through Berkeley's idealism, and in particular through his master argument. The argument, compressed, runs as follows. Berkeley invites you to try to conceive of an object existing entirely unperceived and unconceived by any mind. He thinks the attempt collapses, because the moment you succeed, the object is being conceived by you, the conceiver; hence mind-independent existence cannot even be framed in thought, and to be is to be perceived. The film supplies a rich test case. Truman Burbank has spent his life in Seahaven, an enclosed town run as a continuous broadcast: the sky, the weather, his job, his friendships and his marriage are staged for hidden cameras, all of it produced by a director who watches from a control room above the dome. When Truman finally grows suspicious and sails for the horizon, his boat reaches the painted wall of the sky and he finds a door that leads out of the set.

I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should neither reduce the film to an illustration of Berkeley nor distort Berkeley to fit the film, and it should say plainly where the mapping breaks down. In particular, consider whether Truman's attempt to conceive of a place beyond Seahaven is the very feat Berkeley declares impossible, and whether the film backs Berkeley's deeper point that any imagined outside arrives with a perceiver already built into the picture: the audience at home, the director, even Truman himself as the one conceiving it. Then consider where the film most resists Berkeley. Does the ending, in which the audience sees the broadcast end and Truman steps through the door without being followed, work as a realist reversal: a world beyond the watched world, which continues to exist when no director and no viewer perceives it? I would also like you to draw out the moral texture that the bare argument abstracts away: the difference between a world held in being by a trustworthy God and a world sustained by a commercial, controlling director who can turn on a storm, and the question of whether an idealism with a malevolent or merely unreliable perceiver becomes harder to inhabit. Finally, as an honest critic, specify where Seahaven is not a Berkeleyan world at all, since it is a physical set that exists whether or not Truman perceives it, and say what the film nonetheless gets right about the pull of the idealist thought.

Conclude with a judgement about whether the film vindicates, complicates, or quietly refutes the master argument, and what that tells a general audience about why believing in a mind-independent world is stranger than common sense pretends.
