I am giving the graduation address at a school of engineering whose undergraduate program is unusually intense and technically excellent, but whose faculty have spent two years arguing about whether its graduates learn enough about the ends to which technical competence should be put. I have worked for much of my career on Whitehead's philosophy of education, and I have been asked to speak, in part, as that person. The audience will be roughly half engineers and half family members with no background in any of this. Please write the speech.

Build it on Whitehead's account of education as a rhythm that moves from a stage of romance, in which a subject is first apprehended with excitement and as a whole; through a stage of precision, in which detail, technique, and discipline are acquired; to a stage of generalization, in which technique is placed back in the service of larger purposes and the learner sees the subject whole again, now with power. Present this rhythm as cyclical rather than as a ladder, so that graduation is not a departure from the rhythm but a new entry into it. The speech should also carry Whitehead's warning against what he called inert ideas: the acquisition of beliefs and techniques that are stored but never exercised, examined, or put to fresh use.

The constraint I care about most is this: the speech must not treat the precision stage as a regrettable interlude on the way to something more humane. These graduates have achieved something real in precision, and the speech should honor it; the danger is not precision itself but coming to rest in it, or entering it at the wrong time for the wrong reasons. Do not name Whitehead, cite his writings, or deploy his terminology as jargon; the structure and the severity of the thought should be felt rather than announced. It should land with the engineers without insulting or merely flattering their training, and with their families without becoming a lecture. Aim for about eight minutes when spoken.
