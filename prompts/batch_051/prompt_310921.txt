Consider the following claim, which I will call the Instability Claim: if a rational agent who is deliberating in good faith between two options A and B assigns a precise credence c (0 < c < 1) to the proposition that she will choose A, then c cannot simultaneously be (i) a genuine prediction of her eventual choice and (ii) a stable credence she can retain while that same deliberation continues. Where it seems to be both, it is instead doing something else — registering how far her mind is already made up, or acting as a self-fulfilling commitment — rather than tracking a fixed target.

Give me your actual credence in the Instability Claim as a single number, and show your work. Decompose the credence into the sub-questions that are doing the main work: for example, whether an agent can adopt a detached, third-person standpoint on her own future acts without ending the first-person standpoint of deliberation; whether a self-ascribed probability over an act is best read as an intention with a number attached; and whether self-stabilizing predictions — habitual choices, public forecasts, precommitment through self-image — genuinely bypass the instability or merely postpone it. Assign each sub-question a probability, explain how you combined them into the overall number, and identify the sub-question whose resolution would move your credence the most, along with the kind of argument or evidence that would move it.
