I am a novelist writing a book about political polarization and the way public arguments fail, and I want the novel to be fair to two opposed views of what is going on when the other side argues. Please draft the joint statement that would emerge from an adversarial collaboration between the following two camps.

The insincerity camp holds that a large share of outgroup political speech is genuinely bad faith: the arguments are mostly post hoc, performed for the speaker's own side, and would not survive conditions the speaker would themselves consider fair. The divergence camp holds that the other side is overwhelmingly sincere: what looks like bad faith is mostly motivated reasoning plus different priors, different values, and different networks of trusted sources — echo chambers operating on trust rather than merely on missing information — so that people honestly reason to different conclusions from different inputs.

I want the document such a collaboration would actually produce, not a third-party verdict on which camp is right. It should include:

1. a numbered list of claims both camps can sign, covering what people observably do in public argument, what is already established versus merely assumed, and what kinds of behavior would count as decisive evidence of bad faith if found;
2. a disagreements table stating each residual disagreement in wording each side would accept for its own position, without caricature;
3. the cruxes, by which I mean the sub-questions whose settlement one way or the other would force at least one camp to move. I suspect the cruxes include whether private, low-stakes, or anonymous expression differs from public expression; whether people revise under conditions that remove social and reputational costs; whether speakers can produce the strongest version of the opposing case on request; and whether stated confidence tracks private uncertainty. Include any other crux that belongs;
4. for each crux, a concrete piece of evidence or study design that both camps would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance;
5. a note on which parts of the dispute are, or may be, merely verbal rather than substantive.

Where the two camps rely on different background theories of reasoning — for instance, over whether explicit reasons cause belief or merely license it afterward — state that in terms both would sign. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this material, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
