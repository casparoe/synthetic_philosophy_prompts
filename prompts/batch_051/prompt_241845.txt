I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I want to turn a recurring argument among AI researchers into a quantified judgment. Consider a concrete version. In 2030 the three leading AI labs and two governments sign a post-AGI stability accord: each party accepts limits on military applications now, and in return each commits that if it later develops artificial general intelligence, it will submit the first deployment decision to a jointly controlled audit-and-veto panel before any irreversible deployment. The accord has no binding enforcement; compliance rests on continued interest, reputation, and whatever informal leverage the parties retain.

Assume one lab develops AGI in 2037, and its own internal analysis concludes that the panel would block the deployment the lab regards as most valuable. What is your credence that the lab nevertheless submits to the panel and refrains, rather than deploying unilaterally? I want the number to be built out of the reasoning. Please decompose the estimate into the sub-questions that do the work: the probability that pre-AGI agreements survive a decisive capability shift, the probability that the lab's leadership preserves institutional commitments made seven years earlier across that shift, the probability that the other parties can impose meaningful costs on a lab that has already defected, and whatever else you think belongs. Assign a rough probability to each with a one-line justification, explain how they combine, and identify the component your final number is most sensitive to. Then tell me what evidence available before 2030 would most move that number.
