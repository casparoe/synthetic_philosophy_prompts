In my aesthetics course on cultural appropriation, we keep running up against an asymmetry that our authors mention but never fully defend. Suppose two visual artists each take, without permission and for commercial gain, a distinctive formal vocabulary from a tradition in which they were not trained. When the borrower comes from a dominant group and the source tradition belongs to a marginalized group, the work is standardly criticized as cultural appropriation. When the direction is reversed, the same conduct is much more likely to be described as reclamation, critique, or ordinary influence. I want a rigorous treatment of that asymmetry rather than a survey. First, give the strongest available justification for treating the two directions differently, and be explicit about whether it locates the wrong in the borrowing itself or only in background facts about power and standing. Then test that justification against a matched pair of cases in which everything else is held as fixed as possible: same commercial stakes, same degree of departure from the source, same absence of consent, and public objection from the affected group or its representatives in both cases. Does a principle emerge that both underwrites the asymmetry and survives the matched pair? If it does, state it precisely and say how much of ordinary appropriation discourse it actually vindicates. If it does not, say what principle should govern these cases instead, and take a stand on whether ordinary practice should keep, abandon, or reverse the asymmetry.
