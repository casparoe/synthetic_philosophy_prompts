Two colleagues at my institute got into an argument at lunch about Hilbert's hotel, and I'd like you to adjudicate it move by move.

(1) One of them, a set theorist by training, said the hotel story is a clean reductio of actual infinity: if a fully occupied hotel can accommodate a new guest without adding rooms, that violates the principle that a whole is greater than any of its proper parts, so the only way to make sense of the story is to reject the premise that there is a completed infinite hotel. The other replied that this just begs the question by importing a principle that is true of finite wholes but not of infinite ones; the whole point of the story is to show that the principle does not transfer.

(2) The first replied: But the story is deliberately concrete—rooms, keys, occupants—so if you have to say the principle does not transfer, you have conceded that the phrase "fully occupied hotel" has no concrete meaning in the infinite case; you are no longer talking about a hotel but about a formal abstraction, which is exactly the concession the finitist wants. The second said: That is too fast. Every mathematical structure can be illustrated with concrete props without inheriting their finite constraints; a sphere in geometry does not become false because no physical sphere is perfect. The consistency of the structure is unaffected by the props.

(3) The first said: The consistency question is separate. The real issue is that the story packs in two different things: a rule that for any room n there is a room n+1, and the claim that all rooms can be surveyed at once so that the simultaneous shift is defined. A finitist can accept the rule and reject the completed totality. Hilbert's hotel is precisely where the completed totality does the work, because the move "everyone shifts" is only well-defined if there is a whole collection to shift. The second replied: But the shift is just the function f(n)=n+1 on the natural numbers. You can grant that f is defined for every natural number without first grasping the naturals as a completed object; the function is given by a rule, which is exactly the finitist's own notion. So the hotel does not show that the completed totality is doing any work.

(4) The first answered: The difference is visible in the phrase "fully occupied." A rule for generating rooms gives you no state in which every room is occupied; it only gives you any finite stage. Hilbert's hotel requires the state in which the series is exhausted, because the paradox depends on there being no last room and yet all rooms occupied. If you read "fully occupied" as "every room that exists is occupied," and rooms exist only as generated, then there is no such total state, and the new guest cannot be accommodated because there is no completed hotel. The second came back: That presupposes that "exists" only means "exists at some finite stage," which is the finitist thesis, not a neutral description. If you allow quantification over all natural numbers—as even classical finitism does in its metatheory—then the total state is just the set of all rooms, and the story is coherent.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four exchanges from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating, then say who is right about which specific point and what, if anything, both are missing. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
