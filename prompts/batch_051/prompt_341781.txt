Having come to philosophy late, after a career in the natural sciences, I have been reading the literature on computer-assisted proof, and I would like to work through whether one of its foundational objections survives a later development. In 1979 Thomas Tymoczko argued that the Appel–Haken proof of the four-colour theorem changed the epistemological character of mathematics. His objection was not merely that a computer had been used; it was that the proof requires checking a large catalogue of reducible configurations by machine, so that no individual mathematician can survey the reasoning in the way the traditional conception of proof seems to require. On his account, accepting the theorem came to rest on empirical trust in hardware and software, and mathematical justification acquired something like an experimental character.

Since then the situation has shifted in a way he could not have anticipated. The four-colour theorem was given a fully formal, machine-checked proof in the Coq proof assistant (Gonthier and collaborators, 2005), and the Kepler conjecture was later verified in the same spirit by the Flyspeck project (2014). In these formalizations, the combinatorial case analysis, the relevant correctness claims about the computational procedure, and the logical derivation are represented as formal objects and checked by a comparatively small inference kernel. There is now a sense in which the proof has been surveyed — not by a human reading each case, but by a formal system that reduces every step to explicit rules of inference.

My question is whether Tymoczko's objection survives this development. Does full formalization answer the original argument, or does it merely relocate the empirical trust — to the compiler, the hardware, the translation from the informal theorem to its formal statement, or the designers of the system? More precisely: which premise of the 1979 argument does machine-checked formalization actually bear on, and is there a repaired version of the objection that still captures something important about mathematical knowledge — perhaps that the relevant epistemic relation is no longer the surveyability of a single mind but structured trust in a layered formal system?

I am interested in the fate of the philosophical argument itself, not in a historical narrative or an assessment of the two theorems. I would like an analysis that identifies the premise under pressure, says whether the objection is dead, intact, or in need of repair, and states what conception of proof best reflects the situation formalization has produced.
