Question: Political philosophers often criticize free riding—benefiting from a cooperative scheme while contributing nothing to it. What, if anything, is fundamentally wrong with free riding? Address the objection that an individual free rider usually makes no detectable difference to the supply of the collective good. Is free riding best understood as unfairness, as exploitation, or as a distinctive form of social parasitism?

Student's answer:
The core wrong in free riding is unfairness. A free rider enjoys a benefit that others maintain through costly contributions, and in doing so he claims a privilege for himself: he gets what everyone else must pay for. This remains wrong even when his own contribution would make no detectable difference to the supply of the good, because unfairness is not a matter of causal impact on the final outcome but of the terms on which people stand to one another. A free rider stands to the cooperators as a recipient of their effort while offering nothing in return. A second, allegedly distinct diagnosis is exploitation: the free rider uses the cooperators simply as instruments of his own benefit. But this adds nothing beyond the fairness complaint, because exploitation just is an arrangement in which one party contributes less than they receive. The two diagnoses can therefore be reduced to a single principle: one should never accept a benefit unless one has directly contributed to its production. Since this same principle also covers social parasitism, free riding and social parasitism are simply the same phenomenon.
