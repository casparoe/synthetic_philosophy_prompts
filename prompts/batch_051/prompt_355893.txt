I have a standing rule about evolutionary psychology: I will grant no credence to an adaptationist explanation of present-day behavior unless someone has already derived from it at least one risky, non-obvious prediction that has been confirmed. Until then I file the account as a just-so story, an after-the-fact narrative with roughly the standing of a clever fable. The rule came into play recently in a reading group when a member argued that the nausea and food aversions of early pregnancy are an adaptation protecting the embryo during the weeks of organ formation, by steering the mother away from plant compounds that can disrupt development. I said the story fits the facts, but so would a dozen rivals, and I would not take it seriously until it passed a test that could have refuted it.

A friend in the group objected that this holds a historical science to an experimentalist's standard. Evolutionary biology, she said, often cannot run forward-looking tests on behavior or the deep past; like geology, it leans on consilience — independent lines of evidence, such as comparative data across species, the fossil and archaeological record, engineering or design analysis, and developmental constraints — converging on one account. My rule, she said, would leave me unable to accept plate tectonics or the giant-impact account of the Moon's origin, neither of which earned belief through a single daring forecast.

I replied that I am not demanding an experiment, only a registered implication that could have been wrong and turned out to be right; an account flexible enough to fit whatever we might have found cannot be confirmed by anything we did find. I added that I have a particular reason to distrust consilience from outside a field: people are good at making several weak threads look like a braided rope, and I cannot readily tell genuine convergence from story-shaped pattern-matching. Is my rule a defensible way of rationing belief, or is it a bias against adaptationist explanation dressed in methodological language? Evaluate it as a policy: say when it is reliable and when it fails, whether it can be restated to keep its protection against ad hoc stories while giving historical inference its due, and whether an outsider in my position is entitled to insist on risky forecasts before believing.
