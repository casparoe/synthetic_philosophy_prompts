I keep running into the phrase 'escalation of commitment' in arguments about wars, failing startups, and people who won't walk away from a doomed relationship, and I've realized I dont actually know what it picks out. The standard teaching example is the dollar auction: a dollar bill is auctioned off, except the highest bidder wins the dollar and the second-highest bidder still has to pay their last bid and gets nothing. Once two people have bid, each raise becomes a choice between losing a growing amount for sure and paying a bit more for a chance to avoid that loss, so the bids climb past a dollar. Everyone describes that as escalation of commitment. But I want a working definition that does more than point at wasted money or stubbornness.

Here are the cases I'd like the definition to handle:

- A construction project years late and wildly over budget; management keeps funding it because they've already sunk so much money in that cancelling feels like admitting the money was wasted.
- A general who privately thinks the war is unwinnable but sends more troops anyway, partly because he can't stand the thought of the earlier deaths having been for nothing.
- The dollar auction described above, with both players bidding past the dollar.
- A poker player who calls a big bet on a drawing hand because the pot is now so large that the call has positive expected value.
- Someone who keeps reading a book they hate because they're three hundred pages in.
- A bidder in an ordinary eBay-style auction who shoots past their planned maximum in the last seconds because they just refuse to lose to another bidder, even though the loser pays nothing.
- A team that abandons a failing product line as soon as new data shows it won't recover, even though they'd spent heavily on it.

I'm fairly confident the construction project and the general are in, and that the last one is out; I genuinely don't know about the dollar auction and the eBay-style bid. I have a hunch that the phrase is doing two jobs. One is about sunk costs and identity — the general and the construction project. The other is about how each individual step can look rational inside a game even when entering the game was the real mistake — the dollar auction, and maybe the poker pot. If that's right, give me either one definition that covers both jobs, or two definitions and a short note on why they share a name. If some of my cases resist a clean definition, say which one your proposed definition gets wrong or has no clear verdict on, and tell me which of your verdicts is the least secure. Also, please flag what is settled about this topic versus what is genuinely contested among people who study it, and don't present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
