Two members of my ethics reading group, Elena and Daniel, got into a long argument about whether the situationist critique of virtue ethics succeeds. I was present, and afterward the rest of the group could not agree about who had the better of it. Neither side struck me as strawmanned; both kept refining their claims rather than repeating slogans. What follows is the exchange as I reconstruct it, tightened up but faithful. Take the experimental findings as described as accurate for the sake of argument.

(1) Elena: The classic situationist experiments are close to decisive against virtue ethics. In obedience studies, most subjects comply with pressure to do what they themselves would call wrong; in helping studies, variables like being in a hurry or just having found a coin in a phone booth dramatically change whether someone stops to help a stranger. Behavior makes sense as a response to features of the situation, not as the expression of stable traits. If a person's helping on Tuesday doesn't predict his helping on Friday, then "compassion" isn't doing the explanatory work virtue ethics assigns to it.

(2) Daniel: That's too strong. Virtue ethics, at least in its more careful versions, does not claim that ordinary people already possess full virtues. A virtue is an achieved and unusual state, cultivated by practice, somewhat like a practical skill. The fact that untrained or hurried subjects are swayed by circumstances is exactly what the view should predict: they do not have the relevant virtue in the first place. Showing that most people are not virtuous is not a counterexample to a theory about what virtue is and what it would require of someone who had it.

(3) Elena: But if ordinary people don't instantiate the traits the theory is supposed to explain, then as a psychology the theory explains nothing about the moral life we actually live. And the fallback is not innocent: if we cannot point to anyone who has the virtues, how do we identify the virtuous agent whose characteristic response is supposed to define right action? The theory becomes a circle — we would need an independent grip on right action to pick out the virtuous agent, and an independent grip on the virtuous agent to know what is right.

(4) Daniel: The circularity charge is fair but conceptual, not empirical, and it can be met — by appealing to paradigm cases of virtue and to the rich evaluative language we use before we theorize, or by saying the virtuous agent reliably tracks what a full appreciation of the goods in question would endorse. Returning to the empirical point: the virtue ethicist does not need to insist on global, cross-situational traits of the sort the experiments target. She can allow that virtue is built from local, situation-relative habits — being reliably honest with friends before being reliably honest in business — while still claiming that such habits can be broadened through reflection and practice.

(5) Elena: That "local traits" move hands the situationist the main point. The defining claim of virtue ethics, understood as a view about character, is that there are relatively stable traits — honesty, courage, compassion — that manifest across the ordinary range of situations. Once you downgrade to local, domain-specific habits, you've adopted the situationist picture and merely decorated it with virtue vocabulary. And you still have no account of the obedience and helping results that predicts or explains them better than situationism does.

(6) Daniel: That demand mislocates the disagreement. Virtue ethics is not primarily a predictive social psychology; it's a normative and developmental proposal about what to cultivate and how. But it is not empirically empty either, because habituation appears to work: people can become more dependable through practice, correction, and reflection, even if the process is slow and partial. The honest conclusion from these experiments is not that character is a myth but that virtue is rare and fragile. The tradition always knew that: the hard question has always been how a person is formed, not whether ordinary untutored people are reliably virtuous.
