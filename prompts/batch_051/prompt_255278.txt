You are a political philosopher of discrimination law and egalitarian theory. I am a graduate student preparing a term paper of roughly 6,000 words, to be written in one semester, on what makes a ground of discrimination suspect — or, in a different legal idiom, what makes a characteristic a protected ground. I have a half-formed worry I want the proposal to develop rather than set aside. The familiar tests for suspectness — that the trait be immutable or costly to change, highly visible, irrelevant to capacities, and associated with a history of discrimination and political powerlessness — seem to me to be doing two different jobs. Some of them look political: they identify groups that have been targeted by majorities and cannot protect themselves through the ordinary political process. Others look merit-theoretic: they identify traits it is simply unfair to use as a basis for distributing burdens and benefits. These two jobs can come apart, and I am not sure the standard account has a principled way of saying which wins when they do. Please write the proposal around that tension. State the research question precisely and so that it has a determinate answer; explain why the obvious unifying moves (reducing suspectness to one job or treating the tests as a mere list) leave something out; defend an approach that would actually decide between them; specify the possible conclusions, the considerations that would tell in favor of each, and what would count as failure; and give an honest assessment of scope. The proposal may refer to positions and arguments, but do not include a bibliography or citations — I will insert those myself.

Structure the response in exactly these sections, in this order, with these approximate lengths:

- The question (about 200 words): a precise statement of the research question, including the two-jobs distinction and an operational definition of suspectness.
- Why it is open (about 250 words): characterize the main competing positions by their arguments rather than by citation, and identify what each fails to settle.
- Approach (about 250 words): the method — I am inclined toward pairwise cases in which the political and the merit-theoretic criteria conflict — and an argument for why this is better than defending one master criterion or doing a survey of doctrine.
- Possible conclusions (about 200 words): the main ways the paper could come out, the considerations that would decide between them, and what would count as failure of the approach.
- Scope and risks (about 150 words): what should be excluded, and what would make the project too large for one semester.

Before drafting, brainstorm at least three candidate framings of the question and choose the one that is both genuinely open and tractable in the space available; then write only the chosen proposal in the sections above.
