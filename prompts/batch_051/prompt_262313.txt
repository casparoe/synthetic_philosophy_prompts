In a seminar on the ethics of markets, we have been working through the old dispute between the view that commercial life corrupts character and the view that it disciplines and civilizes it. One classical version of the civilizing view—sometimes called the doux commerce thesis—runs roughly as follows. The market is not merely a device for exchanging goods; it is also, in its daily operation, a school of restraint. A merchant who cheats, overstates, or indulges his temper loses customers, and the news of that loss travels to others who might have dealt with him. The desire for profit therefore trains him in honesty, promise-keeping, punctuality, and a kind of tolerance for those with whom he must repeatedly transact, even in the absence of affection, kinship, or shared belief. Commerce, on this account, does not presuppose virtue so much as it manufactures a serviceable substitute for it.

Now suppose the transactions through which commerce is actually conducted have largely changed shape. Buyers and sellers meet on platforms that keep them anonymous to one another; most exchanges are effectively one-shot, with no expectation that the same two parties will ever deal again; and the relevant news of bad conduct is no longer a personal reputation within a community but an aggregated score compiled by an algorithm from ratings, returns, and complaints. Does the doux commerce thesis survive this change? If it does, explain how the character-disciplining mechanism can still operate once the repeated, face-to-face exchange mechanism has been removed. If it does not, say whether the thesis should be abandoned outright or only restated, and, if restated, identify the new mechanism that is supposed to do the civilizing work and the conditions under which the platform economy would actually satisfy it.
