Nuclear-armed states devote enormous resources to maintaining second-strike forces — missile submarines kept at sea, mobile missile launchers, hardened silos — whose entire purpose is to survive an enemy first strike and be able to retaliate after absorbing it. The credibility of this retaliatory capacity is treated as the foundation of deterrence. Yet at the moment the retaliation would actually be ordered, the state's territory and much of its population may already have been destroyed; retaliating cannot undo the attack, cannot deter the attack that has already occurred, and may draw further strikes on whatever survives. The practice therefore seems to require a state to bind itself in advance to an act that, at the moment of performance, serves no forward-looking interest of the state except fidelity to its earlier threat. What view of rational action, commitment, or agency does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Then say what, if anything, would have to change in a nuclear posture if the presupposed view were replaced with a purely case-by-case account of rational choice, on which only the expected consequences of the act itself, assessed at the moment of action, count as reasons. Give a committed answer: take a definite position on which underlying view the practice best supports and defend it, rather than surveying the alternatives. Lead with your conclusion in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
