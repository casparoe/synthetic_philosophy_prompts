I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm several drafts into an essay about the grammar wars — the recurring fights over whether forms such as singular they, literally as an intensifier, or between you and I are signs of linguistic decline or just of change. Before I commit my own position to print, I'd like it stress-tested. I hold all of the following, or at least I think I do:

1. Grammar, as a scientific matter, is the system of rules native speakers actually possess. A sentence can be called ungrammatical only relative to a specified variety. There is no variety-independent sense in which an ordinary utterance by a competent native speaker is, in itself, "bad grammar."

2. Nonstandard varieties are not failed attempts at the standard. African American English habitual be marks aspect, negative concord is a rule-governed pattern rather than a failure of logic, and calling such varieties "bad English" is a social judgment doing the work of a linguistic one.

3. Edited Standard Written English is nonetheless a legitimate institution. In the copy I edit, I enforce its conventions — I change between you and I, dangling participles, subject-verb discord — and I don't regard that practice as linguistic hypocrisy, because a shared written standard reduces readers' processing costs and signals register.

4. Language change is not language decay. I accept singular they, sentence-adverbial hopefully, and literally as an intensifier, and I take most complaints about them to be status anxiety dressed up as logic.

5. But I don't treat all nonstandard forms alike. I judge irregardless, supposably, should of, and I could care less to be errors in a stronger sense than ain't or y'all: they strike me not merely as different but as worse — unanalyzed, meaning-inverting, or built on a failure to parse the construction they come from.

6. I would like there to be a principled line between harmless variation and genuine error, with my list in (5) tracking it rather than merely reflecting my own dialect's aesthetics.

Before I publish, I'd like an audit of this set. Which of these commitments actually pull against each other, which only seem to, and what is the least costly repair? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equal, say for each what would have to give and what it would cost, and end by identifying the single revision that would clean up the most. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
