I have been trying to get my views on secession into a single coherent position. Each commitment seems right to me on its own, but I am not confident they all fit together, and I would rather have the conflicts identified plainly than be told what to believe wholesale. My commitments are:

(a) Self-determination is at bottom about democratic choice, not ethnicity, language, or history. If a territorially concentrated population repeatedly expresses a desire to leave, that expression of will is what matters.

(b) A legitimate independence referendum needs to be orderly: a clear question, a fair campaign, high turnout, and a supermajority threshold of roughly sixty per cent, so that a bare majority or a low turnout cannot dissolve a state.

(c) Scotland's 2014 referendum was legitimate because it was held with the agreement of the UK government. I also think Scotland should have been able to hold a second referendum without Westminster's consent if a stable pro-independence majority kept asking for one, and I found the Supreme Court's ruling that consent was required unconvincing.

(d) Quebec should be free to decide its future, but the closeness of the 1995 result shows why the question and the margin need to be clear. Once a clear majority has voted No, the matter should be treated as settled for roughly a generation, even if opinion shifts in the meantime.

(e) Catalonia's 2017 referendum was illegitimate, and Spain was entitled not to treat it as grounds for independence, because it was held without Spain's agreement, under flawed rules, and on too low a turnout to express a clear popular mandate.

Are these five commitments jointly tenable? Which pairs are in genuine tension, which merely appear to be, and what is the least costly revision that would restore consistency? Please order the tensions by how serious they are, and say what would have to give in each case.
