Been going back and forth with someone about Whig history, and I've ended up with an argument that seems to prove to much—it looks like it shows the whole anti-Whig project is incoherent. It runs like this:

(1) You can't narrate everything, so every historian has to select what goes into the story.
(2) Any selection criterion—"this mattered," "this was significant"—is applied from the historian's own standpoint, which is located in the present.
(3) An appeal to the past's own categories doesn't escape, because deciding which of the many past categories to privilege is itself a present-day choice.
(4) So every historical account is organised by present interests and present standards of importance.
(5) But that just is the Whig move: you arrange the past around the concerns of the present, especially as a lead-up to it.
(6) Therefore every history is Whig history.
(7) So the standard anti-Whig advice—present the past on its own terms rather than as a runway toward the present—is an impossible demand.

I don't want to accept the conclusion, but I honestly can't see the flaw. Which premise should I give up, and what does rejecting it committ me to? I'd like to keep (1) and (2) if at all possible, since they look airtight to me. And if the argument fails, does that mean a genuinely non-Whig history is available, or only that Whiggishness is a matter of degree and the original criticism still bites? Please answer in British English.
