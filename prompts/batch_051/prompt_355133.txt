As a novelist, I have spent the past year in archives doing research, and I keep noticing a pattern in how institutions treat what the librarians call orphan works: out-of-print books, unpublished letters, photographs, and films whose rights holders cannot be identified or found, typically because the creator is dead and no heirs or publishers can be traced. The same logic seems to govern abandoned software, where an old program whose author has vanished is left to decay even though making copies would preserve it for users. My ordinary assumption would be that property whose owner has disappeared and which no one claims becomes available: lost and abandoned physical objects pass to finders or to the public, and the law generally has settled ways to handle that. But these institutions act as if the work is at once owned and untouchable. They will spend months hunting for an heir who, if found, would very likely never have seen the manuscript; they will digitize a novel to preserve it but restrict the digital file to a single terminal in one reading room; they will pay to restore a stray film but not screen it; they will catalogue letters in detail while refusing to reproduce a single line. The practical effect is that a text still under copyright is less available to a reader than a dropped scarf is to someone who picks it up.

What theory of ownership, or of the value created by authorship, does this practice presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose that an intellectual work remains owned even when no one can or does exercise the ownership? That a right is best understood not as control over a thing but as a relation to the creator that persists after death? That the possibility of a reappearing owner simply dominates every other consideration? Or is the practice underdetermined by the available theories? Is it coherent, or does it secretly assume both that the work belongs to no one and that everyone must treat it as belonging to someone? And if an institution rejected that assumption in favor of something like the idea that rights lapse when they cannot be exercised, what exactly would its policies have to change?
