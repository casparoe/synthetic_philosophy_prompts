I work in a municipal planning office, and much of my job consists in deciding which climate projections to take seriously when we invest in flood-control infrastructure. Over the years I have settled into a rule of thumb, and I want to know whether it is defensible or merely epistemic fastidiousness dressed up as rigor. When the models disagree about a quantity we care about—say, the change in extreme rainfall over our region—I do not treat the multi-model mean as automatically credible. Instead, I sort the models by structure: if several models agree but share a major atmospheric component, a convection parameterization, or the same tuning targets, I count them as roughly one piece of evidence rather than several. I grant a projection the status of decision-relevant only when it appears across what I judge to be genuinely independent model lineages—different institutions, different parameterizations, different tuning datasets—or when a single model whose process representation I have specific reason to trust shows the result. A result that appears only in a cluster of closely related models gets filed as unresolved and does not shape spending. I apply a similar rule to the spread: I am less worried about a projection that the ensemble brackets through its disagreement than about one the models do not enclose.

Is this a defensible policy? Under what assumptions about how model errors correlate is it reliable, and under what assumptions does it mislead? Can it be restated so as to keep the epistemic benefits without discouraging us from acting on provisional information? Please also say which empirical or conceptual finding would most change your verdict, and where you think your own assessment is most likely to fail.
