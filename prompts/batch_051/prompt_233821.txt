I have been preparing a course on seventeenth- and eighteenth-century epistemology, and at the same time working through some of the machine learning literature on generalisation. A parallel between the two fields has been nagging at me, and I would be glad of an independent assessment before I inflict it on my students.

The parallel is this. In Leibniz's rationalist formulation, and in Kant's later vocabulary of a priori forms and categories, sensory experience by itself radically underdetermines what the mind comes to know; some antecedent structure or disposition must already be present in the learner before any particular course of experience, and it is this prior endowment that allows experience to be organised into knowledge. Against this, the empiricist maintains that the mind begins unformed and that all cognitive content enters from without. This looks to me remarkably like a modern learning-theoretic claim: a learner with no inductive bias whatsoever, with no restricted hypothesis class, no prior over functions, and no built-in preference for simplicity, smoothness, or compositionality, cannot generalise from finite data, because any finite body of data is compatible with indefinitely many extrapolations. The no-free-lunch-style results are often glossed as the claim that there is no learning without assumptions, which sounds like the rationalist's anti-empiricist point rewritten in computational vocabulary. The mapping would run: inductive bias is to a learning algorithm what innate structure is to a mind; training data are what sensory experience is; and the fitted model that generalises is what the resulting empirical knowledge is.

I can see reasons to distrust it. An inductive bias need not be something the learner believes, asserts, or represents as a proposition; it is typically injected by a designer rather than possessed by the learner; and the no-free-lunch results are worst-case claims over all possible environments, whereas a mind operating in a structured or benign world might need far less built-in structure. A Lockean can also fairly point out that Locke denied only innate principles and ideas, not innate faculties and cognitive machinery, so a modest empiricism might accept antecedent mechanisms while denying antecedent content. I would like you to state the mapping precisely, say what would have to be true on each side for results to transfer, and tell me whether this is a deep structural identity, a loose analogy, or a confusion. Please end your answer with a final line beginning with 'Verdict:' followed by one of 'deep', 'superficial', or 'mixed', and then a single sentence stating your bottom line.
