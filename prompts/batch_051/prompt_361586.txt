I run a monthly discussion group whose members have spent years reading LessWrong and similar blogs; they are mostly programmers and academics with no neuroscience beyond the usual pop-science stories about split brains. Next session is on split-brain patients and the unity of consciousness, and I want to stay away from the familiar two-selves-in-one-skull treatment. I am not asking you to explain the findings to me; I am asking you to plan the session. The group will likely arrive knowing that severing the corpus callosum interrupts information flow between the hemispheres and that the left hemisphere does most of the speaking. What I want them to leave with is a grip on why the experiments really do not settle whether there is one subject of experience or two, and why the substantive question is what would have to be true about unity, and about evidence, before the question even has a determinate answer. My own suspicion, which should be put to them as a possibility rather than a conclusion, is that unity is a graded feature of integration rather than an all-or-nothing metaphysical given. Please structure the answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. The five items should be: (1) the conceptual distinctions that must be in place first and why the question is ill-posed without them — in particular, phenomenal unity, access or functional integration, and subjecthood; (2) the order in which the classic findings should be introduced — the divided visual field, the object-choice and drawing tasks, and the left hemisphere's verbal confabulations — and why each step has to come where it does; (3) the single concrete example or scenario that should carry the session, and the features it must have and lack if it is to do that work; (4) a misconception map — the mistaken readings to expect, including the move from a speechless right hemisphere to a second full self and the move from felt unity to established unity — and the exact point in the sequence at which to defuse each; (5) a closing question or task that would tell me whether they understand why the data underdetermine the number of subjects, plus what should be explicitly left open or refused at this level.
