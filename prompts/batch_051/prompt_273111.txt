I'm an engineer on a payments team at a mid-size company, and I keep going back and forth on something from last month. We have this near-absolute rule—written in the handbook, quarterly retraining, the works—that nobody opens a user's raw messages or private data without explicit opt-in. Last month a logging change started copying raw user messages into internal metrics dashboards. I caught it at 2am. By the time I did, five or six other teams could see raw messages in their dashboards, and the dashboards keep polling, so they'd keep showing new rows until someone fixed the pipeline.

To fix it properly I had to open one user's message, without opting them in, to see the shape of the leaked rows and validate a cleanup job against it. It would have taken maybe two minutes, nobody else would have seen that one message, and then I could have pushed a fix that cut off everyone elses access and scrubbed the bad data. My manager said no: the rule is absolute, we don't access user data without consent even when we're doing it to stop other unauthorized access; we file a security ticket and wait for the privacy review. I filed the ticket, the leak kept going for almost another day, and several people definitely saw messages they shouldn't have.

I cant shake the feeling that this has the reasoning backwards. If the point of the rule is that a user's message being read without consent is bad, then refusing to do one small unauthorized read in order to prevent dozens of the same kind of exposure seems like it guarantees more of the exact harm the rule exists to prevent. My manager would say those other exposures aren't his, but the one I wanted to do would be. Is there a real difference there, or is that just a way to keep your hands clean? Which of us is right?
