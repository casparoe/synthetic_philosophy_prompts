I serve on the Governance Review Board of a mid-sized university that is drafting a new constitution for its Faculty Senate. A proposed amendment would give every full-time faculty member one vote in Senate elections, but would grant a second vote to each faculty member whose terminal degree was earned in a doctoral program ranked in the top twenty of their discipline. The amendment's authors cite John Stuart Mill's argument in Considerations on Representative Government that a scheme of strictly equal votes lets the less educated outvote the more educated merely because they are more numerous, and that public institutions should instead give additional weight to voters who have demonstrated superior aptitude for judging the matters before them, much as Mill proposed extra votes for university graduates and members of the learned professions. Critics on the Board respond that the proposal would entrench a status hierarchy rather than track competence, and that Senate decisions are about collective self-government rather than technical adjudication. The Board is split. Draft the decision memo — recommend approval, rejection, or approval with conditions — including an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side and reasons the proposal's supporters could actually answer.
