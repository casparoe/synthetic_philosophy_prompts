As a novelist i spend a lot of time building people who never existed and then getting readers—and myself—to treat their motives, memories and relationships as real for the space of the book. Lately I’ve been reading about legal fictions, and I’m struck by how much stranger the legal versions are. A corporation is a person for purposes of owning property, signing contracts, and being sued, but it can’t testify, marry, or go to prison. In some jurisdictions, if someone disappears and is eventually declared dead, transactions made by heirs can remain valid even if the person turns up alive years later, while other parts of the estate are unwound. Adoption severs one legal parent-child relationship and creates another as though the child had been born into the adoptive family; a birth certificate may be replaced rather than annotated. I’m not looking for a history or a list of doctrines. I want to know what this cluster of practices presupposes about what it is to be a person, to exist, to be the same individual over time, and for a statement to be true within an institution. Does the law actually create a distinct kind of entity or a new fact, or is a legal fiction just a shared pretense with coercive power? Are these fictions metaphysically innocent, or do they smuggle in commitments that lawyers don’t want to defend? And if we stopped calling them fictions and talked instead about legal models or legal metaphors, would anything beyond the vocabulary have to change?
