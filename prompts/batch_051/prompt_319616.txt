I retired from a career in the natural sciences a few years ago and have recently been working through the philosophy of mind. The objection I keep coming back to is usually credited to Ada Lovelace: in essence, that a computing machine can do only what it has been told to do, so any appearance of originality really belongs to whoever supplied the instructions. That objection presumes a machine whose competence resides in an explicit plan. A contemporary language model's competence resides instead in billions of learned weights that no one wrote, and giving it a few examples of an unfamiliar task can change its downstream behavior without altering a single one of those weights. I am not asking you to settle whether such systems genuinely achieve novelty or intuitive leaps beyond their training distribution. I am asking what has become of the Lovelace-style objection: does it still go through against learned systems, does it quietly depend on the premise of an explicit plan and therefore need repair, or can it be reformulated so that it applies to learned systems with the same force the original had against instruction-following machines?
