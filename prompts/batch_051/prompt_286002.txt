I've been a lurker on LessWrong and similar forums for years, and I keep reaching for a small piece of homemade epistemology whenever a post stacks heuristic arguments, so I want it stress-tested before I keep using it. I call it the independence tax.

Setup: a heuristic argument for a conclusion C is anything that moves me in log-odds but whose premises are themselves guesses, loose analogies, "this held before", that kind of thing. If I have two such arguments, A and B, with lifts l_A and l_B, the usual forum move is to add the lifts, treating the arguments as independent. My claim is that this is almost always overcounting. I introduce a number r in [0,1]: the correlation between the two arguments' failure indicators—intuitively, roughly how much more likely B is to be wrong in worlds where A is wrong. Then I use

combined lift = l_A + l_B − r·min(l_A, l_B)

At r = 0 you get ordinary independence; at r = 1 you get only the stronger argument; in between you interpolate. Concrete example: an argument that takes me from 25% to 50% has an odds-ratio lift of ln 3. Two fully independent arguments like that would take me to 75%. But if they share an assumption like "the near future will keep the same scaling trend", I'd set r around 0.4 and land near 66%.

The part I actually want checked is the default. I think the presumption of independence should be reversed: for informal heuristic arguments you should default r to something in the 0.4 to 0.6 range, not to zero, unless you can specify in a sentence what would make argument A fail while argument B still stands and vice versa. If you can't write down distinct failure modes, you havn't earned independence, and stacking arguments does less work than it looks like. Feels like the kind of thing that's either a real result I've half-remembered or complete nonsense.

I know it's adjacent to the Condorcet jury theorem and to the forecasting rule about not double-counting correlated evidence, but I want to know whether it's already a named thing, whether the r formula is even coherent, and which parts are just wrong.
