I am a restorative-justice practitioner in Norway, and every year around the anniversary of the July 22 attacks I find myself pulled back into the legal and moral questions that came up at trial. On July 22, 2011, Anders Behring Breivik killed seventy-seven people, first with a bomb in Oslo's government quarter and then by shooting participants at the Labour Party youth camp on Utøya. At his 2012 trial the genuinely contested issue was not what he had done but what kind of mind he had when he did it: one court-appointed psychiatric evaluation diagnosed paranoid schizophrenia and held that he was not criminally responsible under Norwegian law, while a second evaluation found no psychosis and described a stable pattern of narcissistic and antisocial personality pathology. The court accepted the second picture, found him sane, and sentenced him to twenty-one years of preventive detention, the maximum Norwegian law allows, with a ten-year minimum term and the possibility of indefinite extension as long as he is considered dangerous. Norway has no death penalty, and the sentence is officially framed around incapacitation and rehabilitation rather than vengeance.

Because my work brings me into contact with both victims and offenders after sentencing, I keep trying to fit this case into a coherent philosophy of punishment, and the debate between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso keeps surfacing. Dennett argues that competent, reason-responsive agency does not require any contra-causal free will, and that such competence is enough to ground blame and punishment in something like a desert register, even while he stresses the forward-looking social functions of punishment. Caruso denies that we are morally responsible in the basic-desert sense and concludes that retributive punishment is never justified; he defends a public-health model of criminal justice that permits incapacitation, rehabilitation, and deterrence, and treats the offender much as one might treat a carrier of a dangerous disease — controlled and treated, but never punished for its own sake.

Please write me a disciplined case analysis, in American English, that runs in both directions between this episode and these two theories. On the theory-to-case side: for Dennett and for Caruso, spell out who or what the relevant agent is (a competent political actor, a psychotic, a personality-disordered offender, a locus of dangerousness), what question the Oslo court should have been answering, and what response — punishment or non-punitive incapacitation — follows. On the case-to-theory side: identify what each view explains naturally and what it must strain to accommodate. Among the friction points worth addressing: Dennett on the two conflicting psychiatric reports, on fanatical ideology as a possible competence failure, and on what remains of desert when the offender is described as sane but hardly ordinary; Caruso on the public's strong retributive reactions, on the moral luck of which evaluation prevailed, and on whether a public-health model can capture the court's insistence that the sentence not be vindictive. Conclude by saying what the Breivik case does to the Dennett–Caruso debate — whether it confirms, complicates, or simply outruns each view — and be explicit where each theory is silent rather than forcing it to pronounce.
