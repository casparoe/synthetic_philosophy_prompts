I'm on the pharmacy and therapeutics committee for a large hospital system, and we're voting next week on wether to add a new oral anticoagulant to the formulary for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation. The headache is that the pivotal trial's positive result only holds up under the pre-specified analysis; there was a lot of missing follow-up data, and the biostatistics group gave us eight defensible analyses that run from a 1.9 per thousand patient-years reduction in strokes down to a 0.4 per thousand increase. The manufacturer, and two members of the committee, say we should use the pre-specified analysis and state a single number, because a binary formulary decision has to rest on one probability of benefit and clinicians can't act on a spread. Another faction, including someone who keeps muttering about imprecise probabilities, says the evidence doesn't determine any unique probability of net benefit, so collapsing the range into a point estimate is pretending to know something we don't, and the honest move is to carry the whole set of probabilities into the decision or just refuse to prefer the drug. Please write the committee's decision memo: recommend adding the drug, rejecting it, or adding it with restrictions, and say how the summary for prescribers should present the uncertainty. I want a committed recommendation with reasons, not a survey of options, and give the strongest version of the losing side a fair run before you reject it. Also, no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables — just continuous prose, like an email or letter.
