I run an upper-division undergraduate philosophy seminar on the foundations of AI and economics, with about 18 students in one 80-minute session. The unit is on the inference of an agent's objective function and its search space from observed behavior alone. Design an in-class game in which students work in teams as reverse engineers of a mystery optimizer. Across a series of trials, a decision-maker is presented with a small menu of concrete alternatives — for example, ways of allocating a fixed budget across four named services — and selects one; the students see only the menus and the choices, and must propose both what quantity the decision-maker was maximizing and what alternatives it was even considering, meaning its search space.

The game should make students feel the central underdetermination of the unit, before it is named: a decision-maker whose objective is crude but whose search space is heavily restricted can be behaviorally indistinguishable, on any finite choice log, from one whose objective is subtle and whose search space is unrestricted, because penalties written into the objective and exclusions built into the search space can do exactly the same behavioral work. Ideally the students construct their hypotheses, grow confident in them, and only then discover that a very different objective/search-space pair fits the same data equally well.

Please give me the concrete choice domain and a sample choice log of about ten trials, the rules of play, and a scoring scheme that uses points rather than money. Include a prediction round on held-out menus that forces the underdetermination into the open, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the objective/search-space distinction and onto what would actually be needed to tell the two kinds of agent apart. No lecture up front — the students should meet the problem in the activity before the debrief names it. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be explicit about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply myself.

If the request rests on a confused premise, or the task as posed cannot be done well, say so plainly and propose a better version rather than complying politely with a misdirected exercise.
