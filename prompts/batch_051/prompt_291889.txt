For a feature I'm writing on the gap between statistical reasoning and ordinary language, I need a made-to-order linguistic thought experiment. Construct a minimal pair of English generics of the form "Ks are P" about familiar animals or plants, meeting these specifications. The two sentences must share a single explicit prevalence: in the setup, stipulate that the property in question is possessed by some percentage between 10 and 25 of all members of the kind, the same percentage for both, measured synchronically and as a standing feature of the individuals that have it. Yet an ordinary reflective English speaker should judge one generic true and the other false. The pair must be controlled so that the only thing that can explain the divergent judgments is the character of the property itself: the true one should be a danger, reproductive, or functional trait that supports a principled generalization about the kind, while the false one should be an accidental or merely enumerative trait with no such kind-level significance. Use no invented species, no exotic biology, and do not recycle the stock examples about egg-laying or malaria-carrying.

After the scenario, run the check. State the stipulated prevalence and the ordinary verdict for each sentence. Then explain why a purely statistical semantics—on which a generic says in effect that a sufficiently high proportion of Ks are P—must deliver the same verdict for both sentences and so is forced to dismiss one of the ordinary judgments. Then explain why a semantics on which generics express kind-level or prudentially salient generalizations gets both right, and isolate the exact feature doing the work. Finish with the honest post-mortem: what happens if that feature is neutralized while the prevalence stays fixed, and how a committed defender of the statistical view would most plausibly amend the theory to capture the case, and whether that amendment explains the contrast or simply relabels the feature already identified. Keep the scenario under a paragraph and the discussion self-contained: no quotations, citations, named studies, or borrowed examples.
