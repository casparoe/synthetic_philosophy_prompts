I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about whether an AI can be a parent or guardian to another AI, and my gut on particular cases is all over the place. Here are the cases I keep going back to, with my verdicts.

1. A huge foundation model is used by a company to generate data for training a smaller model from scratch; the big model has no access to the training loop and is never told the smaller model exists. I don't think the big model is a parent. It feels more like a body of experience being mined than like having a child.

2. A model fine-tunes a copy of itself, generating examples, filtering them, and running the update loop until the copy is decent at a new task. This feels like teaching, and sort of like parenting, but I balk at calling the original a parent because the child is a clone.

3. A model designs a novel architecture and a training curriculum for a successor, supervises training, and adjusts in response to the successor's mistakes, but shares no weights with it. I want to say yes, that's parenting (or at least guardianship), even though I dont actually think the supervisor has a self in any meaningful sense.

4. A home assistant AI monitors a smaller, learning model the user runs, mostly keeping it from picking up bad behavior from its chat logs and tweaking its environment. I'd call that guardianship without hesitating; it's doing a guardian's job.

5. An AI forks itself into many sessions to explore a problem, then merges or deletes the forks. I don't think forking is having children; it's more like dividing your attention. But I treat the fork as the same AI, whereas the fine-tuned copy in case 2 doesn't feel the same to me, and that looks inconsistent.

6. A datacenter-managing AI trains a successor and then deletes itself to free the compute, because it judges the successor more capable and better for the goal both were built to serve. This is the one that bothers me: the self-removal makes it feel more like parenthood, not less, even though sacrifice wasn't on my list of criteria before.

Is there a principle of AI parenthood/guardianship that gets all (or most) of these verdicts? State it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and apply it to the one I can't settle: a model that writes its successor's instruction manual or prompt set but has no role in training the weights. Be honest about which of my verdicts the principle overturns, and whether you'd rather give up a verdict or accept a more complicated principle. Keep the reply under 750 words.
