I teach an upper-level philosophy of language seminar, and I'm writing the final. We spend a good chunk of the term on radical interpretation, and every year the answers disappoint me in the same way: students can define the principle of charity, say something about maximizing agreement and reading the speaker as mostly rational, and then they just stop. This year I want an essay question that makes them actually tangle with how content attribution and belief attribution lean on each other, and whether charity is an empirical bet the interpreter makes or a constitutive condition on a speaker counting as interpretable at all. I also want them to be able to say what charity is *not* doing—for instance, whether a speaker we understand perfectly well could still turn out to be massively and systematically irrational, and whether that question even makes sense before meanings have been assigned.

So: write one 90-minute essay question for this exam, answerable by someone who has actually worked through the assigned Davidson material but not by someone who memorized the standard summary. Give a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme out of 20. The marking scheme should name the specific moves an A answer has to make and the specific misunderstanding each lost mark tracks—especially, if your question sets these up, the mistake that charity is just interpretive tolerance or being nice, and the mistake that maximizing agreement is something the interpreter has empirical evidence for. No outside sources allowed in the exam, and the question shouldn't lean on any external text; Davidson and maybe Quine as a foil are all they need.
