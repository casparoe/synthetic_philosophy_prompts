I help run a volunteer-maintained public garden in a municipal park. Eighteen months ago the local council made a by-law, under powers delegated by an Act of Parliament, prohibiting the use of certain water-circulation equipment in ponds that are open to the public. We have just received a compliance notice: a pump we installed in good faith in 2019 falls within the prohibition. The by-law was formally promulgated in the sense that it was published in the council’s online register of public notices, buried among the minutes of an obscure committee, and it cross-refers to a technical standard that has since been revised twice. No notice was posted in the park; none of our volunteers had heard of the rule; and the council officer who signed the notice admits she did not know of it until a member of the public complained. We are discovering what the rule requires only now, after the notice arrived.

I had recently been rereading Thomas Aquinas, and this passage in the Summa Theologiae (I–II, Question 90, Article 4) stayed with me:

“a law is imposed on others by way of a rule and measure. Now a rule or measure is imposed by being applied to those who are to be ruled and measured by it. Wherefore, in order that a law obtain the binding force which is proper to a law, it must needs be applied to the men who have to be ruled by it. Such application is made by its being notified to them by promulgation. Wherefore promulgation is necessary for the law to obtain its force.”

Aquinas plainly did not have in mind a rule that is ‘published’ only in a corner of a website no ordinary user knows exists and that incorporates an external standard by reference. The council argues that the by-law is valid because it was promulgated: the text was made available, and we could have found it if we had searched. I cannot tell whether that stretches promulgation beyond anything the passage warrants.

I want a committed reading, not a tour of options. Does promulgation, in Aquinas’s argument, mean a formal act of publication that binds whether or not those bound can realistically learn of it, or does it mean notification that can actually reach the people who must obey? Applied to our case, would the passage count us as bound by this by-law? And if his test would let the by-law count as promulgated, does that expose a defect in how he links ‘notified’ to ‘binding force’, once a legal system contains more law than any citizen could read? Take a definite view, defend it against the council’s reading, and say how confident you are in it.
