I sit on my university's Committee on Campus Expression, which is revising the policy governing invited outside speakers and student-organized demonstrations. The current rule permits any event unless it would cause a material and substantial disruption, a standard the administration says it cannot apply consistently and the campus keeps testing.

A substantial bloc on the committee wants to replace that rule with a campus-harmony review. Under its proposal, an invitation to a controversial outside speaker, or a demonstration expected to draw a significant counter-protest, would first go before a review panel. The panel would assess not only likely disruption but whether the event would erode mutual trust on campus, especially among students who would experience it as an attack on their standing here. The panel could impose mitigations such as ticketing, relocation, counter-programming, or a security plan, and in a narrow class of cases could deny the event outright. Supporters, speaking in a broadly Confucian and conservative register, contend that order and social harmony are positive goods a university may deliberately cultivate: not merely the absence of a riot, but a standing condition of restraint, mutual consideration, and trust, one that can be destroyed as effectively by contempt as by censorship. Opponents, in a liberal-pluralist register, reply that the proposal dignifies a heckler's veto, confuses feeling attacked with being wronged, and treats friction not as evidence that pluralism is working but as a cost to be managed.

Two weeks from now the committee will vote. As the member assigned to frame the decision, I need to circulate a short memo recommending one of these three positions and defending it: (a) adopt the harmony review as drafted; (b) reject it and keep a clarified material-disruption standard; or (c) reject the denial power but adopt a mitigation-only review for high-salience events. The memo should give the committee criteria it can actually apply to specific cases rather than slogans, and it should state the strongest argument on the losing side in a way its advocates would recognize.

Please draft the memo, no more than 900 words and without citations or a reading list. End it with a single final line of the form \answer{...}, where the label is one of: adopt, reject, or mitigation-only.
