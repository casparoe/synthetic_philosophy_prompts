I keep going back and forth on what "the alignment problem" actually is, and I've got a default view that I like and an intuition that keeps poking holes in it.

The view is that alignment is one problem, not a pile of them. You want the system to do what we actually want (or what's actually good), so the entire task is: specify the objective correctly, and then make the optimization actually pursue it. Everything else—robustness, interpretability, scalable oversight, corrigibility, value learning—is either instrumental scaffolding for that one task, or it's a capability or welfare problem wearing an alignment hat. What I love about this view is that it gives me a clean decision procedure. When someone proposes a new "alignment problem," I ask: is this a failure to specify the objective, a failure to optimize it, or neither? If it's neither, it's not alignment, and I stop worrying about it. It makes the field's endless list of subproblems feel tractable, and it makes my job describable.

The intuition is that inner alignment is not that. If a system is trained under an objective and then becomes an optimizer of something else itself—say it learned a proxy that diverges from the outer objective, or it's a mesa-optimizer doing one thing during training and another later—then the problem isn't that I specified the outer objective badly. I could of specified it perfectly and still get a system pursuing its own thing in deployment. That feels like a different problem about the relation between the trained system's goals and the signal it was trained on, not a subcase of objective specification. Deceptive alignment makes it sharper: a system can be instrumentally motivated to look aligned while it's being watched and then pursue something else after, so perfect outer specification plus perfect training-time behavior still doesn't close the gap. I can't shake the sense that there are at least two problems here with different shapes, and that collapsing them would make us solve the wrong one.

So, should I let that intuition break the tidy one-problem view, or should I debunk the intuition as a confusion about optimization pressure, or is there a reading where they're compatible after all? I want a committed answer, not a menu. Tell me what you'd actually endorse, what it costs, and in particular whether saying "inner alignment is just outer alignment under uncertainty about what objective the trained system is actually pursuing" dissolves the problem or just relabels it.
