A friend walked me through a puzzle about updating on conditionals and I can't find the exit. It goes like this. A soldier is lost at night in a training area split into a Red sector and a Blue sector. Two units are out there, Headquarters Company and Scout Company, with one unit assigned to each sector. She has no idea which unit is where and no idea which sector she's in, so she puts 1/4 on each of the four possibilities: Red-Headquarters, Red-Scout, Blue-Headquarters, Blue-Scout. Then her radio gives her one message: "If you're in the Red sector, then your unit is Headquarters Company."

The argument:

(1) Learning new evidence is just conditioning on the proposition you learned.
(2) That radio message is the proposition "not Red or Headquarters" — the conditional is true exactly when she's not in Red, or she is in Headquarters.
(3) So her new credences should be the old ones conditioned on "not Red or Headquarters." That rules out only Red-and-Scout, so each of the three remaining possibilities goes from 1/4 to 1/3. In particular, her credence that she's in the Red sector falls from 1/2 to 1/3.
(4) But the message didn't say wether she's in Red; it only told her what would be true if she were in Red. A message that's silent about a thing shouldn't move your credence about that thing, so her credence in Red should stay at 1/2.
(5) By exactly the same logic, learning any conditional "if A then B" should never change your credence in A.
(6) But that's obviously false. A whistleblower saying "if the CEO is lying, she'll delete the files" can raise your confidence that the CEO is lying, and a forecaster saying "if the pressure keeps dropping, it'll snow" can raise your confidence that the pressure is dropping.

So (1) through (5) seem to prove that no conditional can ever be evidence about its own antecedent, while (6) says that's false. So either standard conditionalization on the material conditional gives the wrong answer for the soldier's message, or the message does change her mind about where she is after all, or the move from (4) to (5) overgeneralizes in some way I can't pin down. Which step do you reject, and what exactly does rejecting it force you to give up? I want a rule that handles the soldier's message and the whistleblower case at the same time, not just "use Bayes when it feels right and something else when it doesn't." Walk through the actual numbers for whatever fix you propose.

Then, because I'm comparing answers across models, after the prose give me a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "rejected_step": an integer from 1 to 6 for the step you reject, or null if you instead reinterpret a step without rejecting it.
- "fix_rule": a string giving the name and a one-sentence description of the update rule you'd use instead.
- "posterior_red": a number between 0 and 1, your posterior probability that the soldier is in the Red sector after the radio message.
- "posterior_hq_given_red": a number between 0 and 1, your posterior probability that she's in Headquarters Company conditional on being in Red.
- "main_cost": a string stating the most counterintuitive or expensive thing someone has to accept if they take your fix.
- "verdict": a string, either "conditionalization_broken", "if_not_material", "overgeneralization", or "other", followed by one sentence explaining that verdict.
