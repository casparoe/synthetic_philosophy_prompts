An adversarial collaboration has been running between two camps on constitutional entrenchment. The first holds that entrenched rights, supermajority rules, and similar rigidities are best understood as rational precommitment: a people binds itself in advance against its own predictable failures of judgment, as Ulysses had himself bound to the mast before the Sirens. The second holds that the Ulysses analogy is systematically misleading, because the founders and the later citizens they bind are not one continuing self, and entrenchment therefore lets the dead govern the living under the cover of a consent they never gave. Write the joint statement these two camps could sign after a good-faith collaboration. Include: (1) numbered claims both sides accept, phrased so neither would flinch; (2) a disagreements table in which each side's view is worded exactly as its own proponents would accept; (3) the cruxes, where settling them would move at least one side — examine at least these candidates and say which survive: whether the binding and bound parties are the same self in the relevant sense; whether precommitment works by foreclosing choices or by merely raising their cost; and whether an entrenched provision's legitimacy depends on how inclusive the adopting procedures were; (4) for each surviving crux, a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance; and (5) any part of the dispute the collaborators concluded was merely verbal, with the reason.
