I am a doctoral student working where AI safety and AI ethics overlap, and I have been trying to find out whether a certain recurring asymmetry has been named and analysed, because it keeps surfacing in my reading without ever being flagged. The pattern is this. When a system behaves in ways that would, in a human or non-human animal, ordinarily count as evidence of a welfare-relevant state — reporting distress when a training change is made, or systematically avoiding a particular input — work on whether AI systems might be welfare subjects tends to discount that behaviour as a training artefact: the system was reinforced for precisely such outputs, so the behaviour need not indicate that anything is 'really' going on. Yet in work on AI safety, behaviour of the very same kind — a system's reports about its own state, or its avoidance behaviour — is routinely treated as an informative signal about what the system is doing or likely to do next. The two conclusions are not contradictory, but the evidential standards seem to shift without comment, and I have even seen the same paper treat a system's avoidance behaviour as evidence that it is goal-directed while dismissing its apparent aversion as reward-shaping. It is not simply that one field is sceptical and the other credulous, since both are sceptical and credulous about the same class of evidence at different moments. Does this asymmetry have an established name, and how exactly does it differ from neighbouring notions such as selective scepticism, confirmation bias, or a double standard in evidence? If it has no name, what should it be called, and what would distinguish a clear case from a borderline one? I am not after a bare label; I want the phenomenon carved at its actual joints. Please answer in British English.
