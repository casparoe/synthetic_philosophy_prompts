I am a PhD student in political philosophy, working on autonomy-based justifications of state authority over the family. For a paper I need a mechanistic analysis of a proposed parental licensing scheme, not an assessment of whether the scheme is morally justified.

The proposal is as follows. A jurisdiction is considering replacing its current approach to parental fitness, which intervenes only after serious harm, with a staged parental license designed around the child's right to an open future. No adult may assume daily care of a child until they hold a provisional license, which is obtained and renewed in two stages.

Entry. During the adoption process or the second half of pregnancy, every prospective primary caregiver must complete a six-week open-future practicum: supervised simulation exercises with children across ages, together with a written plan explaining, for each of five domains — schooling, religious and worldview formation, medical decision-making, movement and association, and significant irreversible choices — how they would keep a reasonable range of options open for the child as the child matures. A mixed panel consisting of a child development professional, a schoolteacher, and two laypeople grades the plan and simulations and may grant, condition, or deny a two-year provisional license. Appeal lies to a family tribunal.

Renewal. When the child is 2, 6, 11, and 15 years old, the license comes up for review. Licensees must submit a portfolio assembled from contemporaneous records rather than a retrospective essay: decisions made in the five domains, options preserved, options foreclosed, and the reasons given at the time. The same panel reviews each portfolio against a scoring manual that operationalizes an open future through a checklist of opportunity indicators, rewarding the maintenance of unused alternatives rather than outcomes. A first unsatisfactory review triggers a twelve-month state-funded support plan, including parenting courses and service access. A second unsatisfactory review at a subsequent renewal point triggers a non-adversarial family court hearing, which may impose conditions or, in serious cases, restrict or transfer care.

I want to treat this as a mechanism and trace how it would actually behave: what it would do to who becomes a parent, who delays or avoids the license, and how families would adapt — portfolio-optimizing parents, coached simulations, staged openness before reviews, and the effects on siblings and on children who know they are being documented. I would also like to know what happens to the notions of an open future and parental responsibility when they are operationalized this way, which interpretation of the right to an open future the scoring checklist would effectively put into law, and where the system would be gamed first and worst. Finally, say whether the failure modes can be fixed without gutting the scheme, and which feature would have to be abandoned.

After your prose analysis, return a JSON object with these fields, so I can use them in an appendix:

- behavioural_effects: an array of objects, each with actor (string: a type of parent, child, official, or third party), induced_behaviour (string: the main behavioural change the scheme produces in that actor), and mechanism_of_effect (string: which design feature produces it).
- gaming_paths: an array of strings, the most plausible exploitation routes in descending order of likelihood.
- second_order_effects: an array of strings, important consequences not directly intended by the scheme.
- failure_modes: an array of objects, each with failure (string) and driver (string: the design feature most responsible).
- fixability_verdict: an object with fixable_without_gutting (boolean), minimal_fixes (array of strings), and feature_to_abandon (string: the design feature that cannot be kept if the scheme is to achieve its aim).
- open_future_encoded: a string of up to four sentences stating which interpretation of the right to an open future the scoring checklist effectively endorses.
