I have spent the past year serving on a civic committee charged with planning how our city commemorates its founding. Our annual ceremony has long centered on a story about an early meeting between two rival groups that supposedly produced a solemn pact of shared government. A team of professional historians recently published a study showing that the date is wrong, the meeting almost certainly did not occur as remembered, and several elements of the ceremony—including a speech we reenact each year—are later inventions. The historians did not call for scrapping the commemoration, but they did call its central narrative misleading.

What has unsettled me is not that the story is factually weak, but that almost no one on the committee thinks that fact changes what we should do. Several members said, in effect, that the ceremony is not about establishing what happened, but about affirming that the community now exists and wants to remain one. That argument reminded me of a passage I later looked up. It is from Ernest Renan's 1882 lecture "What is a Nation?":

"A nation's existence is, if you will pardon the metaphor, a daily plebiscite, just as an individual's existence is a perpetual affirmation of life."

I used to read that passage as saying that a people's present will matters more than any past event. Now I find myself unsure what philosophical position the passage actually commits one to, and whether our committee's practice really follows from it or merely borrows its prestige. The practice seems to say: if the community's self-understanding would be damaged by the historical record, the record should be treated as secondary to the continued affirmation of the community. But is that a coherent philosophical commitment, or is it a pragmatic dodge, or is it a commitment to something else entirely—such as the value of ritual continuity, or the importance of civic trust, or the idea that a founding story is a kind of useful fiction rather than a truth claim?

I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of collective memory and national identity that maintains this ceremony in its present form despite the historians' findings, and to do so in light of the Renan passage. I am less interested in a final verdict on whether the ceremony should change, and more interested in what the practice commits us to.

Please structure your response in four titled sections, in this order:

1. The Passage Read (roughly 250 words): Interpret the quoted sentence, paying particular attention to what it implies about the relationship between the present will of a people and the facts of its past. Does Renan's view require forgetting or overriding inconvenient historical truth, or does it cohere with a more careful account of collective memory?

2. Presuppositions of the Ceremony (roughly 350 words): Reconstruct the implicit philosophy that best explains the committee's willingness to keep the ritual intact while acknowledging the historians' findings. Identify what the practice strictly presupposes about history, truth, memory, and collective identity, and say whether those presuppositions are consistent with one another.

3. Rival Theories That Would License It (roughly 300 words): Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with. Identify at least one coherent alternative theory that could justify keeping the ceremony without endorsing the false narrative as historically true, and specify whether Renan's plebiscite view is one of these or something different.

4. Consequences of a Record-First Practice (roughly 250 words): Describe what would need to change in the way a community commemorates itself if the historical record were made decisive whenever it conflicts with a community's founding narrative. Indicate where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that several theories would license the same ceremony even while disagreeing about why it is legitimate.
