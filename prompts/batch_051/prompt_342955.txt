I am drafting a novel set in a city whose postindustrial decline and school-boundary fights are felt but rarely named by my characters, most of whom are white, and I have found that I cannot tell, line by line, whether the prose should register an utterance as racist, as merely clueless, or as something the narration itself should decline to judge. I keep strong but unsystematic verdicts on my own material. When a warm, well-meaning character tells an elderly Black neighbor, "You're so well-spoken," my gut says the line is racist in the register the chapter needs, even though the character intends a compliment. When another character says of a Latina colleague, "She only got the job because they need to tick a box," I count it as racist, though it contains no slur and the speaker would deny any animus. When a third says, at a dinner party, about a mostly Black school across the tracks, "Those kids need discipline, not empathy — their culture doesn't value education," I count it as racist and treat it as worse than either of the first two. And when a Black official, baited by a hostile audience, answers, "I am not here to perform respectability for you," I do not count it as racist at all, though it names race directly. Two cases are the real trouble: a character reporting an assault to the police says, in the course of recounting what was shouted at a Black victim, a slur outright, and I keep deciding the narration should not tax him for it; yet a different character, retelling the same incident at a bar, repeats the same slur with a kind of relish, and I am certain that one should land as racist.

My working principle has been that an utterance is racist exactly when its content — what it literally says or presupposes — places people in a racial hierarchy, whatever the speaker intends. That principle seems to get the educational-culture line and the well-spoken line, but it plainly misses the dog whistle I want a minor character to deliver in a campaign speech ("I will restore this neighborhood to the way it was when families felt safe"), which I am sure is racist though its content names no race; and it looks as though it must count the police report as racist, which I want to resist. Test that principle against the cases, repair it or replace it, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate or must overturn, and then tell me what it does with two lines I have not placed: a white character who snaps, "Of course she's qualified — why would you assume otherwise?" when a Black colleague's promotion is questioned, and a character who says, sincerely, "I don't see color," during an argument about hiring.
