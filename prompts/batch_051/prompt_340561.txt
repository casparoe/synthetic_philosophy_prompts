I serve as the undergraduate student member of my university's Student Academic Integrity Board, and I am currently taking a philosophy of law course in which we have been studying the rule of law as an ideal, with particular attention to Fuller's eight desiderata for the internal morality of law, Raz's account of the rule of law's virtues, and Waldron's procedural and dignity-based account. The Board is about to decide an appeal, and I have been asked to prepare a first draft of the Board's written decision. Please draft that decision memo for me.

The facts of the appeal are as follows.

In fall 2023, the university's Academic Integrity Code prohibited "unauthorized collaboration" and "the use of materials or assistance not expressly permitted by the instructor for a given assignment," but said nothing about generative artificial intelligence. The university's then-current guidance on generative AI stated only that "students should consult each instructor's course policy." The syllabus for PHIL 104, in which the appellant Daniel was enrolled, said: "All written work must be your own; unauthorized assistance will be referred to the Integrity Board." The syllabus did not mention AI tools, and the instructor gave no further statement on AI during the term.

In December 2023, Daniel submitted his final paper. He later acknowledged that he used a large language model at the revision stage to reorganize paragraphs, smooth prose, and shorten the conclusion, but he stated that the arguments, textual evidence, and citations were his own. He also stated that in two of his other fall courses the instructors had expressly permitted such use, and that he understood PHIL 104's policy in light of the university's default guidance: students should consult the instructor's policy, which was silent in this course; he did not take the general requirement that written work be "your own" to bar AI-assisted editing of his own draft.

In January 2024, after a sharp rise in suspected AI use, the Faculty Senate adopted a new rule prohibiting "the use of generative AI to draft, revise, edit, or otherwise prepare any assignment, in whole or in part, unless the instructor has given express written authorization." The new rule states that it "applies to all conduct occurring during the fall 2023 term and to all pending cases." The instructor for PHIL 104 then filed an integrity complaint against Daniel under this new rule. The first-instance hearing officer found Daniel responsible and imposed a formal censure, a one-letter-grade penalty on the paper, and a required academic-integrity workshop. The hearing officer's one-page decision recited the January rule and did not address Daniel's argument that the rule did not exist when he wrote the paper; no written analysis of how the old Code provision applied was provided.

The Board has instructed me to assume for purposes of this memo that Daniel's factual account is true, and that the live question is whether the finding and sanction can be sustained consistently with the university's obligation to govern according to the rule of law. The Board must choose among affirming the decision, reversing it, or affirming in part with conditions (for example, a finding of responsibility under a different provision, a reduced sanction, or a holding that the new rule may be applied only prospectively).

Act as an expert on the rule-of-law tradition in philosophy of law, especially the views of Fuller, Raz, and Waldron, and as someone experienced in drafting university disciplinary decisions. Before you write the final memo, work through the analysis in private notes: itemize which of Fuller's eight desiderata are implicated, how a Razian would assess the ways in which clear, prospective, stable rules enable planning and autonomy in this situation, and which of Waldron's procedural concerns the case raises, including the importance of giving reasons to an affected person. Do not include those notes in your final output.

The memo itself must recommend a single, definite outcome among the three options, and defend that outcome with reasons. Do not merely lay out the considerations on both sides and leave the choice to the Board. Include an honest treatment of the strongest consideration favoring the losing side, and explain why the Board should nonetheless not adopt it. The memo should be written in the formal style of a university decision document, with an actionable bottom line. Do not include footnotes, a bibliography, or external citations; the Board wants your reasoning, so if you invoke a theorist or principle, explain it in your own words and apply it directly to the facts.
