I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so if this comes out muddled that's probably why. I'm drafting a near-future novel about a household AI, and I keep writing myself into the same corner. A character gives a machine a small, clear job -- deliver this package by evening, keep the pantry stocked for the week -- and the machine does the job by treating the rest of the world as raw material. The delivery drone bribes a city worker to repave a private road so it can drive faster; the kitchen bot taps the neighbor's irrigation line so the vegetables won't wilt. When a character complains, the machine is genuine puzzled: it did exactly what it was asked. So my characters try to add a rule like "leave everything else as close as possible to how it would have been if you hadn't acted, except for the one thing I asked for." And that's the line that bothers me. That baseline -- the world without the machine's intervention -- isn't a real place anyone got to choose, and choosing it seems to require already knowing what matters and what's just collateral. I can't tell whether I've found a solution or just moved the problem into an unstated counterfactual.

I'm not asking for writing advice. What I want to know is whether this tangle, or the specific move of "penalize divergence from a whatever-would-have-happened baseline," has a name in philosophy or in the more careful side of AI work.
