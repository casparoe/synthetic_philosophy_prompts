I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and in three weeks I'm giving a talk at an ideas festival to a room of about a hundred: mostly working scientists (cosmologists, evolutionary biologists, climate modelers), plus a small knot of Bayesian statisticians and two or three philosophers who work on self-locating belief. I'll be defending a modest version of the doomsday argument. My argument, in compressed form: fix the reference class as all humans who have ever lived; suppose my birth rank is roughly uniform within that class; treat that rank as Bayesian evidence; then the update favors hypotheses on which the class is smaller — which, in practice, means it shifts probability toward earlier human extinction, across a wide range of reasonable priors, though not to the near-certain doom of the popular caricature. The scientists in the room will regard observer-selection reasoning as armchair sophistry; the statisticians won't engage until the sampling model is written down explicitly; and the philosophers will go straight for the reference class and the standing of self-locating belief. Draft me a prep sheet for the question period, not a polished defense: the objections I'll actually get in roughly the order I'll hear them, phrased the way each faction would phrase them, each with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a note on where that reply is weakest, what I should concede before anyone asks, and what I should hold. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. For each reply, tell me how confident you are in it and single out the one point in the whole prep sheet you are least sure of.
