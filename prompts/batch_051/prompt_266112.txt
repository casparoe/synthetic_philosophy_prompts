Two people in my reading group got into it over underdetermination of theory by evidence. Dana said that for any finite batch of observations, there are indefinitely many theories that fit it equally well — same predictions about what we've seen, different stories about the unobservables — so believing in electrons is never forced on us by the data; it's a choice is all. Marcus said that's too quick: what matters isn't underdetermination by the evidence we happen to have, but whether rival theories stay tied once all possible evidence is in. If they only agree so far, further testing can split them; and if they'd agree on every conceivable observation, they're not really different theories about the world, just different ways of writing the same one. Dana came back that "all possible evidence" is doing hidden work: which observations are even possible depends on background theory, and any specific failed prediction can be absorbed by fiddling with an auxiliary assumption, so there's no neutral point at which rivals have run out of ways to stay equivalent. Marcus said her argument cuts too deep — if evidence could never distinguish theories even in principle, the same goes for her belief that the table is solid rather than something that merely acts solid whenever checked. So she'd need a principle that lets ordinary observational beliefs survive while theoretical belief in electrons doesn't; otherwise she's just special pleading. Neither would budge. Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what, and flag anything they're both missing?
