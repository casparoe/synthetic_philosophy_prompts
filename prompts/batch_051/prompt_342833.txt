In the United States, courts strike down criminal statutes as void for vagueness when an ordinary person could not tell from the text what conduct is forbidden, and the doctrine is standardly explained as protecting fair notice and preventing arbitrary enforcement. Yet the same legal system routinely obliges people and officials to conform to open-textured standards — probable cause, gross negligence, beyond a reasonable doubt, reasonable suspicion — that are not obviously more determinate than the language the doctrine condemns. What picture of the rule of law, of legal language, or of the authority of officials does this practice presuppose? Is the doctrine best understood as committed to the idea that a statute can have a determinate meaning accessible to the ordinary reader, or is it better reconstructed as a rule about where discretion may be located, with fair notice functioning as a rationale rather than the real engine? Reconstruct the implicit theory carefully, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change in doctrine if that presupposition were replaced by a candid account of how much open-ended judgment any legal system must delegate in applying law. Say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
