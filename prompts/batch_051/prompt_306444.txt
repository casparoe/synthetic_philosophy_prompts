I have just come away from a long argument with a friend about whether there is a principled moral difference between lying and merely misleading. My friend defended the view that, when the consequences are held equal, the lie is always the graver wrong, because only the liar asserts a proposition they believe to be false; a person who misleads by telling truths has at least kept faith with the practice of truthfulness. I doubt this, and I suspect the comparative verdict can be made to run the other way once the right structure is in place.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specification. A person must choose between two ways of producing the same deception: (i) telling a lie, by asserting a proposition they believe to be false, or (ii) misleading, by asserting only propositions they believe to be true while relying on the hearer's background assumptions to reach the false conclusion. The case must make the misleading course morally worse than the lie. It must satisfy these constraints:

- The two courses produce the same foreseeable consequences: the same false belief, the same resulting action, and the same harm.
- The misleading utterance must be an ordinary, true assertion in the circumstances, not an equivocation, a coded message, or a sentence true only on a technicality no competent speaker would accept.
- The greater wrongness of the misleading course must not come from the fact that the misleading agent would be safer from later detection, accusation, or recrimination than the liar.
- Apart from the mode of deception, the two courses must be identical in every respect that could plausibly bear on their moral assessment; any principle invoked to generate the verdict must explain why the difference between false assertion and inference from true assertions can itself bear moral weight.
- The scenario must be realistic, set in a workplace or clinical context, and no longer than one paragraph.

After presenting the case, do the following. First, set out what a defender of the view that lying is intrinsically the graver wrong would say about the case, and state precisely where that defender would locate the moral difference between the two courses. Second, state the principle under which the misleading course is worse, and show step by step how the case activates that principle while the lie does not; do not stipulate the verdict. Third, identify the single feature of the case doing the most work, and explain how the comparative verdict changes if that feature is removed or altered. Fourth, explain how a defender of the view that the lie is always the graver wrong would most credibly resist the case rather than accept it, for example by arguing that the equal-consequences stipulation cannot be sustained, or that some feature beyond the lying/misleading distinction has been smuggled in.

I intend to use the case as the central counterexample in a paper on the ethics of deception, so it must be able to withstand a determined interlocutor; please be explicit about its pressure points. Please write the entire answer in British English.
