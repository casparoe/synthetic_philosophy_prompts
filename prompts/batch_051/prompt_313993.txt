I work as a journalist covering science and ideas, and I am preparing a feature on the modularity of mind. I would like to open the piece with a short poem, and I would like you to write it. Please compose a fourteen-line sonnet whose octave is spoken in the voice of a paradigmatic Fodorian input system — domain-specific, informationally encapsulated, mandatory, fast, and yielding only shallow outputs — as if the module were boasting about the clean design that lets it do one job without consulting the rest of the mind. Place the volta at the ninth line, where the boast runs up against a different kind of cognition. Central systems are not like this: they are isotropic, since any belief can in principle be relevant to any other, and Quinean, since how well a hypothesis is confirmed depends on its fit with the whole web of belief. Let the sestet bring in the massive modularity program from evolutionary psychology, which wants to say that central cognition is itself an assembly of modules, and show that this claim can survive only by loosening standards that mattered to Fodor — above all, informational encapsulation. The closing couplet should state the resulting trade-off precisely: if Fodor's criteria stand, central cognition is not modular, while if central cognition is modular in the massive modularity sense, the term module has stopped meaning what Fodor meant. The poem should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mind would accept the technical picture, and good enough that a general reader meeting encapsulation or isotropy for the first time can feel what is at stake.
