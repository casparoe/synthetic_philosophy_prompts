I am a PhD student in political theory working on the ethics of refugee admission, and I keep returning to a tension I cannot resolve.

On the one hand, I am attracted to a remedial responsibility account of asylum. On this view, a state’s obligation to admit noncitizens is not grounded in general humanitarian need, but in specific causal or remedial relations between the receiving state and the displaced person: the state must offer refuge to those whose displacement it helped produce, to those it has directly harmed, or to those fleeing persecution that the state is bound to prevent because it is party to the relevant human rights regime. The theory has real systematic virtues. It explains why obligations seem especially strong toward former colonial subjects or wartime interpreters while blocking the conclusion that every affluent state must simply open its borders to all who are badly off. It also gives a decision procedure: ask whether this particular person’s displacement stands in the relevant causal or legal relation to us.

But when I read Hannah Arendt’s essay We Refugees, I find myself unable to accept what the theory seems to imply. Arendt describes the refugee not primarily as someone who has suffered a discrete act of persecution, but as someone who has been expelled from the only framework in which his words and deeds could count, and she remarks that the world seems unwilling to acknowledge that contemporary history has created a new kind of human being, one placed in concentration camps by his enemies and in internment camps by his friends. The intuition I take from this is that the deepest injury of refugeehood is not the particular act of persecution or even the material destitution, but the loss of membership in a political community—the condition of having no polity that must answer for you. If that is right, then an asylum regime built around individualized persecution or direct causal responsibility will be structurally blind to some of the worst cases, because it looks for an identifiable persecutor rather than for the more diffuse but devastating fact of statelessness.

I do not want a simple adjudication. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be debunked as an emotional reaction to Arendt’s rhetoric, what it would take for the remedial responsibility theory to be revised so that it can capture loss of membership as an independent ground for admission, and whether there is a reading of refugee or asylum on which both the theory and the intuition can be preserved. Please interpret Arendt’s argument as charitably as possible, present the strongest version of each side, and tell me which should yield or how they might be reconciled.
