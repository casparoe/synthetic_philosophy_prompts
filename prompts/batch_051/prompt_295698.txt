Earlier this month a friend and I got into a long argument about the Condorcet jury theorem and whether its famous guarantee can actually be bought by a real institution. The theorem, as we were both using it, says roughly this: when a group faces a binary choice between two alternatives, one of which is objectively correct; when each voter is individually more likely than not to choose the correct alternative; and when the votes are independent of one another, then the probability that a majority chooses correctly rises with the size of the group and approaches certainty as the group grows without bound. One of us insisted that the independence assumption does almost all the work; the other insisted that the theorem still captures a deep reason why aggregating many imperfect judges beats trusting one. We never settled it.

Here is the scenario we kept circling. Suppose a country amends its constitution so that, from next year, no constitutional challenge is decided by its nine-member constitutional court. Instead, each case is decided by a majority vote of 1,001 citizens drawn at random from the electoral roll. They watch the oral argument through a live feed at home, then vote within twenty-four hours on a single yes-or-no question: whether the challenged statute is invalid under a fixed, clearly worded constitutional test. They are forbidden to discuss the case with one another before voting, and the ballot does not ask them for reasons. All of them receive the same written briefs and watch the same argument; beyond that, each relies on whatever they already happen to know. The fixed test is meant to bring the case as close as a legal institution can to the theorem's condition that one answer be objectively correct; part of what I want is your judgment of whether that succeeds.

Please give me a reasoned prediction of how this institution would perform over its first twenty years, on the accuracy of its constitutional rulings, compared with the nine-member court. Walk me through the mechanisms that matter—individual competence, common information, the way the 1,001 are selected, the fixed test, the ban on deliberation, and the objectivity of the legal question—and say where the theorem's assumptions would plausibly hold and where they would fail. I especially want you to be explicit about confidence: which predicted effects are robust under any reasonable assumptions, which are fragile guesses, and what would have to be true for your overall prediction to be wrong. Do not cite sources; the reasoning should stand on its own. Write in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if you were sending me a long email.
