Schopenhauer's pessimism is often mistaken for a matter of temperament, but he presents it as a deduction from a theory of desire. His claim is that all willing arises from felt deficiency, and that satisfaction is therefore never a positive state: it is only the temporary disappearance of a need — a brief return to a baseline from which the next want immediately arises. On this view suffering is the positive and fundamental thing, happiness is merely its negation, and a life composed entirely of fulfilled wishes would still be a sequence of short respites that lapse into boredom or the pressure of some new want. The judgment that life is not, on balance, a good bargain is meant to follow from this analysis rather than from a gloomy disposition.

Now confront this argument with a development Schopenhauer could not have known: the dissociation in affective neuroscience between wanting and liking. Incentive salience research distinguishes the motivational system that marks a stimulus as salient and drives pursuit from the system that generates the experience of pleasure, and it shows that the two have partly separate neural bases and can be manipulated independently. Wanting can be amplified while liking remains flat; liking can be produced without the prior state of felt lack that Schopenhauer treated as the essence of all willing.

This appears at first to refute the argument at its central premise: if pleasure has its own positive neurobiological basis, then happiness is not merely the absence of pain, and a life could in principle contain positive felt states rather than only the neutralization of deficits. But the same evidence cuts the other way. It suggests that the pursuit system is the more insatiable one, and that much of what drives human behavior is not the pleasure actually obtained but an incentive signal that can outrun it — a physiological version of the treadmill Schopenhauer described introspectively.

Does Schopenhauer's argument for pessimism survive the wanting/liking distinction, or does the distinction destroy the premise that pleasure is merely negative? If it survives, give the strongest repaired version, and say explicitly which premise the development bears on and how much of the argument can be rebuilt without the metaphysics of the will once the empirical distinction is admitted. If it does not survive, say precisely what is left of the pessimism and what would have to be true for it to be revived.
