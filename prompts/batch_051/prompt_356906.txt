I was arguing with my housemate about whether equality matters for its own sake, and he pulled up a summary of prioritarianism that he'd saved from some blog. I've re-read it a few times and I can't tell wether the ending actually follows from the rest or whether the whole thing is smuggling in a confusion. The summary says:

"Prioritarianism is the view that when we distribute benefits, we should give extra weight to benefits that go to people who are worse off. That makes it a kind of egalitarianism, but unlike old-fashioned equality-of-outcome views it doesn't fall victim to the leveling-down objection. Leveling down — making the rich poorer without making the poor richer — is not better in any respect for the prioritarian, because what matters is how well people are doing in absolute terms, not the size of the gap between them. This is why Parfit introduced the priority view as a way of keeping the egalitarian impulse while dropping the absurd implication that leveling down is good. What I don't understand is how this is supposed to work: if the worse off matter more, and what matters is absolute well-being, then moving from a society where a few are very rich and many are very poor to one where everyone is equally poor should still count as an improvement, because the worst off are no longer below anyone else. And if the prioritarian denies that, then they really do care about the gap after all."

Most importantly, does the final dilemma work? That is, is a prioritarian forced to say that a move to an "everyone equally poor" society is an improvement, and if they deny it, are they secretly valuing the gap after all? Think through this carefully rather than just pattern-matching to the standard textbook move, and end with a verdict on whether the blog summary is fair as an introduction.
