I'm a novelist, and I serve on the board of a small foundation that gives one annual prize "for the year's most inspired work of prose fiction." Our guidelines have never defined "work," and in four decades we have only ever awarded the prize to novels written originally in English. This year a translator has been put forward by a small press: a contemporary novel rendered into English by someone who clearly rebuilt its rhythms, jokes, and idioms rather than merely transferring its sense, and the press argues that the translation is itself the inspired work. The board is split. One faction says a translation is derivative almost by definition — its excellences are owed to another mind — and that honoring it would let one book win twice and open the prize to an endless line of versions of the same text. The other says this is literary Platonism: it supposes the "work" is some abstract original hovering behind every rendering, when in fact the prose our readers encounter, and the only inspired thing in front of us, is the English text.

I keep thinking about the theological analogue, and I suspect it is the real question stuffed inside our procedural one. There are traditions, Jewish and Christian, about the Septuagint — the Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures — that treat it as itself divinely inspired rather than as a defective echo of an inspired original. And a strand of Protestant thought treats the King James Version as a providentially preserved, authoritative English text in its own right rather than merely a translation among translations. Against that, most traditions draw a bright line: God may inspire a prophet or an author, but a translator can only ever be accurate or interpretive. The aesthetic version of that bright line is what our board is being asked to choose.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete decision — admit the translation, reject it, or admit it under a narrower rule — articulate the rule the board would be adopting for future submissions, and engage the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly rather than just restating that translations are derivative. Since I'll have to defend the rule in a room of writers who enjoy finding the weak joint in an argument, please also tell me which argument or consideration would lead you to a different recommendation, or where the memo's reasoning is most likely to fail.
