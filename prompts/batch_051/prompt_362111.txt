I serve on a working group that has been asked to review a proposal for governing a mid-sized city through prediction markets, and I keep finding myself uncertain whether the design is clever or brittle in ways I cannot yet name. The proposal is as follows. For each capital project or regulation that qualifies for the ballot, the city would open a ninety-day market in which a YES contract pays one dollar if, eighteen months after implementation, the city's quality-of-life index — a pre-registered composite score combining median household income, average commute times, air-quality measurements, and resident survey responses — beats the city's published baseline forecast by at least two points; otherwise the contract pays nothing. The distinctive feature is the decision rule. Rather than acting on the closing price, the city would draw a single moment uniformly at random from the ninety-day trading window and implement the proposal if and only if the market price sampled at that moment exceeds sixty cents. The draw would be made only after the window closes, using a public randomness beacon, so no trader could know in advance which moment would be decisive. Trading would be open to everyone, including city employees who help produce the index, contractors bidding on the projects, and residents of neighboring towns.

I would like this analyzed as an institution rather than as a statement of ideology. Walk through the incentives the random-sampling rule creates for when different participants trade and how much they risk; how the rule changes the cost and tactics of price manipulation compared with a fixed closing-time decision; how participants who can influence the index, the baseline forecast, or the sampling process might behave once the rules are known; and where the system is most likely to fail first. Finally, say which of those failure modes could be fixed with narrow patches and which ones are inherent to the design.
