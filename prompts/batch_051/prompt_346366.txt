I'm a novelist, and after spending way too long designing a script for a language I invented for a novel, I've talked myself into a pretty hard position: a writing system's job is to encode speech, and a script is good exactly insofar as it's phonemic — one symbol per phoneme, one phoneme per symbol, no silent letters, no two ways to spell the same sound, none of English's mess. By that standard, something like Korean Hangul is the right idea and English orthography is a historical accident that should embarrass us. I'd like you to play it straight and tell me what would count against that position. Not just the obvious line that English spelling usefully signals meanings, but the actual defeaters: what skilled reading tells us about whether we process text phoneme by phoneme, what a script can consistently encode instead of or alongside sound (morphemes, syllable structure, etymological layers, one dialect versus another), and which of those a committed phonemicist would concede genuinely count against the view and which they'd dismiss as changing the subject. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say where the position is well insulated and wether that insulation is a feature or a bug. For what it's worth, I'd be most persuaded by evidence that strict phonemic spelling actually makes reading harder, or that it inevitably anoints one dialect's pronunciation as the real language.
