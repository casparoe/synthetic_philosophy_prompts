Since I retired from a career in behavioral ecology I've been reading philosophy, and I keep turning dinner conversation into decision-theory problem sets, which my wife finds exhausting. Lately I've been collecting verdicts from a woman in my reading group, formerly a game theory modeler, on puzzles about intention and commitment, because she is maddeningly consistent but I can't yet state the rule she's running. Her record so far: on the original toxin puzzle — a billionaire offers you a million dollars if you form the intention tonight to drink an unpleasant but harmless toxin tomorrow afternoon, with the money payed out as soon as some device confirms the intention is genuine, whether or not you follow through — she says you cannot rationally intend to drink it, since tomorrow you'll know there is no reason to, so the money is rationally out of reach; she says the case is a bug, not a loophole. On Ulysses and the Sirens she says it is rational to have yourself bound to the mast in advance, even though you'll hate it once the singing starts. On a toxin variant where a mechanism guarantees you will be made to drink unless you do it voluntarily, she says signing up for the mechanism is rationally fine and then the intention is too. On a variant where the only option is a pill tonight that causes the intention to drink tomorrow without making you any more likely to carry it out, she says the pill is just manufactured self-deception and not a rational way to get paid.

The new case I want to predict before I bring it to her: same billionaire and same intention-detector, but the only step available tonight is making a genuine promise to a friend that you will drink tomorrow. The friend has no stake in it and won't rely on it, nothing about your later incentives, constraints, or reputation changes, and nobody will ever know if you don't drink. The whole question is whether the promise itself gives you a reason tomorrow that can make the intention rational. I can see two principles that fit all her earlier answers, and they come apart exactly here. Give me your best committed prediction of what she'll say about this case, spell out the two principles as you reconstruct them, say which you think is really doing the work in her thinking and why, and tell me what further case you'd run next to settle it if you turned out to be wrong — rather than laying out the options and leaving me to pick.
