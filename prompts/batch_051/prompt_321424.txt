I'm writing a paper on the frame problem, and I keep noticing a slide in the literature: a narrow problem about representing change in a formal system — how a planner concludes that turning on the oven does not change the color of the kitchen walls without writing down a separate frame axiom for every non-change — becomes, in some hands, a sweeping epistemological claim about how any finite system could determine what is relevant to it at all. I want a thought experiment that forces these two readings apart cleanly, rather than letting them talk past each other.

Build me a scenario around a mundane piece of deployed automation — a building management system, a warehouse scheduler, a kitchen robot — that has plainly solved the narrow frame problem. It updates its world model after each action using something like a default or nonmonotonic rule: facts are taken to persist unless some action-specific consequence says otherwise, and it does not enumerate frame axioms. But in that same scenario the system still runs headlong into the broad relevance problem: before its tidy persistence rule can even be applied, something must determine which of an open-ended set of facts about the environment are candidates to be affected by the action at all, and the case should show why no finite increase in speed, memory, or added axioms would settle that. Keep it realistic — no science fiction, no exotic abilities beyond what a well-engineered piece of ordinary software could plausibly have, though you may idealize its competence at the narrow task. The failure must be structural, not a bug, a bad training set, or missing data; and the verdicts must be generated by the two readings themselves, not stipulated into the scenario.

Then do the check. State the scenario, and say what each reading would conclude: why a defender of the narrow reading would insist the frame problem is solved here and what remains is merely a different problem — call it general relevance, or abduction — and why a defender of the broad reading would insist this is still the frame problem, now visible in its proper form. Identify which feature of the scenario is doing the work, what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed, and how each side would try to attack the other's description of the case. Finally, be explicit about where your own diagnosis is shakiest, and say what consideration — for instance, a convincing finite relevance filter, or an argument that the two readings are not merely correlated but equivalent in this case — would change the verdict you give.
