Here's an asymmetry in ordinary mathematical practice that's been bugging me. When I first met complex numbers, I was taught that choosing i picks one of the two roots of x² + 1 = 0, but that the pick is arbitrary because −i does exactly the same job, and nothing is legitimate unless it survives swapping i and −i. But nobody gives the same speech about √2. We say "the" square root of two, meaning the positive one, and treat −√2 as its mirror. Yet over ℚ the two roots of x² = 2 are just as symmetric as i and −i: the splitting field has an automorphism fixing ℚ and swapping them, so the algebra can't tell them apart either. The only difference I can see is that the reals are ordered, so "positive" picks out one root, whereas the complex numbers can't be ordered, so nothing of that sort can pick out i.

Is that enough? Does the ordering turn √2 into a genuinely canonical choice and leave i a mere convention, or are we just so attached to the real line that we've promoted one root and forgot we did? I want the strongest case for the asymmetry and the strongest case that it's overblown. Give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion; each point should state one candidate justification for the asymmetry, or one reason it collapses, with a brief verdict on whether it survives. Write in British English.
