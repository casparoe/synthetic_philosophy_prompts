Ole Peters has argued, in his paper on the ergodicity problem in economics, that expected utility theory evaluates a gamble for an individual as though the relevant expectation were an average over many independent copies of that individual, while a real person instead experiences a single path through time. In non-ergodic settings the ensemble average and the time average diverge, and Peters takes this to show that expected utility theory embeds a conceptual mistake about the object of rational choice, not merely a pragmatic simplification. Treat that as a testable conceptual claim. Design a concrete experimental or observational program to test whether the distinction between ensemble averages and time averages captures something about real individual choice that expected utility theory gets wrong, as distinct from merely showing that the two averages differ mathematically for certain stochastic processes. Specify the task or data you would use, what result would favor the reading that expected utility theory is conceptually confused, what result would favor the reading that the distinction is only a technical modeling issue, and what the test would not settle—for example, whether subjects who avoid ensemble-average reasoning are actually tracking their own time-average objective or are instead using some unrelated heuristic. Be explicit about what your operationalization leaves out.
