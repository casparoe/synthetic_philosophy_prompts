I've been turning over a question that's been nagging me for a while. Whenever a new bit of science, philosophy or technology needs a name, the reflex seems to be to mint the word from Latin or Greek roots — photosynthesis, epistemology, deontology, epigenetics, telemedicine — even though those languages have no native speakers left and only a tiny sliver of the population ever learns them. What is actually going on there? It cant just be habit, and the usual justifications point in different directions: the roots are precise and composable, they travel across languages without needing translation, they carry a certain scholarly prestige, and they make a brand-new idea look safely old. But you could also tell a less charitable story: that it's a way of dressing plain jargon up as something more authoritative, and of putting a velvet rope around specialist knowledge.

I'd like an essay on whether the practice of coining new technical terms from Latin and Greek is mostly justified, or whether it functions more as a kind of institutionalised obscurantism. Weigh the strongest arguments on both sides — including, say, the way an etymology can positively mislead people about what a concept means, and the charge that it makes concepts needlessly inaccessible — and then defend a verdict rather than sitting on the fence. I'm not after a history of loanwords or a classification of word-parts; I want the normative question answered.
