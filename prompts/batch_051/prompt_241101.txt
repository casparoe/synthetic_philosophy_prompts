For a handout for an undergraduate aesthetics seminar on whether photographs are transparent—on whether we literally see the photographed object through the photograph—write a "which position do you actually hold?" quiz of eight to ten questions, with a scoring key. Each question should be a concrete case: a scene viewed in a mirror and then photographed, a live CCTV feed, a photorealistic drawing made to duplicate the exposure exactly, a composite assembled from parts that matches a real scene's pattern, a photograph taken through a telescope, a badly degraded snapshot, and so on. Give each question two or three answer options in ordinary non-theoretical terms (for example, "I see the object itself," "I see only the image," "I get access to the object without seeing it," "I am not sure what I see"), and never use the words "transparent," "representation," or "depict" in the questions themselves. Build the instrument from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of positions: first work out which combinations of answers are possible, then locate at least these four leaves in that space—(1) full transparency, the view that photographs let us see through them as mirrors and telescopes do; (2) distinctive epistemic access without literal seeing; (3) an interpretation view on which photographs are pictures we read, their advantage over drawings being only reliability and detail; and (4) a deflationary option holding that there is no unified phenomenon here. Mark any answer combination no known position occupies and say briefly whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored, and flag which positions your questions cannot separate (appearance-based questions will likely not separate (2) from (3); provenance questions will).
