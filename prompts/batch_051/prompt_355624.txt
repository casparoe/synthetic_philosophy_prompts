Okay so I've been trying to figure out wether Cox's theorem actually gives real support for Bayesian probability, or wether it's mostly a slick derivation from assumptions that already do the work. Can I run an essay sketch by you and get comments? I care less about the prose and more about whether I've got the math-philosophy right and whether the objection lands.

The sketch: people usually focus on the functional-equation part, the associativity move that gives you the product rule. But I want to argue the load-bearing step happens earlier, when the setup assumes the plausibility of a conjunction AB is a function of exactly two numbers — roughly, the plausibility of A given B, and the plausibility of B. That's a substantive assumption, and my suspicion is it's doing most of the real work. To see why, compare Dempster–Shafer theory: you can have mass sitting on the set {A, B} without it being split between A and B, so how plausible the conjunction is isn't determined by how plausible the parts are; you need more than two numbers to capture it. Cox's setup quietly rules that out before any equations get solved. So my claim is that the theorem mostly re-derives probability from an assumption that already has probability-like structure baked in, and a non-Bayesian could reject that assumption without being obviously irrational.

Specific comments I'd actually use: (a) am I right about the role of that two-input function assumption, or did I misread the theorem? (b) is there a version that drops or weakens it and still gets the sum and product rules? and (c) if you had to rank the usual Cox desiderata by how much heavy lifting they do, where would this one land relative to, say, the assumption that plausibilities sit on a single real-number scale?
