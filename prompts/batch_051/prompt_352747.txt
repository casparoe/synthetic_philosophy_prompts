I work as a reliability engineer, building and reviewing safety cases for industrial systems. My working vocabulary is hazard identification, design-basis events, failure modes, fault trees, residual risk, and fail-safe defaults; I have no academic philosophy background. On my own I have spent a lot of time reading arguments about whether atheism or agnosticism is the rational default, and about who bears the burden of proof when it comes to God's existence. The debate keeps reminding me of how engineering assigns the burden of proof: the person proposing a design must show it meets its safety criteria, a claim that a system is unsafe usually has to identify some specific failure mode, and the absence of observed failures during testing is not by itself proof of safety.

I would like you to restate the main positions in the burden-of-proof debate in that engineering vocabulary. Include at least: the view that theism bears the burden because it asserts the existence of something, like a novel design; the view that atheism is itself a factual claim about a universal negative and so cannot escape a burden, like an engineer claiming that no failure mode of a certain class exists; and the agnostic view that neither default is warranted until more comes in, like a system left in an unclassified or low-information state. For each, say what translates cleanly, what had to be stretched or fudged, and what has no expression in a safety-case framework at all.

Then give me a residue report. I want to know whether the places where the translation breaks down reveal something about the God debate — for example, that it lacks the base rates, cost functions, or shared acceptance criteria that make engineering safety judgments tractable — or whether they expose limitations in how engineers assign burdens. I am trying to determine whether my intuition that the whole argument is basically a safety case with different labels is correct, or whether the engineering vocabulary quietly prejudges the matter.
