In a recent conversation, someone told a story about an uncanny coincidence: she had spent the morning thinking about an old friend she had not spoken to in years, and that afternoon the friend rang her out of the blue. Another guest ended the discussion by saying 'there are no coincidences', as if that established that the event could not really have been chance and that some hidden connection, purpose, or pattern must have been at work.

I would like you to unpick what that one-liner could actually be asserting. Please set out the distinct precise claims that 'there are no coincidences' might be used to make, and evaluate each of them. I would like the readings to include at least the following, though you should add any further readings you think are important:

1. A definitional or near-tautological reading: the sentence is true because of how we choose to use the word 'coincidence' — for example, that nothing is ultimately causeless, or that 'coincidence' merely labels an unexplained pattern rather than explaining it away.
2. A probabilistic reading: apparent coincidences are never, or almost never, genuinely improbable once one accounts for the number of opportunities for them to occur — in the spirit of the birthday problem, according to which quite modest groups are surprisingly likely to contain a shared birthday even though any particular pair's match feels remarkable.
3. An epistemological reading about anecdote: first-person reports of surprising events should not be treated as evidence for hidden causes, because of selection effects, memory biases, and our weakness at judging base rates.
4. A metaphysical or causal reading: behind every apparent coincidence there really is some non-chance connection, agency, or design.

For each reading, please say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or substantive but false or dubious, and explain how the readings relate to one another. Then address the work the saying was doing in the conversation: which of these readings, if any, would actually support the inference from 'this coincidence feels too improbable to be chance' to 'something deeper is going on'? In doing so, please discuss why the subjective feeling of surprise is not by itself a reliable probability estimate, and what selection effects are being overlooked when a single striking anecdote is offered as proof. Please answer in British English.
