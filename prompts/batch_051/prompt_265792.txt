My philosophy of religion professor works in a broadly Wittgensteinian idiom, but she never states the doctrine outright; she only rules on cases. Here are the cases students have put to her this term, with her verdicts.

Ruth says she believes she will be reunited with her dead husband. She does not treat this as a forecast; she says the question of how probable it is does not arise for her, that no future experience could refute it, and that it is what her grief, her daily discipline, and her dealings with other people are organized around. The professor calls this religious belief, full stop, and says it would be a misunderstanding to call it an empirical hypothesis held with unusually high confidence.

Daniel says he believes God exists because the apparent design of the universe makes it likely. He puts the probability at around 70 percent, says a sufficiently strong version of the problem of evil would push him below 50, and admits the belief makes very little difference to how he lives. The professor says this is not religious belief in the sense she cares about; it is scientific-style theorizing that happens to be about God.

Claire is completely certain that her dead grandmother will return as a ghost on the third of next month. No evidence or argument can move her, but the belief sits isolated: it does not organize her life, and she mostly uses it to decide whether to lock the door that night. The professor says this is superstition, not religious belief, and that unshakeable certainty by itself proves nothing.

Andre is a secular activist who holds that history is on the side of justice. The belief is passionate and structures his political work, but he also treats it partly as an ordinary factual claim: he cites past progress as evidence, and he concedes that a century of deepening cruelty would show the belief is false. The professor says the live part of this is what she would call a picture, but it has been bolted onto an empirical prediction about how events will go, so it is not the same thing as religious belief.

Tomas says that when he prays for a sick friend he is not making a prediction about the illness; he says the prayer would not be shown to have failed if the friend died, and he cannot specify what experience would count against the God he addresses. The professor calls this religious belief in the full sense, despite its deliberately loose relation to observable outcomes.

Miriam recites the creed and says she is certain of every clause, but when asked what difference it makes to how she responds to birth, failure, other people's pain, or her own death, she says it makes no difference at all: it is simply a list of things she assents to. The professor says this is not religious belief as she uses the term; it is bare propositional assent.

Next week we are to ask her about a new case, which she has not heard. Lena was raised without religion and now describes herself as a naturalist. She holds, with exactly the attitude Ruth has, that the universe is oblivious to us but that nothing genuinely good is ever wholly lost. She says this is not a factual hypothesis, that she cannot imagine an experience that would count against it, and that it is the fixed point around which she organizes her work, her grief, and her sense of what she owes other people. She has no theistic beliefs, no expectation of an afterlife, and no belief in any supernatural order.

What should I expect the professor to say about Lena: a religious belief in the relevant sense, not a religious belief, or something in between? Identify the two or three principles that fit her entire record of rulings so far; say what each principle predicts about Lena; give your best guess as to which principle she is actually running, with the reasoning; and tell me which further case you would put to her to settle the matter between the principles you take to be live. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
