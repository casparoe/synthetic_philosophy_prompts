You're an expert on open-source licensing and the copyright concept of derivative works. Think carefully, and start by laying out the strongest case for each side before you settle on a recommendation.

I chair my university's software release review board, and we have a licensing case to decide. A campus lab built a ~30,000-line computational-chemistry pipeline that wraps a well-known GPLv3 solver. Most of the time the pipeline launches the solver as a seperate process and feeds it input files on the command line. But ten years ago a grad student copied roughly 400 lines of the solver's core loop into the lab's codebase, ported it from Fortran to Python, and it's now a module that talks to the rest of the pipeline through a small, hand-rolled key-value exchange format rather than normal function calls. The module and the main program are distributed together, and the main program is pretty useless without the module.

The lab wants to release the code open source: MIT for the main pipeline and GPLv3 for just that one module. Their argument is that the module is a separate work merely aggregated with the rest, and that the subprocess calls are obviously fine. One committee member disagrees: the module's history plus the bespoke exchange format make the whole pipeline a derivative work of the GPL solver, so the entire thing has to go out as GPL. Another thinks the lab is overcomplicating it and should just reimplement the module clean-room, since the Fortran-to-Python port's status isn't worth the legal risk.

Draft the committee's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and reccomend the specific license terms the lab should use. Include an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side, and end with a paragraph saying what would change your recommendation (which disputed fact or legal interpretation, if it went the other way, would flip the verdict) and where your reasoning is most likely to fail given how underdetermined this area of law is.
