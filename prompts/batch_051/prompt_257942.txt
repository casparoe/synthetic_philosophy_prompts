I run a fortnightly seminar on the regulation of hateful expression, and two of its members—one committed to a recognisably First Amendment position, the other to a recognisably European one—have agreed, after a year of talking past each other, to produce an adversarial-collaboration joint statement. The first holds that expression may be restricted only when it is intended to produce and likely to produce imminent unlawful conduct, and that the remedy for even vile speech is counter-speech rather than criminal punishment. The second holds that a liberal democratic state may and sometimes must prohibit expression whose public meaning is to deny the equal standing of a group or to intimidate its members, because a society organised around equal dignity need not subsidise its own corrosion. They are not chiefly debating the wording of particular statutes; they are disputing what, if anything, the state may legitimately criminalise.

I would like you to draft the document they could jointly sign. It should contain: (1) a numbered agreed-claims section recording what both accept in common, about what hate speech is, which interests it engages, and what the empirical record on the harms of such expression and on the effects of legal prohibition does and does not establish; (2) a disagreements section that states the residual dispute in terms each side would accept for its own position, attending especially to whether the dispute is about the intrinsic wrongness of certain expression or about its downstream consequences; (3) a list of cruxes—sub-questions whose settlement would move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, at least one concrete case, natural experiment, or body of evidence that both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

I suspect the real cruxes may include whether dignitary harm is a distinct and sufficient ground for coercion or merely a dressed-up form of offence, and whether the two camps ultimately diverge over principle or over institutional trust in legislatures versus courts; test those suspicions rather than assume them. If any part of their dispute seems to you merely verbal, identify it and explain why.
