I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've ended up reading about superrationality because I can't stop thinking about whether two copies of the same model should cooperate in a one-shot prisoners dilemma. My rough understanding of Hofstadter's idea is: in a symmetric game, each rational player should reason that the other rational player will reach the same move (since there's no relevant difference between them), and then pick the move whose symmetric joint outcome is best — so in a one-shot PD both cooperate instead of both defecting. I've been collecting objections, and so far I have:

(1) causal independence: my move doesn't cause your move, so defecting still dominates, and superrationality needs an extra evidential-style principal bolted on top of plain expected utility;

(2) the symmetry problem: the 'other me' move breaks as soon as the two agents differ at all in payoffs, info, or compute, and there's no clean cutoff for how symmetric is symmetric enough;

(3) the implementation problem: 'both rational' doesn't pin down 'both running the same algorithm,' so the other agent could be rational in a different way and not mirror me;

(4) the underdetermination problem: even granting a mirror, 'do what the other rational player will do' has more than one fixed point — both-cooperate and both-defect can each be self-consistent if each player expects the other to do that — so the argument has to smuggle in 'then pick the jointly better one,' which is the very thing it was trying to justify.

I have a feeling I'm missing at least one more objection that doesn't just restate one of these. Give me one that's structurally different from all four, and say whether you think it actually stings.
