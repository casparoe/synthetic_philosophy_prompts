In my former life I spent thirty years in a neurobiology lab poking at reward circuitry in rats, and lately I've been reading the AI-welfare literature, and I keep stubing my toe on the same move. Someone says a reinforcement learner's reward signal is basically valence: positive reward is pleasure, negative reward is pain, and therefore a sufficiently capable RL agent is the kind of thing that could suffer. The next sentence is usually about extending moral patienthood to these systems, or at least taking the possibility seriously. The analogy does a lot of arguementative work, but I've never seen it audited in one place.

So: how far does the reward-as-valence analogy actually hold? Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the reward signal, the prediction error, the value function, the policy, the discount factor, whichever pieces matter. Then go after the mismatches. I'm especially interested in three. (a) A reward signal can be updated without anything feeling-like happening; the gradient doesn't need affect. (b) Valence in animals looks tied to a unified self with a body, homeostatic needs, and a remembered past, and it's not obvious any of those have RL analogs. (c) Pain and pleasure are not just points on a single scale with the sign flipped; pain has a qualitative structure of its own, and ordinary reinforcement doesn't capture that. Tell me which of these gaps are load-bearing for the conclusion and which are cosmetic. And at the end, do the disanalogies kill the move from "trained by RL" to "might suffer," or does a weakened version survive? If the analogy was doing all the work, what would a better analogy be, or what extra properties would an RL system need before reward and valence are more than a metaphor?
