Here are my verdicts about when relying on an AI either prevents someone from acquiring understanding or erodes understanding they already had. I would like to know what principle, if any, captures them.

1. A child who has not yet been taught long division does all his division homework with a calculator. I do not think he comes to understand long division; the tool prevents the understanding the homework was meant to build.
2. A working mathematician who can do the relevant integration by hand uses a computer algebra system to check and accelerate her work. I do not think her mathematical understanding is eroded; the tool just saves time.
3. A strong club chess player stops analyzing his own games and instead always asks an engine which move it prefers and reads its evaluation. I think his chess understanding deteriorates.
4. A radiologist begins relying on an AI system to flag suspicious nodules and, over time, signs off on its findings without inspecting the images herself. I think her diagnostic understanding is eroded, even if patients benefit.
5. A translator whose second language was once fluent now mostly runs source texts through machine translation and post-edits the output. I do not think her command of the source language is eroded; she still has to read and correct the translation against the original.

Cases 2 and 4 trouble me: both are experienced professionals offloading part of their judgment to a system, yet I treat the mathematician and the radiologist differently. Is there one principle that captures all five verdicts? State the principle precisely, and say which of my verdicts, if any, it would force me to give up. Then apply it to a sixth case: a law student uses an AI to draft briefs but rewrites each section until he can explain every legal claim in his own words. Does that student understand the brief, or has his reliance on the AI undermined the understanding he should be developing?
