Here is an asymmetry in how people judge similarity. A portrait resembles its subject more than the subject resembles the portrait; a cover version resembles the original more than the original resembles the cover version; a satellite is more like a planet than a planet is like a satellite. In each case the comparison seems to run in one direction: the less canonical thing is measured against the more canonical reference point, and reversing the comparison sounds subtly wrong. Yet the standard geometry of meaning in a language model encodes words and sentences as vectors in an embedding space, and vector similarity—say, cosine similarity—is symmetric by construction: the similarity of A to B is exactly the similarity of B to A. So ordinary judgment and the geometry of meaning appear to disagree about whether similarity can have a direction. What, if anything, justifies the directional judgments? Are they tracking a real feature of semantic structure, or are they an artifact of salience, prototypicality, or conversational convention that an ideal representation of meaning should ignore? If they track something real, what non-symmetric structure would an embedding space have to add in order to represent it? If they do not, why do the asymmetric judgments nonetheless feel so natural, and should we treat them as an inconsistency to be eliminated rather than accommodated? Push the question through a hard case: a young writer's style resembles a canonical writer's style, while the canonical writer's style does not, in the same way, resemble the young writer's. Conclude by saying whether the asymmetry in similarity judgments should be kept, reversed, or given up.
