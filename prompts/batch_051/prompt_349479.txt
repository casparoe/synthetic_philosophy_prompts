I've been mulling over what makes someone guilty — not in court, just actually guilty — and I keep going back and forth on it. I think I almost believe a principle: what counts is what you say yes to in your head, not what actually happens. Here are my gut verdicts on some cases. Most feel solid, but a couple pull against each other. I want you to find the principle that gets all or most of them, and tell me which verdicts I'd have to drop.

Case 1. My cousin spent a month planning to burn down his ex-wife's shed: bought the gas can, scouted the fence, waited for a dry windy night. In the end he sat in his truck for three hours and couldn't do it, and went home. I think he's guilty in basically the same way an actual arsonist is — maybe not guilty of what it did to her, since nothing happened, but guilty of what he had already settled on in himself.

Case 2. A delivery driver hits a five-year-old who darts out between parked cars on an icy road. Perfect record, under the speed limit, not on his phone, not tired. It haunts him. I don't think he's guilty at all, not even a little, though I understand why he'll never forgive himself.

Case 3. A woman gets a sudden vivid flash of wanting to shove her mother-in-law down the stairs, complete with the image of where she'd land. She's horrified and it passes in a second. I don't think she's guilty of anything. It's like a bad thought that came to her, not a decision.

Case 4. Here's the one that bothers me. A married guy gets blackout drunk at a conference and, he's told the next day, kissed a coworker at the hotel bar. He remembers nothing, and the coworker didn't realize how far gone he was because he was walking and talking fine. I can't make myself blame him for cheating, because there's no real yes from him anywhere in it. But that means I blame my cousin, who never actually did the thing, and excuse this guy, who did the thing. That needs explaining.

Case 5. My nephew, who's 17, told me he "cheated on his girlfriend in his head" — he spent a whole weekend deliberately imagining it, knowing he'd do it for real if there were no consequences. I told him I don't think he owes his girlfriend an apology, since he never did anything to her, but if the question is whether he did something wrong in himself, then yes, I think he did. The wrong was in settling into it, not in the images.

Case 6. A hospice nurse is ordered to give a dying patient only a quarter of the morphine his doctor prescribed, as part of a cost-saving rule she personally thinks is cruel. She does it, hating herself, because she'll be fired otherwise and she believes the replacement would be worse. I blame her some, but I blame the people above her much more, and I'm honestly not sure her obeying was its own sin or just a sad situation.

Case 7. A woman was raised in a strict vegetarian home and taught from infancy that eating meat is as bad as killing a person. She stopped believing that years ago, and one day she eats a bacon sandwich just to see what the fuss is about. I don't think she's guilty at all. My cousin in case 1 does think she's guilty: he says a sin is a sin even if you think its fine. I can't feel that.

So the closest I can state my principle is: guilt follows the inward yes, not the outward act, and it follows what you honestly think is wrong, not what actually is wrong. But cases 4, 6 and 7 each put pressure on it in different ways. When you answer, please give me the principle in one sentence first — bottom line up front, no preamble, don't restate the question — then work through my cases and say which verdicts that principle forces me to change. Finally, apply it to a case I still can't settle: a man is completely certain, wrongly, that his wife has already decided to divorce him, so he sleeps with someone else genuinely believing the marriage is over. Is he guilty of adultery if he never consented to "cheating," only to having sex as a single man?
