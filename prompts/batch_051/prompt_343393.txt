In debates about long-run institutional change and AI alignment, people sometimes warn about value drift by invoking a version of the Ship of Theseus puzzle. An institution's or a civilization's values, on this picture, change the way a ship's planks are replaced one at a time over years of ordinary repair: each replacement is locally sensible and unremarkable, the ship keeps its name and seems to be the same vessel throughout, and yet at the end none of the original planks may remain, so the thing that arrives is a different ship in the sense that matters even though no one ever chose to replace the whole and no single moment of betrayal occurred. The analogy is then made to carry an argument: because gradual, locally tolerable value change can add up to a wholesale transformation, long-run value drift is a serious risk, and a future civilization or AI system could end up with values that people alive today would regard as bad without any dramatic takeover or announced revolution.
