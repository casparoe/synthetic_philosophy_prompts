I cover science and ideas for a living, and I'm a few drafts into a piece on why history-repeating-itself talk keeps coming back — the 1930s comparisons, rise-and-fall stories, all that. I've been trying to seperate genuinely cyclical views of history from linear-with-bumps views, and I've settled on four working criteria:

1. Recurrence of phases: the view says history runs through a recognizable sequence of stages, in roughly the same order, more than once.
2. Return: the end of a sequence restores something close to the starting state, so the process can begin again.
3. Internal engine: each stage produces the next through its own dynamics, not because of outside shocks or interventions.
4. No net direction: the cycles aren't adding up to anything — there's no accumulated progress or decline the cycle story tracks.

The case I keep getting stuck on is Polybius. As I understand him, he's the one who systematized anacyclosis: simple constitutions rotate through kingship, tyranny, aristocracy, oligarchy, democracy, mob rule, and then tip back into kingship. That looks like it hits all four critera, especially the internal engine — each regime breeds the next. But his actual point in the Roman material seems to be that Rome's mixed constitution stops the cycle, or slows it down enough that it's no longer a fate. The cycle describes the default for simple states, not the path of a well-mixed one.

So: is Polybius a cyclical historian on my criteria? Go through them one at a time and be honest about where the answer is yes-but or only-if-you-read-the-criterion-that-way. If the problem is with my criteria rather than with Polybius, tell me which one to fix and how. And give me a sense of how confident I should be in your verdict: does the factual record settle this, or is it a judgment call about definitions? Finally, don't be polite if I've rigged the setup — if I'm misdescribing Polybius or the whole cyclical-versus-linear sorting project is the problem, say so plainly instead of playing along.
