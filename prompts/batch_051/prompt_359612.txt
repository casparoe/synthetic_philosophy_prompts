I keep noticing that whenever US-China tensions flare up, somebody reaches for the Peloponnesian War and says "Thucydides trap." Usually they mean Graham Allison's version: when a rising power begins to scare an established one, the structural stress makes war the rule rather than the exception, and people throw around his case-file count as if it were a law of nature. But I've read enough of Thucydides to feel like that flattens him. He spends a huge amount of the book on the messy specifics -- the Corcyra debate, the Spartan assembly votes, the plague, the personalities, all the miscalculations -- and he's constantly separating the pretexts people gave from the deeper cause he thinks actually mattered. So my question: bring Thucydides back and show him how his name is being used in modern US-China strategy debates. Would he recognize the "trap" as his own argument, or would he say the modern version ignores most of what he actually wrote? Would he think the US and China are in an Athens-Sparta situation at all, given nukes, trade interdependence, no obvious Melian moment, and very different domestic regimes? And would he end up more or less fatalistic than the modern trap-talkers -- does his actual method push toward structural inevitability or toward contingency and misperception? Try to answer from what you know of his narrative and method, not the one-line version.
