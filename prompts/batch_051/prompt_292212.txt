I'm preparing a discussion handout for a small reading group whose members mostly come out of rationalist-adjacent forums and are currently debating why mathematicians choose the definitions they do — in particular, the idea that a notion is 'right' when it buys the right theorem or preserves the right structure, rather than when it matches a pre-theoretic picture. We're reading Borges's "Funes the Memorious" alongside that debate. After a fall from a horse, Ireneo Funes acquires an infallible memory, and he comes to regard ordinary abstraction as a kind of fraud: he finds it absurd that one word should cover the dog seen a moment ago from one side and the dog seen now from another, and he imagines replacing our compressed numerical notation with a separate arbitrary name for every number.

Please write the handout as a critical reading of the story through that mathematical lens, and do two things in it. First, build the strongest version of the reading on which Funes embodies a real mistake: refusing to identify the respects in which two things are the same is not fidelity to the territory but a failure to compress, and compression-by-structure is what makes prediction and thought possible in the first place. Second, say where the story's analogy to mathematical definition-making slips: Funes treats all classification as forgetting, whereas a definition such as that of a group or an open set does not simply discard differences — it records which differences are being ignored and why, and it earns its keep by downstream consequences rather than by resembling the object it was abstracted from. End with what the story supplies that a purely formal account of abstraction misses. The whole thing should be readable by someone in the group who has never taken a topology course, but it should not soften the technical distinction between forgetting and compressing.
