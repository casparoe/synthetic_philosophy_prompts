You are a grader for a philosophy of probability course. The exam question given to students was:

In Chapter VI of *A Treatise on Probability*, Keynes introduces the weight of the argument. Explain how the weight of the argument differs from the probability of a proposition, and address whether, on Keynes's view, the weight of the argument should be identified with a second-order probability—that is, the probability that one's first-order probability judgment is correct. The answer should connect this issue to Keynes's claim that probability is a logical relation between a proposition and the evidence for it, not a psychological degree of belief.

The student's answer is below. Grade it against the following rubric: accuracy about the distinction between probability and weight; accuracy about whether weight is a second-order probability; and accuracy about the relation to Keynes's logical conception of probability. Provide a numeric score out of 20 or a letter grade, and give point-by-point comments that justify the score. Your comments should identify exactly which claims earn or lose credit.

Student answer:

Keynes's main point in the Treatise is that probability is a logical relation between a proposition and the evidence for it, not a psychological feeling of confidence. Probability, so understood, is often non-numerical: two probabilities may be ordered in strength, but there is no guarantee that every probability can be assigned a real number. In Chapter VI he adds another dimension. A proposition can have the same probability relative to two different bodies of evidence, even though one body of evidence is much more complete than the other. Keynes calls this difference the weight of the argument. For example, suppose an urn contains black and white balls in an unknown proportion. The probability that the next draw is black is not numerically determinate; the weight of the argument is low because the available evidence is thin. If you then learn that the urn contains exactly half black and half white balls, the probability becomes determinate and the weight of the argument increases because the evidence is fuller. So probability and weight can vary at least partly independently: adding irrelevant evidence can increase weight without changing probability.

The real question is whether weight itself is a probability. Keynes does not explicitly say it is, but he is committed to it. Since probability is a logical relation, the claim that a body of evidence supports a hypothesis to a particular degree is itself a proposition, and one can ask how probable that proposition is on the evidence. That is exactly what the weight of the argument is: the probability that the first-order probability judgment is correct. If the evidence is thin, a rational person should be less confident in her estimate of the probability itself, and this lower confidence is a second-order probability. So weight is a probability, just of a higher order. This also shows why the distinction matters for Keynes's logical conception of probability: on a purely subjective view we could not make sense of being uncertain about our own degrees of belief, but on the logical view we can. The only remaining difficulty is that Keynes sometimes writes as if weight cannot be measured, but this must be a slip, because a second-order probability is still a probability and so must fall under the same principles as any other probability.
