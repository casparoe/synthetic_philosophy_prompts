Where I work we run an anomaly-detection system on our stamping presses, and the vendor documentation says the whole idea is to flag any reading that was generated by a different mechanism than the one that produced the training data. This week it lit up on press four, and two coworkers got into it over whether that's even a well-defined thing to say about what happened. I was just eating lunch nearby, and I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it.

Here's the exchange as best I remember it. (1) Sam said it's clearly a false positive: the press has the same ram, die, motor, and control program, doing the same stamping motion. The cooling oil is just warmer and thinner then usual because the plant got hot, which shifted the vibration spectrum a little. A parameter changed, not the mechanism. (2) Priya said "parts list" is doing too much work there. A mechanism is the parts plus how they interact, and once the oil viscosity drops enough, force transmission changes; the ram flexes and rebounds differently under load. Let parameters drift far enough and you get a different generating process, and saying it's still the same mechanism because the inventory didn't change is just refusing to draw the line. (3) Sam came back that if mechanism identity is read off the vibration signature, then "mechanism" becomes a fancy name for "vibration signature," and that can't be right. Two presses set up quite differently could produce overlapping spectra by coincidence, while the same press produces different spectra on a cold morning versus a hot afternoon, so output similarity isn't what makes mechanisms the same. (4) Priya said fine, but the parts-list test has the mirror-image problem: a bearing can wear down smoothly without a single part leaving the inventory, and at some point the press is no longer stamping the way it was, it's flexing and ringing and tearing itself apart by degrees. Every failure in history was a continuous parameter change right up until it wasn't, so the parameter-versus-mechanism line won't do either.

They never settled it, and since then the detector has flagged the same press twice more and been overridden both times. I'd like you to adjudicate the argument move by move: rate each numbered move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence or two justifying each rating, then say who if anyone is right about which specific point, and what both of them are missing. I'm less interested in the practical sensor settings than in the underlying conceptual question: what could make it true, or false, that a changed system is still running the same mechanism, and can an anomaly detector use "same mechanism" without first saying same-mechanism-for-what.
