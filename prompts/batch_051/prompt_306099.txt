I've been going back and forth with a friend in a church history seminar about the C. A. Briggs heresy trial, and I keep feeling like the standard tellings make it out to be modern scholarship vs. stubborn dogmatism, which misses most of what's actually interesting. I want you to analyze the case through the lens of the conflict between the historical-critical method and faith, but honestly and in both directions.

Just so we're working from the same facts: in the early 1890s Charles Augustus Briggs, a Presbyterian minister and professor at Union Theological Seminary in New York, defended the application of historical criticism to the Bible. He argued that the Bible contains errors, that Moses probably didn't write the Pentateuch, and that the book of Isaiah is the work of multiple authors from different periods. He framed these as honest results of historical study and insisted they didn't have to destroy the idea that scripture is revelation. The Presbytery of New York tried him for heresy in 1892 and acquitted him, but on appeal the General Assembly defrocked him in 1893. UTS responded by rescinding the General Assembly's power to veto its faculty appointments, making itself independent, and Briggs stayed on. Within a few decades, much of what got him condemned—multiple authorship of Isaiah, for instance—had become ordinary in mainline Protestant scholarship.

What I want is a careful mapping, not a verdict on who was right about the Bible. Treat the historical-critical method and faith as two different stances toward scripture: the first reads it as historically situated human documents with sources, redactions, agendas, and errors; the second reads it as revelation whose authority isn't dissolved by any of that. What does that lens explain about what each side was actually afraid of? Where does it misdescribe the trial (institutional authority, who gets to train ministers, denominational politics, Briggs's own manner)? And then run it in reverse: does the episode show that historical criticism is intrinsically corrosive to faith, or that the explosion was pretty contingent on how and where the criticism arrived? I'd rather you say where the lens goes silent then force it to speak.
