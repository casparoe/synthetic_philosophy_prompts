Two agents, A and B, are placed in separate, causally isolated rooms after being told the full setup. No signal, resource, or force can pass between the rooms, and neither agent's choice can causally affect the other. Each must choose Pay or Pass. Pay costs the chooser 3 units and delivers 10 units to the other agent; Pass costs nothing and delivers nothing. Each agent knows all of this, and each knows that both are running the same deterministic decision procedure on the same information. Consider this argument:

(1) Since A and B run the same deterministic procedure on the same information, their choices must coincide: either both Pay or both Pass.
(2) Therefore, conditional on A's paying, B pays; and conditional on A's passing, B passes.
(3) So A's expected payoff for paying is −3 + 10 = 7, while A's expected payoff for passing is 0.
(4) A rationally ought to take the option with the higher expected payoff.
(5) Hence A rationally ought to pay, even though A's paying cannot causally bring about B's paying.
Conclusion: a rational agent can be required to act on a correlation it cannot causally influence, which is the core idea behind acausal trade.

Is this argument valid? If not, identify the exact step that fails. Does the answer turn on whether 'expected payoff' is read causally or evidentially, and if so, which steps are true under which reading? Can the argument be repaired without simply assuming the disputed conclusion?
