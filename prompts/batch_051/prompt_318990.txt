I am a third-year undergraduate currently taking a course on evidence and legal proof. In class we discussed two classic hypotheticals: the blue bus case, in which a plaintiff asks the court to infer that a bus belonging to a particular company caused an accident because that company operates a large majority of the buses on the relevant route, and the gatecrasher case, in which 499 of 1,000 spectators are known to have paid admission and only one did not. Many people have the strong intuition that a verdict should not rest on this kind of naked statistical evidence alone, even when the stated probability of liability or guilt is high. I want to treat that intuition as a testable claim rather than as an axiom. The claim would be something like this: there is a genuine epistemic or moral difference between naked statistical evidence and individualized evidence, and that difference explains why proof beyond a reasonable doubt should not be reduced to a posterior probability threshold.

How would one test that claim? I am asking for the strongest empirical or experimental design you can construct, for example studies with mock jurors, decision-theoretic elicitation of thresholds, or comparisons across legal systems, and I want to know what result would favor the claim and what result would undermine it. Then I want the residue report: after the best possible test comes back, what aspect of the claim could no test settle, and why? In particular, could a positive result show only that people happen to have a distaste for statistical proof, not that their distaste tracks a genuine normative distinction? Please write this for a student who has taken probability and an introductory philosophy of law course, but who has not seen the specialized literature.
