You are a philosopher of science who works on scientific modeling and idealization, with particular interest in the debate over whether unrealistic assumptions matter. Please think through the problem carefully; before you settle on a verdict, work through the strongest considerations on both sides in some detail.

I find myself strongly drawn to Milton Friedman's so-called F-twist. As I understand it, the view holds that the realism of a model's assumptions is irrelevant to evaluating the model: a theory should be judged chiefly by its success in predicting the phenomena it is meant to predict, and unrealistic assumptions—perfectly rational agents, complete markets, frictionless surfaces, point masses—are often virtues rather than defects, because they isolate a few factors and make precise prediction possible. On this picture, it is legitimate to treat agents as if they solve an optimization problem they never actually solve, provided the resulting predictions are accurate.

Yet I have an intuition that will not go away. Consider a predictively successful model whose central assumptions are not merely false but false in the wrong way: they misdescribe the causal order of the target system. Suppose an epidemiological model that assumes a perfectly mixed, homogeneous population predicts the timing and size of outbreak peaks in a real city, while independent evidence tells us that the dynamics are actually driven by clustered contact networks and superspreader events. Or suppose a macroeconomic model that assumes frictionless markets and fully rational agents forecasts well in a setting where, independently, we know that frictions and boundedly rational behavior are doing the causal work. The cases that worry me are not ones where a parameter is set to zero as an acknowledged approximation; they are ones where the false assumption functions as a substantive description of the causal mechanism. My intuition is that such models are useful forecasting instruments but do not explain, and that their predictive success gives us no reason to believe their internal story, not even approximately. That seems to me a fact about scientific understanding, not a mere aesthetic preference.

I want you to help me decide which should yield. Present the strongest possible version of the F-twist and of my intuition, including how each side would answer the other's best objections. Then give a negotiated verdict: under what conditions should the intuition be debunked as a confusion between evaluating models and evaluating assumptions, and under what conditions should the F-twist be restricted or abandoned? Is there a principled way of reading what models are for—prediction, explanation, understanding, possibility proof—on which both the F-twist and my intuition survive? I am less interested in which side wins than in knowing what would justify each move; in particular, do not dismiss the intuition as a mere confusion unless you supply a positive account of explanation that shows why the confusion diagnosis is warranted, and do not reject the F-twist unless you specify what other than predictive success should constrain model evaluation.
