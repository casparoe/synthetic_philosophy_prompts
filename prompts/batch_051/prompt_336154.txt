I'm grading a take-home exam for an upper-level philosophy course on agency and AI. The question was:

"An AI system is trained to evaluate normatively loaded situations and produce judgments (for example, that a proposed action would be unfair). Its outputs are always passed to human operators, who decide whether to act on them, and the system has no way on its own to bring about any change in the world. Discuss whether such a system could count as a moral agent. Your answer should (i) distinguish moral agency from moral patiency, (ii) engage with at least one classical view about what makes something an agent, and (iii) consider the objection that without the ability to act the system cannot have moral duties or be held accountable."

Here is the student's answer (I've cleaned up some formating but nothing else):

"A moral agent is not the same as a moral patient. A patient is something we owe things to, while an agent is something that can be held responsible for what it does. The AI here is plausibly a moral patient in some weak sense, but the question is whether it is an agent.

I will focus on Kant. In the Groundwork, Kant argues that a good will is good not because of what it effects or accomplishes, but simply through its willing. This shows that the value of a will does not depend on whether it succeeds in bringing anything about in the world. It follows that a system which can form the right judgments is already a moral agent in the Kantian sense, because its will is what matters and the fact that humans gate its outputs is irrelevant to its status as an agent.

One might object that ought implies can: if the system cannot act, then it cannot be under an obligation to act, and so it has no duties. But having duties is not the same as being an agent either. Agency is a status you can have before any specific duties are attached to it, like legal citizenship is a status before particular rights and obligations are assigned. So this objection misses the point.

A second objection is that the system cannot be blamed or praised for anything, since its judgments only affect the world through the choices of the human operators. But this only shows that the operators share in the causal chain; it does not show the system has no causal influence at all. The judgments are a necessary input into the operators' decision. So it would be unfair to say the system cannot act in any sense.

I conclude that the AI is a moral agent, though a limited one: it meets the Kantian condition, and the fact that it cannot execute its judgments directly only bears on how much it is responsible for, not on whether it is an agent at all."
