I'm a backend engineer and I've ended up owning the "knowledge attribution" module in a data-audit tool at my company. The module's job is to say, after an incident, what a given employee account should be treated as having known at a particular time, because that determines whether something counts as a reportable exposure under our internal policy. Right now the module is basically a knowledge graph with a Datalog-flavored forward-chaining inference engine on top: if any record a user touched plus the rules in the graph imply some fact, we stamp that fact as "known by the user at time t." This is producing nonsense in both directions. It'll say a support agent who saw a ticket with a customer's zip code "knew" the customer's city because there's a zip-to-city mapping loaded in the graph. And it'll say people knew logical filler like "the ticket is either closed or not closed," because the inference engine can derive that from nothing and so every user gets stamped with it. I gather this is basically the problem of logical omniscience in epistemic logic, and that people have tried fixes like adding an "awareness" requirement or bounding how many inference steps you're allowed to model. But I dont know that literature beyond a few blog posts, so I don't know which ideas are solid and which are just somebody's preferred patch.

Could you turn whatever's actually usable from that into a decision procedure our on-call engineers can run on a candidate derived fact, to decide whether to list it as known to a human user at time t in an incident report? This happens a few times a quarter, the engineer has maybe twenty minutes per fact, and the report may get read by lawyers, so the procedure has to be honest about where it's making a judgement call rather than resolving one. The engineer on call hasn't read any of the formal stuff and isn't going to, so the deliverable has to be a plain sequence of questions about what records were touched, what rules exist in the graph, what the person was trained on, and so on, with what counts as a sufficient answer to move on, where the procedure should stop with a plain "do not attribute," and where it should punt to a person because it's become a policy call. Then run it on three cases: (1) a support agent looked at a screen showing a customer's zip code, and the company graph has a rule mapping zip codes to city names; (2) an engineer read a schema file from which, combined with a few other files, the last four digits of a customer's SSN follow, but only by a derivation nobody would actually do in their head; (3) a fact that follows from something an employee saw by applying a rule they'd been explicitly trained on, though we can't show they registered it at the time. Include at least one case where your own procedure says "do not attribute" even though our compliance lead would really want it attributed. And while you're laying it out: please flag which parts rest on settled ground among people who work on this problem and which parts are genuinely contested, instead of presenting one school's fix as the consensus.
