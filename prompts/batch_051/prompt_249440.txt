Consider the following passage from the opening of Parmenides' poem, in John Burnet's translation from his *Early Greek Philosophy* (1920). The goddess is speaking to the narrator:

"Welcome, O youth, that comest to my abode on the car that bears thee tended by immortal charioteers! It is no ill chance, but right and justice that has sent thee forth to travel on this way. Far, indeed, does it lie from the beaten track of men! Meet it is that thou shouldst learn all things, as well the unshaken heart of well-rounded truth, as the opinions of mortals in which is no true belief at all. Yet none the less shalt thou learn these things also,—how passing right through all things one should judge the things that seem to be."

Now suppose a counterfactual divergence. From roughly 450 BCE onward, the characteristically Eleatic conclusion—that what genuinely is must be ungenerated, imperishable, whole, and altogether without plurality or change—ceases to be a disputed philosophical thesis and becomes a shared axiom of educated Greek intellectual life. For the next century, no major school publicly rejects it. Natural philosophy, in the ordinary sense of giving explanations of a changing, many-membered world, is thereby foreclosed: the world of change is officially the realm of mortal opinion, not of knowledge.

Give your best reasoned prediction of how Greek intellectual culture develops over the hundred years from 450 to 350 BCE under this constraint. I want the chain of reasoning, not a story: trace which forms of enquiry lose prestige and which expand into the space left behind. In particular, say whether you expect mathematics to rise in status, whether ethical and political thought develops faster or more slowly once nature can no longer be used as a foundation for norms, and what happens to medicine, astronomy, and engineering—all of which still have practical work to do even if their objects are condemned as mere appearances. Also say whether you expect the Eleatic position itself to be sharpened into a rigorous logic of being, hollowed out into an ethics of detachment, or gradually transformed into something else. For each major prediction, distinguish what follows robustly from the new orthodoxy itself from what depends on further assumptions about institutions, literacy, political life, or who has the authority to police the axiom. State which predictions you are most and least confident of, and say what would have to hold for the whole intellectual equilibrium to break down.
