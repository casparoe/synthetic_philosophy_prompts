Plea bargaining in most criminal justice systems has roughly this structure. A defendant charged with a serious offense is offered a substantially reduced charge or sentence in exchange for a guilty plea and a waiver of trial, and the offer is explicitly backed by the threat that, if the defendant rejects it and is then convicted at trial, the sentence will be far more severe. A judge may ask whether the plea is knowing and voluntary, but the fact that the defendant acted under intense pressure is not by itself treated as invalidating the agreement. The same legal systems, however, generally refuse to enforce ordinary contracts made under duress: a signature obtained by a threat is not treated as binding consent. So the law appears to treat a guilty plea extracted by a threat as valid, while treating a contract signature extracted by a threat as void.

What theory of consent, coercion, or obligation does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguishing what plea bargaining strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Some philosophers have thought the practice makes sense only on something like the Hobbesian view that covenants entered into through fear are nonetheless binding, and that the accused in the dock is in a position structurally akin to someone in the state of nature who covenants out of fear. Is that right, or is the practice philosophically underdetermined among several competing theories? Finally, suppose we replaced the operative theory with the view that duress vitiates consent whenever the threat leaves the agent with no reasonable alternative to pleading guilty. What would have to change in the practice, and would the resulting institution be internally coherent?
