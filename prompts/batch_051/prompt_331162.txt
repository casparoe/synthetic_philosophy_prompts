Write a decision tree that sorts a respondent into a position on conditionals, using the contrast between indicative and counterfactual conditionals as the central axis. The audience is advanced undergraduates who know the material conditional but not the formal semantics literature; aim for six or seven leaves and no more than eight branch questions. Build the tree from concrete judgments first, not from theory labels: every branch node should be a sentence or mini-scenario the respondent accepts or rejects, phrased so it can be answered without knowing which view it leads to. The leaves should include at least the truth-functional account, the strict conditional, a Stalnaker-style variably strict semantics with a unique closest antecedent-world, a Lewis-style variably strict semantics that allows ties, and an Adams-style view on which conditionals lack truth conditions and express conditional probabilities or assertibility conditions; add further leaves only if the logical space demands them. Use these cases, or close variants, as branch tests: an indicative and a counterfactual pair about an order that was in fact given by the mayor, such that the respondent accepts 'If the mayor did not order it, someone else in her office did' but rejects 'If the mayor had not ordered it, someone else in her office would have'; a Sobel-style sequence in which a second sentence strengthens the antecedent of the first and reverses the consequent, such as someone who would have moved for a job but would not have moved if a parent had fallen ill; and a pair of counterfactuals about two people who were not compatriots, where neither sentence about who would have adopted the other's nationality seems forced and it is open whether exactly one of them must be true. Work from the full space of answer combinations, label the empty combinations, and say for each whether it is empty because the resulting position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; flag any pair of leaves the questions cannot separate, and say what further case would separate them. Also consider whether the tree needs a leaf for someone who accepts the Sobel-style sequence because the first conditional changes the context in which the second is interpreted, and check whether your questions can distinguish that view from a variably strict one. Write the whole instrument in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write an email. End by stating which branch point you are least confident in, and what sort of example or argument would persuade you to redraw that part of the tree.
