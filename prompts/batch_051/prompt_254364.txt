I'm a journalist who covers philosophy and cognitive science for a general-interest magazine, and I've got a side gig running a 90-minute workshop at a local library. It'll be about 12 adults, mostly 30s to 50s, some who've read a bit of philosophy but most haven't. I want the session to be on Kant's view that we only have indirect duties to animals — that cruelty to a dog is wrong because it damages our capacity for compassion toward humans, not because the dog itself has a claim on us. The idea feels both counterintuitive and weirdly plausible, and I want participants to actually run into that tension instead of hearing me explain it up front.

Could you design an activity or small game that gets them to feel the pull of that view? Something like a structured choice where they have to rank or allocate moral concern under constraints, or a role-play where a commission has to decide what gets protection and why. There's a passage I keep coming back to from Kant's Doctrine of Virtue. I don't trust myself to quote it exactly, but the gist is that Kant argues there that tormenting an animal deadens a shared feeling for its suffering, and that this feeling is a natural predisposition that happens to be very useful for morality in our dealings with other human beings. I can paraphrase it for the group later but I don't want to open with it.

The activity should end with a debrief that maps what happened onto Kant's distinction between direct and indirect duty, what it costs to resist the view, and what the view still leaves out. No lecture at the start. And it would be great if you could flag what will predictably go wrong — like someone blurting out "so Kant thinks kicking a dog is only bad because it might make me kick my neighbor?" — and how to respond without strawmanning either side.
