So my friend and I spent like two hours last night going back and forth about epistocracy, and there's one thing neither of us could settle. He was pushing the line that if we require a type rating to fly a plane and a license to perform surgery, we should require some minimal competence to vote, because the stakes are obviously higher. I said that felt obscene to me, but I couldn't say why. Then I realized the weird asymmetry is actually the other way. In medicine we don't let expertise override consent: a surgeon can't remove your appendix just because she knows better than you and you've got the risks all wrong from a Facebook video. Your refusal wins even when its flat-earth-level ignorant. Yet in an election the least informed person's vote binds everyone else just as much as the best informed person's, and the expert gets no veto. So which is the anomaly: how we treat knowledge at the ballot box, or how we treat knowledge at the consent form? Maybe the distinction is just that surgery is about your own body and voting is about everyone, but that seemed too quick because a medical decision can also kill other people (say, untreated TB). Give me the strongest principle you can find that either dissolves the asymmetry or shows one side is right, and then an honest verdict on whether it actually works.
