Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether the Appel–Haken computer proof of the Four Color Theorem is a proof in the same sense as an ordinary surveyable proof, and by the end I suspected the argument was really about a cleaner distinction: procedural fluency versus understanding. I want you to help me work that distinction through the episode itself.

The episode, as I understand it, is this. In 1976, Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken proved the Four Color Theorem by reducing it to the claim that every relevant configuration contains one member of a finite unavoidable set, and then having a computer verify that each of those configurations — nearly two thousand, by the standard accounting — is reducible. No single human could survey the full verification. A good part of the mathematical community accepted that the theorem had been established, but many also felt that something important was missing: the proof seemed to certify that four colors suffice without giving anyone the sort of understanding that a traditional proof gives. Later proofs simplified the case analysis but remained computer-assisted, and the entire proof was eventually completely formalized in a proof assistant.

The lens I want to use is the distinction between two things that usually travel together. One is procedural correctness: the ability to check that each step of a derivation follows from previous steps or from valid rules, whether a human or a machine does the checking. The other is understanding: grasping why the theorem must hold, being able to state the central idea in a way that compresses the proof, and having the argument hang together as a surveyable whole. My friend's view was that once the steps have been checked, understanding is a psychological extra, not a mathematical requirement. My own view was that an un-surveyable derivation is a certification that the result is true rather than a proof in the epistemically significant sense.

Analyze the Appel–Haken episode through that distinction, disciplining the mapping carefully. First work from the theory to the case: who or what are the agents — the individual mathematician, the refereeing process, the community's shared standards, the software? What did each face, and what would the theory have said they should accept, reject, or reserve judgment about? Second, test the fit: which features of the episode does the procedural-fluency/understanding distinction explain well, and which does it misdescribe or miss? I want the awkward features taken seriously, not waved away — for example, the possibility that the real unease was partly about software reliability, about who can certify correctness when expertise shifts from mathematics to programming, about the design of the discharging algorithm, or about the new division of labor in verification. Third, run the episode back against the theory: does it confirm that procedural correctness and understanding can come apart, or does it show that the distinction is too crude — because, for instance, one might understand the proof at the level of the method and the architecture of the case split even though no one holds all the individual cases in mind? Conclude with what, if anything, your analysis leaves unexplained, and say where the lens is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
