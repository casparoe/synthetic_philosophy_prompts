For the final session of my graduate seminar on the metaphysics of causation, I have been running a year-long adversarial collaboration between two faculty colleagues: one who defends an event-based counterfactual theory of causation in the spirit of David Lewis, and one who defends a variable-based interventionist theory in the spirit of James Woodward. The collaboration has worked because each side has stopped attacking a caricature of the other, and they now want a single document both can sign. I would like you to draft that joint statement.

The document should have three sections.

First, a numbered list of agreed claims. These must be substantive, not diplomatic or vacuously worded. They should capture what the two camps genuinely share: their common rejection of pure regularity theories and of merely probabilistic association as an account of causation; their shared commitment to the thought that causal claims are answerable to how things would differ under some suitably directed kind of variation; their joint recognition that directed graphs and structural equations are, at minimum, an indispensable working representation of multiply realizable causal systems; and their shared acknowledgment that token causation, type causation, preemption, omissions, and double prevention each put real pressure on any theory. Where a formulation is agreeable only because it is vague, tighten it or mark it as still unresolved.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should state: the issue; the claim as the counterfactual theorist would word it; the claim as the interventionist would word it; and a verdict on whether that residual difference is substantive or merely verbal, with a sentence explaining why. At a minimum, the table must address these four matters: whether the notion of an intervention can be specified without already drawing on causal notions; whether token causation or type causation is the more fundamental target of analysis; how each account handles actual causation in late preemption, early preemption, and double prevention once the stock counterexamples have been set aside; and whether the correctness of a causal model presupposes a privileged choice of variable set, grain, and modularity, and what, if anything, makes such a choice non-arbitrary.

Third, a section on cruxes. Identify the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side toward the other. For each crux, specify a concrete case or class of cases that both parties accept as probative, and record in advance what each side predicts about it. Do not waste space on cases both sides already handle identically. Where you judge that a part of the dispute was verbal, say which part and what reformulation dissolves it; where it is substantive, say what would have to turn out to be true for one side rather than the other to be vindicated.

Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; refer to the positions and authors by name and view only. The document's value lies entirely in the precision of the agreed claims, the fairness of each side's own wording, and the discriminating power of the cruxes.
