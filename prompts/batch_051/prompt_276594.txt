I'm in a seminar on arguments for and against theism and I've been chewing on the hiddenness argument. I think I've found a version of it that proves too much, and now I'm stuck about whether the original argument still works.

Here's the argument as I'd state it.

(1) If a person A genuinely and deeply loves a person B, then A will not allow B to remain in nonresistant ignorance of A's existence and love, whenever A can remedy that ignorance without harming or coercing anyone.
(2) To remedy B's ignorance is to make it so that B can reasonably believe A exists and loves B — not to force the belief or make doubt impossible.
(3) If God exists, God is omnipotent and omniscient, and could remedy every person's nonresistant ignorance of God's existence and love without harming or coercing anyone.
(4) So, if God exists and deeply loves every person, there would be no nonresistant nonbelievers.
(5) But there are nonresistant nonbelievers: people who sincerely seek God and still, through no obvious fault of their own, can't reasonably believe.
(6) Therefore God does not exist.

What bothers me is that the principle doing the work in (1) seems to apply just as well to ordinary human relationships, where it proves way too much. Consider a mother whose adult daughter is going through a mental health crisis and has become convinced her mother never loved her. The daughter isn't resisting the relationship out of spite; she's just ill. The mother could do more than she does — leave voicemails, write letters, show up at her door — and in many cases that would give the daughter some reason to believe she's loved, even if it wouldn't fix everything. By (1), if the mother doesn't do what she can to remedy her daughter's ignorance, she doesn't genuinely love her. But plenty of mothers in that spot don't do everything they could, becuase they're exhausted, or scared of being screamed at, or sure it'll make things worse. And it would be monstrous to say they don't love their daughters.

So either the argument overgeneralizes and I should give up (1), which kills the original argument too, or there's some asymmetry between God's case and the mother's case that I'm not seeing, or one of the other steps fails. Where does it actually go wrong? I'd most like to keep both the conclusion that God doesn't exist and the claim that those mothers love their daughters, but I want to know what I'm paying for that.
