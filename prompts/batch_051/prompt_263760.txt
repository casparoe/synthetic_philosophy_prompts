I am the director of clinical quality at a regional hospital system, and I want a standardized way for our staff to decide whether a prognostic risk score applies to a particular patient. We use such scores before several recurring decisions: a readmission-risk score during discharge planning, a heart-attack-risk score in primary care, and a sepsis-risk score at triage. Each score has been validated in a large population and performs well in the group it was derived from, so this is not the usual problem of an unvalidated or biased model. The problem is that a score is always a frequency attached to a class, and a patient belongs to many classes at once. A 48-year-old patient is simultaneously a smoker, a long-distance runner, a recent immigrant, a woman with well-controlled diabetes, and a resident of a postcode with poor follow-up care; the base rates for these overlapping classes do not agree with one another or with the score's output. I am told philosophers call this the reference class problem, but no one has told us how to act under it. Clinicians either trust the score naively or disregard it on grounds they cannot articulate, and the chart reviewers cannot distinguish the two cases afterwards.

Please write a decision procedure a clinician can actually run in five minutes, before acting on a score, that decides which reference class should govern the score's use for this patient, or decides that the score should not be used for this patient at all. The procedure must be a sequence of questions, each with a rule for what counts as a sufficient answer. It must mark the points where the decision is a value judgment rather than a statistical one, and it must identify any step that would only hide judgment inside a rule instead of removing it. It should say where the procedure halts and hands the matter to a clinician, but clinical judgment may not function as a loophole; the procedure has to narrow what a defensible exercise of that judgment would be. The users are clinicians, not statisticians or philosophers; the procedure will be used dozens of times a day; the stakes range from a missed phone call to a fatal deterioration at home; and every decision must be defensible to a quality-review committee.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, a 70-year-old man whose heart-attack score is just above the treatment threshold, but only because the score's table classifies him by a broad age band; every other item in his chart is unremarkable, and he is a lifelong non-smoker with normal blood pressure. Second, a 52-year-old woman whose readmission score falls below the high-risk cutoff, but who belongs to a small, well-documented subgroup of patients on the same medication whose readmission rate is more than twice the score's estimate. Third, a 35-year-old patient flagged high-risk for sepsis at triage by a score derived from a population with a very different age distribution and comorbidity burden. In at least one of these cases, the procedure should yield an answer the institution will not like, and you should say why it is nonetheless the right one. End by stating what the procedure cannot do.
