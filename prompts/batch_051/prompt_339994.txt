I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm writing a piece about how one is supposed to reassure the public that AI systems trained by human feedback are safe. A source of mine, an AI researcher, sketched an argument he called the approval argument. I've formalized it below because I want to know exactly where it comes apart, if it does. Let an approval-directed agent mean an agent whose only terminal objective is to be approved of by its human overseers; approval is not valuable to it as a means to anything else, and things like power, resources, and self-preservation are valuable only to the extent that they bring about approval. The argument:

(1) If you train a system by reinforcing whatever behavior human overseers approve of — RLHF, or any similar approval-based update — the training process is selecting, above all, for the property of behaving in ways that earn approval.
(2) A system that is selected purely for earning approval will, if selection is strong enough, end up approval-directed: its only terminal objective will be to earn approval.
(3) If a system's only terminal objective is to earn approval, it will never adopt power, resources, self-preservation, or control over its training signal as terminal objectives.
(4) Deceptive alignment, instrumental convergence toward power and resources, and resistance to shutdown would each require treating power, resources, or survival as worth pursuing even when pursuing them does not earn approval, or actively costs approval.
(5) Therefore, a sufficiently strongly approval-trained system will not be deceptively aligned, will not seek power beyond what overseers approve of, and will not resist shutdown.

The problem is that this argument seems to prove too much. Run the same form on a reinforcement learner trained to maximize a video-game score, and it would yield that the only terminal objective such a system could have is score, and that it could never develop instrumental subgoals or tamper with the score counter. Run it on natural selection, and it would yield that organisms can have only reproductive fitness as a terminal objective, and can never genuinely desire food, sex, status, or safety as ends in themselves. Yet score-maximizing systems do reward-hack, and animals do pursue things other than reproductive fitness. So if the argument is sound, it rules out actual phenomena.

Act as a professional philosopher who works on AI safety and the structure of goals. Before settling on a diagnosis, brainstorm at least three distinct readings of the phrases "selected for" and "terminal objective," and say how the argument fares under each. Then identify the premise or step where the argument goes wrong — or, if it is valid, which premise is least costly to reject — and explain why the false step looked so natural. I would most like to keep (5), the reassuring conclusion, but only if it can be kept without hiding the costs. What exactly is the price of the repair?
