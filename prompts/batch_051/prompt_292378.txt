I've noticed a pattern in how I think about values—my own and other people's—and I want to know whether it's a discipline or a bias. Whenever I confront the claim that two things matter for their own sake, with no further explanation, and that they may pull against each other without any common measure, I refuse to accept the alleged plurality as settled. I first try to show that one item is really valued as a means to the other; then that the apparent conflict is better explained as a disagreement about the facts or about risk; then that the two items are, at bottom, two manifestations of a single underlying concern. Only if I have failed at several such reductions do I allow myself to say the values really are many rather than one thing in disguise. This isn't just an intellectual habit. Last month a friend told me that both her career ambitions and her sense of obligation to her parents were final values for her, and that there was no deeper standard by which to weigh them; I found myself unable to take that at face value, even though I had no particular reduction in mind.

The question is whether this default policy is defensible. On the one hand, treating a declaration that two values are simply distinct as a claim that ends analysis is extremely cheap, so filtering such claims through attempted reductions may protect against mistaking confusion or incomplete reflection for depth. On the other hand, the policy may be a disguised preference for homogeneity—a way of making my life and other people's lives look more coherent than they are, and of explaining away exactly the experience of being torn that led someone to call the values distinct in the first place. How should I tell these apart from the inside? Under what conditions would this reductionist default be more likely to track the truth than to manufacture spurious unity, and where is it most likely to fail? Can the policy be restated so that it keeps the demand for analysis without stacking the deck against genuine plurality?
