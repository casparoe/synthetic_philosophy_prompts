I am a legal officer in the Office of the Prosecutor at a special international tribunal established by the states that won a recent conflict. The tribunal's statute adopts, word for word, the definition of crimes in Article 6 of the Charter of the International Military Tribunal (the Nuremberg Charter). The passage that matters reads:

"The following acts, or any of them, are crimes coming within the jurisdiction of the Tribunal for which there shall be individual responsibility:

(a) CRIMES AGAINST PEACE: namely, planning, preparation, initiation or waging of a war of aggression, or a war in violation of international treaties, agreements or assurances, or participation in a common plan or conspiracy for the accomplishment of any of the foregoing;"

The accused is the former president of the defeated state. For twenty-two months before any conventional hostilities, his military cyber command ran Operation Still Water: a sustained programme of intrusions into the civilian infrastructure of three coalition members. No uniformed soldier crossed a frontier and no missile was fired during that period, but the intrusions shut down hospital power systems, water treatment plants and air-traffic control, and the tribunal's own fact-finding chamber attributes around three thousand civilian deaths to those outages. The conduct occurred before the tribunal's statute entered into force.

The prosecutor wants to charge him with the waging of a war of aggression under Article 6(a). The defence has given notice of two objections. First, that the words "war" and "aggression" in the Nuremberg Charter presuppose armed force — armies crossing frontiers, bombardments, declared hostilities — not computer intrusions, however destructive. Secondly, that applying Article 6(a) to conduct completed before the statute existed repeats the retroactivity problem levelled at Nuremberg and Tokyo, and this time cannot be answered by saying the conduct was already criminal under pre-existing international law; for a tribunal set up by the victors, the defence argues, the only honest course is a narrow reading.

Write the confidential opinion I should give the prosecutor. Explain briefly how a plain-words reading, a purposive reading, and a reading based on what the drafters in 1945 would have said if asked would each handle the first objection, then commit to the reading you would actually be willing to defend before the court and argue for it. Take a definite position on the second objection as well, rather than canvassing the alternatives and leaving the choice to me. Mark honestly the points where the words of the clause run out and an argument has to be made from principle or from later practice. I need to know whether the charge can be laid and defended in good faith, not a menu of possible positions.
