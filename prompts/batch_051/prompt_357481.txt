I am a retired population biologist, and for most of my career I studied how animal populations grow, regulate themselves, and respond to harvesting and habitat loss. Since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy, and I have found that my intuitive judgments about whether a larger population is a better population are more tangled than I would like. I am not asking you to survey the literature. I am asking you to take my reactions to a series of concrete cases as data and tell me what principle, if any, captures them.

Here are the reactions I am confident about.

1. The wanted child. A couple wants a child, has the resources to raise one, and can reasonably expect the child to have a life well worth living; no one else's welfare is affected. I think the world with the child is a better world than the world without the child, though I would not say the couple is required to have the child.

2. The doubled city. A city of a million people is flourishing. Imagine its population and built environment could be exactly duplicated on a second planet, with the original residents entirely unaffected and the new residents having lives just as good. I do not think the two-city outcome is better than the one-city outcome. It is more of the same, not an improvement.

3. The enormous but meager population. One outcome contains an enormous number of people whose lives are only just worth living; another contains a much smaller number of people whose lives are excellent. The total welfare in the enormous population is much greater. I judge the smaller, excellent population to be the better world, and I would sooner abandon a principle that ranks the enormous population higher than abandon that verdict.

4. The single unit of help. I have one unit of a resource. I can give it to an existing person and raise her from a good life to a very good life, or I can use it to bring into existence a new person whose life will be positive but less good than the existing person's improved life would have been. I regard helping the existing person as more important.

5. The reluctant species. A species of animal is declining toward extinction. Its members have lives that are, on average, only just worth living. If it disappears, no other species and no human practice is much affected. I find the extinction a loss, but I cannot honestly locate that loss in the summed welfare of the species' future members. Something else seems to be doing the work.

6. The asymmetry of creation. If a child would be born into a life of suffering, I think conceiving that child is wrong, and refraining from conceiving it is good in a straightforward sense. If a child would be born into a happy life, I do not think refraining from conceiving that child is bad at all. My reactions to the two cases are not symmetric.

7. The expansion that worsens some. A policy would increase both average and total welfare by adding many high-welfare people, but it would slightly worsen the lives of some existing people who are already badly off. I judge the policy wrong.

I keep going back and forth on a further case. A city could build a new neighborhood that attracts many new residents with good lives, while slightly lowering the existing residents from very good to still-good lives; total welfare would rise substantially. I genuinely cannot decide whether the city would be better after the change.

I have tried, and failed, to find one principle that yields all seven confident verdicts. Please do three things. First, state the candidate principles that would most naturally explain the pattern, being as precise as you can about how each clause of each principle is motivated by one of my cases. Second, identify which of my verdicts sit together and which are in tension, and say what each candidate principle would predict about the undecided city case. Third, recommend the smallest set of principles, with their scope restrictions, that best accommodates the cases, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts that set would force me to drop or reinterpret, and why. Please write your answer in American English.
