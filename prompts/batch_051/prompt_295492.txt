I have recently been conducting annual performance reviews at work, and I have been struck by how readily people claim credit for good outcomes and blame bad ones on circumstances. This has set me thinking about Machiavelli's treatment of virtù and fortuna in The Prince. Please write a mock human-resources performance review, of about 450 words, assessing a prince in Machiavelli's Florence. Use the deadpan voice of a corporate appraisal document, and aim the satire at the practice of treating outcomes as transparent evidence of merit or of luck, and at the appraisal genre that pretends to separate the two. The jokes must turn on the actual content of Machiavelli's view rather than on generic office anachronism: fortune governs only part of what happens; virtù consists in anticipating what fortune may do, preparing flood defences in good time, and acting boldly and flexibly rather than with cautious delay, so that a ruler ruined by a foreseeable change of circumstances has shown a lack of virtù rather than mere bad luck. Include a rating of the prince on virtù and on fortuna, and include one passage in which the reviewer rejects the prince's plea that fortune was against him by pointing to his own failure to prepare. Keep the satire deadpan throughout, and write in British English.
