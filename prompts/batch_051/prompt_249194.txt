I have been developing a view I call the Legibility Requirement. Its core claim is that whenever someone takes part in an ongoing cooperative practice—a friendship, a joint project, a workplace, a political community, a market exchange—they incur a defeasible, pro tanto obligation not merely to be honest but to be legible. By legible I mean that a person's observable conduct (their words, actions, acceptances, and refusals) gives a reasonably attentive other enough structure to form warranted expectations about what the person is doing, what they can be relied on for, and what would count as their objecting, withdrawing, or refusing. Legibility, as I intend it, is weaker than transparency: it does not require disclosing one's reasons, history, or inner states. The wrong I want to isolate is that of forcing someone to guess about matters they need in order to coordinate with you—or to decide whether to continue with you—when your participation has given them a stake in that coordination.

Three examples motivate the view for me. A collaborator, asked whether they can meet a Friday deadline, says "I'll handle it," raises no further concern, and then misses it; they never lied, but they left the rest of us unable to plan around them. A partner, asked what is wrong, says "nothing" and becomes withdrawn; they may be perfectly sincere, but the surface is unreadable, and the other person cannot tell whether to intervene, wait, or leave. A public agency publishes an accurate but impenetrable account of new rules; nothing asserted is false, but compliance now requires a specialist's translation rather than ordinary attention. In each case I want to say a distinct duty has been breached, one that is not captured by the categories of honesty, candor, or promise-keeping.

I would like a candid assessment rather than a defense. Specifically: (1) Is this a coherent moral category, or does legibility collapse into honesty, candor, promise-keeping, or the background conditions of valid consent? (2) Reconstruct the strongest version of the view, with whatever qualifications it needs to remain defensible. (3) Give me the three strongest objections. I expect them to come from the value of privacy and the right to remain partly opaque to others, from the suspicion that such a duty places unfair burdens on those already under structural pressure to explain themselves, and from the possibility that deliberate opacity is sometimes justified resistance or self-protection. Say whether any of these is fatal or only limits the view's scope. (4) Show me where the view breaks: describe one case it would wrongly count as a wrong, and one case it ought to condemn but cannot. (5) Tell me whether the duty should bind all participants equally or scale with power and dependence—and whether, once scaled, the view loses the clean universality that made it interesting in the first place.
