I cover debates in the biomedical, behavioral, and computational sciences, and I have noticed how much weight rests on the distinction between a result that is an anomaly within a known mechanism and a result that merely shows a different mechanism at work. A replication fails, the original laboratory says the replicators changed an auxiliary mechanism, and the replication is set aside; an effect shows up where it should not, and the authors say it is real but mechanistically distinct. Before I can referee any of that, I need an account of what it is for two instances to be the same mechanism, so I have written down my own verdicts on six cases and want to know what principle, if any, holds them together.

First, a drug lowers blood pressure in rats by dilating small vessels, but in humans it lowers blood pressure to the same extent mostly by increasing salt excretion in the kidney; I call this a different mechanism, and I would not say the rat mechanism was confirmed in humans. Second, two analgesics relieve pain by blocking the same pro-inflammatory enzyme, but one also has a mild sedative side action; I call them the same mechanism for analgesia, and I treat the sedative action as an extra mechanism rather than a change in the first. Third, a pendulum clock and a quartz wristwatch both keep time through an oscillator and a counter; I call these different mechanisms, not two instances of one. Fourth, a cognitive scientist describes a person's and a robot's path-planning with the same search algorithm, and I say the two share the mechanism at the algorithmic level at which the behavior is explained, even though the physical substrates differ. Fifth, two image classifiers are trained separately and reach the same accuracy, but one classifies mostly by shape and the other mostly by texture; I call these different mechanisms. Sixth, the same trained classifier is copied onto two different kinds of hardware, a graphics processor and a custom chip, and I call the mechanism the same.

Cases five and six trouble me most, because in both pairs the outward behavior is the same and the difference is internal, yet I make the internal difference mechanism-changing in one and mechanism-preserving in the other. I also notice that in the second and third cases I seem inconsistent about how much component detail matters, since I let the second drug's sedative side action pass but not the clock's shift from pendulum to quartz. Is there one precise principle that delivers all six of my verdicts, or nearly all, and if not, which of the six should I revise? If there is such a principle, state it, say which case motivates each of its clauses, and then apply it to a further case I have not settled: two laboratories construct the same genetic modification in mice and observe the same anatomical malformation, but in one lab the malformation arises through excess cell death and in the other through failed cell migration. I am inclined to call these different mechanisms, but I cannot currently say why without adopting a principle that might also force me to call the pendulum clock and the quartz watch the same mechanism. Please answer in continuous prose, as you would write an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
