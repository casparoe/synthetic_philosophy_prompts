I have been trying to reconstruct an argument I encountered about a repeated coin-flip gamble, and I cannot tell whether it is a genuine problem for expected-value reasoning or a piece of sleight of hand. The gamble works as follows. You stake your entire current wealth on a fair coin; heads multiplies your wealth by 1.5, tails multiplies it by 0.6; the flips are independent, and if you play you must stake everything each round, for as many rounds as you choose. The argument below concludes that a rational agent should not merely decline this bet but should abandon expected-value reasoning as the standard for evaluating repeated decisions.

(1) The expected multiplier per round is (1.5 + 0.6)/2 = 1.05, which is greater than one. (2) By linearity of expectation, expected wealth after n rounds is 1.05^n times initial wealth, which grows without bound; so the gamble, repeated sufficiently many times, increases your wealth on average. (3) A rational agent should therefore accept the gamble in every round and stake his or her entire wealth, since this is the policy that maximizes expected wealth. (4) Now consider a single agent followed through time. By the law of large numbers, after many rounds the number of heads is very likely close to n/2, so the agent's wealth is very likely close to W · 1.5^{n/2} · 0.6^{n/2} = W · 0.9^{n/2}, which tends to zero. (5) Thus, with probability approaching one, the single agent who keeps playing ends up ruined, even though expected wealth across an ensemble of parallel agents grows without bound. (6) A policy that almost surely ruins each individual who follows it cannot be rational, whatever the cross-sectional average does. (7) Therefore expected-value reasoning gives the wrong prescription for this bet, and decisions should instead be evaluated by their time-average growth rate — roughly 0.949 per round here — rather than by the ensemble expectation.

I am fairly confident that the computations in (1), (2), (4), and (5) are correct, and I find (7) intuitive. But if the argument goes through it seems to overturn a good deal of standard decision theory, so I suspect one of the transitions is too quick. Is the argument valid? If it is, tell me exactly what it forces one to give up. If it is not, identify the first step or inference that fails, state the corrected version of that step, and say whether the deeper criticism of expected-value reasoning survives the repair or collapses.
