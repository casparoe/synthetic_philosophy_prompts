I spent thirty years in experimental physics, and I kept watching the same thing: subfields where you could build the apparatus, get an answer within months, and pose the next question moved fast, while fields where every result could be interpreted three ways barely budged across a whole career. Now I'm retired and sit on the board of a small family foundation that funds research in oncology, education interventions, economic history, and occasionally philosophy. Three times a year we decide which renewals to recommend for grants in the few-hundred-thousand range, and I keep making the same argument about funding work that sits inside a tight feedback loop between a claim and a check. The other trustees nod and then we fund what we funded last year.

I want to turn that argument into a decision procedure the board can actually run in a two-hour meeting, most of them with no scientific training. The deliverable should be a sequence of questions to ask about a proposal or the field it lives in, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a values judgment rather than a factual one. Ground it in serious philosophy of science about verification, falsification, prediction, and progress, but translate it into plain language for a wealth manager. Then run it on three cases: a randomized trial of a school nutrition program, a decade-long observational study of coastal ecosystems, and a purely theoretical project in macroeconomics. Include one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, and say explicitly what the procedure can't decide. I'm also aware I might be smuggling in a bias for physics-style clean experiments, so include a step that checks whether the procedure is quietly defining feedback as just the sort that looks like my old lab.
