In his 1969 paper "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility," Harry Frankfurt claims that the principle of alternate possibilities is false: a person may be morally responsible for what he has done even though he could not have done otherwise, and he says the principle's plausibility is an illusion that dissolves once we look at the relevant cases more closely. His original example involves a man, Jones, who decides on his own to do something, but unbeknownst to him, another agent, Black, would have made him do exactly that if he had shown any sign of deciding otherwise. I'm putting together a survey of objections to Frankfurt-type cases, and I want to make sure I have not missed a structurally distinct line of criticism.

So far I have these objections: (1) the flicker-of-freedom objection, according to which the agent still has some alternative, such as a prior inclination, intention-formation, or neural event, even if the action itself is unavoidable; (2) the indeterministic-world objection, which says that in an indeterministic setting the intervener cannot have a reliable prior sign of the agent's choice without that sign either being determined or undermining the agent's responsibility; (3) the actual-sequence objection, which charges that the counterfactual intervener never actually does anything, so the example merely shows that an agent can be responsible when he acts on his own, without showing that the absence of alternatives is irrelevant to responsibility; and (4) the higher-order alternative objection, which claims that Frankfurt cases still contain alternatives at a different level, such as the alternative of not forming the intention or of letting the intervener act. I want an objection that is not on this list and is not merely a restatement, special case, or combination of these items. It should diagnose a different feature of the cases. Please give me one, and then say how it relates to the objections I already have and whether you think it is any good.
