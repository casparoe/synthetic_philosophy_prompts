Last month I sat through a long argument between two colleagues, and when they turned to me for a verdict I could not give one. The disagreement concerned whether human extinction would be bad for any reason beyond the individual deaths and suffering it involves.

(1) Rosa opened with a person-affecting claim: if a scenario killed no one prematurely and left no one behind to grieve, she said the end of the species would add no further badness, because something can be bad only if it is bad for some actual or possible person. A world with no people, she concluded, is not bad for anyone. (2) Eli answered that this misses how much of what we value is oriented toward a human future we will not live to see — the novel under revision, the language being preserved, the research program one contributes to. If we learned with certainty that humanity would end a generation after our own deaths, he said, these activities would come to seem pointless even though no death had been hastened, which shows that the badness of extinction outruns the deaths. (3) Rosa replied that this is a psychological claim about how the news would make us feel, not a claim about value. A discovery about what happens after we are gone, she argued, cannot reach back and make an already-lived life less good; at most it would demoralize us, and we could respond by refocusing on projects that can be completed within a human horizon. Demoralization is a reaction we can criticize, not an argument. (4) Eli said the reaction is not a mere mood; it tracks the structure of the values themselves. The worth of teaching a child an endangered language, for example, is partly constituted by the language's having speakers after us. If that future fails, he argued, the activity is genuinely less worthwhile, not merely felt to be so, because at least some values are collective and forward-looking in a way that cannot be reduced to how an individual life feels from the inside. (5) Rosa came back that this lets value depend on facts that obtain after everyone involved is gone, which she found incoherent. If no one ever experiences the difference between a language dying out and a language continuing, where could that difference in value reside? She could understand bad for someone, but not bad for the project. (6) Eli's final move was that the difference resides in what the activity is. An effort that contributes to something that continues is a different kind of deed from an identical effort that terminates with the agent, he said, in something like the way a promise kept differs from a promise broken even when no one feels the difference. The continuation is part of the description under which the activity is valued, not an external consequence added on.
