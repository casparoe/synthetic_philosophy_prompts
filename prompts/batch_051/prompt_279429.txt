Two friends of mine got into it at a cookout after someone brought up a news story about a lifeguard who was scrolling on his phone while a kid drowned. They went rounds about whether the lifeguard caused the drowning, and I got drafted as referee but genuinely couldn't sort it, so I'm bringing it to you.

(1) Dan said the lifeguard caused the death, full stop. If he'd been watching and gone in, the kid wouldn't have died. That's just the but-for test, and it comes out true. So an omission — doing nothing — can be a cause.

(2) Mara said that overgenerates. If that's the test, then every able-bodied swimmer on the beach caused the death too, because each of them could have tried. But nobody would say the woman reading a novel 200 meters away caused it. Absences aren't events; an omission is a whole in the world, not something with causal oomph.

(3) Dan: the lifeguard isn't on a par with random sunbathers, because he had the role — he was the one person supposed to watch the water. Picking out which absence matters takes a norm or a default, sure, but all causal talk works like that: saying the drunk driver caused the crash, rather than the sober alternative, is the same sort of selection. Norms don't make it fake.

(4) Mara: that's exactly the giveaway. Once a norm does the selecting, what you're calling the cause is just whichever absence we're morally annoyed about. At most it's quasi-causation, useful shorthand for blame. The actual cause is the drowning process — water in the lungs — not the lifeguard's not-doing. Blameworthiness isn't causation.

(5) Dan: but then you can't even call "the plant died because I didn't water it" a causal explanation without adding scare quotes, which is absurd. And if you say that sentence is fine but not "real" causation, you've just invented a metaphysical distinction to protect a slogan. Your view relocates the same norm problem: you still need to say why the lifeguard, not the novel-reader, gets blamed.
