Since taking up a research post in Oslo, I have been drafted onto the committee that organizes our institute's annual public debate, and this year's motion is whether Norway should retain its monarchy. In the course of reading for the event and arguing with friends on both sides, I have hit the same impasse from several directions. Defenders of the institution say it supplies a non-partisan head of state, continuity across changes of government, and a focal point for national life that an elected president, being by definition a politician, could not. Critics say an office filled by inheritance is a standing affront to the equal standing of citizens, and that the monarch's reserve powers are either a dormant threat to democratic control or so purely ceremonial that the institution is expensive theatre. The strange thing is that the two camps do not seem to disagree about the empirical record: everyone grants that Norway, like the United Kingdom and Japan, is a stable democracy in which the monarch does not in practice govern, and everyone on both sides describes themselves as a committed democrat. Yet the argument has not moved in all the years anyone I ask can remember.

I am not looking for a verdict on the monarchy itself but for a diagnosis of this stalled disagreement. Is it a substantive moral disagreement about what democratic legitimacy requires? A merely verbal disagreement about the word 'democracy'? A clash between two incommensurable sets of values—stability and symbolic continuity against equality and self-rule? A difference in bedrock intuitions about inherited status that neither side can argue for? Or are the two camps really answering different questions, one about the legitimacy of particular office-holders and the other about the health of the constitutional order as a whole? My tentative hypothesis is that both sides are weighting the same symbolic facts in opposite directions, but I do not trust it. Given whatever diagnosis you arrive at, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction that unblocks the argument, an empirical question whose answer would actually change anyone's mind, a reformulation under which the two sides stop talking past each other—or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
