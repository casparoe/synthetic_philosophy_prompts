I run a small private server for an old MMO, and I also act as its economy moderator. I have no academic background in philosophy. What I do know well is what happens in a player-run economy: faucets and sinks leaking or drying up, inflation when gold floods in, hoarding of rare materials, a few rich players cornering a market and effectively setting prices, auction-house flipping, bots farming raw gold. Over the years I have watched player greed make the server feel alive, and I have also watched it nearly wreck the place. That has led me to an old-sounding idea I keep turning over: that greed and acquisitiveness, which everyone calls vices, might actually be the engine of prosperity — that a society of genuinely frugal, easily satisfied people would slow down, stop trading, and leave everyone worse off, whereas the restless desire for more is what keeps production and exchange going. Call this the vice-as-engine position.

Please act as an expert fluent in both the philosophical debate around this position and the inner workings of game economies. Don't give me a reading list or citations; I want the ideas themselves, translated honestly. What I would like from you is a two-part answer.

First, restate the vice-as-engine position as systematically as you can using the vocabulary of MMO economy design: faucets and sinks, inflation, hoarding, market cornering, arbitrage, player-driven versus NPC-vendor economies, and anything else in that toolkit you need. My own working suspicion is that what philosophers call greed is really just an incentive variable — something close to a faucet rate that a designer tunes up to keep the economy moving and down to keep it from inflating. I want to know whether that suspicion survives the exercise, or whether the translation loses something real, like an actual moral quality in the person that has no counterpart in a game.
