I was trained as a physical chemist, and for most of my career I treated an unattributed number as worthless: a measurement entered the record only after the instrument had been calibrated against a standard and the chain of comparisons was written down. In retirement I have been reading classical Indian epistemology, and the Mīmāṃsā account of testimony has unsettled that habit. As I understand the school, it holds that the Veda is authorless (apauruṣeya) and that knowledge from words is intrinsically valid (svataḥ prāmāṇya): when one understands a heard statement one is prima facie justified in accepting it, and it stands unless a specific defect or contradiction arises. Its authority is not borrowed from the speaker's honesty or expertise. My reconstruction of the doctrine separates four claims that something must satisfy to count as intrinsically valid testimony in this sense. First, understanding the words produces a cognition that presents itself as true, so the burden of proof falls on the person who would reject it. Second, the statement's authority is not conferred by any author's track record. Third, error is traced to a further, case-specific defeater rather than to a standing presumption against uncredentialed speech. Fourth, the paradigm case is a text with no human author at all, so that an entire genus of doubt — deceit, bias, incompetence — cannot get started.

Now consider a case that seems to me to sit on the edge of this category. A consortium trains a language model on a large and carefully curated corpus of scientific literature, then releases it so that its answers appear as plain assertive text: no named speaker, no signature, and no institutional pledge of reliability attached to any particular output. In practice, users treat its fluent and confident answers almost exactly as they treat a colleague's testimony — they accept them unless something specific clashes. Yet the case also pulls the other way. There is no single human testifier whose good faith could be assessed, but the system is plainly the product of human agency: engineers chose the architecture, selected and cleaned the training data, and tuned the model, and its words are, in some sense, the statistical residue of millions of earlier human authors.

Does this case qualify as intrinsically valid testimony according to the four criteria? Work through the criteria one by one, and say honestly where they underdetermine the verdict. Then step back and tell me whether the case shows that the fourth criterion, authorlessness, is doing all the real work while the other three are dispensable, or whether the first three can stand even when the fourth fails. I am less interested in historical exegesis than in whether the reconstructed doctrine survives contact with the case. Please say how confident you are in each stage of your verdict, and single out the point on which you are least sure.
