I am a PhD student in formal epistemology and the philosophy of probability, and I have been arbitrating a running dispute in our reading group between two people who disagree about what Bertrand's paradox establishes about the principle of indifference. One party defends what I will call the invariance position: the paradox shows only that a probability is well-defined relative to a specified statistical model, and that in cases with genuine symmetries the principle of indifference should be reconstructed as an invariance constraint, which this party claims can single out a unique distribution in many of the contested cases. The other party defends what I will call the deflationary position: the paradox shows the principle is empty, because any maneuver strong enough to avoid contradictory answers either imports a substantive measure or replaces indifference with pure stipulation, and no purely epistemic tie-breaking rule survives the paradox. Please draft the joint statement that the two of them could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The document should have this structure. First, a numbered list of substantive agreed claims, including at least: a neutral description of the classical randomization procedures for the chord problem (random radius, random midpoint, random endpoints) and their different verdicts (the 1/2, 1/4, and 1/3 results); the observation that applying naive indifference across different parameterizations of the same problem yields incompatible distributions; the distinction between treating the paradox as an ill-posedness problem and treating it as a refutation of the principle; and a precise statement of what invariance under a transformation group would have to accomplish for the invariance position to count as rescuing rather than replacing the principle. If part of their disagreement was merely verbal, identify which part and state why, in wording each would accept.

Second, a disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, give (a) each side's position phrased so its own side would sign it, (b) the crux the disagreement turns on, and (c) one concrete case or argument both accept as bearing on the crux, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I suspect the cruxes include: whether the circle's symmetries fix a unique measure on the space of chords and thereby privilege one of the classical answers, and which one; whether a cube-factory-style case, in which the same ignorance is described as uniformity over edge length, face area, or volume all confined to a unit interval, shows that parameterization invariance is an unreasonable demand or instead shows that indifference has no content until a scale is specified; and whether the principle's defensible scope is limited to finitely many symmetric alternatives, with the continuous cases showing only that no canonical limiting procedure exists. If you judge that some of these cruxes collapse into one another, say so and mark the collapse in the table.

Do not write this as a dialogue and do not adjudicate the dispute. Write it as a constructive text both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact.
