I am committed to a package of epistemological views that individually seem almost unavoidable, but together seem to license a conclusion I find intuitively unacceptable. I would like you to present both sides at their strongest and then give a negotiated verdict on which should yield.

The package is this. First, ordinary perceptual knowledge is basic: I can come to know mundane propositions — that the wall in front of me is red, that there is a car in the driveway — simply by looking, without first needing independent evidence that my vision is reliable. Second, knowledge is transmitted by competent deduction: if I know that p and deduce q from p, I am in a position to know that q. Third, a belief formed by a generally reliable process, under locally unproblematic conditions, can amount to knowledge.

The trouble is that these commitments seem to make a certain kind of circular vindication of perception all too easy. From a single look at a red wall, I know that the wall is red. I can deduce from this that my color vision did not misrepresent the wall's color on that occasion, and so come to know that. If I repeat the maneuver across many colored surfaces, I can come to know that my color vision is generally accurate — all without any independent check on the faculty, using only its own deliverances to certify its reliability. On the package, this easy knowledge is genuine knowledge. But my firm intuition is that a succession of red-looking walls cannot by itself tell me that my eyes are working properly; it can tell me a series of wall-colors only on the assumption that they are. Using a faculty's outputs to establish the trustworthiness of that same faculty seems illicitly circular, self-licensing, or question-begging.

So the clash is between a tidy package — roughly reliabilism plus closure plus the banality of ordinary perceptual knowledge — and a firm intuition of epistemic circularity. Present the strongest case for each side. Then give me a considered verdict: should the intuition be debunked and the easy knowledge accepted, should the package be revised (and if so, which principle gives, and what restriction or replacement would do the work), or is there a reading on which they are compatible? In particular, address whether the entities in question are compatible on a reading where the deduced self-certifying claim is too thin or indexical to constitute objectionable bootstrapping, or where the intuition tracks an inability to use the conclusion as a premise rather than a failure to be in a position to know it. Say what would justify each of these moves, and what would count as reason to treat the intuition as a byproduct of processing difficulty or an internalized epistemic norm rather than as detecting a real defect in the package. Do not settle for labels; run the moves.
