I've never studied philosophy properly, but I've been down a rabbit hole with Locke on personal identity, and a friend showed me Reid's old example of the boy who gets flogged for robbing an orchard, then becomes an officer, then an old general. As I understand it: the officer can remember being flogged as a boy but not the actual stealing, so by Locke's memory criterion the officer is the same person as the boy; the old general can remember taking the standard as an officer but not being flogged, so the general is the same person as the officer; and yet the general isn't the same person as the boy, because he can't remember the flogging. Reid's point, as far as I can tell, is that identity has to be transitive, and Locke's criterion gives you a chain where A equals B, B equals C, but A doesn't equal C.

Now here's the variant I can't shake. What if the old general hasn't got any direct memory of the flogging, but he *does* have a vivid, from-the-inside memory of being the young officer in the act of remembering the flogging? He can call up the feel of that younger mind reaching for the flogging, but the flogging itself is blank. It's a memory of a memory, not a memory of the event. Does Locke get to say the general is conscious of the flogging after all, and so identical to the boy? Or does Reid's contradiction just jump up a level? And if Locke lets a memory-of-a-memory count, does he have to let a whole chain of them count, so that somebody could be me because I remember my past self remembering my past self remembering... even if nobody in the chain can actually recall the original deed? That seems to turn the criterion into a different animal, but I'm not sure wether that's a bug or a fix.
