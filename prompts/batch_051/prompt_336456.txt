I'm a backend engineer and I've been going down a bit of a rabbit hole on the philosophy of maths, specifically proofs that no single person can actually check by hand. The classic example is the four-colour theorem: the 1976 proof by Appel and Haken did a large case analysis on a computer, and a philosopher (I think his name was Tymoczko) argued that this wasn't really a proof in the traditional sense, because a proof is supposed to be something a mathematician can survey and be convinced by. That always seemed like a decent point to me. But the landscape has shifted with proof assistants. Gonthier formalised the four-colour theorem in Coq, and the Flyspeck project did the Kepler conjecture. These don't make the case analysis any smaller — the proof terms are, if anything, more enourmous than the original computer code. What changes is that the whole argument is expressed in a formal system whose checking goes through a small trusted kernel; you can inspect the kernel, and everything else is mechanical.

I use a model checker occasionally at work, so the question of when a mechanical check counts as a proof is more than academic to me, and this is the thing I keep going back and forth on: does the old objection — an unsurveyable derivation isn't really a proof — survive these developments? If it survives, pinpoint which premise of the objection the proof assistants fail to address. Is it that the surveyability problem just moves into the formalisation, the statement of the theorem, or the semantics of the language? If it doesn't survive, then what should the objection be replaced with: is 'we know it's true but we don't understand why' a legitimate remaining complaint, and does that matter for what counts as mathematics? Please answer in British English, and don't worry about citations — just work the argument through.
