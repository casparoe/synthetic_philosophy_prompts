My housing co-op has been fighting about this and I got asked to help think it through. We're considering a rule that when someone does you a favor, what you owe back is set by what it cost the giver, not by how much it helped you. So if a member with a truck spends their whole Saturday moving your stuff, you owe them big even if a small car would have done; but if they make one five-minute phone call that gets your kid a job, you owe them almost nothing even though that call changed your kid's life. The point is to stop people from claiming big debts for easy favors and to stop recipients feeling crushed by debts for help that was trivial for the giver. What does this actually do to a community? I want the incentives: do people start hiding how easy something was or exagerating how much it cost them? Some of their cost is just luck anyway—they happened to own a truck or know someone—so does the rule end up making us owe more to the lucky? Does it kill off the favor that's cheap to give but huge to receive? And is there a tweak that keeps the good part without turning every kindness into a cost audit?
