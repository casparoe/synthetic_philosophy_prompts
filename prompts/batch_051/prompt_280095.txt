I'm a PhD student in bioethics, and my dissertation is on the ethics of randomized controlled trials. The field has a few named positions — theoretical equipoise, clinical equipoise, the uncertainty principle — but I keep noticing that people argue about individual trials by invoking whichever candidate standard gives them the result they want. Before I go further, I want the standard itself made explicit: not a literature review but a specification.

You are an expert in research ethics and the philosophy of medicine. Work through this carefully. Begin by brainstorming candidate desiderata in note form, stress-test each against real trial designs and controversies, and only then present the refined list. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp. Flag any requirement that may be smuggling in a prior commitment, say which single requirement does the most to exclude the leading views, and tell me which subsets of the list look jointly unsatisfiable — that is the part I most want to see. Then, briefly, score theoretical equipoise, clinical equipoise, and the uncertainty principle against the list; keep that section short, because the criteria are the deliverable. No bibliography, no citations, no summaries of individual papers.

The theory should be good enough to tell an institutional review board or a data monitoring committee when a trial may begin, continue, or must stop, and good enough to explain — not merely assert — why randomization can be consistent with a physician's duty to a particular patient.

To fix the level of grain, here are some of the demands I already suspect belong on the list:

- A physician enrolls one of their own patients in a trial even though the physician personally believes, on clinical impression more than published evidence, that one arm is superior for this particular patient. The account must say whether the relevant uncertainty is a state of the individual clinician or of the community of experts, and must not let the two quietly collapse into each other.

- A placebo-controlled trial for a condition that already has a proven effective treatment. The account must explain when, if ever, withholding or delaying that treatment is not exploitation, and why the participant's consent does or does not do the justifying. It must also keep separate the question of no prospect of direct benefit from the question of acceptable risk where every arm offers treatment.

- A non-inferiority trial of a me-too drug, or a placebo-controlled trial run when an established active comparator exists. The account must be able to say why such a question does not license randomization if it is not a question the community of practitioners actually needs answered; otherwise the standard cannot rule out trials run mainly for regulatory or marketing purposes.

- A healthy-volunteer or human challenge trial, where the participant has no prospect of direct benefit and the risks are borne entirely for others. The account needs upper bounds on what may be asked, and a story about why those bounds differ for adults who can consent, children, pregnant people, and people who cannot consent and for whom consent is at best surrogate.

- A trial stopped early by its data monitoring committee because one arm is clearly winning, and a response-adaptive or platform design of the kind used in some intensive-care and COVID-era trials, in which randomization probabilities shift as data accrue. The account must say what disturbs the initial permission to randomize — at what point continuing becomes wrong — and whether a design that knowingly tilts the randomization toward a favored arm is compatible with the theory.

- The cross-border case: the 1990s controversy over placebo-controlled trials of short-course AZT regimens to prevent perinatal HIV transmission, where an established longer regimen existed but was unaffordable in the trial sites. The account must say whether the relevant standard of care is local or global, and why — and what that answer does to the claim that uncertainty alone legitimates a placebo arm.

I expect some of these to force choices rather than allow reconciliation. For example, if the community-level condition that authorizes a trial in the first place and the individual-priority condition that forbids knowingly assigning a participant to an arm the emerging evidence marks as inferior cannot both be satisfied once the data start arriving in, show me that tension explicitly and say which requirement you would relax and why. If the best list ends up excluding all three leading positions, that is an acceptable answer — just make the requirements carry the argument.
