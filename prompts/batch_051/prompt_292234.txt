You are grading a philosophy of mind examination. Below are a unit description, the examination question, and one student's answer. Assign the answer a mark out of 20 and write the point-by-point comments that justify the mark. Do not give only a general impression; indicate which claims earn credit, identify any conflation or unsupported inference, and make the final mark follow from those comments.

Unit description. The unit took as its focus Daniel Dennett's proposal that a self is a center of narrative gravity. On the interpretation used in the unit, a center of narrative gravity is an abstraction posited because it organizes and predicts a person's behavior; it is not a concrete component of the physical world. Two contrasts were central. The view was distinguished from eliminativism, which takes the self's status as an abstraction to show that there is no self, and it was distinguished from the claim that self-narration is unconstrained, since on the unit's reading self-constitution is answerable to a person's history, embodied situation, and the need to remain intelligible to others.

Examination question.
(a) Explain, in your own words, the point of comparing the self to a center of gravity, and identify two things the analogy is not meant to suggest.
(b) Explain the sense in which, on this view, the self is a product of narrative rather than the source from which narration proceeds.
(c) Discuss the objection that this view makes the self a mere fiction, with the result that no self-narrative is better warranted than another, and explain how the contrasts set out in the unit bear on that objection.

Student answer. A center of gravity is the point at which a body's weight can be said to act; it is not an extra physical component of the body, but an abstraction used for calculation and prediction. Dennett's idea is that the self is like this. It is not a little person in the head, and it is not a Cartesian theatre, but a point around which a person's actions, utterances, and history form an intelligible pattern. Just as it would be a mistake to open up a body in search of its center of gravity, it would be a mistake to open up a brain in search of the self. On this account, the self is also not the author of the narrative. The narration produces the self; the self is the product, not something that pre-exists the stories that are told.

The unit stressed, however, that centers of gravity are abstractions, and an abstraction is not part of the physical world. If that is what a self is, then the self is a fiction, and a fiction is not real. Dennett's view therefore collapses into eliminativism: strictly speaking, there is no self, only a useful way of talking. This makes the view revisionary, since attributions of agency and responsibility would be literally false, useful rather than true. The same reasoning shows that no self-narrative is more accurate than another. If the self is only a story, then each person is free to tell whatever story she likes, and questions of correctness do not arise.
