I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so I'm hoping someone can check a piece of reasoning I want to put in a character's mouth. My character — a retired cardsharp named Emil — keeps a notebook of "pumps," little schemes that show how people's own choices can bleed them dry. One of the entries goes like this.

(1) If a person prefers one thing to another, and the two are otherwise alike, she'll accept a swap from the thing she has to the thing she prefers even if the swap costs her a small amount; that's close to what preferring amounts to, at least in situations where nothing else is at stake. (2) No genuinely rational way of running your life should let somebody reliably extract money from you while handing you back, at the end of the cycle, exactly what you started with. (3) Take Nadia, whose tea preferences are stable within a day but flip from Monday to Tuesday: on Mondays she prefers oolong to rooibos, on Tuesdays she prefers rooibos to oolong. She isn't forgetful or indecisive; it's just how her tastes move. (4) On Monday, Emil sells Nadia oolong for her rooibos plus a dollar. On Tuesday he sells her rooibos for her oolong plus a dollar. Each trade, at the moment she makes it, is one she prefers to making no trade, otherwise she'd refuse. (5) So the two-day cycle is built entirely out of choices that are rational from the inside, yet from the outside it's insane: Nadia ends every Tuesday with the same tea she had Monday morning and two dollars less, forever. (6) Since the only thing doing any work is the Monday-to-Tuesday flip in her preferences, the flip itself must be irrational. Which means changing one's preferences is irrational. Which is obviously false — otherwise half of growing up, changing your mind, and outgrowing old loves would be a rationality failure.

Emil thinks the argument is airtight and that the "obviously false" part just shows people are less rational then they'd like to be. For the scene, I want him to be answered by a friend who can actually see what's wrong with it. I can't tell whether the friend should push on (1), on (2), on the move from rational parts to an insane whole, or somewhere else entirely. Which premise or step should she reject, and what does rejecting it force her to say about how rational a person has to be across time?
