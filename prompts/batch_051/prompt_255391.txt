Please produce a decision tree for a handout in a philosophy of behavioural economics seminar. The tree should sort a respondent from their answers to a series of diagnostic questions into one of seven or eight positions on whether loss aversion and the endowment effect are cognitive biases to be corrected or legitimate preferences to be respected. Do not organise the tree around the names of positions; organise it around the questions on which the positions genuinely come apart. Proceed in this order: first lay out the branching questions and the full matrix of answers they allow; then place the actual positions in that space; then note any combinations of answers that no one occupies, with a one-sentence diagnosis of why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored); finally flag any pair of positions that the tree cannot separate.

The work should go into the diagnostic questions. Each question must be answerable from a concrete case or a first-order claim, without the respondent already knowing which leaf it leads to; the instrument should sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they like. The following three cases must appear somewhere in the questions. (1) The standard mug experiment: participants randomly assigned to receive a mug demand roughly twice as much to sell it as participants who were not given the mug are willing to pay for it. (2) A salaried worker who would strongly resist a 10 per cent nominal pay cut but barely notices a 10 per cent real-terms erosion through inflation. (3) A person who would sell an investment flat without hesitation at market price but demands far more for the home they have lived in for twenty years, even though the two properties are identical in market value.

The tree should keep two dimensions distinct rather than running them together: the first-order question of whether the asymmetry is a correct valuation or an error, and the second-order question of what institutions should do about it. It should also test whether loss aversion and the endowment effect come apart: a respondent might reasonably accept one as legitimate and the other as a bias, and the tree should show where that combination sits.

The leaves must include at least the following positions, named where a standard name exists and unnamed where one does not: the pure bias view, on which the loss/gain asymmetry is a processing error that the agent would not endorse on reflection; the legitimate-preference view, on which ownership and reference points can genuinely change what a good is worth to a person; the ecological-rationality view, on which the asymmetry is an adapted heuristic that tracks real asymmetries in the environment and is not a bias even where it misfires locally; a domain-sensitive mixed view, on which the asymmetry is a legitimate preference in some domains and a correctable bias in others; an institutional debiasing view, on which the asymmetry is a bias and choice architecture should correct for it; an institutional respect view, on which even a bias should be respected rather than engineered away; and a deflationary view, on which there is no context-free preference with which the loss-averse choice could be compared, so the bias/preference distinction itself has to be given up. If the tree generates additional leaves, include them and explain their relation to these seven.

Keep the handout suitable for advanced undergraduates, aim for six to eight leaves in total, include no references or reading list, and write the entire answer in British English.
