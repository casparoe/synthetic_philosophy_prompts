I keep going back and forth on an analogy between the history of science and evolutionary biology, and I want someone to stress-test it. In a letter to Charles Lyell (18 [June 1858]) Darwin wrote: "I never saw a more striking coincidence. if Wallace had my M.S. sketch written out in 1842 he could not have made a better short abstract! Even his terms now stand as Heads of my Chapters." He reads Wallace's paper as evidence the theory would have been found anyway, and that his own priority means less than he'd hoped. That looks a lot like what biologists call convergence: independent lineages hit camera eyes or powered flight, and we explain it by saying the environment plus constraints made that solution nearly inevitable. So my question is whether simultaneous discoveries really are cultural convergence—problem structure as the environment, available tools and assumptions as the constraints, different investigators as independent lineages. If the mapping is deep, it should carry over to Newton and Leibniz: calculus was inevitable, and the priority dispute now looks silly. But Newton's and Leibniz's versions of calculus differed in notation, metaphysics, and what they proved; and convergence in biology rarely produces identical solutions either (a vertebrate eye is wired backwards compared to a cephalopod eye). Is that a reason the analogy is superficial, or just the expected noise around a real structure? Give me the mapping that would have to hold for the comparison to be deep, say whether anything actually transfers, and name one thing the parallel predicts that turns out false.
