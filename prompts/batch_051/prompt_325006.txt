I've been turning over a puzzle in my head—not sure if it has a name. A driver is on a road with two identical exits. She's absent-minded: at each exit she can't tell whether it's the first or the second, and she has no memory of what happened earlier. If she takes the first exit, payoff 0. If she passes the first exit and takes the second, payoff 4. If she passes both, payoff 1. Before the trip, she can pick a rule like "at each exit, take it with probability p." I worked out that before the trip she'd want p equal to one third, but then at an actual exit she might want to change her mind because she's not sure which exit she's at. I don't fully trust my math, but thats the background.

Now here's the counterfactual I'd like someone to reason through. Suppose before driving she's offered a free gadget. She knows it works like this: at each exit it lights up either red or blue. If she's at the first exit, the light is red with 70% probability and blue with 30%. If she's at the second exit, the probabilities are flipped: blue 70%, red 30%. The gadget is unreliable but symmetric in that way. She still has no memory of earlier exits and no memory of earlier lights; each time she only sees the current light.

Please predict, with your reasoning visible step by step, how a perfectly rational driver would use this gadget. Specifically: after seeing red, what probability of taking the exit should she choose? After seeing blue, what probability? Would she, before the trip, prefer having the gadget to not having it (assume it's free)? And does the gadget actually fix the odd feature of the original problem where she seems to disagree with herself before the trip versus at an exit, or does it just make that disagreement more complicated? I want to know which of your conclusions are forced, which are guesses, and what assumptions about rational choice are doing the most work.
