I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of religious diversity, and we have been working through exclusivism, inclusivism, and pluralism about salvation. The textbook taxonomy feels too blunt to capture my actual reactions to individual cases, so I have written down my raw verdicts below in the hope of extracting a principle from them. Could you act as a professional philosopher of religion and help me think this through carefully? Before settling on a view, please brainstorm a few candidate principles, test them against the cases, and then tell me plainly which of my verdicts, if any, have to go.

I will use "saved" in the broad sense we use in class, meaning attaining the final good in relation to God, without committing to any particular account of what that consists in. My cases are these. (1) Amara has lived her entire life in a remote Himalayan village as a devout Hindu and has never encountered Christianity except through passing rumors; she is generous, prayerful, and morally serious. Verdict: saved. (2) Mateo was raised Catholic, studied philosophy of religion at university, and left the faith after years of sincere study because he came to find the arguments for God unconvincing; he now lives a morally serious secular life. Verdict: I reluctantly say not saved. (3) Priya is a non-resistant agnostic: the problem of evil genuinely torments her, she wishes God existed, she offers tentative prayers to a God she half-hopes is there, and she keeps searching. Verdict: saved. (4) Daniel grew up evangelical and was publicly devout for years, but he now admits he never really believed and used his faith mostly for status; he still invokes God to manipulate religious relatives. Verdict: not saved. (5) Blessing is a devout Nigerian Muslim whose Christian friends have articulated the gospel to her clearly; after sincere study, she finds the doctrine of the Trinity incoherent and the incarnation idolatrous, and she remains a faithful Muslim. Verdict: I am pulled toward saying saved. (6) Sam was abused by a priest as a child and left the church in fury; he now says he hates God. Verdict: saved. (7) Nia has a severe cognitive disability and cannot form or assess propositional beliefs about God; she is affectionate, joyful, and forgiving. Verdict: saved.

Two of these verdicts worry me because the cases look alike but feel different to me: Mateo and Blessing have both received a fairly clear articulation of Christianity and both sincerely reject it on intellectual grounds, yet I lean toward saving Blessing and not Mateo. Am I tracking a real difference between apostasy and never having accepted the faith, or should I overturn one of those two verdicts? My half-formed principle is that God saves everyone whose basic orientation toward the true God is not a culpable rejection of God, where culpable rejection requires both a genuine presentation of the truth and a free, adequately informed refusal. But that principle seems to break on my own cases, especially the Mateo–Blessing pair, and perhaps also on Nia, who cannot orient herself propositionally at all. What principle, or small set of candidate principles—a repaired version of exclusivism, inclusivism, or pluralism, a hybrid, or something else altogether—comes closest to capturing all seven verdicts? Please state it precisely, explain what it predicts about a new case I have not listed (someone raised in a polytheistic tradition who once encounters Christian missionaries through a badly translated tract and remains in her own tradition), and identify which of my verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate.
