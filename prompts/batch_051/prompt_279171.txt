ive been reading Rousseau's Social Contract for a class and i'm stuck on the general will. The view seems to give a really clean answer to a problem that bothers me about democracy: if the majority votes for a law I hate, in what sense am I still free? Rousseau's answer, as I understand it, is that I have two wills—my private will and my will as a citizen. The law tracks the latter, so when the state enforces it against me, it's enforcing what I already will qua citizen. That's tidy and it explains why laws can bind us even when we disagree with them. But I also have a pretty firm intuition that being fined or jailed for something I sincerely oppose is just coercion. No amount of saying "this is your real will" makes it feel like freedom. I've heard people defend Rousseau by saying the coercive part is only about forcing you to be a citizen, not forcing you to agree with each law. But that still sounds like forcing, and I don't see why relabeling the part of me that wants it changes anything. I don't want a history lecture—I want to know what the strongest versions of each side are, and what it would actually cost to give up either the theory's neat solution or my intuition about coercion. Is there a reading where they're compatible, or am I fooling myself?
