I got roped into giving the toast at the retirement party for the guy who has run our department's machine shop for thirty years. Officially he's a technician, but a depressing number of dissertations in our philosophy of science group only got finished because he kept some finicky piece of equipment alive. The room will be about half phenomenologists and philosophers of technology who know Heidegger's tool-analysis cold, and half his family and the shop staff, who will start to glaze over the second it sounds like a seminar. So I need a five-minute speech that is genuinely built around readiness-to-hand and the breakdown of equipment — the way a hammer disappears into the work until it breaks and then suddenly shows up as an object with properties — rather than a speech with the terminology just sprinkled on top.

Ideally the speech should do the thing it describes: talk about him mostly through the moments when things broke (the vacuum pump dying two days before a deadline, the cracked cryostat, the morning the mill was down — invent plausible details as needed), because he was most invisible exactly when he was most load-bearing. It needs to stay warm and funny for the family while the Heideggerians can tell the structure is doing real philosophical work, and it shouldn't curdle into a lecture or into the cliché about only noticing people once they're gone.
