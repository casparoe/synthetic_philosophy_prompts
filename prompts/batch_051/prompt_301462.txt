I have been trying to work out what it takes for a legendary tradition to count as history, and I do not trust the pattern of my own verdicts. Here is where I currently stand on the familiar cases. (1) The Trojan War seems to me historical in a thin sense: I accept that a Late Bronze Age city at Hisarlik was destroyed around 1200 BCE, that Hittite diplomatic texts mention a polity called Wilusa in northwestern Anatolia, and that a Hittite treaty names one of its kings as Alaksandu. I do not accept Helen, the Wooden Horse, or a literal ten-year siege as historical; those feel like legend. The label I want is something like legend anchored to a real place and a real conflagration. (2) King Arthur I treat as essentially legendary. The earliest narrative sources come centuries after any plausible setting, no contemporary document names him or an Arthurian court, and there is no excavated site that commands agreement the way Hisarlik does. I am open to the thought that a memory of some sub-Roman British war leader was absorbed into the stories, but I do not think we can individuate a historical Arthur. (3) The Exodus I accept neither as history nor as pure invention. I reject the biblical account as an event: nothing like six hundred thousand people leaving Egypt through miracles. But I cannot shake the sense that there is a small historical kernel. The Merneptah Stele, from the end of the thirteenth century BCE, places a people called Israel in Canaan; Egyptian sources attest Levantine or Semitic-speaking groups in the Delta at various points; and it would be surprising if no Levantine group ever left Egypt for Canaan. So I find myself wanting a verdict of some departure happened and was later scaled up. (4) The Dorian invasion looks to me like a legend that scholarship turned into history and then turned back into legend. The Greek story of the return of the Heracleidae was once read as evidence of a single great Dorian migration into the Peloponnese after the Mycenaean collapse; I now treat the invasion largely as a modern myth, even though the Doric dialect map is real and needs some explanation other than a single event.

The principle I keep reaching for is an anchor principle: a tradition deserves the status of history only when evidence independent of the tradition, and roughly contemporary with its setting, anchors the particular element we care about, such as a named place, a named people, or a named ruler. On that principle, Troy gets its anchor from Hittite texts and archaeology; the Exodus gets a thinner anchor from the Merneptah Stele for the people Israel, though not for any departure from Egypt; Arthur gets no anchor; and the Dorian invasion gets none. But I can already see the strain. A Hittite mention of Wilusa anchors the existence of a city, and possibly a king, but not a Mycenaean attack on it; and the Merneptah Stele anchors an Israel already in Canaan, not a group that had left Egypt. So either I have to surrender the Exodus kernel, or I have to add a clause under which generic background plausibility (it would be surprising if no one ever left Egypt for Canaan) may carry real weight. I want to know whether there is one defensible principle that delivers all four of my verdicts, and if not, which verdict I should overturn.
