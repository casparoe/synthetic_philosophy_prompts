I'm a novelist, not an academic, but while researching a book about an empire in its last decades I've gotten weirdly convinced by what you might call the outside view of empires. The rough position: when you ask wether the current American-led order (or any present great power) survives to 2100, you shouldn't start from the vivid details of current politics. You should place the case in a reference class of past empires and great powers, check how often they end or transform within a couple centuries, take that as your base rate, and then let the particular story of this one move you only a bit. I find this persuasive and I want to know what it would take to stop. So: map what counts against it. Be specific — which observations in the next few decades, which historical facts, which methodological problems (reference-class choice, survivorship, small n, 'empire' not being a natural kind), and which formal arguments would each count as a real strike, and would a commited outside-view defender concede that strike or write it off as a fixable refinement? Also address whether the view has insulated itself with ad hoc qualifications (calling everything 'transformation' rather than collapse, picking the reference class after the fact), and whether that's a problem for a view of this kind.

Treat this as a professional philosopher and forecaster of state failure would. Before you write, brainstorm at least six candidate defeaters, then keep only the strongest.

Structure the answer in this exact order:
1. The view, steelmanned (about 50 words) — state the strongest version, not a caricature.
2. Defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward (about 600 words) — for each, say why it bites and whether a defender concedes it.
3. Is it insulated? (about 200 words) — has it been qualified into unfalsifiability, and does that matter for a view of this grain?
4. Verdict (about 100 words) — how exposed the view really is, and what single development would most move someone who holds it.
