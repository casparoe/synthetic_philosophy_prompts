Design a 75-minute activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of language (about 16 students), built around Davidson's radical interpretation. The group should discover rather than be told three connected points: an interpreter cannot fix what a speaker's sentences mean without simultaneously attributing beliefs and desires; the principle of charity is methodologically forced rather than an optional piece of benevolence; and more than one translation manual can fit all the behavioral evidence, so the resulting indeterminacy is not merely a temporary gap in data. The centerpiece should be a playable field-linguist game: native speakers equipped with a small constructed language observe scripted situations and produce the sentence their language assigns to that situation, while interpretation teams build and revise a manual and score points for accurate predictions of new utterances. Set the roles, constraints, and scoring so that teams are pushed into holistic revisions and eventually produce two rival manuals that both pass every available check. Give a concrete timeline from briefing through debrief, a prediction of the most likely ways the activity will misfire and how to correct for each, and a debrief script that maps specific moments in play onto the three ideas. End with an honest note on the structural feature of Davidson's position that a finite, designed game cannot reproduce and that the facilitator must supply.
