I have been reading about Taurek-style rescue cases alongside some recent discussions of algorithmic fairness, and I keep noticing a structural parallel that I do not trust myself to assess. In the moral case, you can save either one person or each of five people; Taurek's thought is that the five are not allowed to pool their separate claims into a single, weightier claim that outweighs the one, so a coin flip between saving the one and saving the five can be perfectly appropriate. In the machine-learning case, I see a similar resistance to aggregation: a model can have better overall accuracy, yet critics will object that the accuracy gain was bought by concentrating serious errors or burdens on a few people. The small gains spread across many users are not, on this objection, a sum that silences the severe losses of a few. Both positions seem to deny that adding things up across separate persons is a legitimate way to justify imposing a cost on one person.

Please act as a philosopher who works seriously on both normative ethics and the conceptual foundations of algorithmic decision-making. I would like you to stress-test this parallel rather than simply approve it. Is it a genuine identity of structure — for instance, are both expressing the same separateness-of-persons point, or a prohibition on adding utilities across persons — or is it a loose analogy that leans mostly on the word "aggregate"? What would need to be true of both sides for results to transfer from one debate to the other? Concretely, could Taurek's coin flip be restated as a fairness constraint on a classifier, or would that import life-and-death assumptions that the machine-learning setting does not share? Begin by brainstorming several possible mappings and their failure points, then give your verdict on which parts of the parallel are deep and which are superficial.
