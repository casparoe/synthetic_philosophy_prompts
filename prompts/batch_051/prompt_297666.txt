You are a moral philosopher with expertise in the ethics of authority, virtue theory, and the social psychology of obedience and conformity. Think carefully and at length; before you commit to any ranking, brainstorm the different kinds of evidence, cases, and arguments that would bear on the view below.

I have come to hold the following position, and I want a rigorous map of what would count against it. The position is this: deference to authority is a genuine moral virtue, not merely a coordination device or a lamentable necessity. Within a well-functioning social practice with internal standards of competence and conduct — law, surgery, military command, air traffic control — and when an authorized role-holder is acting within the warrant of that role, an agent often ought to subordinate her private judgment to the role-holder's decision, and it can be a mark of good character that she does so without experiencing her agency as diminished. On this view, the wrongs dramatized in Milgram's obedience experiments and in mid-century totalitarian bureaucracies do not show that deference itself is vicious. They show what happens when role-bound deference is misdirected: toward people acting outside the warrant of their role, or inside practices that are themselves corrupt because they lack transparency, answerability, or genuine protection for the vulnerable. The virtuous deferrer is therefore not passive or abdicating. She monitors for exactly two things — authority acting beyond its role, and corruption of the practice as a whole — and withdraws deference when she finds either.

I want to know what would have to be true for this position to be false. Give me the evidence and arguments that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Address at least these lines of attack: (a) superficially well-functioning practices that encode serious systemic injustice, so the view appears to license deference into wrongdoing rather than deference within a practice that protects the vulnerable; (b) the psychological evidence that deference tracks arbitrary cues — uniforms, proximity, diffusion of responsibility — rather than the role-boundary and practice-quality conditions the view requires; (c) the concern that an agent cannot discharge the required monitoring without drawing on precisely the private judgment deference was supposed to supplement, so the view is either self-defeating or collapses into act-by-act consequentialism; (d) the charge that whenever a counterexample is raised, a defender can reclassify it as a case of role-exceeding or a corrupt practice, insulating the view from falsification; and (e) the military case of the subordinate ordered to do something she suspects is unlawful, where the chain of command insists otherwise — say whether the view gives her a determinate rule or merely returns the decision to private judgment under another name. For each line of attack, say whether a committed defender of the position would concede that it counts against the view or would dismiss it, and give the defender's reasoning. Then step back and assess how exposed the position actually is. Does it risk anything at all? If it turns out to be well insulated, is that a problem here — a sign that talk of deference as a virtue is doing no work — or is it what we should expect of a structural account of a practice-relative virtue?

Finally, I would personally find most persuasive a defeater that shows the position cannot draw the line between the virtuous deferrer and the Eichmann-style functionary in a way that carries moral rather than merely verbal weight. Say whether any of the defeaters above delivers that, and whether the position can be repaired in response or should be abandoned.
