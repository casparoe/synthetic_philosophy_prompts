I am a retired physical chemist, and philosophy has become a serious late-life pursuit rather than a field in which I can claim expertise. I have been working through Bernard Williams's distinction between internal and external reasons, and it has changed the way I read; I should like a careful philosophical reading of Dostoevsky's Crime and Punishment through that lens, not a summary of the novel. I intend to share the result with a small reading group of retired scientists, several of whom are wary of philosophy, so please write for intelligent non-specialists who have the novel fresh in mind.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of moral psychology who also reads fiction attentively. Before you write the final reading, brainstorm in a short opening paragraph: list the candidate reasons Raskolnikov gives for killing the pawnbroker, and list separately the motives the novel actually shows at work in him. Think carefully about which are which; the novel seems to me to insist on the difference.

Williams's position, as I understand it, is this: an agent has an internal reason to do something only if there is a sound deliberative route from the agent's present subjective motivational set — desires, projects, attachments, dispositions of evaluation, patterns of emotional response — to a motive for doing it. Reasons that would apply to an agent regardless of that motivational set are external reasons, and Williams is sceptical about whether such reasons can be properly asserted. For this exercise, use that as your working account.

Now for the reading itself. Take each of Raskolnikov's stated justifications — the utilitarian arithmetic he rehearses about the pawnbroker's money and the good it could fund, and the Napoleon or extraordinary-man theory from the article Porfiry later probes — and test them against the internalist criterion: is there a sound deliberative route from Raskolnikov's actual motivational set to a motive to kill, or does Dostoevsky present these as rationalisations that do not pass through his real concerns? What elements of that set — destitution and cramped circumstances, pride, shame, tenderness towards his mother and sister, a buried religious sensibility — does the novel treat as the actual springs of the act? Then address the opposing pressure in the novel. Sonya and Porfiry each urge upon Raskolnikov something he cannot yet reach by deliberation from his present motives: public confession, acceptance of suffering, and some form of moral regeneration. Are these best read, on Williams's terms, as external reason claims — claims that he has a reason to do these things whether or not his motivational set so much as points towards them? Or does the novel suggest that they are internal reasons after all, reachable from elements of Raskolnikov's own set that he has lost contact with but not lost? Finally, say plainly where the novel resists the internalist reading. Does Crime and Punishment ultimately vindicate Williams's scepticism about external reasons, or does it dramatise the cost of giving them up?

The reading should be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the novel to an illustration of a theory, and do not distort the theory to fit the novel. I should like to know what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not deliver.
