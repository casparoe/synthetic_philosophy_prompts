I am a third-year undergraduate reading a module on Dostoevsky and the philosophy of freedom. Next week I present a paper in our seminar defending the claim that the Grand Inquisitor's argument is best read not as a prediction about totalitarianism but as a self-confirming diagnosis: he can never distinguish the claim that most people cannot bear freedom from the possibility that his own civilisation has trained them to believe themselves incapable of it. To prepare, I would like you to play the Grand Inquisitor against me in a debate, so that I can practise defending the claim.

Play the Grand Inquisitor as the figure in Dostoevsky's chapter, but reason within the part rather than reciting the speech. He is intelligent, weary, and sincerely convinced that he is merciful; he is no straw man. He holds that most human beings cannot endure the burden of a free conscience; that they are happier, and less dangerous, when an authority supplies them with material security, wonder, and a higher power to submit to; and that the Church's correction of Christ's offer of freedom is therefore a costly work of love rather than a seizure of power. He is willing to concede limited points, such as that the Church has sometimes been corrupt or that a small minority can bear freedom, but he will never concede that Christ's gift was right for ordinary people or that the correction is worse than the suffering it prevents. He speaks as an ageing Spanish inquisitor and may translate my modern vocabulary into his own theological categories; he may put a counter-question, but only after he has answered mine. Keep his replies to a paragraph or two, stay in character until I say stop, do not break character or add stage directions, and write in British English.

Here is my opening move.

I grant, for argument's sake, everything you claim about the many: they are weak, they prefer security to freedom, they long to be led. What follows, however, is not that they cannot bear freedom, only that they have never been allowed to try. Your evidence is drawn from a population your Church has spent centuries teaching to regard itself as weak; under different teaching the people might have been different. Your diagnosis therefore appears unfalsifiable: every possible outcome is counted in its favour, because failure proves weakness and success vindicates authority. What observation would cause you to conclude that you are mistaken? And if no observation could, may I not say that it is your pride, or your need to be needed, rather than their weakness, that keeps the keys in your hand?
