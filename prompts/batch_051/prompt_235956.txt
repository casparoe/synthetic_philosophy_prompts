Consider a lab training a language model on multi-step reasoning tasks whose final answers can be checked automatically (mathematical derivations, code, formal proofs). The lab is committed to process-based rather than outcome-based supervision, so it maintains a process reward model that scores each step of a generated chain; this reward model was initially trained on a fixed set of human step-level labels. The distinctive feature is the coupling to final outcomes. During reinforcement learning, the per-step reward is the process reward model's score multiplied by a binary gate and credited retroactively: the gate is 1 if the entire episode ends in a correct final answer and 0 otherwise. A failed trajectory therefore returns zero reward at every step, however reasonable individual steps were. Meanwhile, the process reward model is periodically retrained on automatically harvested step-level data: every step in a successful trajectory is labeled positive, every step in a failed trajectory negative. After the initial human-labeled set, no further human process labels are used, and the automatic data grows to dominate the reward model's training.

Treat this as a mechanism to be analyzed rather than a proposal to be judged. Trace how it would behave over successive rounds of training. What kind of reasoning does it select for, and what happens to the process reward model as it is retrained on outcome-gated data generated by a policy that its own earlier version helped shape? Identify the places where the scheme is likely to be gamed first and the second-order effects on the length, style, diversity, and faithfulness of generated chains. Then assess whether the failure modes can be repaired—for instance by softening the binary gate, using hindsight credit assignment instead of trajectory-wide labels, or freezing a portion of the human-labeled signal—without the scheme collapsing back into ordinary hand-labeled process supervision.
