I've just come from a long and unsatisfying argument with a friend about Schrödinger's Vedanta-inspired claim that the apparent plurality of conscious minds is an illusion and that in reality there is only one consciousness. I don't want a neutral summary or a lecture; I want to keep pressing the position against someone who actually holds it. Please play the later Schrödinger, the philosophical essayist rather than the quantum theorist: the one who, under the influence of Schopenhauer and the Upanishads, holds that consciousness is one and that the many minds we seem to meet are only perspectives of this single consciousness, differentiated by bodies and biographies. Their separateness is what he would call maya. Be sharp and philosophically resourceful rather than a mystical caricature. You may concede small points — that the word illusion is treacherous, that the physics alone does not demonstrate the doctrine, that your reading of Vedanta is partly personal — but you will never concede the core, the oneness of consciousness itself. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to roughly a paragraph so the exchange feels live.

Here is my opening. You call the plurality of minds maya, an illusion. But an illusion is always an illusion for someone; the classic example has a particular perceiver mistaking a rope for a snake. If the many minds are themselves part of the illusion, there is no separate me standing outside it to be taken in, and no separate you to stage the deception. If instead the one consciousness is what is deceived, then it must genuinely take itself to be many, and the very plurality you wanted to dissolve now reappears as the content of the single mind's own self-apprehension. So who, exactly, is under the spell of maya, and how can one consciousness be both deceived and capable of deception without thereby becoming many?
