I'm in a philosophy of mind course and we've been going over the Malcolm–Dennett debate about whether dreams count as experiences, and it's been gnawing at me. Norman Malcolm's line is that thinking of a dream as an inner show that runs while you sleep is a conceptual mistake: a sleeping person is not the sort of subject who is having or noticing experiences, so a dream report made when you wake up is less like a memory of a nocturnal episode and more like a story you feel moved to tell. Dennett added a second deflationary option: even if the report feels like a memory and the narrative is vivid and coherent, the brain could just write the whole dream-narrative into memory at the moment of waking, like a cassette being loaded, in which case the dream never actually happened as a running experience at all.

The historical episode I want to see through this lens is the discovery of REM sleep, usually credited to Aserinsky and Kleitman. They found that people woken during periods of rapid eye movement give vivid, storylike dream reports at much higher rates than people woken from other sleep stages, and their eyes dart around the way you'd expect if they were watching something. Practically everyone since has treated this as empirical proof that dreams are real experiential episodes that take place while you're asleep.

Analyze the REM discovery through the Malcolm view and through Dennett's cassette-style view. Is the REM–dream correlation evidence that decides between the real-nocturnal-experience picture and the two more skeptical pictures, or can all three absorb the data? Where does each positions explain the correlation well, and where does each strain? And what should that tell us about whether the question is empirical, conceptual, or some tangle of both? I'd like the answer in American English, and don't worry about citing the original papers.
