Last year I moved countries and a word I'd used my whole life stopped behaving: the stuff sold here under the same name looks exactly like the thing back home, but it's a different thing underneath, and the difference matters (one you can eat, the other makes you sick). So the Twin Earth story finally stopped being a philosophy-class curiosity for me. The idea that the hidden stuff in your environment fixes what you're thinking, not the pictures in your head, made sense. But then I followed that idea and built an argument out of it that seems to prove too much, and I can't find the exit.

(1) What a speaker's word like "water" picks out is fixed by the hidden nature of the stuff in the speaker's environment: an Earthian and a Twin Earthian, internally identical, are talking about different things when each says "water."
(2) So if a person's environment changes, their word can change what it picks out without anything inside them changing. Drop an Earthian on Twin Earth without telling them and leave them long enough, and their word "water" now picks out XYZ, not H2O.
(3) Memory doesn't automatically correct for this. When that person now says "before all this I believed there was water in the glass," the word "water" in their memory report picks out XYZ, so the report attributes a belief about XYZ to a past self who actually believed something about H2O.
(4) So on this view the person can't tell from the inside whether they've been thinking about the same thing all along or were silently swiched between kinds; their memories of their own past beliefs could be wrong about what those beliefs were about.
(5) But that's absurd. A person can know what they believed yesterday, and whether its the same belief they have today, without checking the hidden structure of what's around them.
(6) So the idea in (1) must be false, or else it proves too much.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I'd most like to keep (1) and (2), and I suspect (5) might only look certain. But I want to know the price of whichever premise I have to give up. Please separate what's actually settled here from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, instead of presenting a contested line as the consensus. And put the final verdict as the very last line, in the form \answer{...} with the number of the premise you reject—or \answer{valid} if you think the argument is valid and I should just bite the bullet.
