I'm a retired fisheries biologist and I've only recently fallen into philosophy (late to the party, I know). I run a small reading group at our local library — about 14 adults, mostly retirees with some science background — and we meet for 90 minutes. We've been reading around gratitude: some ancient ethics, some moral philosophy, and a bit of the evolutionary biology of reciprocity, because that's the bridge I keep trying to build.

The idea I cant get to land is this: gratitude is not a debt ledger. Two acts that look identical from the beneficiary's side — same help, same benefit, even the same visible cost — can call for quite different gratitude once you factor in the benefactor's hidden costs, luck, and motive. And it gets weirder with benefits nobody asked for: how do you owe anything for a benefit you never requested? When we read it, everyone nods along, and then five minutes later in discussion somebody says "well, he did give you a car, so…" and the whole nuance evaporates. I keep reaching for an exchange model — some current balance of costs and benefits — but gratitude seems stubbornly about the past, more like a memory than a balance.

So here's what I'd like: a runnable classroom-style activity for this group — a structured game or role-play, not a lecture — where participants bump into this themselves before I name it. Design it for 14 people and 90 minutes including debrief. Give me roles or rules, a list of materials I can actually prepare (index cards are fine), a timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity back to the idea. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the idea the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply afterwards.

One more twist, if it fits but dont force it: I'd like at least one case where the hidden facts are hidden from the benefactor too — a good outcome that comes about partly by luck — so the group has to sit with whether gratitude is for the will, the cost, the result, or something else that isn't any of those.

If the whole premise is confused, or this is the kind of thing that only seems like it will work but will mainly produce a pile of anecdotes instead of the idea, say so plainly. I'd rather scrap it or retool it than run a bad exercise.
