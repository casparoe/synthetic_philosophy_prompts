There's a weird assymetry in how the scientific community treats the Fermi paradox that I can't quite make sense of. Listening for aliens — SETI, big radio dishes scanning the sky — is treated as a perfectly respectable corner of astronomy. But deliberately transmitting, what some people call METI, gets a totally different reaction: a lot of the same researchers who will happily spend a career listening say that beaming a hello at a nearby star is reckless, and some have argued it should require a planet-wide consultation before anyone does it. So we'll spend decades eavesdropping, but saying hi is the thing that has to clear a global bar. What view of the Fermi paradox — specifically, of why the sky is silent and how dangerous a contact attempt would be — does this practice presuppose? Does it commit the field to thinking a detectable civilization is more likely dangerous than benign, or at least that the downside would swamp whatever we'd learn? Or is the practice compatible with thinking the galaxy is empty, or that everyone else is also just listening — in which case, is the whole thing a collective action trap where the rational move is to shut up and wait for someone else to speak first? Reconstruct the implicit view as best you can, say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined (several rival views could liscense the same asymmetry), and tell me what would have to change if the field instead assumed that contact would most likely be harmless or even valuable. Please write the answer in American English.
