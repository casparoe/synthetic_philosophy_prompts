I work on LLM-based coding agents, and I've settled into a view I keep having to defend in design reviews. Whenever there is a trustworthy terminal signal—unit tests that pass, a patch that merges cleanly, a benchmark score—I argue that supervision should be outcome-based: reward the model only for the final result, and treat any reward assigned to intermediate steps (which intermediate edits were good, which tool calls were sensible, how the model decomposed the problem) as a crutch we reach for only because the terminal signal is sparse, noisy, or expensive. I've come to think this is not just an engineering convenience. The outcome-only position has a principled rationale: it refuses to smuggle in an opinion about what correct reasoning looks like, and so it leaves the model free to find strategies we would not have blessed in advance. Process-based supervision, in contrast, bakes the supervisor's taste into the credit assignment, and I suspect it mostly steers what the model learns toward conformity rather than capability.

The position I want stress-tested is roughly this: wherever a reliable terminal outcome signal exists, outcome-based supervision is the correct default, and process-based supervision is only ever a statistical tool for variance reduction under sparse reward—never a source of normative constraints on how the model should reason. I'm an engineer rather than a philosopher, so I'm not sure what the full map of evidence against a view like this would even look like. Tell me what would count against it. Cover at least: (a) empirical results or experiments, including ones that haven't been run yet, that should move me; (b) thought-experiment-style cases where the terminal signal is reliable but rewarding only the outcome still seems wrong; (c) arguments from analogy, especially the human-education analogy in which teachers correct a student's intermediate steps even when the final answer is right, and whether that analogy has any real force; and (d) considerations about how credit assignment shapes generalization rather than just sample efficiency. Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and distinguish the ones a committed holder of my position could reasonably dismiss from the ones I should concede are real costs. For calibration, the result that would trouble me most is evidence that outcome-only training with a reliable terminal reward systematically narrows the space of strategies a model learns even while the metric keeps improving. Finally, give me an honest assessment of whether I have insulated the view into unfalsifiability by interpreting every benefit of process supervision as "just variance reduction"—and if I have, whether that is a problem for a claim like this.
