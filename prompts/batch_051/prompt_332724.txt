I've been using a distinction i want stress-tested before I lean on it too hard. I call it 'contamination logic' versus 'desecration logic' in moral psychology. Contamination logic is about physical or biological uncleanness: it's contact-based, spreads by touch or proximity, and the response is cleansing or avoidance. Desecration logic is about damage to a thing's meaning or standing: burning a flag, using a family heirloom as a doorstop, spitting in a holy place. It doesn't seem to need contact, and washing doesn't fix it; what fixes it is punishment, repair, or some kind of re-consecration. My claim is that a lot of what gets lumped together as the 'purity/sanctity' moral foundation is really desecration logic, and that when people say they feel 'dirty' after being lied to or cheated they're borrowing contamination talk metaphorically to describe something that's actually about their standing being degraded. But sexual purity norms seem to be a problem for me, because they look like contamination logic even when nothing sacred has been desecrated. So: is this distinction already in the literature under some name, is the central claim actually true, and where exactly does it break down? I'd like the honest version, not just encouragement. Also, please separate what's fairly settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus. And if you could write it as continuous prose in British English, with no headings, bullets, bold or tables, that would be great.
