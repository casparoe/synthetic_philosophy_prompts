I've been thinking about promises nonstop since a friend broke a serious one to me last month, and I've read just enough Hume in a class to be annoying about it. He argues in the Treatise (Book 3, Part 2, Section 5, "Of the obligation of promises") that the obligation of a promise can't be natural, and the line that really got me is this: "A change of the obligation supposes a change of the sentiment; and a creation of a new obligation supposes some new sentiment to arise. But 'tis certain we can naturally no more change our own sentiments, than the motions of the heavens." I tried to turn his whole case into the following argument, and I half-believe the conclusion (it would kind of let my friend off the hook), which makes me suspicious. I'm pretty sure something goes wrong, but I cant pin the step.

(1) If a promise obliges me, the obligation has to come either from the mental act behind saying "I promise" or from the words themselves. (2) No mental act can supply it: a resolution won't do, since resolving to run tomorrow doesn't oblige me; a desire won't do, since I can promise while dreading the thing; willing the promised action won't do, since the will concerns present acts and the performance lies in the future. (3) The only remaining mental act would be willing an obligation itself into existence, and that's exactly what the quoted passage rules out: I can't conjure a new sentiment, and so a new obligation, by an act of will. (4) The words won't do it on their own either: apart from a prior convention, "I promise" is just a report of my current intention, and reporting an intention creates no more obligation than the intention itself did. (5) So a promise can bind only because a convention already gives "I promise" its obligating force. (6) But a convention of promise-keeping just is a general practice of taking "I promise" to obligate — so invoking it presupposes the very bindingness it was supposed to explain. (7) Therefore nobody is ever genuinely obligated to keep a promise; the pull we feel is self-interest and habit dressed up as duty.

Is the argument valid, and if not, which step exactly fails? If you think it is valid, which premise is the weakest and what's the strongest replacement for it? Be specific about what "convention" has to mean for step (6) to work, and whether that's the only way to read it.
