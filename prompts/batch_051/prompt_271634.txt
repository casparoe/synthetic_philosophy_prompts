I am a journalist who reports on mathematics and the philosophy of science, and after a year of reading and interviewing people about what mathematicians actually do when they produce new results, I have come to hold — tentatively, and with some unease — a quasi-empirical view of mathematics in the spirit of Lakatos's *Proofs and Refutations*. As I understand it, the view says that mathematical knowledge grows through conjectures, informal proofs, and the search for counterexamples; that proofs are themselves fallible artifacts, revised under pressure from recalcitrant cases; and that mathematical concepts are stretched and amended in response to anomalies in much the way scientific theories are adjusted in response to data. I want to stress-test this conviction the way a good editor stress-tests a story. What would count against it? I want the decisive defeaters separated from the merely awkward ones, and ranked within each group. Which observations about real mathematical practice would undermine the quasi-empirical reading — for example, a demonstration that formalization can in fact eliminate the fallibility of informal proof, or that the historical revision of proofs is better explained by the routine correction of gaps than by anything resembling an empirical feedback loop? What would the view predict about mathematics that a formalist or logicist rival would not predict, and which of those predictions, were they to fail, would do the most damage? Which philosophical arguments — the context-of-discovery/context-of-justification distinction, the claim that mathematics lacks the kind of independent data the natural sciences have, the charge that the view collapses into a merely sociological account of mathematical acceptance — would a committed quasi-empiricist concede have force, and which would they dismiss as aimed at a caricature? Finally, I want an honest verdict on whether the position, once the necessary qualifications are added, becomes so well insulated that nothing could count against it — and whether, if so, that insulation is itself a reason to distrust it.
