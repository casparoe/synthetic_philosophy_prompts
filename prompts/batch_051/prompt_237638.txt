Please give me your credence that Davidson's denial of metaphorical meaning is false for at least some central cases, and show me the derivation. By Davidson's denial I mean this: a metaphor means only what its words mean literally; it has no non-literal content of its own; and what it appears to convey is really a set of effects that the literal meaning causes in a hearer. By central cases I mean both fresh metaphors in ordinary conversation (for example, calling a colleague a bulldozer) and entrenched or dead conventional metaphors such as argument as war and time as space, which the conceptual metaphor tradition associated with Lakoff and Johnson treats as traces of real cross-domain mappings in thought rather than mere stylistic embellishment.

Answer in six titled sections, in this order.

1. Precisification (2–4 sentences). State the exact proposition your credence concerns, which cases it ranges over, and what would count as resolution.

2. The number (1 sentence). Give a single credence between 0 and 1 and a one-clause gloss of it.

3. Decomposition (6–8 items; roughly 20–40 words each). State the sub-questions on which your credence depends, give each a rough probability, and add one sentence of justification. Cover at least these seven sub-questions:
   (i) Novel interpretation: for at least some fresh metaphors, comprehension is a constrained interpretive recovery rather than free association triggered by literal falsity.
   (ii) Novel content: what is recovered is a propositional speaker meaning, not merely an image, affect, or loose set of associations.
   (iii) Conventional mappings: patterns such as argument as war and time as space are better explained by real conceptual mappings than by the historical sedimentation of once-literal usage.
   (iv) Dead metaphors: at least some dead metaphors carry a conventional non-literal content that a competent speaker must recover even after the metaphor is no longer felt.
   (v) Mapping as meaning: if such conceptual mappings exist, they belong to meaning in a defensible sense, rather than being a pre-linguistic cognitive substrate that only causally shapes speech.
   (vi) No patch: even the strongest version of the Davidsonian causal account cannot explain away the best-supported of the above.
   (vii) Substantive dispute: the disagreement is substantive rather than a definitional wrangle over how broadly to use the word meaning.

4. Combination (2–4 sentences). Show how the item credences combine into your final number. State explicitly which items you treat as conjunctive, which as alternative routes, and what independence or conditional assumptions you make.

5. Sensitivity (3–5 sentences). State which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and say roughly what your final credence would be if that item rose to 0.9 and if it fell to 0.1.

6. What would move me (2–4 sentences). Name the concrete evidence or argument—behavioral, linguistic, developmental, or neurocognitive—that would most shift your final credence.

Aim for 450–700 words total. Do not give citations, a bibliography, or further-reading list; keep the argument self-contained.
