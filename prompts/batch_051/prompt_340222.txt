I keep noticing that people argue about the right to strike as if strike is a clean category, but as soon as you push on work-to-rule or a sympathy walkout the boundries go fuzzy. I'd like a working definition of a strike — the kind I could use to sort cases when thinking about what the right to organise should actually protect, not a dictionary gloss.

Here are the cases I've been testing candidate definitions against:

- A standard walkout by unionised factory workers over pay. I'm confident this should count.
- An employer lockout. I'm confident this shouldn't count as a strike, but a good defintion should say why not.
- A consumer boycott of a firm.
- Work-to-rule, where staff follow every rule literally and the whole operation slows to a crawl.
- A coordinated sick-out by nurses.
- A sympathy strike by dockers who have no grievance with their own employer but refuse to handle cargo for a struck firm.
- A wildcat walkout that the union hasn't authorised.
- A one-day stoppage by employees purely to protest government climate policy.
- Gig workers collectively logging off a platform for an afternoon.

My prior, for what it's worth, is that the deep thing about a strike is that it's a coordination move — it only bites because enough people withdraw together — so a definition that makes it all about individual grievance expression feels wrong to me. Give me the definition, run it across the list, and tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, then either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. If your definition drops the coordination element, say why I should let go of that prior.
