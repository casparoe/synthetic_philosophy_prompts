I teach metaethics, and I have spent the last several years watching the evolutionary debunking literature grow without, as far as I can tell, changing anyone's mind. The argumentative landscape has become almost perfectly predictable. The debunking side — the side that descends from the Darwinian dilemma associated with Sharon Street — argues that natural selection shaped our moral judgments because they motivated adaptive behavior, not because they tracked any independent moral facts, and that this off-track causal history defeats, or at least seriously debunks, those judgments unless realists can supply a vindicating story. Realists reply, in the third-factor style associated with David Enoch and others, that selection and moral truth can share a common cause: the same facts that made certain moral beliefs fitness-enhancing can also be the truth-makers of those beliefs, so the causal influence need not be off-track after all. Debunkers answer that third-factor stories are merely possible, gerrymandered to save realism, and that a merely possible vindication does not remove a defeater. Realists answer that the debunker's defeater principle — roughly, that a belief is unjustified if its holder would have held it regardless of its truth — is far too strong, since it would rule out beliefs we plainly take to be justified. Debunkers answer that no such sweeping principle is needed, because the moral case is special: moral beliefs are especially insensitive to the moral facts. And so on. Guy Kahane's now-standard distinction between narrow debunking, which targets stance-independent moral truths, and wide debunking, which targets evaluative judgment generally, has been absorbed into the standard vocabulary, and recent papers increasingly recast the argument in modal terms of safety and sensitivity; but the basic standoff — whether off-track causal influence defeats, and what a vindicating explanation would have to look like — has not moved.

I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement about a defeater principle that could in principle be settled, a verbal disagreement about what "debunking" means, or two groups answering different questions — one doing causal genealogy of moral judgment and the other doing first-order epistemology of defeat — under a single heading? Do the sides differ about facts, about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions neither can argue for? Is the hidden disagreement really one between internalist and externalist pictures of epistemic defeat, or about whether vindication requires an actual causal connection or only a possible one? My own working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than confirmed, is that the debate is three debates fused together: a causal-historical one about how our moral judgments were actually formed, a modal one about whether those judgments are safe or sensitive, and a dialectical one about what a realist is entitled to assume when replying to a debunker. The fusion, I suspect, is doing most of the work in keeping the argument going.
