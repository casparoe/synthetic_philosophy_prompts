After a long argument with a friend about what is lost when work is automated, we found ourselves stuck on a variation of Nozick's experience machine. The only change from the standard version is the content: the machine simulates a life of difficult, meaningful work — a craft slowly mastered, setbacks worked through, students or colleagues who seem to learn from you — indistinguishable from a real working life from the inside, though nothing made or taught inside it ever reaches anyone real, and nothing persists once you unplug. One of us said this showed that full automation could cost us nothing of value, since the experience of working could be preserved exactly; the other said it showed the opposite, since the simulated work has no real effects. Please work through what Nozick's standard objections to the machine can say under this variation. In particular, does the objection that we want actually to do things, not merely to have the experience of doing them, still have purchase when the simulated content is exactly the kind of difficult doing people claim to value for its own sake — or does that objection now collapse into the objection about being a certain sort of person, or into the objection about contact with reality, and what follows for the automation dispute?
