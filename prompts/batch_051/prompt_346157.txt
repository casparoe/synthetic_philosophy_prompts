I'm taking a graduate seminar on democratic theory, and we've been comparing three forms of direct democracy: Swiss citizen-initiated referendums, California ballot propositions, and the 2016 Brexit referendum. A recurring question is whether any of these actually count as exercises of the general will, or whether they are something more like a partial or factional decree. I keep getting stuck on a passage from Rousseau's *Social Contract*, Book II, Chapter 1. I've copied it below from an online translation:

"the general will alone can direct the State according to the object for which it was instituted, i.e., the common good: for if the clashing of particular interests made the establishment of societies necessary, the agreement of these very interests made it possible. The common element in these different interests is what forms the social tie; and, were there no point of agreement between them all, no society could exist. It is solely on the basis of this common interest that every society should be governed."

I understand that Rousseau is saying the general will has to aim at the common good, but I'm not sure how to use that as a criterion in practice. If a California proposition passes with 55% of the vote, and the winning side genuinely believes it serves the common good while the losing side believes it serves a particular interest, how would Rousseau tell which it is? Is the fact that citizens voted at all enough to make it an exercise of the general will, or does the content matter independently of the procedure? And does his phrase about "the clashing of particular interests" suggest that a referendum win by a narrow majority is exactly the kind of factional outcome he's worried about, or is that an anachronistic reading? I'd also like to know how this passage bears on the debate over whether referendums should be binding on representative legislatures. Please separate what is settled in the scholarship from what is genuinely contested among Rousseau scholars and democratic theorists, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
