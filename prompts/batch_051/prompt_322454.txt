I have been working through some recent literature on the "wrong kind of reasons" problem, and I want to check that I have understood what is actually being claimed. The canonical example is an evil demon who threatens to harm you unless you admire him. The literature generally grants that, once the threat is in place, you have some reason to admire the demon; the puzzle is supposed to be about what sort of reason this is. But this is where I lose the thread. If you have a reason to admire him, then admiration is presumably the correct, fitting attitude for you to have in that situation—the attitude that gets things right. And if that is so, why is the demon case treated as a problem at all? It looks like a straightforward case of admiration that is genuinely fitting but normally outweighed by stronger reasons not to admire cruel beings. Is that right? If not, I want a committed answer rather than a survey of positions: tell me exactly where my reasoning goes wrong, explain the difference between having a reason for an attitude and the attitude's being fitting, and say which of those two things the demon's threat actually gives you. If the threat does not make admiration fitting, what does it contribute to the normative situation instead?
