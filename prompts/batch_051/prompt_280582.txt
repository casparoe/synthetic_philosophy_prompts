I've been trying to pin down what makes something a real optimizer, as opposed to just a thing that ends up looking optimized after the fact. The three criteria I keep coming back to are these: first, the thing has some representation of the objective it's after, even if it's a weird one; second, it actually searches over a space of alternatives instead of just running a fixed rule, and that searching is part of what explains the result; and third, the objective is doing the causal work, meaning the objective explains the behavior rather than just being a story you can tell about the behavior afterwards. Now here's the case that's been bugging me. Imagine a chess program that was never told to win. It was made by an evolutionary setup where lots of small programs got copied and mutated if they survived longer against a randomly changing pool of opponents, generation after generation. After a huge number of generations it plays shockingly strong chess, but when you look at the code theres no variable anywhere that means win or checkmate. There is, however, a number buried in the program that, when you manually adjust it, predicts whether the thing will launch an attack; and a person who spent way to long reverse-engineering it found that most of its better moves also make its opponent's clock more likely to run out. So you can tell a story where it's a ruthless clock-flagging machine, and you can tell a story where it's a roundabout chess-winner, and both fit the behavior pretty much perfectly. Is this thing an optimizer in my three-criteria sense? If so, what is it optimizing, and what's the search space it's optimizing over: the moves it considers during a game, the space of programs the evolutionary process wandered through, or both? Please go criterion by criterion, say where my criteria underdetermine the answer, and then say whether the criteria themselves need fixing.
