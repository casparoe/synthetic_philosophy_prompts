I retired a few years ago from a career in atmospheric chemistry and I've got really into moral philosophy in a way I didn't expect. Lately I've been trying to work out what I actually think about one thing: very small harms spread across enormous numbers of people. My verdicts on particular cases look like they might not fit together, and I'd like you to do the reflective-equilibrium work for me. Give me a principle that captures as many of them as possible, stated precisely, plus a honest list of which of my verdicts it can't keep and which I should revise. If no principle gets them all without being silly, say so.

Here are the cases.

1. Dust specks against torture. One person is tortured for fifty years, versus some astronomically large number of other people each getting a single dust speck in the eye for a second or so. I choose the dust specks. And I don't think there's any number of dust specks, however huge, that would make me switch: each speck on its own is just about nothing.

2. Mosquito bites, equal totals. One person receives ten thousand mosquito bites over an afternoon, versus ten thousand people each receiving one bite. I choose to spread the bites out. The total number of bites is identical, so anyone who simply added up harms across people would be indifferent, but I'm not. Ten thousand bites on one body is a sort of horror, while one bite each is a mild nuisance. Within a single life, repeated tiny harms accumulate; across lives, maybe they don't in the same way.

3. Small change. One person loses £5,000 — their savings, say — versus fifty thousand people each losing a pound. I choose the fifty thousand one-pound losses. The aggregate sum is large, but it doesn't move me much, because each loss is trivial and the concentrated loss is severe. I'd probably still do it if it were a fiver each.

4. Headaches. One person gets a solid month of migraines versus a billion people each getting a single one-hour mild headache. I choose the billion mild headaches. Same shape as the others; no individual is badly off, and the one person is badly off.

5. Toothaches. One person is tortured for fifty years versus a billion people each getting a two-minute toothache — not agony, but definitely felt and definitely unpleasant. Here I start to wobble. I think I'd spare the tortured person and hand out the toothaches. But then why don't the dust specks aggregate? It feels as though a toothache is above some threshold where harms start to count toward the cross-person sum, and a dust speck is below it.

So my half-baked principle is this. Within one person, harms add up more or less in the usual way, and intensity trades off against duration. Across different people, by contrast, a harm only enters the sum once each episode clears a certain minimum of bad enough to count. Below the threshold it's treated as zero, and no number of zeroes can outweigh a severe harm; above it, enough episodes can eventually outweigh. That would handle 1 through 5 more or less.

But I don't much like this principle. It draws a sharp line through something that is plainly a continuum: make the speck a little grittier, again and again, and you will pass through the threshold by steps too small to notice. In my old field we'd never have tolerated a threshold without a mechanism, but maybe ethics just has to. Still, either there's an arbitrary knife-edge, or the threshold is really a very steep curve and dust specks do have some finite, maybe inconcievably large, number that flips me — which contradicts case 1. I also have no idea where on that continuum toothache falls, or how many toothaches overwhelm a torture.

I'd like you to fix this. Rebuild the principle, or replace it, so that it captures these verdicts without the knife-edge — or else tell me plainly which of verdicts 1–5 have to go, and why. Can I really say both that no number of dust specks outweighs torture and that a billion toothaches might? What decides where the line between speck and toothache falls? And what should the principle say about a borderline bit of grit, or about a harm that is just below the threshold but inflicted on a billion people every day for a year? Be explicit about which of my intuitions your preferred principle ends up rejecting.
