A friend and I have been going back and forth for weeks about whether being "coherent" in the decision-theoretic sense has real normative force, so he challenged me to write down my actual commitments and let people poke at them. Here's what I settled on, as honestly as I can state it:

(1) I think Dutch book and money-pump arguments track something real: if your credences or preferences can be arranged so you'll pay to lose money for sure, that's not just embarrassing, it's a defect in rational standing. (2) In the Allais paradox I prefer the sure $1M over the 89/10/1 gamble, and I also prefer the 10% at $5M over the 11% at $1M; I've known about the independence axiom for years and still doesn't feel irrational to me. (3) I think ordinary risk aversion about money is rational and needs no justification beyond itself. (4) I'm suspicious of independence specifically where the common component involves near-certainty or tiny probability — sure outcomes feel different to me in a way I can't fully articulate. (5) I take dynamic consistency seriously: if without any new information I'd predictably abandon my own plan just by re-arriving at the same choice point, then the original plan was irrational. (6) I also don't think I'm required to have a complete preference ordering; I remain genuinely undecided about some exotic pairs of options and I'm not ashamed of that.

Which of these are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and how badly? I want an audit rather than a lecture on what I should believe: order the real tensions by severity, say which pairs dissolve, and for each genuine tension tell me what the cheapest revision is and what it costs. If there's a single change that would clean up most of the mess, tell me that too.
