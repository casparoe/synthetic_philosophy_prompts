I'm a statistician, not a philosopher, and I'd like the propensity interpretation of fitness translated into my language. From what I've gathered, the tautology objection goes roughly: if fitness just means actual reproductive success, then saying the fitter have more offspring is just saying the ones with more offspring have more offspring, and natural selection stops explaining anything. The propensity interpretation responds that fitness is really a probabilistic disposition to survive and reproduce, like a biased coin's true probability of heads, not whatever happened in some particular generation. Restate that whole exchange in the vocabulary I actually use — fitness as a parameter, actual reproductive output as a noisy estimator of it, variance around the estimate, a sample versus a population — as faithfully as you can. Then give me a residue report: which parts of the propensity view map cleanly onto parameter-plus-estimator, which ones you had to stretch to fit, and which parts (causal role, drift, the original worry about circularity) have no counterpart in the statistical picture. I suspect the whole controversy is just the difference between a probability and an observed frequency, but I want to know whether that reading captures the debate or quietly changes the subject.
