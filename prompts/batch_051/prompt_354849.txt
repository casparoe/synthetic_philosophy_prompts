Write a short story whose central element is an antimeme: an idea or piece of information that resists being remembered or transmitted. Give it a concrete mechanism and make that mechanism load-bearing in the plot. For example, the act of understanding it might start a process that degrades the memory it creates; any attempt to paraphrase it might collapse into a harmless variant; recordings might be overwritten or mislaid; or the antimeme might survive only as a felt absence, so that people can reliably know that something is missing without being able to say what. The key constraint is that the resistance should be part of the story's causal economy, not merely a description of ordinary forgetfulness.

Set the story in a concrete institutional or personal situation — a research station, an archive, a call center, a family, a translator's office, a clinical practice — and show how one or more characters work around a thing they cannot hold in mind long enough to examine. Make at least one of the following questions felt in the story rather than argued about: Can someone act rationally on a belief available only in short intervals? Can a person know something they cannot retain or transmit? What happens to an institution whose founding purpose is itself an antimeme? The story should end with the reader understanding something specific about the original loss, without collapsing into a puzzle-box explanation.
