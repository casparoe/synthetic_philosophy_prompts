Since retiring from a career in the physical sciences, I have been reading philosophy of history, and a recent discussion group left me unable to referee a dispute I think you may be able to sort out. Two members argued about whether overdetermination makes singular causal claims about historical events illegitimate, taking the outbreak of the First World War as their example.

A political historian argued as follows. By 1914 there were several serious routes to a general European war: the alliance system, the momentum built into mobilization plans, imperial rivalry, and the July Crisis after Sarajevo. Because removing any one of these would at most have delayed or reshaped a war rather than prevented a war of that general kind, no single factor passes the usual counterfactual test for causation. Statements like "the alliance system caused the war" are therefore false or empty; the event was overdetermined, and the honest conclusion is to abandon singular causal claims at that level.

The other, who had trained in philosophy of science, replied that this proves too much. Large historical events are almost always overdetermined at some grain, so requiring strict difference-making would make most historical explanation impossible. Historians do not merely list equal conditions; they weigh which factors raised the danger most, which mechanisms did the actual work, and which were merely background. Causal explanation can select among such factors without any single factor meeting the removal-to-prevent test, because explanation answers to the question asked, not to an absolute standard of making the difference.

The historian countered that, without a counterfactual difference, calling something a cause is rhetorical emphasis. The philosopher answered that his own claim that the war was overdetermined is itself a causal-explanatory judgment about the plurality of causes, so he cannot consistently dismiss causal language as empty. She added that the counterfactual can be made to work by fixing the effect precisely: without the July Crisis escalating as it did, the particular war that began in August 1914 would not have begun then, even if another war would have come later.

The historian called this re-describing the effect to rescue a preferred cause; any condition, he said, can be made necessary by narrowing the description of what happened. The philosopher accepted that but denied it was a defect: causal explanation is always relative to a chosen effect, contrast class, and grain, and there is no standpoint from which the cause of a historical event is fixed independently of the explanatory question one is trying to answer.

I have not been able to determine which moves are sound. Would you adjudicate the exchange point by point — indicating which claims are right, which are overstated, and what both are missing — and then give an overall verdict on overdetermination and multi-causal explanation in history?
