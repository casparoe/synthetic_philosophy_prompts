So I've been turning the dogmatism paradox over in my head and I've realized I don't actually have a grip on the notion doing all the work: misleading evidence. The whole setup — if I know p, then I can know that any evidence against p is misleading, and it seems irrational to knowingly soak up misleading evidence, so I can rationally ignore any counterevidence — that whole inference lives or dies on what 'misleading evidence' means. But every time I try to define it I end up somewhere different. I want a working defintion, tested against these cases:

(a) A fair coin lands heads ten times in a row, and you're trying to figure out whether it's biased. The run really happened and the coin really is fair. Is the run misleading evidence about the coin?

(b) A friend tells you, sincerely and with perfectly good eyesight, that she saw Marcus at the party; Marcus wasn't there. Her testimony supports something false. Is it misleading evidence?

(c) Same friend, same claim, but this time she's lying to throw you off. Is her testimony misleading evidence, or is 'misleading' the wrong word because lying is something stronger than merely misleading?

(d) A real, unaltered photograph that's cropped so it makes two people look like they're shaking hands when they were actually six feet apart. Misleading evidence, or just a misleading picture?

(e) A randomized trial where the drug actually works, but by pure luck the treatment group starts out healthier, so the trial data come out supporting 'no effect.' Misleading evidence?

(f) A thermometer that's broken but happens to read the correct temperature on this one occasion. It's not misleading about the temperature — but is that verdict right, given that the instrument is unreliable?

(g) Back to the paradox itself: I know p on the basis of a proof I've checked carefully. A refutation of p gets published in a good journal. For me, given that I know p, is the refutation misleading evidence — or is it not even evidence against p for me, since misleadingness is relative to a person's total evidence and my evidence already includes p?

I think (a) and (b) are clearly misleading and (f) clearly isn't. I want to say (c) is still misleading, but then 'misleading' starts to sound thin, like it just means 'would lead a trusting person astray,' which might be the point or might be a bug. (g) is where I keep flip-flopping: sometimes I think misleadingness has to be assessed relative to a person's total evidence, and then the refutation isn't misleading for me — but then the paradox seems to dissolve for the wrong reason. Can you propose a definition, run it through all seven, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts?
