I've been developing a concept I call the annotation-loss model of reproducing propaganda for critique, and I want an honest assessment before I lean on it any further.

The phenomenon I'm trying to capture is this. A fact-checker embeds a manipulated video in a debunking; a journalist quote-posts a smear to refute it; an instructor shows extremist propaganda in class to analyze its rhetoric. In each case, the justification for reproducing the thing is that the critical frame travels with it at the moment of presentation: the harm is supposedly neutralized because the item is being handled as evidence or as a teaching object. My claim is that the frame does not travel with downstream copies. Once the video circulates as a clipped repost, a screenshot, or a half-remembered image, the frame has been stripped away, and the thing continues to function as propaganda anyway. Every framed reproduction therefore has two lives: a first life in which it is embedded in a critical context, and a long-tail second life in which it circulates bare. I call the process of losing the frame annotation loss.

The thesis I'm testing is that the distinctive harm of reproducing propaganda for critique comes mostly from the bare second life, not from the framed first use. If that's right, then the standard defenses of such reproduction—that the frame changes the act's meaning, or that the critic merely quotes rather than endorses—are incomplete: they grade the act by its first life and ignore its second.

I already know this sounds close to a few existing ideas: context collapse, insofar as the audience may not share the speaker's frame; the familiar finding that repetition can increase perceived truth even when a correction follows; and the more general point that attention is scarce enough that giving any of it to propaganda is a cost. My intuition is that annotation loss is more specific than all three, because it is not about the audience lacking shared context and not just about familiarity—it is about the object being copied out of its evidentiary frame and re-entering circulation with a new and dangerous autonomy.

What I'd like from you is a candid check: (1) Is the model coherent enough to carry the weight I'm putting on it, or does it collapse into one of the existing ideas I just named? (2) Reconstruct the strongest version of the thesis for me—the version a sympathetic but rigorous critic should attack. (3) Give the strongest objection to treating the bare downstream life as the locus of the harm. (4) If that objection succeeds, is the distinction worth keeping for anything at all?
