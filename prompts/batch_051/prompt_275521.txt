I work on the conceptual side of a central bank's digital-currency project, and I keep getting caught on a passage from Aristotle. In Book V, chapter 5 of the Nicomachean Ethics he writes:

"Now money serves us as a guarantee of exchange in the future: supposing we need nothing at the moment, it ensures that exchange shall be possible when a need arises, for it meets the requirement of something we can produce in payment so as to obtain the thing we need. Money, it is true, is liable to the same fluctuation of demand as other commodities, for its purchasing power varies at different times; but it tends to be comparatively constant."

A little later in the same chapter he adds: "Money then serves as a measure which makes things commensurable and so reduces them to equality."

The case that worries me is this. We are designing a retail central-bank digital currency with a built-in holding fee: any unit that sits unmoved in a household wallet loses one percent of its face value on the last day of each calendar month. The erosion stops only if the holder sends the units to a commercial bank, where they are exchanged at par for an ordinary bank deposit; if the holder later wants the sovereign digital units again, freshly issued units appear in the wallet. The stated aim is to make idle balances costly and to keep the instrument circulating.

My question is interpretive, not a policy question. Does this instrument count as the sort of thing Aristotle's passage is trying to pick out as money, and more specifically does it satisfy or defeat the function he identifies when he says money is a guarantee of future exchange and a measure that makes things commensurable? Is a small, predictable, administered decline in value just one more instance of the "fluctuation of demand" Aristotle says money is liable to, or does it abandon the constancy he says money "tends" to have? And if the only way to stop the erosion is to turn the units into commercial-bank money, has the guarantee migrated to the bank deposit, leaving the digital unit as a pure means of payment that no longer stores value — so that the two roles Aristotle ran together come apart?

1. The passage in Aristotle's argument (roughly 200 words): what problem money is introduced to solve and which roles he ties together; keep this tight and tied to the quoted lines.
2. The case, restated precisely (roughly 150 words): isolate the features of the holding fee that put pressure on the quoted claims rather than on general monetary economics.
3. The passage applied, approach by approach (roughly 500 words): take at least these four interpretive stances — the words of the passage, the purpose money serves in it, the most plausible answer if Aristotle had been asked directly, and what a reasonable modern reader of the same sentences would be committed to. Mark where these come apart.
4. Verdict and confidence (roughly 200 words): state whether the instrument is money in the passage's sense, defective as money, or something the passage is silent about; say how confident you are in your reading and single out the point you are least sure of.
