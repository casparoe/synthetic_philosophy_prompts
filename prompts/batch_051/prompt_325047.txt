I run a small forecasting unit inside a policy planning office. Our current task is to estimate, for a set of present-day great powers, how likely each is to undergo imperial-style decline within the next twenty-five years: breakdown of its constitutional order, loss of hegemony over its region, or a halving of its relative power. I have imposed a rule on my analysts: every forecast must begin with an outside-view anchor, selected before the analyst discusses the distinctive features of the case. The analyst must state the proposed reference class — all great powers since 1648, all democracies that have survived at least one full transfer of power, all empires that outlived their founding century, or all empires matched to the case on a short list of structural features — and justify its inclusion criteria in writing. The initial probability of decline must be the base rate of decline within that class, computed as exactly as our internal counts allow. Only then may the analyst introduce case-specific factors, such as a recent near-miss coup or the weakness of the party system, and each factor must carry a stated direction and a cap on how far it can move the probability away from the anchor. A second analyst must approve both the reference class and the adjustments before the forecast goes up the chain.

I adopted the rule because our office has a history of forecasts that were much more optimistic than the historical record about the durability of the present order, and the optimists' arguments always sounded, after the fact, like special pleading about how this time is different.

My deputy director objects that the rule is precision theater. The reference class problem, he says, is the whole game: an analyst can move the final number almost anywhere by choosing one defensible class rather than another, so our written justifications are just gerrymandering with extra steps. He adds that base rates from pre-nuclear empires or agrarian regimes are not informative about a state with nuclear weapons, a modern administrative apparatus, and mass literacy, and that treating them as informative dignifies noise with a decimal point. He wants to replace the anchor with structured scenario exercises, in which analysts construct several concrete pathways to decline or survival and argue over which are most plausible, without starting from historical frequencies.

I suspect he is right that the choice of reference class is doing much of the work, but I also suspect that abandoning the anchor lets motivated reasoning back in through the narratives, because scenario plausibility is even easier to bend than a base rate. Is the rule defensible as it stands? Is there a version of it that keeps the base-rate anchor while answering the reference-class objection, and would that version be better or worse than the deputy's scenario exercise? How should the size of case-specific deviations from the base rate be disciplined, if at all? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study forecasting and judgment under uncertainty, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
