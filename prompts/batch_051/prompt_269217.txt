I've spent the last year or so trying to pin down what I actually believe about violence, force, and pacifism. Part of it comes from arguments I keep having in a tenants' union, where the question whether I would ever use force, and whether refusing to use it just means offloading violence onto the police, stops being abstract pretty fast. I keep oscillating between the rationalist take that violence is just another policy instrument with costs and an older intuition that it's a seperate kind of wrong which consequentialism refuses to see. My current attempt at a settled position is a list of six commitments, and I want it audited for mutual consistency before I write it up anywhere public, because I can't tell which commitment is doing the sneaky work or where the contradiction actually is. The commitments, as I'd defend them today, are:

(a) Violence is intrinsically pro tanto wrong: it has a distinctive badness over and above the expected harm it causes, and a purely consequentialist account of that wrongness misses the thing that matters.
(b) Defensive force, up to and including lethal force, is usually permissible when it is necessary and proportionate to stop an imminent threat of grave harm, and this covers defending a stranger, not just oneself.
(c) Political violence by oppressed groups is never justified, no matter how cruel the regime, because nonviolent resistance is at least as effective and carries less of the distinctive wrong in (a). On my view the necessity defence simply never becomes available politically.
(d) If someone attacks my neighbour and the only realistic way to stop it in time is to call the armed police, I should call them, even though I cannot control how much force they use and they might kill the attacker.
(e) Most states lack the standing to use violence, but an individual police officer can still be justified in using force to stop a concrete, immediate attack.
(f) The distinction doing the real work is between force and violence: (b), (d), and (e) involve force, not violence, so they never trigger the prohibition in (a); (a) and (c) concern violence properly so-called.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture: which of these are in genuine tension, which only look as if they are, and what would have to give in each case. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, then say what the cheapest repair is and what it costs. I don't need citations or a reading list—just work from the commitments as I've stated them. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. And please write in British English.
