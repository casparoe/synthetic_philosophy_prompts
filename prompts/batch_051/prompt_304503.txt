I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been going back and forth with a few friends about a claim that keeps resurfacing in a piece I'm writing on whether genuine altruism exists. The claim is that all human action is ultimately self-interested, even the stuff that looks selfless. The soldier who jumps on a grenade does it because he couldn't live with himself otherwise, or because he wants to be remembered a certain way. The person who gives to charity anonymously still gets a warm glow or a little spike of self-satisfaction. The person who stays up all night helping a depressed friend does it to avoid feeling guilty, or because the friendship has payoff down the line. At bottom, so the claim goes, the motive is always about the self.

I'd like you to act as an expert in moral psychology and the philosophy of the social sciences. Take the claim in its strongest, most slippery form — not the strawman where people consciously calculate benefits, but the version where the self-interest can be unconscious, delayed, or just a pleasant feeling. Now: is this even testable, and if so, how? Design the best empirical test you can of the claim that putatively altruistic behavior is really self-interested all the way down. Be concrete about the study: who are the participents, what do they think the study is about, what choice do they face, and what exactly would you measure. Then spell out what a positive result would and would not show, what a negative result would and would not show, and what no test could settle even in principle — the part of the claim that will survive any evidence. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming two or three candidate approaches before you commit to the strongest one.
