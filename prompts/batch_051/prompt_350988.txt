I've been working through the two-envelope paradox and would like to see how a small, precise change to the setup affects the standard reasoning. In the usual version, two sealed envelopes are described only by saying that one contains twice as much money as the other. You pick one, open it, and find x dollars, then reason that the other envelope is equally likely to contain x/2 or 2x, so its expected value is (x/2 + 2x)/2 = 5x/4, which exceeds x; therefore you should switch. Since the same reasoning would apply whatever you found — and, indeed, before you opened anything — it seems to license switching even without looking, which symmetry says cannot be a genuine free lunch.

Now make the following change. A host first draws an integer n uniformly from {1, ..., 100}, then places 2^n dollars in one envelope and 2^{n+1} dollars in the other, and hands you one of the two envelopes at random. You may open your envelope and then decide whether to keep it or switch. Please work through the conditional expected value of switching for each kind of possible observation — in particular, the smallest possible amount, an interior amount such as 2^50, and the largest possible amount, 2^101 — and say exactly where the "switch no matter what you see" argument still goes through and where it breaks down.

Then reconcile the following pair of facts: for every possible observed amount except the largest, the conditional expected value of switching is greater than the amount actually observed, yet the policy of always switching has exactly the same overall expected value as the policy of never switching. Explain how both can hold, and identify which implicit premise of the unbounded original was doing the work in generating the apparent free lunch. Does the bounded setup dissolve the paradox, or does it simply relocate it into a single enormous, improbable loss?
