I've been going in circles on the old puzzle about why anyone votes. The chance that one ballot flips a large election is essentially zero, so a purely instrumental reason for showing up seems to vanish, and yet turnout is a real, stable thing. The standard reply is that voting is expressive, less like investing and more like wearing a team scarf or putting a sign in the yard. I find that answer suspiciously convenient, because it seems like you can retrofit it to anything: if people vote it's expression, if they stay home that's also expression, of alienation or whatever. So I want to know if the claim that most people vote primarily for expressive rather than instrumental reasons is a genuine empirical claim or just a story we tell.

People I talk to throw around possible tests—comparing turnout in races that are already safe against close ones, or fiddling with how visible the "I voted" sticker is—but those feel confounded. Safe races differ in campaigning and attention, and a sticker nudge might tell you about salience, not about underlying motives. So I'd like you to design the strongest concrete test you can think of to separate expressive voting from voting because you think there vote might be pivotal. Be specific about the setup, the population, and exactly what result would count for each reading. Point to where the identification problem is hardest.

And then be honest about what even a clean result couldn't settle: whether people are rationalizing after the fact, whether lab results carry over to real elections, or whether the expressive/instrumental distinction is itself too sharp. Please also flag what's actually settled among people who work on this versus what's still genuinely contested, rather than presenting one camp as the consensus. One formatting request: write it all as continuous prose, like an email—no headings, bullets, bold, or tables. I want to read it straight through.
