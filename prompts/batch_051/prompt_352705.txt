I've been teaching a seminar on Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the theatre, and I keep running into an interpretive puzzle I'd like you to work through rather than settle with the usual slogans. Rousseau's attack, as I read it, has at least three separable strands: a moral-psychological worry that spectators are moved without acting and so exhaust their virtue in safe emotional discharge; a social worry about amour-propre, according to which the audience goes to be seen and judged as much as to see; and a specific objection to the actor's trade, which he treats as the skill of appearing other than one is. Against the closed theatre he sets his positive image of the open republican festival, where the separation between actors and spectators dissolves and the people are themselves the spectacle.

Suppose, then, that a contemporary company stages what it calls "distributed-role immersive theatre": a three-hour piece occupying a disused civic building, in which each participant is assigned a minor named character with a few scripted lines and small tasks in the first twenty minutes; the action repeatedly halts until participants carry out their tasks; actors and strangers address participants in character; scenes branch according to participants' choices; and at one point the cast watches the participants enact a scene while the participants watch back. Attendance is ticketed and limited, and throughout the piece participants are visibly performing for one another's appraisal. This is not a form Rousseau could have known.

The question is what Rousseau, reasoning from the commitments of the Letter, would most likely conclude about it. Would he see it as answering his objection to the passivity of the spectator, or as universalizing the corruption he attributed to both actor and audience—or as something closer to his beloved festival? I want a single, argued prediction, but it should be built by walking through (a) which of his objections the form clearly blunts, (b) which it leaves untouched or intensifies, and (c) which features of the form—the ticketing, the scripted roles, the assigned characters, the look of others in the room—would do the most work in his eyes. End by saying where your prediction is most fragile, and what one additional fact about the performance would most change your mind.
