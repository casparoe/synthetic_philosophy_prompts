At my school everyone pretty much agrees on two things about tests, and I can't figure out whether both can be right at the same time. If a teacher raises class scores by teaching to the exam — drilling the question formats, chopping anything that won't be on the test — then adults say the scores stopped meaning anything, even though the number went up. But if another teacher raises the exact same scores by teaching well, everyone treats the improvement as real. Same metric, same improvement, completely different verdict. I see the mirror version with students too: a kid who memorizes worked problems from past papers to get through math is treated as someone who gamed the system, but a kid who does a ton of practice problems and basically learns to recognize problem types fast is treated as someone who learned the material, even though alot of the underlying skill looks identical from the outside. So what I want to know is: is there an actual principle that separates legitimately improving a metric from just exploiting it, or is the line mostly drawn after the fact, based on a story we tell ourselves about real understanding? If you give me a principle, run it on the case where the gamer still remembers the stuff a year later and the so-called real learner forgot everything the week after the exam, and tell me honestly whether the principle survives.
