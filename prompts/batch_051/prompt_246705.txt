I've been stuck on a variant of Kavka's toxin puzzle and I want a case built to order. Quick setup in case you need it: someone offers you a million dollars, paid tomorrow morning, if at midnight tonight you intend to drink a mildly nasty but harmless liquid tomorrow afternoon. By the time you'd have to drink, the moneys already yours, so at drink-time you have no reason to do it; the usual line is that you therefore can't form the intention, since you know you won't drink and you can't intend to do what you know you won't do.

Here's what I want: construct a case inside that exact payoff structure where two views of intention give opposite verdicts about whether the agent intends at midnight. On a belief-entailment view, intending to A requires believing you'll A, so there should be no intention. On a planning-role view, a state counts as an intention if it settles your deliberation, guides preparatory actions, and makes later non-performance register as a failure by your own lights. Constraints: no commitment devices (no promises, bets, contracts, or other people involved), no self-deception or memory tricks, the agent stays fully certain at midnight that they won't drink tomorrow, and the state has to do real preparatory work — you can't just stipulate a special feeling. The drink itself stays mildly unpleasant and otherwise pointless.

Then walk through what the agent says and does between midnight and tomorrow afternoon, why the belief-entailment view says there's no intention, why the planning-role view says there is, and which single feature of the case is doing the work. Say whether removing that feature collapses it back into the standard toxin conclusion. Finish by being honest about whether the case actually refutes Kavka's claim or just trades on a shifted sense of 'intend', and give the strongest comeback a belief-entailment defender would have.
