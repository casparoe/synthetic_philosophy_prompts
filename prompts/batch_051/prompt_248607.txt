I have been arguing with a colleague about a toy universe, and I want to find the exact point where our judgments come apart. Imagine a sealed arena — call it a careenium — containing millions of tiny colored spheres, simmballs, governed only by local, deterministic rules: each rolls until it strikes another sphere or a wall, and what it does next — bounce, stick, change color — depends only on the colors and speeds of the things it touches. Seen from close up, there is nothing but those local collisions. Seen from above, the collisions compose into large, slowly shifting patterns: blobs that approach the red patches, steer around obstacles, split, merge, and follow one another. It is almost irresistible to describe those blobs semantically: this one represents where a food patch is, that one wants to block the other's route. The question is whether those semantic-level patterns ever push the individual simmballs around, or whether every micro-trajectory is already fixed by the local rules and the symbols merely ride on top of the physics.

I put my credence that the semantic patterns are causally efficacious — not merely useful summaries, but doing genuine causal work over and above the microphysics — at roughly 0.4; my colleague puts hers at roughly 0.8. I want your own number, but more importantly I want the reasoning decomposed into the sub-questions your credence actually depends on: how much turns on whether the macro-level patterns are real rather than just compact redescriptions; on whether a real higher-level pattern can be causally relevant without adding any extra force to the micro-level, given that the micro-rules are complete; on whether the semantic content of a pattern contributes anything beyond its bare statistical or syntactic shape; and on which notion of causation is appropriate in a deterministic system — energy transfer, difference-making, information, or something else. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: items one through four should state the sub-questions your number most depends on, each with your rough probability and a one-sentence justification; item five should state your overall credence, how the terms combine, which single sub-question would move your number most if you changed it, and which of the two of us — 0.4 or 0.8 — the disagreement most likely lives in.
