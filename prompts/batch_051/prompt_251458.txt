Recently I was in a design review for an internal benchmark suite meant to select among models coming out of our RLHF runs. A senior engineer dismissed the whole proposal by invoking Goodhart's law, in the version often rendered as: a measure that becomes a target ceases to be a good measure. From that one line he concluded that any benchmark we actively optimize against is worthless for model selection, so the suite should not be built. I have since heard the same maxim used for everything from discouraging performance-based bonuses to arguing that learned reward models are inherently unsafe because they are proxies for human judgment. I want to know what the maxim actually commits someone to. Please lay out the distinct precise claims the Goodhart one-liner could be expressing: a statistical claim about correlations breaking down under selection, a claim about agents gaming the measure, a claim about the target itself shifting, a claim about the impossibility of stable proxies, and any other readings worth separating. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a defensible empirical claim, or false. Then tell me which readings would actually license my colleague's conclusion that benchmark-based selection should be abandoned, and which readings the one-liner does not support. I want the readings laid out as a menu with verdicts, not a single preferred interpretation.
