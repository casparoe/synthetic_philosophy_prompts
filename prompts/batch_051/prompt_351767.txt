After a career in the physical sciences, I have recently turned my attention to philosophy, and I have become absorbed by Gödel's modal version of the ontological argument. As I understand the proof, it first establishes that if a God-like being exists at all, then it exists necessarily, and then, by a principle of the modal system S5, passes from the bare possibility of such a being to its actual, necessary existence. I have worked through the derivation and find nothing formally amiss. That appears to leave, as nearly the sole substantive premise, the claim that the existence of a God-like being is possible. To my scientist's sensibilities, this sounds like a minimal concession: possibility is weaker than actuality, and we ordinarily grant that something is possible unless a contradiction has been demonstrated. The burden would therefore seem to fall on the atheist, who must show that a God-like being is impossible, not merely that its existence is unproven. Yet I keep encountering the view that most philosophers find the argument unconvincing, which suggests that my framing must contain an error. I would like to understand where the mistake lies. Is it in my slide from formal validity to rational compulsion? In the assumption that the principles of S5 apply unproblematically to metaphysical possibility? Or is the premise that a God-like being is possible far less innocent than it appears, and if so, what exactly does it already smuggle in?
