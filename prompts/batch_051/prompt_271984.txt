In my US history class we just finished a unit on the Lost Cause, the story white southerners built after the Civil War about how the war was never really about slavery and Lee was a saint and the South was this doomed noble civilization that just got crushed by numbers. Everyone in class treated it as obvious propaganda, and I get that. But then over break my dad put on Dunkirk and afterwards kept talking about the Dunkirk spirit and the little boats, and how Britain turned this total military disaster into something almost proud. And I realized I have a weird asymetry here: both are stories where a defeat or retreat gets turned into a myth about the special character of a people, and both involve leaving stuff out or twisting it, but we (the class, my dad, everyone I know basically) treat the Lost Cause as a shameful lie and Dunkirk as warm and admirable. Is there an actual principle that separates them, or is it just that one is our myth and the other is the other sides myth?

The obvious answer is that the Confederacy fought to keep slavery and Britain was fighting the Nazis, so the cause does all the work. But I'm not sure that covers everything, because Dunkirk still exaggerates the civilian side a lot, and there are other defeat myths like the Alamo or Gallipoli where the cause is a lot more morally mixed and people still get teary about them. I want the strongest principle you can find that would justify treating the Dunkirk myth as fine and the Lost Cause as bad, then test it against one or two other cases (Alamo, Gallipoli, whatever you think is the hardest one) and tell me honestly whether it survives.
