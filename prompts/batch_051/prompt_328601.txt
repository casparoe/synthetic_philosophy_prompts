You are a mechanism designer and welfare economist who specializes in institutional incentives, environmental decision-making, and wild animal welfare. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the relevant actors and their strategic options before you commit to a structured analysis.

A large conservation trust is considering a new decision rule. Any project that would degrade or destroy habitat—draining a seasonal wetland, clearing scrubland, logging a forest stand, converting grassland to agriculture—must file, alongside the usual environmental impact statement, a net welfare impact statement. This statement must estimate, over a rolling thirty-year horizon, the net change in nonhuman animal welfare caused by the intervention: roughly, the expected balance of positive and negative experience among affected sentient animals, weighted by the analysts' confidence in each estimate and discounted at a fixed annual rate of 3 percent. A standing panel of ecologists and welfare scientists scores each statement. Projects projected to reduce net wild animal suffering are approved even if they destroy habitat; projects projected to increase net suffering are refused unless they can demonstrate an extraordinary biodiversity benefit. The same test applies in reverse to the trust's own conservation and restoration work: a restoration projected to create a large population of animals whose lives are on balance dominated by suffering would lose its funding.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a moral position. I want to know how it would actually behave. In particular: (a) what it incentivizes among the developers, farmers, foresters, and conservation NGOs who supply the estimates and whose projects stand or fall on the panel's score; (b) how the scoring process would be gamed—in the choice of model species, in the choice of which animals count as sentient, in the treatment of insects and small fish, in the discounting of future welfare, in the handling of uncertainty, and in the framing of the counterfactual baseline; (c) what second-order and ecosystem-level effects would be systematically favored or suppressed, including which kinds of habitat and which kinds of animal life tend to win under this test; (d) where the scheme would fail first and most badly; and (e) whether any of those failure modes could be fixed without gutting the scheme—for example, through pre-registered models, adversarial audits, caps on certain parameter choices, or other safeguards. Conclude with a short assessment of whether the scheme selects for better welfare accounting or for better welfare accounting theater.
