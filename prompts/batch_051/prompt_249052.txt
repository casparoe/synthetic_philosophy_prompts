I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am preparing a long-form interview for a radio programme with a philosopher who has recently been defending a distinctive account of Warren Quinn's self-torturer scenario. As I understand her position, she holds that the self-torturer's step-by-step choices are each rationally permissible, that the pairwise preferences revealed by those choices are genuinely intransitive, and that the correct response to the puzzle is to give up transitivity as a requirement of rationality rather than to convict the agent of error. She also rejects the familiar diagnosis that the case is merely a sorites about imperceptible differences, denying that the real problem is simply the agent's inability to discriminate between adjacent settings.

I would be grateful if you would draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond asking her to restate the view. I would like at least one question, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, on the following pressure points: the money-pump objection, whereby an agent with intransitive preferences can supposedly be exploited through a cycle of individually acceptable trades; the charge that the underlying phenomenon is coarse-grained perception rather than intransitive value; the stepwise-aggregation problem, whereby a sequence of choices that are each rational in isolation appears to leave the agent in a state she would have declined had it been offered as a single step; the consequences of her conclusion for expected-utility theory and for the standard assumption that a rational agent's preferences form a complete transitive ordering; and the question of what formal structure, if any, should replace transitivity in the representation of rational preference. The follow-ups should be designed to pre-empt familiar defensive retreats rather than to elicit biographical colour.
