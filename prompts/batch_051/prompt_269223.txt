I've been going back and forth on the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, and I'd like you to stress-test the position I keep gravitating toward. The position is: the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction carves reality at the joints, and one side of it isn't really a reason for the attitude at all. Take the usual sort of case, where a demon threatens to punish you unless you admire him. You clearly have some sort of reason to get yourself to admire him, but it feels wrong to say that the admiration would thereby be fitting, or that the demon's threat counts in favor of him as an object of admiration. My inclination is to take that feeling at face value: the only genuine reasons for an attitude are ones that bear on whether the attitude fits its object, and the demonic 'reason' is really just a reason to do whatever would produce admiration in yourself. So the so-called wrong-kind considerations aren't a second species of attitude-reason; they're reasons for action wearing a costume.

What I want is an honest map of what would count against this view. Not generic objections, but the specific cases, arguments, or theoretical results that should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be. What would have to be true for the two-species picture to be right after all — for pragmatic, state-given considerations to be genuine reasons for the attitude itself, rather than reasons to manipulate myself? Which cases would be merely awkward for my view and which would be fatal? Are there examples where a state-given consideration seems to change how fitting an attitude is, and not just whether it's worth having? And I want the self-directed version as well: what would a defender of this kind of view themselves concede as a real problem, and has the view been insulated into unfalsifiability by the 'that was just a reason for action' maneuver? If it has, is that a deeper problem for this view than for the alternatives, or just the admission price of doing this area at all?
