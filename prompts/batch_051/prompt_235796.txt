I'm putting together a seminar on philosophy of AI and I want to use Turing's "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" as a core text, specifically section 7 on learning machines. The part that interests me is where he argues that instead of trying to programme an adult mind directly, we should build something more like a child machine and then teach it. He floats several components of that education: a mostly blank mechanism with a small amount of fixed structure, instruction via imperatives, punishment and reward, and a kind of experimental selection where you try different child machines and pick the ones that learn better, much like evolution. I want students to get past the usual "Turing predicted machine learning" line and actually evaluate his proposed education.

So here is the task I want you to write for me: rank the components of Turing's proposed child-machine education by how much philosophical weight you think they should carry today if we are trying to understand large language models as learning machines. Give me a tier list or a ranked list, and for each component say what it does and doesn't explain about contemporary ML, e.g. why reward/punishment is or isn't like reinforcement learning from human feedback, why imperatives and "the teacher says so" might map less cleanly onto pretraining than people think, and whether the evolutionary selection bit is doing real conceptual work or is just a metaphor. I'd like it to be a ranking, not just a discussion, and I'm also curious whether you think his notion of a child machine as mostly blank with only a little fixed mechanism is a helpful way to think about inductive biases in modern systems. Feel free to be critical—like if one of his components seems to be doing almost no work, say so and put it at the bottom.

Oh and please don't give me the usual reading-list answer; I care about the ranking and the reasons, not about citations.
