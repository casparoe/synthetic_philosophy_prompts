In government class we've been talking about why constitutions are so hard to change, and my teacher compared it to Ulysses having himself tied to the mast so he couldn't steer into the sirens. The US one needs two-thirds of both houses of Congress plus three-quarters of the states to be amended, and some countries go further and have parts you cant amend at all — my teacher mentioned one where the human dignity guarantee is supposedly locked in forever. The idea is that the people who wrote it are deliberately binding the people who come later, because the future might panic or get swept up by a demagogue and do something dumb.

But I keep getting stuck on what this actually assumes. Does it assume that a country in 2025 is literally the same "person" as the country in 1789, so it counts as self-binding rather than one generation bossing around another? Or does it assume that people in the future are worse at deciding things than people in the past, which seems backwards? Or maybe it just assumes that certain values (like basic rights) are more important than democracy, so they shouldn't be up for a vote at all? And are these assumptions even compatable with each other? I'm trying to figure out what the practice of entrenched constitutions actually presupposes — not what any particular author said, but what you'd have to believe for it to make sense as more than just old people making rules for young people.
