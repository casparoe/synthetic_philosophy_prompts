I spent thirty years in biochemistry, where we had a version of Goodhart's law before I knew it had a name: hand someone a metric and they will pipette exactly what the metric rewards. Now that I'm retired and reading philosophy of science plus some AI alignment material on specification gaming, I keep wanting to push on the thing Goodhart quietly assumes. The slogan says that when a measure becomes a target it stops tracking what you actually wanted. That assumes there is a real target out there, distinct from any measure of it, that the measure is a measure of. I'm no longer sure that its empirically meaningful, but I'm also not convinced it's meaningless.

Here's the claim I want you to help me test: for a trained system there is a fact of the matter about whether it has the target concept — say, lung cancer on a chest X-ray — rather than merely latching onto proxies like annotator habit, scanner model, or edge artifacts. A colleague trains a vision classifier to detect lung cancer and it aces the held-out set. Then we notice it still "finds" cancer at absurd rates when the lungs are cropped out of the image, leaving only the corners. It was reading the radiology workflow, not the lungs. So: did that system ever have the concept of a nodule, or was the concept only ever in our heads, projected onto a signal-processing pipeline that was doing something else all along?

Design the best test you can that would distinguish "has the target concept" from "has latched onto a general-enough proxy for now." Tell me what result would count for each reading, what a clean positive result would actually settle, and where the residue is. I suspect it settles less than people want it to. Is the leftover gap a limitation of our measurements, or a defect in the notion of "having a concept" itself?
