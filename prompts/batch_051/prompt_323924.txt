I have been reading Pascal's *Pensées* for a course on early modern political thought, and a pair of closely connected remarks about justice and force keep striking me as impossible to hold together. On the one hand, Pascal observes that justice is endlessly open to dispute, whereas force declares itself unambiguously and is recognized without argument. Because human beings could not make force obey justice, he says, they did the reverse: they made force count as just, so that authority is ultimately force dressed in the forms of law and custom. On the other hand, he insists that justice deprived of force is helpless, since there will always be people who refuse it, while force deprived of justice is condemned as tyranny. The remedy he proposes is to unite the two, so that the just is given effectiveness or the effective is given the standing of the just.

These two moments look like the same operation described from opposite moral standpoints: the first reads as a debunking genealogy according to which law is force with a veneer of justice, while the second seems to recommend producing exactly that union. Can the two be reconciled? I want the strongest candidate readings — for instance, a descriptive versus a prescriptive register, different senses of 'justice' or 'force', a change of subject from how authority arises to how it should be constituted, or a distinction between a fraudulent union and a legitimate one that Pascal never draws explicitly. Give a verdict on which reading is most honest to the text and which ones are special pleading, and say what you take him to mean by uniting justice and force, and whether he can criticize the reduction of justice to force without quietly endorsing it.
