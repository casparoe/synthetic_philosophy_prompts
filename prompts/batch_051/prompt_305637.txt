My grandpa signed a living will in the late 90s. It says: 'If I am in a terminal condition and cannot make or communicate my own medical decisions, I direct that all life-sustaining treatment be withheld or withdrawn.' He signed it after watching his brother die of cancer on a ventilator, and he always said he never wanted to be kept alive like that. Now he has pretty advanced dementia but he's not actively dying: he recognizes my dad some days, smiles alot, eats if someone feeds him. Last week he got pneumonia, and the doctors say that without IV antibiotics he'll likely die within days, but with them he'd probably recover and live a couple more years much like he has been. The hospital's ethics people say the living will applies, because in someone with advanced dementia pneumonia is basically a terminal condition and antibiotics are life-sustaining treatment, so they should withhold them. My dad says that can't be right: Grandpa is only dying of a treatable infection, and the whole point of the living will was to avoid being kept alive as a vegetable on machines, not to refuse a simple fix for something curable. Who is right? Please argue it from (a) the plain words of the clause, (b) the purpose Grandpa had when he signed it, (c) what he would say if we could explain this exact situation to him now, and (d) what a reasonable person reading the clause would take it to mean. Then say which of those four should actually win and why. I'm less interested in the medical outcome than in whether his earlier self gets to decide this or whether the person he is now overrides that. End with exactly one of these on the last line: \answer{treat}, \answer{withhold}, or \answer{cannot be settled}.
