I have been trying to work out what I actually think about appeals to etymology in disputes about what words mean, going by my gut on specific cases rather than by any worked-out theory, and I would like to know whether a single principle lies behind my pattern of verdicts.

When a relative corrects "the town was decimated by the hurricane" on the ground that decimate originally described the practice of killing every tenth soldier in a Roman legion, I think the correction is pedantic and wrong: the sentence means the town was devastated, and the ancient practice is part of the word's history, not a constraint on what it now means. I have the same reaction when someone appeals to roots to claim that "nice" really means ignorant (from Latin nescius) or that "silly" really means blessed or innocent (from an older sense): those are facts about where the words came from, not about what they now mean. But my verdicts are less consistent elsewhere. I do correct people who write "which begs the question" when they mean "which raises the question," and I want "begging the question" reserved for assuming what one was trying to prove, even though the newer use is widespread. I also wince at "he was nonplussed" used to mean "he was unfazed," a use I know is common in American English, though I would not defend the older sense by etymology; it feels instead like a loss of a useful distinction, or like the language changing without my permission. On a recent novel's use of "gay" to mean homosexual I have no objection at all, but I would correct a student who read a nineteenth-century character's "gay mood" as a reference to his sexuality: the older sense is not the true meaning today, and the present sense is not what the older text meant. And I notice that I accept "the town was decimated" while pausing at "the general literally decimated the regiment" used to mean he devastated it: in a military context, with "literally" attached, the Roman sense suddenly feels like a live technical possibility rather than a dead origin.

I suspect that when I correct "begs the question" or wince at "nonplussed" I may be committing the very fallacy I attribute to the relative, but I am not sure the two are the same. I would like to know what principle, or small set of principles, best captures these verdicts. Should a consistent view distinguish living technical or domain-specific senses from dead etymologies, and time-index a word's meaning to its community of use? Which of my verdicts, if any, should I give up? And what should the resulting view say about cases I have not settled: "hopefully" as a sentence adverb, singular "they," and "ironically" used to mean "coincidentally"?
