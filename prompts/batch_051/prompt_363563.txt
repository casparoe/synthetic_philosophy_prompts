I serve on the committee that interprets the founding charter of a regional Union, whose operative clause attaches legal consequences to whether a polity "has joined the Union" but never defines the phrase. Our committee has effectively defined it through a run of classification opinions, and next month we must classify a new arrangement that our precedents do not settle cleanly. I want to know which verdict is most consistent with our own record, which past case is the closest analog, and what principle we would be adopting with each possible outcome.

The arrangements we have already classified are:

1. Polity A is a party to the founding treaty, votes in the Council, holds seats in the Assembly, is subject to the Union Court, participates in the customs union and the full common market, and contributes to the budget. Verdict: joined.
2. Polity B has only a free-trade agreement in goods with the Union, keeps its own external tariff, has no presence in the Union's institutions, and is not subject to the Union Court. Verdict: not joined.
3. Polity C is not a party to the treaty and has no vote, only observer status in the Assembly; but it is inside the customs union, applies the common external tariff, aligns its goods regulations with Union law, contributes to the budget, and its courts treat Union law as supreme and directly effective, referring preliminary questions to the Union Court. Verdict: joined.
4. Polity D is a party to the treaty, has a non-voting seat in the Council and a full delegation in the Assembly, and contributes to the budget; but it holds a permanent opt-out from the customs union and the goods-market rules, and its courts are subject to the Union Court only under a narrow dispute-settlement annex. Verdict: not joined.
5. Polity E has no economic agreement with the Union but joined the regional confederation for defense and foreign policy, with a seat and a veto in its intergovernmental security council; its courts are not subject to the Union Court. Verdict: not joined.
6. Polity F is a microstate, not a treaty party and with no vote; under a bilateral protocol it is entirely inside the Union's customs territory, applies the common external tariff, contributes to the budget, and its courts treat Union law as supreme and directly effective, referring preliminary questions to the Union Court. Verdict: joined.

The new arrangement, Polity G, is not a treaty party and has no vote, only observer status in the Assembly. It is inside the customs union, applies the common external tariff, adopts the Union's goods and services regulations, and contributes to the budget. But its association agreement and its constitution expressly deny Union law supremacy and direct effect in G's courts, which do not refer preliminary questions; G's obligations are enforced by its administrative agencies and treaty-level arbitration rather than through its judiciary.

Before we write the opinion, analyze our track record as precedent: identify the candidate rules that fit our classifications, say what each predicts for Polity G, name the closest analog or analogs, give your recommended verdict with the reasons for preferring it over the alternatives, and state which further arrangement would best separate the candidate rules. End with a final line of the exact form \answer{...}, containing either joined, not joined, or underdetermined.
