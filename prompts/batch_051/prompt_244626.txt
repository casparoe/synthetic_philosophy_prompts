I'm in a moral-psychology reading group thats been going for a couple years, and there's a pattern I've started noticing but I don't have a word for. We'll be arguing about, say, whether it's wrong to spend money on a fancy wedding while people are starving, or whether you owe more to your own kids than to strangers. And what happens is: whoever is on the demanding side of the argument explains the other side's view by pointing to a bias. "You only think family comes first because of kin selection," or "that's just status quo bias, you grew up with it." Then the other side does the same thing right back. "You only think that because of a scrupulosity thing," or "that's just the halo effect from the effective-altruism crowd." But nobody ever describes their own view that way. Their own view is a value, or a preference, or just "what's actually right." The asymmetry is total and it's symmetric — both sides do it, and the same person will switch which explanation they reach for depending on which side of the argument they're on, without noticing.

I know there's a general name for seeing biases in other people but not yourself, but that feels broader than this. What I'm after is the specific thing where moral convictions get sorted into "bias" when they're someone else's and "value" when they're mine, plus the deeper problem of whether there's any non-question-begging way to draw that line. Does this have an established name? If it's just the bias blind spot, tell me why and what I'm missing; if it's something else, what's the nearest concept and where exactly do they part ways? And if there's no name, what would you call it, and what are the boundary cases where it's genuinely unclear whether something is a preference or a bias?
