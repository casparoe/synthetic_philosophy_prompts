John Stuart Mill, in Considerations on Representative Government, describes one school of political reasoners this way: "To these stand opposed another kind of political reasoners, who are so far from assimilating a form of government to a machine, that they regard it as a sort of spontaneous product, and the science of government as a branch (so to speak) of natural history. According to them, forms of government are not a matter of choice. We must take them, in the main, as we find them. Governments can not be constructed by premeditated design." I cover electoral-system reform, and a country I follow has used first-past-the-post in single-member districts for more than a century. A constitutional convention has now drafted a mixed-member proportional system: sixty percent of seats would remain in single-member districts, while the remaining forty percent would be allocated from national party lists by a divisor method, with a five percent threshold. The plan would be adopted in a single referendum and used at the next general election. The existing system was never designed as a package; it accreted through local compromises, population shifts, and ad hoc boundary changes. The new plan is plainly an act of premeditated design, closer to Mill's first school of thought. I want you to interpret the passage and test it against this case. Does the organic view imply that a designed proportional system will fail in a polity whose electoral habits formed under first-past-the-post? Does Mill's own treatment of representative government give any reason to treat an electoral allocation rule differently from other political institutions? And if the passage's warning and the proposed system's mechanics pull in opposite directions, which should decide the design question?
