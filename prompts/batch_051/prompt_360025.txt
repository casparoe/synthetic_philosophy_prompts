Streaming and social platforms are now built around defaults of frictionless continuation. Netflix automatically loads and begins the next episode after a short countdown unless the viewer intervenes; YouTube's autoplay queues a follow-up video and starts it on a timer; Spotify's radio and algorithmically personalized playlists keep the music going without the listener choosing each track; TikTok opens directly into an endless "For You" feed rather than a menu of options. These are not merely features a user can activate: they are the default state of the interface, and the user must act to stop them. The overall effect is that cultural material arrives as a stream one steps into, rather than as discrete works one selects, attends to, and finishes.

I want help reconstructing what philosophical commitments are built into this design. The official justification is given in the vocabulary of preference: the system learns what I like and gives me more of it, which seems to presuppose that my revealed tastes are the authoritative standard for what should come next. But the same design seems to commit itself to further views: that attention is a resource to be held by whatever is up next rather than a capacity to be given to what is in front of me; that endings are frictions to be removed; and that the relevant unit is the feed or channel rather than the bounded work. Adorno and Horkheimer argued that the culture industry turns cultural objects into interchangeable commodities, packages pseudo-novelty on top of an underlying sameness, and positions the audience as passive consumers whose responses are managed rather than addressed. A personalized endless feed can look like the opposite of that system, because it is tuned to an individual; it can also look like its perfected form.

What theory of (a) the cultural work, (b) the audience or subject, and (c) the value of cultural experience does the autoplay/endless-feed design strictly presuppose? Where does it merely fit with such a theory without requiring it, and where is it genuinely compatible with several rival theories? In particular, is the design best read as a continuation of the culture-industry logic Adorno and Horkheimer diagnosed, a break from it, or evidence that their diagnosis is now obsolete? And if we rebuilt these interfaces around the rival commitment that a cultural work is something to be met on its own terms — something that may demand sustained attention, resist one's current tastes, and be an end in itself rather than a unit of engagement to be optimized — which specific features of the design would have to change?
