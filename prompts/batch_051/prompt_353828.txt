I write long-form narrative histories of institutional failure—newspapers, record labels, once-dominant technology firms—for a general readership. My settled method is to begin from the moment of collapse and reason backward toward its causes, and for years I have followed a specific rule: before I draft the explanation, I do not read the contemporaneous record of expectation. That means no forecasts, internal memos, board minutes, or pre-mortem documents in which the people involved said at the time what they believed would happen. My rationale is that absorbing the participants' ex ante perspective too early would trap me inside their frame and forfeit the clarifying advantage of hindsight. The cost, as a colleague keeps pressing on me, is that my published accounts make failure look nearly overdetermined: every choice appears as another link in a chain that could hardly have gone otherwise, and the live branches the participants actually saw get flattened into inevitability. Her charge is that I have not removed bias but automated it—that by deliberately postponing the ex ante record until my backward-looking story is already fixed, I have fed hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy rather than guarded against them.

I want to evaluate this rule soberly rather than defend it reflexively. Is deliberately delaying the contemporaneous record until after I have formed a backward-looking explanation a defensible epistemic discipline for this kind of narrative history, or is it a self-protective rule that shields my explanations from disconfirming evidence? Under what conditions would it improve the accuracy of my accounts, under what conditions would it systematically distort them, and could the rule be restated so as to keep whatever discipline it provides without the distortion? Finally, is there any way I can tell from the inside whether I am practicing defensible selection rather than retrospective storytelling? Please answer in American English.
