I've been trying to get clear on what people even mean by "free-floating rationales" — the idea that there can be reasons or justifications that don't belong to any particular mind. Here's six glosses of the idea I keep bumping into. Sort them into tiers (S through D, or however you like) by how well they handle the two worries underneath, and give me a sentence for each explaining its tier.

1. Platonist gloss: a free-floating rationale is an abstract object, like a structured relation among propositions, that exists and has normative force whether or not any mind ever grasps it.
2. Idealized-agent gloss: a free-floating rationale is the reason a suitably idealized, impartial version of an agent would endorse, even if no actual agent does.
3. Functional gloss: a free-floating rationale is a "design rationale" of a trait or institution — the function that explains why it persists — so natural selection or institutional evolution can have rationales without anyone representing them.
4. Institutional gloss: groups encode rationales in rules, procedures, and documents that no single member fully endorses; think of a committee that passes a resolution nobody individually supports.
5. Coherence gloss: free-floating rationales are global requirements of rationality, such as avoiding jointly inconsistent beliefs, that apply to any possible agent and so don't need a particular reasoner.
6. Deflationary gloss: the phrase is loose talk; strictly speaking there are only reasoners' reasons, and "free-floating rationales" just gestures at reasons someone could have.

Worries:
a. The belonging worry: a reason seems like the kind of thing that has to be a reason for someone. If no one actually has it, in what sense is it a reason rather than just a fact that happens to count in favor of something?
b. The inertness worry: even if free-floating rationales exist in some thin sense, they don't seem to do the work reasons are supposed to do — they don't explain anyone's actual reasoning, guide anyone, or make anyone criticizable. So why keep them around?

Now: tier the six glosses on how well they survive both worries, and say in a sentence why each lands where it lands.
