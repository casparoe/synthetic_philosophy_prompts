I keep getting pulled toward the revisionist, individual-liability view of killing in war, mostly because it refuses to treat war as a special moral zone. The thought is: in wartime, the same principles govern killing as everywhere else. You're liable to be killed only if you pose an unjust threat, and being ordered to fight, or being told your side's cause is just, does no more moral work than it would in a mafia hit. I like that it is unified, that it does not flip a switch labeled combat and start excusing what would be crimes anywhere else, and that it gives definate verdicts about who may kill whom. On this view, a soldier in an aggressor state's army is not permitted to shoot the soldier defending her country, because his threat is unjust and hers is not. Tidy.

But the intuition that will not go away is the moral equality one. When I picture a nineteen-year-old conscript in a poor, aggressively-run state, drafted under threat, lied to by every source of information he has ever had, and sent to the front, I cannot bring myself to say that every time he fires his rifle he's doing the moral equivalent of murder. And I have the further sense that a theory whose verdicts depend on facts about jus ad bellum that ordinary soldiers cannot possibly assess is not a theory that could guide them, be taught in basic training, or anchor the laws of war. It feels like it punishes people for epistemic bad luck while calling it deep morality.

I want to know which one has to give. Could the intuition be debunked as a confusion between justification and excuse, or between the deep facts about who may be killed and the legal rules we sensibly adopt? Could the theory be amended with a role-based or epistemic permission for soldiers, and would that amendment just quietly bring back the old symmetric view? Tell me what would justify each move, what each one costs, and whether there's a reading on which the theory and the intuition are compatible after all. Don't just pick a side.
