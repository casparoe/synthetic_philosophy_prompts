I'm a PhD student in political philosophy with a side interest in collective epistemology, and while preparing a seminar on group rationality I keep returning to a puzzle about the discursive dilemma that I would like someone with real expertise in judgment aggregation to stress-test. Let me set up the standard sort of case. A three-member panel must decide whether to renew a research grant, and all three members accept the same evaluative rule: the grant should be renewed if and only if the proposal is both original and feasible. Each member holds a consistent set of judgments. Adele thinks the proposal is original but not feasible, so she votes against renewal. Ben thinks it is feasible but not original, so he votes against renewal. Cass thinks it is both original and feasible, so she votes for renewal. If the panel votes on each proposition separately under majority rule, the result is that the panel accepts that the proposal is original (2-1), accepts that it is feasible (2-1), and rejects renewal (2-1), while unanimously accepting the rule linking the three propositions.

An argument I have been turning over runs as follows. (1) Under proposition-wise majority rule, the panel's collective judgments are simply the majority outcomes on each proposition, even when the winning majorities are composed of different members. (2) Therefore the panel collectively accepts that the proposal is original. (3) Therefore the panel collectively accepts that it is feasible. (4) Therefore the panel collectively accepts that it should not be renewed. (5) The panel also collectively accepts that renewal is warranted exactly if the proposal is original and feasible. (6) Any agent, individual or collective, that accepts that a proposal is original, that it is feasible, and that originality-plus-feasibility warrants renewal is rationally committed to accepting renewal; refusing that inference is a failure of rational consistency. (7) Yet the panel refuses renewal. (8) So the panel is collectively irrational even though none of its members is, which means the aggregation rule itself manufactures the inconsistency. (9) Hence either there is no well-defined collective judgment in such cases, or proposition-wise majority rule is untenable as an account of group judgment.

I would like you to assess this argument step by step. Is it valid? If it is, say so and explain what a premise-based procedure would have to give up to avoid the dilemma. If it is not, identify the first step that fails and the reading under which it fails. My own suspicion is that something goes wrong in the move from majority outcomes on separate propositions to a single agent-like set of collective commitments, but I want that suspicion either confirmed with precision or overturned. Please approach this as an expert in judgment aggregation and collective epistemology. Begin by reconstructing the argument and making any suppressed premises explicit, then evaluate each step rather than offering a general impression. Say how confident you are in your assessment and single out the point you are least sure of. Please answer in American English.
