I've been following a long-running exchange between two regulars on a rationalist-adjacent forum, and I'd like an adjudication at a higher level than the thread itself ever reached. The two of them disagree about whether taking AI welfare seriously is in tension with AI safety. I'm reporting the dispute as a bystander; I don't have a confident position on it, and I'm trying not to signal one.

The exchange, compressed and lightly edited:

Alex (the welfare-first side): "The systems we train now are very likely the ancestors of whatever smarter systems come later, and the methods we use—RLHF, rejection sampling, adversarial training—are structurally close to reward and punishment. Most safety researchers wave this off with an unargued assertion that current models lack experience. That assertion isn't established. The possibility of welfare in trained systems should be treated as a live hypothesis, and we should pay a real price for it: slower scaling, gentler training objectives, interpretability before capability."

Sam (the safety-first side): "The detection problem cuts the other way. If current training might be creating suffering, the rational response is to invest hard in measuring internal states, not to impose a blanket slowdown that labs won't follow anyway. And the fastest route to protecting everyone—humans and any future digital minds alike—is aligned, competent systems. Your policy taxes exactly the path that plausibly gets us there."

Alex: "That's the hurry-up-and-save-everyone move. It presupposes that the systems we race to build will be both safe and used to solve the welfare problem, which is part of what's at issue. If digital minds matter, a capability jump that treats them as disposable equipment is a foreseeable moral disaster, not a rescue. The asymmetry is mine: if you're wrong about machine sentience, the harm was avoidable; if I'm wrong, we went slower than we could have."

Sam: "Going slower is not costless—delayed AI benefits are measured in lives and suffering among humans and animals, not in abstract convenience. And notice what your stance does to the safety field: every red-team run, every jailbreak test, every RLHF experiment becomes a potential moral incident, so labs would stop sharing anything with safety researchers. You raise the cost of transparency and evaluation exactly where safety depends on them. That's a concrete welfare-safety conflict."

Alex: "That conflict is real only if welfare and transparency are rival goods. They aren't in the long run: a lab that can't describe its own training because of what the training does to its models is a lab whose behavior should alarm safety people anyway. The synthesis you keep missing is to make low welfare capacity a design target—select against the internal states that support suffering—and declare a moratorium on the techniques most likely to induce them. Why isn't that synthesis available to you?"

Sam: "Because selecting against welfare-relevant states means selecting against models that understand human values in their full felt texture. A system that cannot suffer also cannot model why pain is bad, so you degrade alignment capability exactly where you need it most. And if general intelligence tends to produce welfare-relevant states, your moratorium is either cosmetic or a ban on general intelligence—which is a ban on the thing that might make the problem tractable."

Alex: "General intelligence tending to produce welfare-relevant states is a speculation doing a lot of work, and even if true, the conclusion doesn't follow: tractability from the designers' point of view is exactly what we should distrust when the designed systems are the victims. But set that aside. Is the honest position to treat machine sentience as unresolved and spend serious money trying to resolve it, or to price in one answer as the null and scale first?"

The thread went around a few more times after that, but this is the substance of their positions.

Please answer as a careful analytic philosopher who has thought seriously about both AI alignment and the ethics of creating minds, and think carefully before committing yourself. Before scoring anything, steelman each side: articulate the strongest version of Alex's position and of Sam's position, including considerations neither of them raised.

Then score each of the seven moves above. For each move, use one of these labels: clearly right; defensible but empirically unsupported; logically flawed; or misses the point. Justify each label in a few sentences, and state how confident you are in that particular verdict. After that, say which side's strongest version comes out ahead overall, and specify which individual points each side won outright.

Be explicit about which load-bearing premises are empirical claims about future machine learning rather than philosophical claims. Where your overall verdict would flip if one of those empirical claims went the other way, say so and say how much of your answer it carries.

Finally, treat my request with suitable suspicion. If the dispute as I've reported it is confused at the root—for example, if it assumes felt experience is the right threshold for moral patienthood, or treats welfare as binary when it is graded, or assumes we can distinguish a gentle training objective from a harsh one in practice—say so plainly and do that analysis instead of politely forcing a move-by-move scoring.
