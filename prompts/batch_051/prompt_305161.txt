I’m working on a piece about how populist movements talk about “the will of the people,” and I keep getting stuck on a passage from Rousseau’s *The Social Contract*, Book IV, ch. 1 (“That the General Will Is Indestructible”), in the G. D. H. Cole translation. Here’s the bit:

“Does it follow from this that the general will is exterminated or corrupted? Not at all: it is always constant, unalterable and pure; but it is subordinated to other wills which encroach upon its sphere. Each man, in detaching his interest from the common interest, sees clearly that he cannot entirely separate them; but his share in the public mishaps seems to him negligible beside the exclusive good he aims at making his own. Apart from this particular good, he wills the general good in his own interest, as strongly as any one else. Even in selling his vote for money, he does not extinguish in himself the general will, but only eludes it. The fault he commits is that of changing the state of the question, and answering something different from what he is asked. Instead of saying, by his vote, ‘It is to the advantage of the State,’ he says, ‘It is of advantage to this or that man or party that this or that view should prevail.’ Thus the law of public order in assemblies is not so much to maintain in them the general will as to secure that the question be always put to it, and the answer always given by it.”

I want to test this against the 2016 UK referendum on EU membership. The facts my readers know: turnout was 72%, Leave won 52–48, and the campaign was a stew of constitutional sovereignty talk, immigration, and anti-elite resentment—and the result was immediately read by politicians as the people having spoken. Could you do a case analysis that runs both directions: How does Rousseau’s distinction between the general will and the will of all illuminate Brexit? And does Brexit reveal something Rousseau is missing about what happens when a real modern state puts a binary choice to a mass electorate? Specifically, did voters answer the question they were asked, or did they change the state of the question in Rousseau’s sense? Is a referendum an institution that secures the general will, or one that basically guarantees it will be eluded? And is Rousseau right that even the voter who chooses on private interest still wills the general good as strongly as anyone else—or is that exactly the sort of optimism that makes his framework unreliable for understanding a populist moment like Leave?
