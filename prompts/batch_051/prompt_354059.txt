I've been trying to write up a little argument about why some fields make progress and others spin their wheels, and the conclusion is so strong it's making me want to throw the whole thing out. But I can't find the bad step, and my study group is split three ways about it.

The argument, cleaned up:

(1) A field genuinely progresses only if, over time, its practitioners converge on more and more correct answers to their central questions.
(2) Convergence on correct answers requires some signal that reliably favors right answers over wrong ones.
(3) A signal can do that only if it comes from outside the practitioners' shared assumptions: if the check is just more of what the group already accepts, the field only reinforces its current views and can't correct systematic error.
(4) The only signal that comes from outside a field's shared assumptions is empirical: a prediction against observation, where the world can tell you no.
(5) So a field with no empirical feedback can't progress.
(6) Philosophy, pure math, and big parts of economics and literary studies go on with almost no empirical feedback.
(7) Therefore those fields don't progress; their apparent advances are just changes of style.

I won't accept (7). Math is the clearest counterexample: the nineteenth-century proof that some polynomial equations can't be solved by radicals (what's now called Galois theory) isn't a shift in taste, and I don't think the ancient Greeks were one proof away from it. But I can't find the step to reject. My first move was to say proof is the "outside signal" for math, but then (3) and (4) seem to just define outside as empirical, and I'm not sure that's a fix or a cheat. And if proof counts, I don't know why philosophy doesn't get the same deal -- philosophers give arguments too, and they sometimes change their minds.

So: where exactly does this go wrong, or if it doesn't, which premise is the least bad to give up? I'd rather keep (1) or (2) than lose the difference between engineering and ethics. I also need to know whether whatever seperates math from philosophy here is survivable, or whether the same fix that saves math is going to let a lot of obviously stagnant fields off the hook.
