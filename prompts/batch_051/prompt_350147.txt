I've been arguing with a friend about whether the standard Axelrod/evolution-of-cooperation story still works when the thing doing the enforcing is your reputation rather than the person you're currently dealing with. The classic setup is an iterated prisoner's dilemma: two players know they'll likely meet again, so tit-for-tat works because defection gets punished later by the same pair. The premise doing the work is the shadow of the future plus the ability to recognize each other and remember. But a huge amount of actually existing cooperation is one-shot in the strict sense: I buy from a seller I'll never see again, I answer some stranger's question in a forum, I get in a car with a driver I'll never meet. The only thing carrying the history is a username, a rating, a public log. That feels like the shadow of the past (portable and third-party) rather than the shadow of the future (the same two players meeting again). Its easy to say "its just reputation, not reciprocity," but I'm not sure that's right, because the public identity does persist and in some sense the 'same player' keeps playing against a community rather than a partner.

What I want is a verdict on the Axelrod argument itself, not a survey of reputation research. Does it survive this setting, does it need repair, or is it dead in these cases (with something else doing all the work)? Be specific about which premise the development puts pressure on, and whether there's a repaired version that saves the core of the argument or whether the repaired version is just a new mechanism wearing the old one's clothes.
