I had two friends over last night and watched them argue for almost an hour about the word "lie," and now they both want me to referee, so I'm bringing it here.

(1) Marcus said we should reserve "lie" for intentionally false statements that actually do some harm or that the hearer has a right not to be told. A lie is a kind of betrayal, so a "white lie" shouldn't count as a lie at all — calling it a lie just lumps your grandma telling you she loves the sweater in with fraud. He said words are tools and we should sharpen the tool so it cuts at the right place.

(2) Dana said that's not what the word means. People use "lie" for any deliberate false statement, nice ones included — that's exactly why the phrase "white lie" isn't a contradiction. If you redefine "lie" so it only covers bad untruths, you make "lies are bad" true by definition, which settles nothing: you've hidden the actual question of whether this particular untruth was wrong inside the dictionary.

(3) Marcus said all definitions are choices about where to draw lines, not just reports of where the lines already are, and the only question is which choices help us live and judge better. The harm-based line helps, he said: it stops us from treating a kid who says "great haircut" to his mom as the same kind of thing as a scammer.

(4) Dana said if everyone gets to redraw words to suit the conclusions they want, the conversation becomes impossible, because we can't even state the disagreement anymore. The honest order is to first describe how the word actually works and then make a seperate moral argument about whether this use of it was okay. If the word doesn't mark the distinction you want, she said, you make the distinction in the argument — you don't rebuild the word.

(5) Marcus said that's treating language like a museum piece. Words like "marriage" and "planet" have been deliberately re-cut before, and nobody now thinks the meaning was ruined.

(6) Dana said those fights were different, because they were responding to new facts or new social realities, not trying to make one side of a moral argument win by definition. And she added that "harmful" just moves the fight into the definition anyway, since people disagree about whether a kind lie harms anyone.

They then asked me to say who was right. I honestly couldn't untangle it. Please go through the exchange move by move and judge each point on its own — where each of them is strong, where each is weak, and whether there's anything important both of them are missing.
