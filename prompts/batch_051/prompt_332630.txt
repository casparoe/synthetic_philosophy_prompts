I'm a software engineer and I keep having the same thought about time travel, and I can't decide if its clever or just pattern-matching. The grandfather paradox looks to me exactly like a database consistency problem. A history is a set of rows — events, people, ancestry links — and "you can't kill your grandfather before your parent is conceived" is just an integrity constraint on those rows: an event can't delete an ancestor row that its own row has a foreign key to. The self-consistency view of time travel (the one where paradoxes simply can't occur, history always works out somehow) sounds like saying the universe only commits histories that satisfy the schema, and inconsistent ones get rolled back or never make it past validation. I can map the pieces one-to-one: constraint violation = paradox, serializable history = self-consistent timeline, and so on.

But here's where I get stuck. In a database, a constraint isn't a fact about which states are physically possible — it's a rule someone declared, and a real process, the DBMS, checks it against every write. The self-consistency view seems to want it both ways: it asserts that contradictory timelines don't happen without positing any equivalent of the constraint-checker. So is the database analogy deep, and if so, what plays the role of the enforcer in the time travel case — or is the self-consistency view literally a schema with no DBMS, i.e. a list of invalid states plus "and they don't occur" and no mechanism? Or did the analogy break right at the start: maybe a law of nature isn't like a declared constraint at all, because constraints can be dropped or changed and laws can't. I want to know whether this is a real structural resemblance or me overfitting on the word "constraint," and what a timeline would have to be like for the mapping to actually go through.
