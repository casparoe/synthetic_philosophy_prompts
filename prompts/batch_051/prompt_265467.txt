The boundary problem in democratic theory asks who belongs to the demos — who gets a vote or some other say in a self-governing unit. Ecology has its own version: when you're trying to manage or protect a species, you have to decide where one population ends and the next begins. And I'm struck that the same two broad criteria show up in both places. In the democratic case you might include people because they're subject to the decisions being made (the all-subjected line) or because they're caught up in an ongoing web of interaction with the others doing the deciding (a more affected-interests or civic-republican line). Ecologically, you split populations by gene flow and regular interaction, or you lump individuals together because they're exposed to the same drought, fire regime, or management plan.
