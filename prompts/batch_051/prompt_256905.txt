I have recently retired from a career in the physical sciences, where I spent much of my time teaching thermodynamics, and in retirement I have begun reading philosophy of action and decision theory. A parallel keeps recurring to me, and I cannot determine whether it is substantive or merely a formal coincidence. In thermodynamics one distinguishes state functions—internal energy, for example—whose change between two equilibrium states depends only on the endpoints, from path functions such as work and heat, whose value depends on the particular route taken between the endpoints. The familiar contrast between exponential and hyperbolic discounting appears to me to have exactly this shape. Exponential discounting compounds multiplicatively: discounting a reward over six months and then over another six months yields the same factor as discounting it over the full year at once, so that the total devaluation of a future reward depends only on elapsed time, not on how the interval is divided or where along the timeline one begins. Hyperbolic discounting lacks this property; because the implied discount rate declines with distance from the present, the value assigned to a given delayed reward depends on whether one evaluates it in a single long step or through a sequence of intermediate delays, and on where in time the evaluation starts.

I am tempted to say that exponential discounting is to hyperbolic discounting as a state function is to a path function, and that this is the whole reason hyperbolic discounters can exhibit preference reversal—the behavioral signature of akrasia, or weakness of will—while exponential discounters are dynamically consistent. But it may be that I have only noticed that exponential discount factors multiply over subintervals while hyperbolic ones do not, and have dressed up that elementary algebraic fact in thermodynamic language. I would like the analogy examined seriously: state the mapping precisely, identify where thermodynamics and intertemporal choice come apart, and tell me whether thinking of hyperbolic discounting as path dependence genuinely illuminates the connection between time-inconsistent preferences and weakness of will, or whether it misleads more than it reveals.
