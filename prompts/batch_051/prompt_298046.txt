My friend Brian has a move I've seen all over the rationalist internet: he says "to understand a system just is to have a shorter description of it." The way he tells it, a good theory of a thing is a program shorter than the thing itself that outputs its behavior — Newton's laws being a tiny rule that generates the whole planetary dynamics, rather than a step-by-step trace of every orbit. The cash value, in his hands, is this: given enough cleverness there should allways be a shortcut, so we should never have to actually run a process to know what it will do; if you cant write down the compressed version, you don't understand the system yet. This does real work in our arguments, because he uses it to dismiss the idea that some systems (economies, brains, protein folding) can only be studied by simulation rather than by finding the underlying principle.

I recently read the "Computational Irreducibility" section in chapter 12 of Stephen Wolfram's A New Kind of Science, and this bit stuck with me:

At first one might have thought this must be some kind of temporary issue, that could be overcome with sufficient cleverness. But from the discoveries in this book I have come to the conclusion that in fact it is not, and that instead it is one of the consequences of a very fundamental phenomenon that follows from the Principle of Computational Equivalence and that I call computational irreducibility.

If one views the evolution of a system as a computation, then each step in this evolution can be thought of as taking a certain amount of computational effort on the part of the system. But what traditional theoretical science in a sense implicitly relies on is that much of this effort is somehow unnecessary—and that in fact it should be possible to find the outcome of the evolution with much less effort.
