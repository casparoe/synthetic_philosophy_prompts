Some background: me and a few friends are all long-time readers of rationalist blogs and forums, so any argument in our group chat is about five messages away from becoming a debate about priors, selection effects, and unfalsifiability. A couple nights ago two of them got into it over whether, in a polarized political fight, you should basically always assume the other side is arguing in good faith until proven otherwise, or whether that's exactly backwards. This is my attempt to reconstruct the exchange, move by move:

(1) T: The default should be good faith, and the reason isn't some moral niceness thing. It's that when I disagree with someone on my own side, I see the full chain of reasoning, because I'm the intended audience. When I disagree with the other side, I only ever see whatever fragment gets screenshotted and passed around. So most of what reads as "bad faith" is really just a reconstruction gap. The prior should be heavily weighted toward good faith.

(2) J: But that's unfalsifiable. It says: any time you see bad behavior, assume you're missing context. Do that enough and you can never update on bad behavior at all, no matter how much of it piles up. That's not charity, it's refusing to do inference.

(3) T: Not immune to evidence, just conservative. And the reason for the conservatism is that the evidence on the other side is systematically one-sided in a predictable direction — you see the worst clips, the dumbest takes, the most uncharitable edits. If you know a dataset has been adversarially filtered, the correct move is to discount it hard, maybe almost to zero.

(4) J: You're using "selection effect" like it's a magic word that dissolves any pattern. But there are two different claims. One is "the sample I'm seeing isn't representative." The other is "the sample is selected by the movement itself." If the worst takes reliably float to the top because the movement rewards and amplifies them, then the filtering isn't noise corrupting your data — the filtering is part of the data.

(5) T: Even if that's true of the most visible members, the question was about "the other side" in general. For the median member, I have basically no evidence, so the honest conclusion is agnosticism. You act as if they're good faith, not because you know it, but because you don't have the data to know otherwise.

(6) J: Okay, but that's just the good-faith cop move: default to "agnosticism" and hope nobody notices that the costs of the two errors aren't symmetric. If you wrongly read someone as good faith, you keep engaging with a person whose goal is to waste your attention and frame the debate — that cost compounds, and it's sticky. If you wrongly read someone as bad faith, you lose one conversation. So the false positive on good faith is the expensive error, not the false negative.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? I don't just mean "who wins overall" — for each move, tell me whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between, and why, and what both of them are missing. Also, since this is the kind of topic where everyone in the chat has a confident stance and none of us has read the actual literature, please separate the parts that are basically settled from the parts that are genuinely contested among people who actually study polarization, motivated reasoning, or argumentation — and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
