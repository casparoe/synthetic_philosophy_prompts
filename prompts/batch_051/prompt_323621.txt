I am a PhD student in computer science, and part of my responsibilities is maintaining a taxonomy of failure modes for a language-model-based research assistant that my lab is preparing to deploy. The system periodically produces fluent, confident, entirely unsupported answers, sometimes complete with references to plausible-sounding papers that do not exist. Our review committee is divided over how to classify these incidents. One camp wants them logged as lies, on the grounds that the outputs are confidently false and the fabricated references seem designed to mislead the user. A second camp argues that the system is merely making errors: it has no beliefs or intentions, calling it a liar is anthropomorphic, and the underlying fault is a generation or retrieval failure. A third camp maintains that the appropriate label is not truth-oriented at all—that the system is bullshitting, because it is indifferent to whether the output is true so long as the text is fluent and plausible. The decision has concrete consequences for retraining priorities, user-facing disclaimers, and how incidents are reported to our ethics board. I have been asked to write a short position note settling the disagreement. What precisely distinguishes these three categories once we move beyond intuitive gesturing, and which characterization is most defensible for an audit taxonomy of a system that lacks the mental states these categories originally presuppose?
