I work as a software engineer, and I spend most of my time training models rather than reading philosophy, so I would appreciate a reconstruction in plain terms. Lately I have been bothered by a tension inside my own field. The systems I build are trained around a reward signal. For a language model, we train a separate reward model on human preference data and then optimize the policy against it; for a game-playing agent, we give it a scalar score to maximize. Nothing in the training loop requires drives, needs, or any representation of what is good beyond a number that goes up. Yet as soon as the trained system runs, my colleagues and I describe it in motivational language as a matter of course: the agent wants to reach the flag, the model is trying to game the reward, it prefers the answer that flatters the user. When someone asks whether these systems actually want anything, the same people will say no, that the language is only a convenient shorthand for gradient descent on a loss. But the shorthand does real engineering work: we use it to predict what a policy will do in unobserved situations, to debug surprising behavior, and to decide when a system is misaligned with the objective we had in mind. I suspect the shorthand is carrying some unstated commitments about what wanting is, and I cannot quite articulate them.

I keep hearing that evolution is the standard story for why biological organisms want anything at all. Our systems skip evolution: we hand them an objective and optimize against it, so the engineering setup is, in effect, doing the work that natural selection supposedly did. That makes me want to know what assumptions about motivation are baked into the practice. Does the practice presuppose that wanting just is being structured so that behavior tends to maximize some reward, in which case a policy trained on a reward function wants something in whatever sense matters? Or does it presuppose the opposite, that wanting in the full sense requires something our systems lack—a felt, first-person experience of wanting, or the capacity to represent alternative outcomes and care about them—so that the motivational language is a projection? Are those two views even consistent within one and the same practice? And if we took the second view seriously, what would have to change in how these systems are trained, described, or governed: would reward hacking, alignment talk, or phrases like model preferences have to be reformed because they would be systematically misleading? Last, I would like to know where the practice is genuinely underdetermined: which features of it strictly rule out some account of wanting, and which are compatible with several accounts and merely look like commitments.
