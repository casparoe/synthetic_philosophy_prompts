Ok here's a thing I keep going back and forth on. I'm a physics PhD student, and a friend of mine who works in finance keeps pushing me to think about leaving academia for a startup with a lot of equity. His argument is basically: the startup has positive expected value, and once you accept that, turning it down is irrational. And he adds that if you'd take the bet once, you should be willing to take it many times over a career, so a whole sequence of such bets can't be worse than the single one.

But I made a little simulation to see what happens if you take that bet repeatedly, starting from my meager savings, and something strange shows up. The average wealth over all possible histories grows nicely, but almost every individual history ends up at zero or near zero. The mean is carried by a tiny number of absurdly lucky paths. My friend says this is just my loss aversion talking, or some fallacy, because expected-value maximization is the rational rule, and if I reject the sequence I should reject the single bet too.

I can't tell wheter my simulation is pointing at a real problem with expected-value reasoning for repeated multiplicative gambles, or if I'm just confused about how to apply the theory. Is he right that I should take the bet (and the sequence)? If not, what's the principle that separates cases where expected value is the right guide from cases where the typical path is what matters? Like, is it enough to point out that my wealth multiplies rather than adds? I'd like a clear-ish way to carve this up, not just career advice.
