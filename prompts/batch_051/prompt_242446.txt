I'm a PhD student in moral psychology, and I've been TAing a medieval philosophy course this term. We spent two weeks on Abelard's view of sin and intention, and the more I sit with it, the more I find myself holding a view I'd have rolled my eyes at a few years ago. Roughtly: the entire moral quality of a human act is settled by what the agent consents to in doing it — the inner yes, the moment of settling on "let this be done" — and the outward deed, its causal consequences, whether it succeeds or fails, all of that is morally inert except as evidence of, or a partial cause of, the state of the will. So a failed attempt is exactly as blameworthy as the completed act, and a completed harm done without consent to wrongdoing is no sin at all, however much repair and prevention it might still call for. I've started defending this as a general principle about the moral assessment of action, not just as a piece of Abelard commentary.

I can already feel the places where it strains. Negligence and recklessness, where there's no clean, localizable moment of consent. Drunken or otherwise impaired wrongdoing. People who do the right thing for appalling reasons and the wrong thing in blameless ignorance, or under a mistaken description of what they're doing. Standing states of character — hypocrisy, vice, settled indifference. The moral luck results from my own field, where ordinary subjects' blame tracks outcomes and not just intentions. The intuition that some act-types are intrinsically wrong, out of bounds no matter what the agent was aiming at. What I'd like from you is an honest map of what would count against this position, not just the standard first-year objections. Treat it as a live view I actually hold. Tell me which defeaters are fatal, which are merely awkward, which a sensible defender would concede count against it, and whether the view can be qualified to absorb them without going empty — and if it can, whether that's itself a problem. I'm personally most moved by the negligence worries and by the outcome-bias data; I'm least moved by appeals to good consequences, since I think of this as a claim about culpability rather than about which acts happen to be beneficial.

Act as an expert working at the intersection of action theory and moral psychology. Think carefully, take your time, and start by brainstorming six or seven candidate defeaters before sorting them into a ranked answer.
