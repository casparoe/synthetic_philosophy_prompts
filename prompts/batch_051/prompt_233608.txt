I've been trying to work out how the value of a state of experience depends on two dimensions I can separate in my thinking: the level of consciousness — from zero (a dreamless coma) through a bare flicker to full, vivid wakefulness — and the quality of the experience, from agony to bliss. For years I have assumed the value is simply the product: consciousness times happiness, with consciousness measured from 0 to 1 and happiness from -1 to +1. But my own verdicts on cases keep breaking the formula, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them.

Here are the cases. (1) A permanently unconscious person whose body shows every measurable sign of being in excellent shape is, to me, in a state with no positive value, not merely very little. The product formula gets this one, because the consciousness term is zero. (2) For a single hour, I would rather be fully awake and mildly pleased than drowsy and strongly pleased, even though the product formula ranks the drowsy hour higher (0.5 × 0.8 = 0.4 against 1.0 × 0.3 = 0.3). (3) When two states involve the same level of consciousness, I always prefer the more pleasant one; I have no urge to treat wakefulness as all that matters. (4) For a barely conscious creature whose whole experience is a faint pleasant warmth, I do think it matters whether the warmth is slightly more or slightly less pleasant, so I am not a pure threshold theorist who says low consciousness makes pleasantness worthless. (5) The case I cannot settle is a wide-awake person in a completely flat state: no pleasure, no pain, no interest, just blank, vivid awareness. Sometimes that seems to have a small positive value just in virtue of the vividness; sometimes the formula says it must be worth exactly zero.

Is there a single principle that gets all of these, or close? Please state it precisely, say how it handles each case, tell me which of my verdicts it would force me to drop if any, and what it predicts about the flat-awareness case.
