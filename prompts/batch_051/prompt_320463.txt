I've been reading about the old maxim that the king can do no wrong and the way American law inherited it as sovereign immunity, and I keep wondering what would actually happen if you just repealed the whole thing at one stroke. So here's the setup, for the sake of argument. In 2027, after a pretty bad string of disasters involving state-run facilities, a constitutional amendment is ratified with suprising speed. It reads: "Neither the United States, nor any State, nor any officer, employee, or agent thereof, shall be immune from suit in any court on the ground of sovereign immunity; and any judgment against the United States or a State shall be satisfied from public funds without further appropriation." The amendment is understood to override the Eleventh Amendment and every judge-made immunity doctrine, state and federal. It doesn't create any new causes of action—plaintiffs still need some underlying claim in tort, contract, property, constitutional or statutory law—it only strips the defense. So from day one, any citizen who can state an ordinary claim against a police department, a child-welfare agency, a zoning board, the EPA, the IRS, or the federal government itself has a way to sue for damages, and the money actually gets paid.

What I want is not a story of the first week but a reasoned prediction of the equilbrium twenty years later. Where do the damage awards concentrate—frontline policing, permitting, prison administration, benefit processing, regulatory inaction? Which agencies become more careful and which become gun-shy in ways that actually hurt people, like pulling back from enforcement or refusing to make decisions at all? Do courts, faced with a flood of claims, quietly rebuild something like immunity through the back door—narrow interpretations of duty, an expanded "discretionary function" idea, procedural barriers, or limits on what counts as "state action"? And here's the part I'm most curious about: does the legal system, once it routinely enters money judgments against the government, actually concede that the state can be a wrongdoer in the same sense a person can, or do judges keep the old idea alive by holding that some things the state does aren't wrongs to anyone in the first place?

Please make the causal chain explicit: which effects follow fairly strongly from the incentives the amendment creates, which depend on assumptions about how courts and legislatures react, and what would have to be false for your prediction to be wrong. Also tell me how confident you are in each major claim and single out the one point you are least sure about.
