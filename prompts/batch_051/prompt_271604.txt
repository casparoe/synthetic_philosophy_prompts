I'm a software engineer who works on perception and planning systems, and though I have no formal training in philosophy, I keep running into a dispute I'd like you to help me think through carefully. On one side, representationalism about cognition has the systematic virtues I find natural as an engineer: positing internal models and representations gives a clean, unified account of memory, planning, abstraction, off-line reasoning, and acting on information acquired in a different context. On the other side, I have a firm intuition that much intelligent real-time behavior—catching a fly ball, moving through a crowded sidewalk, tracking the felt dynamics of a conversation—is best described as tight perception-action coupling, in which the agent tunes to the environment and the environment itself functions as the model, making a rich internal representation both superfluous and too slow. I am not asking you to declare a winner. I am asking you to specify, for each side, the conditions under which it ought to give way: what evidence or pressure would justify debunking the intuition as an illusion of fluency, and what would justify amending or abandoning representationalism. Then consider whether there is a reading on which both survive—for example, by reserving representations for genuinely representation-hungry problems, or by holding that representations are enacted and constructed on the fly rather than retrieved—and say whether such a compromise is a real resolution or only a relabeling of the conflict. Act as a philosopher of cognitive science who knows both the representationalist and enactivist literatures well. Before writing, brainstorm the strongest versions of each position and the most widely discussed reconciliation strategies. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
