I've been roped into giving the homily at a university chapel service in two weeks. The congregation is going to be unusually heavy on biology people — a couple of evolutionary biologists, a paleoecologist, and a lot of grad students — because the service is the Sunday right after the big paleontology conference on campus. I'm not a biologist, but ive read enough to know I can't stand up there and say death and predation entered the world through Adam's sin; the fossil record is full of animals killing and dying long before any human existed. At the same time this is a Christian service, and the readings are fixed: Genesis 1, with its repeated refrain that God sees creation as good, and Romans 8 on the whole creation groaning. The congregation splits down the middle: some want me to defend the "good creation" doctrine no matter what, and some want me to quietly drop it and talk only about human hope. I dont want to do either.

Please write the homily, roughly 850 to 1000 words. It has to do a few things at once. It must not deny or soften the science. It must not pretend animal suffering is unserious, or that animals probably don't feel much anyway. It must not reduce "good" to "efficiently functioning ecosystem." It must not use "it's a mystery" as an escape hatch. But it also has to still say something genuinely Christian about creation being good that isn't sentimental — something a working biologist could hear without wincing and a traditional parishioner could hear without feeling the doctrine had been abandoned. You can draw on the Genesis and Romans readings, but not as a way of waving the problem away.

After the homily, give me a JSON object with these fields: "thesis" (the single sentence the whole homily is trying to make true), "constraints_honored" (a list of the specific moves I ruled out and where in the homily each is declined or avoided), "biblical_use" (how Genesis 1 and Romans 8 are actually handled in the piece), "turn" (the sentence or paragraph where the homily moves from stating the tension to resolving it), and "risks" (one or two places where the homily might still feel too hard for the scientists or too soft for the traditionalists).
