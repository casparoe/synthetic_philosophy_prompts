I spent thirty years doing crystallography and only got into philosophy of property after I retired, so of course I've now volunteered to lead a session at our local evening ideas group. The audience is about twenty adults—some retired engineers, a nurse, a Latin teacher—no formal philosophy or law, and I've got forty-five minutes and a whiteboard. The topic I want to teach is what should happen, ownership-wise, when the owner is gone: orphaned letters and out-of-print books where the rightsholder can't be found, and the abandoned lab software I know from my own field—code that still runs an instrument but was written by a postdoc who left science and whose email bounces. I want them to get past two quick moves: 'someone created it so someone owns it forever' and 'nobody's maintaining it so it's basically free.' I suspect the whole thing turns on seperating three situations: the owner is unknown, the owner is known but absent, and the owner deliberately walked away. Plan the explanation for me, not the explanation itself. Give me the order the ideas have to go in and what each one buys (what depends on what), a single worked example to keep returning to and what it must have and lack, the misconceptions I should expect and where in the sequence to defuse each one, a short check-for-understanding for the last five minutes, what I should leave out entirely at this level and how to say so, and what I should openly flag as unsettled rather than pretend to resolve. No readings or legal citations please—they won't do them anyway.
