I'm putting together a session on complicity — specifically wether you can be complicit in wrongdoing without causing any of it in any ordinary sense — and I want to open with a poem that makes the students feel how small, causally negligible acts (initialing a form, replying "ok", not asking a question) can still amount to being in a wrong. So: write a sestina from the point of view of a minor functionary who half-knows that what she's helping with is wrong. Set it in some unglamorous office — customs paperwork, a loan file, a shipment manifest, whatever lets the six verbs feel like a normal shift. Choose six small verbs for the sestina's end-words (something like sign, file, count, nod, watch, lend — pick whichever work), and use the sestina's obsessive rotation of those six words as the structural engine: each stanza is her trying out a slightly different description of the same acts and failing to make the queasy feeling go away. The three-line envoi should use all six end-words, and in doing so it should sharpen the difference between the causal question — did she make it happen? — and the participatory question — was she in on it? — without closing the matter. I'd like the philosophy to be genuinely right rather than name-dropped: a person who works on the ethics of complicity should recognize the participatory-intention picture of complicity in it, but someone who has never read that literature should still find it a good, uneasy poem.

Work like an expert on the moral philosophy of complicity who also knows how a sestina actually functions. Before you write, think through the end-words and what each stanza's particular move is, so the repetition earns its keep instead of just spinning. Then give me the sestina first, followed by one commentary section titled "Commentary on the choices" of roughly 350–500 words. In the commentary, walk through the structural choices (why these six words, what the envoi pays off), and — this part matters to me — separate what's settled in the debate about complicity from what's genuinely contested. If the poem leans on a contested view (for instance, that intention matters more than causal difference-making), say so plainly rather than dressing it up as the consensus.
