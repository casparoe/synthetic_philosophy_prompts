I am taking a seminar this term on collective responsibility, and we have been reading Karl Jaspers alongside older sources on whether guilt can be transmitted across generations. Two biblical passages keep surfacing in our discussions, and they seem to me to contradict one another directly.

The first is Exodus 20:5 (KJV), from the second commandment:

"Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them, nor serve them: for I the LORD thy God am a jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me;"

The second is Ezekiel 18:20 (KJV), from a chapter in which the prophet is disputing a current proverb about fathers who eat sour grapes while their children's teeth are set on edge:

"The soul that sinneth, it shall die. The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father, neither shall the father bear the iniquity of the son: the righteousness of the righteous shall be upon him, and the wickedness of the wicked shall be upon him."

Read at face value, the first says the iniquity of the fathers is visited on the children to the third and fourth generation, while the second says the son shall not bear the father's iniquity at all. Are these genuinely in conflict, or can they be reconciled, and at what cost? Lay out the most plausible candidate reconciliations — different senses of "bearing iniquity" (punishment versus consequence, legal liability versus moral stain), different contexts (covenantal idolatry versus individual legal accountability), different subjects (a people versus a person), or a deliberate revision of the older view — and then commit to the one that requires the least special pleading. Explain where the others go wrong. Finally, say plainly whether the reconciliation you chose supports or undermines the idea that a later generation can inherit guilt in a sense strong enough to make Jaspers's question about national guilt a live one. I would rather have a definite, argued position than a menu of possibilities left for me to choose from.
