I have been thinking about the standard justification for randomizing patients in clinical trials: the requirement of clinical equipoise. As I understand it, a trial must not start unless the community of relevant experts is in genuine, honest disagreement or uncertainty about which arm is better for a participant, so that no enrolled participant is knowingly assigned a treatment believed to be inferior. The usual story is that this is what lets a physician enroll a patient without violating an obligation of care to that individual.

I keep noticing a Rawlsian structure underneath this. Clinical equipoise looks to me like a veil-of-ignorance test applied to treatment assignment. A trial is acceptable just when a person choosing behind a veil — not knowing which arm they would receive, but knowing the state of the evidence about the disease and the candidate treatments — could rationally accept being assigned to either arm. On this reading, the public state of expert disagreement is not doing independent moral work; it is a proxy for what a chooser behind the veil could accept, and equipoise is fundamentally about the fairness of a choice made under uncertainty about one's own position rather than about the mere absence of evidence.

I want to know whether this parallel is deep or superficial, including where it breaks if the analogy has some truth. Specifically:

(a) Is the veil doing the same work in the two uses? A Rawlsian veil thickens ignorance of identity, talents, and one's conception of the good, while equipoise leaves all of that intact and imposes only uncertainty about which treatment one receives. Is the thinner veil all equipoise needs, or does it silently depend on the thicker one?

(b) The Rawlsian veil forces choice from a standpoint no actual person occupies, so that self-interest cannot bias the principles selected. In equipoise, however, the physician is the one required to be uncertain, while the participant is the one who bears the consequences. Does the parallel confuse the standpoint of the physician with the standpoint of the patient, and can a physician's duty be analyzed as a hypothetical choice made by someone else?

(c) Suppose the parallel could be made precise. Would it change anything in practice — which trials an ethics committee should approve, how trials should be monitored, or when they should stop early? In particular, would a veil-of-ignorance version of equipoise yield different verdicts about adaptive designs, or about trials in which individual clinicians have strong hunches but the expert community is genuinely divided? Or does it merely re-describe the standard verdicts in fairer-sounding language?

If the honest assessment is that the parallel misleads more than it illuminates, say exactly where the structures come apart and whether anything worth keeping remains.
