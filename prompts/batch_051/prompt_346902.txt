Suppose a large foundation in the orbit of effective altruism decides that its grantmaking suffers from measurability bias: proposals with legible, near-term, quantifiable payoffs—cash transfers, bed-net distribution, deworming campaigns—routinely outrank proposals whose expected value may be very large but whose payoff is diffuse, distant, or hard to specify—movement-building, policy reform, institution-strengthening, technical AI-safety research. To correct the bias, it adopts the following two-part rule.

First, every application is evaluated independently by two modeling teams. The foundation records each team's expected-value estimate and computes an opacity premium equal to the normalized gap between the two estimates. Applications are then ranked by expected value multiplied by (1 + the opacity premium), so that measurable disagreement about a project's value raises rather than lowers its rank.

Second, twenty percent of the annual budget is reserved as a high-opacity fund. Applications that fall in the top quartile of the opacity-premium distribution but below the median on unadjusted expected value are funded from this reserve, regardless of where they fall in the ordinary expected-value ranking.

The foundation describes this as a structural corrective meant to keep diffuse, long-horizon work from being starved by its own measurement culture. Treat the policy as a mechanism rather than a moral stance. Analyze how it would actually behave. What does it incentivize among applicants, among the modeling teams, and among the foundation's program officers? How would each group learn to game it, and with what second-order effects? Identify the most likely failure modes—for instance, rewarding the appearance of uncertainty rather than the reality of it, making the results of reserve-funded projects even harder to learn from, or attracting applicants whose main skill is producing strategically divergent cost-effectiveness models—and say which, if any, can be patched without gutting the correction the rule is meant to apply.
