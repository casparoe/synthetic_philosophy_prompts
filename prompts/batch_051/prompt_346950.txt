I spent my career as a chemist and only recently started reading philosophy of mathematics, so I'm at the stage where I can tell something is off but can't always say what. I'm reading a popular book on twentieth-century mathematics, and its chapter on Brouwer summarizes intuitionism like this:

'Brouwer held that mathematics is a mental construction, so to say a proposition is true is to say we have a proof of it. Because truth means provability, intuitionists reject the law of excluded middle: for a proposition like Goldbach's conjecture, we have neither a proof nor a refutation, so the intuitionist refuses to assert that it is either true or false. It follows that intuitionists allow some propositions to be neither true nor false. Intuitionistic logic, on this telling, is what you get when you drop the double-negation rule—from not-not-P you can't infer P. This is also why intuitionists reject proof by contradiction. The book then concludes that, by intuitionist lights, classical mathematics is false whenever it relies on excluded middle.'

The bit about being neither true nor false sounds wrong to me—I'd of thought rejecting excluded middle just means you don't assert the disjunction, not that you posit some third status. And I'm suspicious that "rejecting proof by contradiction" is being equated with "dropping double-negation," when those sound like different moves. But I'm an outsider, and the book writes with confidence. Can you go through the summary claim by claim and seperate what's accurate, what's a common simplification, and what an intuitionist would deny? In particular I want to know wether the concluding sentence about classical mathematics being false follows from anything true in the summary.
