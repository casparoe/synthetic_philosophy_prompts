I have been keeping a dream journal recently, and the habit has drawn me into an older philosophical debate that I keep returning to: the exchange between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett over whether dreams are experiences at all. The position I find hardest to shake is Malcolm's. As I understand him, he argues that a person who is genuinely sound asleep cannot at that time make judgments, form intentions, or attend to anything; consequently, the idea of a dream as a stream of experience occurring during sleep rests on a conceptual confusion. On this view, what we call a dream report is not a recovered memory of something that took place during the night. It is instead a narrative the person is disposed to produce upon waking.

What unsettles me now is the subsequent research on lucid dreaming. Investigators have described trained dreamers who, while physiologically in REM sleep, produce an agreed-upon signal — a distinctive pattern of eye movements — to indicate that they are aware they are dreaming, and in some cases to answer simple questions presented to them while they remain asleep. This appears to exhibit exactly the sort of judgment, intention, and report that Malcolm took the concept of sleep to rule out. My question is whether Malcolm's argument actually survives this development. Does the signaling evidence refute his position, or can a Malcolm-style view absorb it — for instance, by treating the eye movement as an element of the dream's content rather than a waking action, or by insisting that these rare trained cases do not touch the ordinary concept of dreaming he was analyzing? I am less interested in the empirical details than in whether the philosophical argument still goes through, needs repair, or is dead. If it survives, please say which premise the signaling evidence fails to reach; if it does not, say what, if anything, remains of Malcolm's way of drawing the line between dreaming and experience.
