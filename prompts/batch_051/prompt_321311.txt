I'm a high school student, and I've been trying to make sense of my school's technology policy. The school issues every student a laptop, but the device runs monitoring software that logs the websites we visit, flags messages and search terms it considers concerning, and sends the flagged material to administrators; during class a teacher can also remotely view our screens without asking. At the same time, the school requires a "digital citizenship" course in which we are taught that our personal information is valuable, that we should keep our passwords secret, and that we have a right to privacy online. Last month a student was suspended for reading a classmate's messages after they left their account logged in, on the grounds that this was an invasion of privacy — the same administration that had been reading students' flagged messages all year. I can't tell what view of privacy the school actually holds: it seems to treat student privacy as worth protecting against other students but not against the school itself.

What theory of privacy, if any, does this set of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view: is it that privacy means controlling who has access to your information, that it means protection from harm, that minors in school simply have no strong privacy claim, or something else? Then say whether the practice really presupposes that view or is in fact compatible with several rival theories, and whether the different parts of the policy — the monitoring, the digital-citizenship lessons, the suspension — presuppose consistent things. Finally, tell me what would actually have to change in the policy if the school replaced its implicit view with a more coherent theory of privacy. In doing this, please separate what is settled among people who study privacy from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
