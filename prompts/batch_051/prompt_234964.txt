One of the standard arguments for statutes of limitations is epistemic: with enough time, evidence rots — witnesses die, memories blur, records get lost — so the risk of a wrongful conviction rises and we'd rather let a guilty person walk than convict an innocent one. But that argument was built for a paper-and-testimony world. More and more wrongdoing now leaves a digital trail that doesn't decay: chat logs, geolocation histories, financial records, cctv. A fraud from 2003 can be every bit as provable today as it was then, maybe more so. Does the statute-of-limitations argument survive that development, or does it collapse? Be specific about which premise the digital-evidence development undercuts, and whether the argument can be rebuilt without it. If it can't, say what's left: is there any good reason to bar prosecution when the evidence is still solid, or does the real question become whether elapsed time is itself a morally relevant fact about punishment?
