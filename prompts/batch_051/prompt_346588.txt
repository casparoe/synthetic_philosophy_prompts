I hold a view about the moral limits of the ignorance-of-law doctrine. The doctrine, not as expressed by Blackstone but as it operates in modern administrative states, is defensible only when paired with a publicity condition: a legal system may treat ignorance of a rule as no excuse only if the rule's content is reasonably knowable by those it binds. Once the law becomes so voluminous, changeable, or technically opaque that a reasonable person cannot acquire the relevant knowledge with ordinary effort, punishing for unknown violations is morally no different from punishing people for breaching secret rules. I want to know what would count against this view.

Which observations, arguments, possible cases, or results in neighboring debates would undermine it? For instance, could one show that the moral objection rests on a false analogy between unknowable law and secret commands? Could one show that the real wrong is not ignorance itself but something else, such as an unfair distribution of interpretive risk? Could one show that a knowledge requirement would be self-defeating because it would reward strategic ignorance? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a committed defender of my view should themselves concede as counting against it. Also tell me whether the view, as I have stated it, risks being insulated by qualifications—for example if the phrase reasonably knowable is doing too much work—and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. I would be moved by evidence from actual legal systems where ordinary people demonstrably cannot know what the law requires of them, but I do not want that to bias your ranking.
