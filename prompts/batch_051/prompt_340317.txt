I keep encountering the phrase 'Western values' used as though it named a stable, coherent set of commitments, but whenever I try to pin down its membership conditions the category seems to slide. I want a working definition—one or more criteria that would let us say of some proposed value whether it counts as a Western value—not a genealogy, a historical narrative, or a list of examples. Please propose such a definition, then run it against the following cases and give each a definite verdict (in, out, or genuinely indeterminate under the definition), saying where the definition gives the wrong answer or no answer, and defending any verdict that goes against ordinary usage.

Begin with the near-consensus core that most users of the phrase seem to mean: individual rights, constitutional government, and freedom of conscience. Then take the clear non-cases on my own untutored view: caste-based purity rules governing occupation and marriage, and ancestor veneration treated as an overriding obligation to continue the family line. The hard cases are these. Equality before the law, which Western constitutionalism claims but Soviet Marxism and many anticolonial movements also claimed—can a value be simultaneously Western and universal? The postwar European welfare state, with its social rights to health, education, and a minimum standard of living, which is central to Europe's self-understanding yet often downplayed when American politicians invoke Western values. Enlightenment universalism itself: the claim that certain rights belong to all humans simply as humans, a value that explicitly denies being geographically bounded. Classical Athens and Roman law, routinely claimed as ancestors of the West but embedded in slave societies with sharply restricted citizenship—do we count the articulated ideal or the institutional practice? Christianity, Middle Eastern in origin and now often folded into the compound Judeo-Christian—on what grounds does the command to love one's neighbor count, if it does? Japan, which has constitutional democracy, the rule of law, a market economy, and strong speech protections, yet is almost never called Western. Latin America, Catholic and Iberian and republican, but often treated as standing outside the West. Russia, European in its literature and music and Orthodox in religion, but presently illiberal in politics. And finally the internal variation on a core candidate: free expression, where American near-absolutism and German or French hate-speech restrictions are both called Western, which raises the question whether the definition remains informative if both count.

After applying the definition, say what it implies about how the phrase is used in argument—for instance, when it is invoked to include Poland but exclude Turkey, or to mark a boundary between the West and some other civilization. Is that boundary-drawing part of the concept itself, or merely one use of a term whose descriptive content is independent of it? And please be explicit about what would change your mind: identify which of the hard cases is doing the most work against the definition you settle on, and what specific consideration—a historical finding, a semantic point, a further counterexample—would most likely force you to revise or abandon it.
