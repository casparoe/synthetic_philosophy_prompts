I'm giving a talk at a workshop for moral philosophers and private-law people, and I want to walk in ready for the q&a. My thesis is that a promise can fully bind even when the promisee never hears it or hasn't accepted it: promissory obligation comes from the promisor's settled intention to be bound, and the promisee's right to performance or release is downstream of that intention — a promise is closer to a unilateral self-binding like a vow than to a bilateral contract. The audience is mostly neutral, but I know whose likely to speak up: one person who thinks promising is fundamentally about assurance, so uptake is the whole point; one contract-remedies person who says that without reliance there is no real wrong, only a minor moral offence; a rights person who insists an obligation no one is yet in a position to claim or release is just a private resolution wearing a promise costume; and an old-school conventionalist who says promising just is a social practice built from offer and acceptance. Please give me exactly five objections, in the order I'm likely to hear them, each phrased the way that particular member of the audience would actually put it, and in the same item say in a sentence what I should concede and what I should hold. No introduction, no conclusion, just a numbered list of five items, each one or two sentences. Make the fifth objection the one I should be scared of but that probably no one will be bold enough to ask.
