I've been going back and forth with a friend about a game-theory argument, and I'm stuck on whether it holds up. He's an economics PhD student, so he states it with tottal confidence, and every individual step sounds right when he says it, but the conclusion feels like it proves too much. The claim is that you can get Nash equilibrium almost for free, just from rationality plus common knowledge. Quick background: common knowledge of X means everyone knows X, everyone knows that everyone knows X, and so on without end. Here's the argument, cleaned up a little:

(1) A rational player chooses a strategy only if it maximizes her expected payoff given her beliefs about what the other players will do.
(2) Suppose the game — strategies and payoffs — is common knowledge, and the fact that every player is rational is also common knowledge.
(3) A strategy that is not a best response to any possible belief about others' actions would never be chosen by a rational player; and because rationality is common knowledge, all players know this, know that everyone knows it, and so on.
(4) So players can iteratively delete strategies that are never best responses, at each round restricting possible beliefs to the strategies that survived the previous round.
(5) At the end of this procedure, every remaining strategy is a best response to some possible conjecture about what the others will play among their remaining strategies. Hence, for any combination of remaining strategies, each player's strategy is a best response to what the others play in that combination.
(6) But a combination of strategies in which each player best-responds to the others is exactly a Nash equilibrium (pure strategies, to keep things simple).
(7) Since rationality and the game are common knowledge, every player can and will run this same procedure; so the profile actually played has to be one of the remaining combinations.
(8) Therefore, common knowledge of rationality implies the players play a Nash equilibrium.

I half-suspect the sleight of hand is around (5) or (7), but I can't articulate what exactly goes wrong. Please think it through carefully, like an expert in epistemic game theory.
