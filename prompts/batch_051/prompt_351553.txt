I run a small seminar in philosophy and economics, and next week's guest is a philosopher-economist who defends a Harsanyi-style argument for classical utilitarianism. Her position, as she summarizes it, is this: if each person's preferences over lotteries and the social ranking of lotteries each satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and the social ranking respects unanimity in the Pareto sense, then Harsanyi's aggregation theorem shows that social welfare must be representable as a weighted sum of the individuals' utility representations; from there she invokes an impartial-observer argument to claim that the weights should be equal. I do not want the Q&A to consist of her restating the theorem or of technical quibbles she can wave away. I want questions that press the conceptual load-bearing joints of the argument.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions. Include at least one question on each of the following, and for every question provide (a) the reply a sophisticated Harsanyi defender would most likely give and (b) a follow-up question keyed specifically to that reply:

1. The gap between the weighted sum the theorem actually yields and the equal-weights utilitarian conclusion: whether the impartial-observer move does new moral work rather than following from the theorem itself.

2. Interpersonal comparisons of utility: von Neumann–Morgenstern utility functions are unique only up to positive affine transformations, so the sum depends on individually arbitrary choices of scale. Where do the required scaling conventions come from, and are they defensible?

3. The ex ante character of the theorem and the familiar fairness objection: when two lotteries look the same to every individual in expected-utility terms, the Pareto condition forces society to rank them together, even where one would deliver a fair lottery for an indivisible good and the other would assign the good outright.

4. Why the social ranking must itself satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms: society is not literally a decision maker, so what does it mean to call social preferences "rational" here, and is that a normative constraint or a modeling convenience?

5. What counts as the "utility" being summed: if the utilities are just representations of actual preference, the view seems committed to summing whatever people happen to want, including adaptive, manipulated, or antisocial preferences. Does the theorem show that this is the right thing to do, or only that it is consistent with certain axioms?

Add whatever further questions you think a rigorous interviewer should put to her. Format the response as a numbered list in which each item gives the main question, the likely reply, and the follow-up. Keep the entire response to no more than 1,200 words, and write in American English.
