A friend of mine who works on technical AI safety and I spent an entire evening arguing about whether the instruction to avoid side effects can be made precise without anything like a theory of value. She defended an inaction-baseline account: define a side effect as the divergence between the trajectory the world takes under the agent's policy and the trajectory it would have taken had the agent done nothing, and penalize that divergence as computed inside a predictive world model. On her view, a good world model is all the machinery required; no specification of what humans care about is needed beyond the narrow task reward, because the no-op trajectory supplies the entire standard of comparison. I pressed two objections. First, the choice of a no-op baseline treats the world's default course as the thing to preserve, which is already an evaluative stance toward the status quo. Second, and more tellingly, a world model does not come with a distance measure between two trajectories: the world has more features than any finite metric can track, so someone must choose which features to compare and how much each matters before any divergence penalty exists, and those choices look like value judgments smuggled in as if they were merely representational. She replied that this was exactly the kind of abstract hand-waving that collapses as soon as you try to exhibit a concrete case.

Rather than keep asserting it, I want to exhibit the case. Please build a thought experiment to order that gives the objection its best run. The case is for our argument, so it must not simply stipulate the verdict: you need to define the divergence metric, compute the penalized payoffs, and show the policies each agent actually selects. The setting should be realistic and small enough to be solved by hand — a household robot performing a single chore in an ordinary room, say — with no exotic physics and no superintelligent powers. Give the agent a narrow task reward and a side-effect penalty, and put two agents in the same decision problem, with the same task reward and planning horizon. Agent A uses the inaction-baseline penalty: it minimizes the divergence between the world its policy produces and the world predicted under a no-op policy, using a fixed, finite inventory of world-features with the weights a competent engineer would naturally choose; the metric must not be an obvious strawman that simply ignores the damage from the start. Agent B uses the same kind of metric but a different baseline: the world a competent, ordinarily careful person would produce while performing the same narrow task. Engineer the problem so that at least two task-fulfilling policies are available. Agent A must prefer a policy that completes the task but causes damage or disruption any human would call a serious side effect, while Agent B completes the task and avoids the damage. Agent A's preference must arise from the baseline-and-metric itself, not from ignorance, malice, or computational error: the agent must have an accurate world model and full information about the damage, and must still choose the harmful route because that is what the penalized objective selects.

After presenting the scenario, walk through both agents' calculations — the trajectories, the feature inventories, the weights, and the penalized values — and explain why each policy wins. Then identify the single feature of the case doing the work; describe what happens to Agent A's verdict if that feature is removed or repaired; and answer the question the exercise was built to force: is the repair value-free, or does making Agent A avoid the damage require an evaluative judgment of the kind the inaction-baseline account claimed to do without, even if it stops short of a complete specification of value? Keep the exposition self-contained: someone who has never read the alignment engineering literature should still be able to follow the arithmetic.
