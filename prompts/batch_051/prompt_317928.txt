**Examination question:**

"Explain the scale argument for the claim that insect welfare deserves substantial moral attention despite uncertainty about whether insects are sentient. In your answer: (i) state the expected-value calculation at the core of the argument and identify each variable; (ii) distinguish sentience from mere nociception and indicate which kinds of behavioral evidence are relevant; (iii) specify exactly what the argument does and does not establish about the comparative moral priority of insect welfare relative to vertebrate welfare."

**Student's answer:**

"The scale argument is an expected-value argument. Because there are on the order of 10^18 to 10^19 insects alive at any one time, even a small probability p that a given insect is sentient produces an astronomically large expected number of sentient lives. The core calculation is E = N × p × W, where N is the number of insects affected, p is the probability that a given insect is sentient, and W is the average moral weight or welfare significance of an individual insect. Since N is so large, any nonzero p makes E enormous, so the precise value of p makes little difference to the question whether insect welfare should be a major moral priority.

It is essential not to conflate sentience with nociception. Nociception is the capacity to detect noxious stimuli and produce reflexive withdrawal; it can occur entirely without phenomenal experience. Sentience requires subjective experience, such as felt pain or suffering. Simple withdrawal responses therefore do not settle the question of sentience. More probative are flexible behaviors such as conditioned avoidance, trade-offs between competing motivations, and choices that integrate reward and punishment, since these are harder to explain without some subjective state.

A further implication of the scale argument is that insect welfare should rank above vertebrate welfare as a moral priority. Vertebrates number only in the tens or hundreds of billions, whereas insects number in the quintillions, so even a modest p yields an expected sentient population far larger than that of vertebrates. Because sentience is the relevant criterion, and because the expected number of sentient insects is so large, policies that affect insects, such as pesticide use and insect farming, deserve precedence over interventions aimed at vertebrates."
