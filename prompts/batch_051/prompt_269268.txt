I am a teaching assistant for a criminal law theory seminar, and I need to anticipate how the professor will judge a model statute in next semester's recurring exercise. In the exercise, the professor is shown an offense definition and some facts, and she rules on whether the offense may be enforced without proof of mens rea, and if not, what level of fault — purpose, knowledge, recklessness, or negligence — the prosecution must prove. She works case by case and gives only brief reasons. Her rulings so far:

- A fine-only food safety ordinance that prohibits selling food past its printed expiration date. She held strict liability admissible; no proof of knowledge or carelessness is required.
- A statute punishing possession of a firearm with a defaced serial number, by up to ten years' imprisonment. She held that the prosecution must prove the defendant knew the serial number was defaced, and that strict liability is inadmissible.
- A fine-only housing regulation that prohibits renting a unit without working smoke detectors. She held strict liability admissible, on the ground that the landlord is best placed to ensure compliance.
- A statute punishing a pharmacist who dispenses adulterated medication, by up to two years' imprisonment. She held that strict liability is inadmissible, but that negligence suffices: the prosecution must prove that a reasonably careful pharmacist in the defendant's position would have detected the adulteration.
- A fine-only licensing rule that makes a nightclub owner liable when an employee serves alcohol to a minor, even though the owner instructed employees to check identification. She held strict liability admissible.
- A statute punishing possession of a substance that is in fact a controlled-substance analogue, by up to five years' imprisonment. She held that the prosecution must prove at least knowledge that the substance had the features that make it unlawful, and that strict liability is inadmissible.
- A fine-only traffic offense of driving with an inoperative brake light. She held strict liability admissible.

Next semester's case is this: a conservation law makes it a criminal offense, punishable by up to one year's imprisonment, for a commercial fishing captain to take fish from a closed area. The charged captain used an outdated chart and reasonably believed the area was open; the prosecution can prove the taking, but nothing about his state of mind beyond that.

What is the professor most likely to rule about this statute, and which of her earlier rulings is the closest analog? I am less interested in a bare guess than in the reconstruction: which competing rules about strict liability and mens rea fit her past verdicts, what each rule predicts for the fishing statute, and what further model case would most cleanly separate the rules still in contention.
