In physics, a situation can be described from any coordinate system, but the laws are written so that nothing important depends on which frame we pick; vectors and tensors let us drop the coordinates entirely. It seems to me that pronouns and indexicals — "I," "you," "here," "now" — do for language what an origin and axes do for a coordinate system: they fix a deictic center (speaker, addressee, place, and time of utterance), and everything else gets interpreted relative to it, much as a position vector is measured from (0,0). The parallel I cannot decide about is this: could a language eliminate indexicals the way physics eliminates privileged coordinates, by replacing "I" with a uniquely identifying description of the speaker and "now" with a date, without losing part of what an utterance means or does? Some philosophers of language treat indexicals as essential and not fully paraphrasable, while others treat them as convenient shorthand for context-sensitive descriptions. Is my physics analogy a deep guide to that dispute, or does it break down because coordinates and indexicals are special for different reasons? Please mark clearly what is settled and what remains genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present one side of a live debate as the consensus. Answer in American English.
