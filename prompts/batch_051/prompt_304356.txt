You are a professional speechwriter with a background in moral philosophy, but this speech must read as if it were written by the speaker: Maura's closest friend and fellow volunteer, who helped her patch the boat each winter.

Maura has just retired, at seventy-eight, from forty-two years as the unpaid keeper of the village foot-ferry, a small boat she rowed across a tidal river each day. Without it, the village would have been cut off from the railway station and the shops. She never charged, because she wanted the crossing to belong to everyone. A handful of villagers quietly helped with money and repairs over the years; most people crossed freely and never contributed anything. Many simply assumed the ferry was provided by the council, or never thought about who kept it going.

At the party in the village hall to mark her retirement, Maura has asked the speaker to say something she can live with. There must be no comforting fiction about how the whole community kept the ferry going, because it did not. But there must also be no scolding: she does not regret the work and does not want to end her time in the village as its accuser. Above all, she wants the speech to make people understand, gently, what it means that the service they used all those years was not part of the landscape — not a taken-for-granted given of village life — but something that existed only because a particular person kept deciding, day after day, to sustain it.

Write the speech. The audience is the whole village: the regulars who helped, the many who never lifted a finger, the parish councillor who never found a budget line, and Maura herself. The speech should honour her honestly, say something true about taking without contributing — without using the term free riding or lecturing — and leave the room understanding why using a shared good without contributing is not a victimless thing, while still being a speech rather than a sermon or an essay. Think carefully about what the speaker can and cannot say, and begin by briefly drafting two or three possible openings before choosing the strongest and giving the speech in full.
