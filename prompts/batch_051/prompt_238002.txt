So here's a thing I can't get straight. A court in, say, Germany says a post on a US platform defames a German resident, and it orders the platform to remove it not just for German users but everywhere—even where that exact speech is legal, and even though the publisher never aimed it at most of those places. The court isn't quite saying the poster broke every country's law; it's saying the harm done in Germany somehow gives Germany a say over what people in Brazil or Canada gets to read. What view of speech is that practice presupposing? It seems to assume speech 'happens' wherever it's read, or that harm travels with the content rather than living where the speaker acted. But the same legal systems sometimes talk as though speech is an act performed where the speaker is, and that the reader's country's standards shouldn't bind a foreign publisher. Are those commitments in tension, or is there a coherent theory of what speech is—and where a wrong is located—that licenses the global-takedown practice but not, say, one country banning books printed abroad? And if you swapped in the rival view (speech happens where the speaker speaks), what would the practice have to look like instead? I guess I'm asking: what's the implicit philosophy of the global takedown, and how much of it is forced versus just one of several defensible takes?
