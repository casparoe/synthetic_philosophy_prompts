I spent my working life in the natural sciences and have only recently begun reading philosophy of probability seriously. I have agreed to lead one ninety-minute session on the reference class problem for a reading group of retired scientists and engineers—people entirely comfortable with statistical reasoning but with little formal philosophy. I can state the problem competently, but I do not trust my sense of how to teach it, and that is what I am asking you for. Do not explain the problem to me; plan how I should explain it to them.

For our purposes the problem is this: to assign a probability to a particular event—say, that a specified patient will survive a certain number of years—one must place the event in some reference class of similar events and read off a relative frequency; but the event belongs to many such classes at once, and the frequencies can genuinely diverge. I want the group to leave seeing why this is not merely a defect in the available data, and why the familiar move of choosing the narrowest class does not, by itself, settle the question.

Plan the session for me. Please address, in order: (1) the sequence of ideas and what depends on what—which notions must be secured first, and which distinctions will do no work for this audience until something earlier is in place; (2) the one example that should carry the whole session—I had thought of a medical prognosis for a single patient, but tell me what that example must have and must lack, and why a die roll or a coin toss would be the wrong carrying case; (3) the misconceptions this particular audience will predictably bring or form—my own guess, from years of watching scientists stratify data, is that they will propose conditioning on every available variable and treat the residual spread as mere noise, but I want the full map—and exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be headed off rather than allowed to harden; (4) what I should deliberately leave out at this level, and how I should say so; and (5) one question I can put to them in the final ten minutes that would show whether the idea has actually landed, with a brief note on what a good answer would look like.

Finally, I would like you to be explicit about your own uncertainty: as you make substantive claims about the problem, indicate your level of confidence in each, and at the end single out the point you are least sure of.
