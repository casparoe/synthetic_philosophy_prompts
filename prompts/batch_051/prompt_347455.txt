I teach an upper-level seminar in the philosophy of machine learning, and I am setting the take-home final. Please write one essay question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, on the idea of training models to be legible to their users rather than merely accurate. The question should not be answerable by reciting the familiar point that there is a trade-off between accuracy and interpretability; it should force students to take a position on whether optimizing for legibility can amount to genuine understanding of what a model is doing, or only to equipping the model with a just-so story.

Base the question on the following scenario, but phrase the question in your own words. A clinical prediction team has deliberately accepted a measurable loss in predictive performance relative to their current model so that every risk estimate the new model produces can be accompanied by a short justification drawn from a fixed, human-readable vocabulary of clinically meaningful rules. They call this a legibility-first model, and they defend the trade-off by saying that a slightly less accurate model is more trustworthy and more useful in practice if clinicians can see at a glance why each score was produced. A critic replies that this changes the subject: the mechanism that produces the justification is not necessarily the mechanism that produces the score, and a model can be trained to emit plausible rationales without those rationales explaining the decision. The question should ask whether the legibility-first approach can make the model more understandable in any respect that matters, and what the team would have to establish for the produced justifications to be genuinely explanatory rather than merely persuasive.

The essay should be answerable in about a thousand words in a take-home setting. It must not assume familiarity with any particular named paper, algorithm, or dataset; this is a conceptual question, not a citation exercise, and nothing should turn on recalling literature. Then write a model answer of about eight hundred to a thousand words, and after that a marking scheme out of twenty marks built around five must-hit points worth four marks each. For each point, add a sentence on how weaker candidates typically get it wrong, and make the marking scheme separate a first-class answer from a merely competent one by naming the specific moves each must contain.

Write the entire reply as continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold text, and no tables. The marking scheme should be written in paragraphs, with each point and its marks indicated in the flow of the sentences. When you move from the question to the model answer and then to the marking scheme, signal each transition in a full sentence rather than with a heading.
