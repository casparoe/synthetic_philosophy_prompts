i've been convinced by a line in William Morris's essay *Useful Work versus Useless Toil*. He says every kind of work has “beast-like pain” but the difference between good and bad work is hope. Then he drops this verdict:
“there is some labour which is so far from being a blessing that it is a curse; that it would be better for the community and for the worker if the latter were to fold his hands and refuse to work, and either die or let us pack him off to the workhouse or prison - which you will.”
Earlier he says the only work worth doing carries three hopes: rest, product, and pleasure in the work itself. I basically accept this. What I want is a map of what would count against Morris's position, now, in a world where automation may actually remove a lot of the work he'd call useless toil. Not objections to his socialism, but to the criterion itself. For example, if people in objectively "hopeless" work still report satisfaction because of pay, companionship, or the fact the job is easy, is that a defeater? Which cases would Morris have to concede as damaging—say, workers who prefer the mindless part of the job, or craft work that is meaningful but destroys the hope of rest? Does his view make any empirical predictions about work under automation, or has "hope" become so inner and subjective that no data could falsify it? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me if the position is so protected that that itself is a problem.
