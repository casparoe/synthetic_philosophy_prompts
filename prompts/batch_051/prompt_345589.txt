I recently found myself listening to two friends argue over a vote I will have to cast soon, and I still do not know what to make of it. In our upcoming general election, the district is one that the same major party has won by a wide margin for years. A smaller party matches my actual political commitments far better than the major party I would otherwise be left choosing from. Maya says I should vote for the smaller party anyway. Her reasoning is that, in a district this safe, my vote has essentially no chance of deciding the outcome, so its real effect is mostly expressive: it records what I actually believe, it helps deny the dominant party the look of a full mandate, and it signals that there are voters who want something different. Liam says that this very reasoning is the problem. If everyone who preferred the smaller party thought their district was safe and voted expressively, the politicians who rely on those districts would never have to earn those votes, and if the district ever tightened unexpectedly, that settled habit of thought would be exactly what let the candidate those voters most wanted to keep out win. A vote, he says, is not a statement to yourself; it is a share in a collective decision, and you cast it for the least bad of the outcomes that can actually come about.

They kept at it. Maya answered that Liam is quietly assuming the district might not be safe after all. The district's history is what it is, she said, and in this particular race the risk of the worse candidate slipping through is negligible; in a genuinely close district she would vote differently. The meaning of a vote changes with the context, and treating every vote as though it carried the same chance of deciding the result is simply false. Liam replied that the line between a safe district and a competitive one is itself produced by choices like mine. A district becomes safe partly because one side's potential voters stay home or cast protest votes, and after the election the losers are still counted and read as evidence about where the party could gain support. A small-party vote is therefore not an instrument that happens to be inert in a safe district; it is one of the forces keeping the district safe. Maya said this cuts the other way. If losing votes are read as evidence, then a reluctant vote for the lesser evil is read as approval of the lesser evil's whole platform, and the dominant party learns that it does not need to earn my support, only to be less frightening than the alternative. The only reliable way to make a party improve, she said, is to deny it a vote rather than to gift it one while holding your nose.

Liam then changed tack. He said that, even setting expression aside entirely, voting for a party you know will lose is a kind of free-riding on the voters who are willing to do the unpleasant work of keeping the worse candidate out of office; if we owe something to the people who would be harmed by the worse candidate, then clean-principles voting lets somebody else carry that burden while I get to enjoy the principle. Maya replied that this proves too much, because it would make primary challenges and new parties wrong in every general election, since there is always some candidate who is the lesser evil and always someone worse to point to. If harm reduction is the only thing a vote is for, then the expressive channel Liam himself seems willing to allow gets squeezed to nothing, and the only vote that ever counts as responsible is the safest vote for whichever major party is currently ahead. I could not referee them, and I suspect they were each right about some sub-claims and wrong about others. Please adjudicate the argument move by move: list each distinct claim, rate it from clearly right to clearly wrong with a short justification for each rating, state what either of them is missing, and then say who has the stronger overall position for a voter in a reliably non-competitive district.
