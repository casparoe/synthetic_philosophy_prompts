A jurisdiction is considering the following institution, designed to address the unfairness of binding people to legal rules they had no realistic chance of knowing. All statutes continue to mean exactly what their text and settled interpretation make them mean, independent of any summary. But the state must publish, for every statute, an official Plain-Language Notice stating in non-specialist terms what the statute prohibits, permits, or requires, with illustrative examples where the drafters think them useful.

The Notice has no legislative force: it cannot make punishable conduct that the statute does not reach, and courts may not consult it when interpreting the statute. Its only legal role is negative, and it operates as follows:

(1) In any criminal prosecution or regulatory enforcement, it is a complete defence that the defendant actually relied on a then-current Notice and that the Notice materially misdescribed the statute as applied to the defendant's conduct, for example by omitting a prohibition that reached that conduct or by making the conduct appear lawful.

(2) Reliance is presumed if the Notice was current and the conduct fell within what the Notice appeared to permit; the prosecution may rebut the presumption by showing that the defendant in fact knew the true rule by other means.

(3) If the defence succeeds, the defendant is acquitted or the citation is dismissed, the Notice is corrected, and the correction applies only to conduct occurring after the correction is published. The statute itself is untouched and remains in force for everyone else.

(4) If no Notice exists for a rule at the time of the conduct, the absence of a Notice is a complete defence only for a first-time offender; a defendant with any prior conviction under any statute cannot raise the absence of a Notice.

Finally, the office that drafts the Notices has its budget and performance reviews tied to the average word count of its Notices and the speed with which they are issued. The office is not penalized when a Notice turns out to have overstated the law, but it is penalized when a Notice turns out to have understated the law.

Walk through how this institution would actually behave in practice. Trace the incentives facing the drafting office, prosecutors, and potential defendants; identify the first places the system would be gamed; distinguish carefully the dynamics of overstated Notices from understated Notices; and assess whether the institution solves the original unfairness—binding people to rules they cannot know—or merely relocates it, and whether the failure modes can be fixed without abandoning the basic design.
