I'm a retired physical chemist who started reading philosophy of action and AI ethics last year, so I'm still getting the terms under me. Something keeps nagging me. I was in a discussion on a forum I lurk on about whether a language model that can produce nuanced moral judgments but cannot do anything in the world—it can't refuse a task in any way that matters, it just emits tokens—counts as a moral agent, or whether it is more like a very articulate weathervane. One person waved the whole thing off with the proverb, "actions speak louder than words." Their argument was: the model only has words and no actions, so no matter how good the judgments are, it isn't the sort of thing we should call a moral agent, let alone hold responsible.

I feel like that little sentence is carrying a lot of weight, and that "action" and "word" are not as clean a split as the proverb wants them to be. I can think of a few things it might mean—maybe behavior is better evidence of someone's real commitments than their self-report; maybe only what you actually do, not what you judge, makes you praiseworthy or blameworthy; maybe the capacity to act is a necessary condition of being a moral agent in the first place; maybe there is a real difference between saying something and doing it even when saying is itself a kind of doing. But I can also see these readings pulling apart: is emitting tokens an action? Is refusing to answer an action? Is a speech act an action in the relevant sense? I can't quite keep it all straight.

So here's the ask. Lay out the distinct precise claims that "actions speak louder than words" could be expressing when it is used to deny moral agency to a judgment-capable system that cannot act on its judgments—go ahead from readings that are nearly vacuous to readings that are surprising or probably false. But then commit. I don't want a neutral menu that ends with "it depends." For each candidate reading, say whether you take it to be platitudinous, substantive and defensible, or just false, with your reasons. And then say which precise reading the "no action, so no moral agency" argument actually needs in order to go through, and whether that reading survives being made explicit. If the argument only works on a reading that is false or unsupported, say that straight out.
