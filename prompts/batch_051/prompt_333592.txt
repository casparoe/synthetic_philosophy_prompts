I'm a novelist, nearing the end of a draft set in a small country on the edge of the EU, a place that's a bit Catalonia, a bit Scotland, and theres going to be a unilateral independence vote near the end. My main character is a constitutional lawyer, and in one chapter she gives a public lecture laying out the moral case for why a territorially concentrated group may secede. The problem: she works through every standard argument, and the chapter reads like a blog post, because she's saying things my readers will have seen a hundred times. I want one more argument for her to make that isnt in the standard set.

Here's what she already covers:

- The remedial-right view: unilateral secession is justified only as a remedy for serious and persistent injustice, like ethnic cleansing, a state that is destroying the group's institutions, or long-term discriminatory extraction of resources from the region.
- The national self-determination view: a distinct people with a shared language, institutions, and common identity has a right to self-government, and when that people is territorially concentrated, that can require its own state.
- The plebiscitary view: a territorially concentrated majority that votes to leave in a free and fair referendum has done enough; the governed have the right to choose who governs them, including choosing not to be governed by them.
- The freedom-of-association view: legitimate states rest on consent, so a group that withdraws its consent and wants a new political association is exercising exit, not committing treason, provided the seceding group grants the same right of exit to its own internal minorities.
- The decolonization view: peoples under colonial rule have a right to self-government within their colonial borders, which is why territorial integrity has a principled exception for colonies.

I'd like you to give me one more moral argument for a right to secede that is not any of those, not a simple recombination or special case of them, and not just the mirror image of a standard unionist argument. It also can't be a purely pragmatic argument (not 'we'd be richer alone' or 'the center will never grant it anyway'). It has to be something my lawyer could say in public without sounding unhinged, and ideally something my editor won't have read.

And tell me whether it's actually any good: would a smart unionist in the lecture hall have an easy reply, or does it put real pressure on the standard anti-secession position?

Give me the response in three sections, in this order: "The argument" (about 150–200 words, written the way she might deliver it), "Why it's not on the list" (about 100 words), and "How hard it hits" (about 100–150 words: your honest assessment plus the strongest reply against it).
