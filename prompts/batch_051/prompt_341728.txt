I've been trying to pin down a tension in how community forecasting platforms actually operate, and I'd like a philosophically careful reconstruction of what the practice presupposes. Take Metaculus and similar forecasting communities as the concrete case. On one side of the ledger, forecasters' predictions are scored with proper scoring rules — usually the Brier score or log score — which compare a stated probability only to the eventual outcome, and much of the platform's epistemic furniture is built on those accuracy scores: leaderboards, track records, calibration curves, talk of being well-calibrated, and the implicit sense that the fundamental epistemic failure a forecaster can exhibit is simply being farther from the truth than they could have been. That half of the practice looks as if it presupposes a version of accuracy-first epistemology: the view that accuracy is the sole fundamental epistemic good, and that norms like obeying the probability axioms, conditionalizing, and avoiding over- or underconfidence earn their keep only because they tend to maximize expected accuracy as measured by a strictly proper scoring rule. But the same platforms run a second reward system alongside the first: points for contributions that are not straightforwardly about matching the eventual outcome, such as peer or relative scores that reward a forecaster whose prediction moves the community's aggregate toward the value at which it later resolves, or reputation for anticipating where the consensus is heading before the crowd converges. These systems can pull apart: a forecaster can get the outcome right while being penalized for doing so at the wrong time or against the grain of the community, and a forecaster can track the consensus and gain from it while adding little or nothing to whatever is being aggregated.

What I want to know is this: what view of epistemic value does each half of this practice presuppose, and are the two halves consistent with each other? In particular, I want the answer to distinguish what the accuracy-based half strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Does using Brier scores and building a leaderboard on them genuinely commit a platform to accuracy-first epistemology — the claim that there is one fundamental epistemic value, accuracy, and that everything else derives from it — or is that practice compatible with several rival pictures, for example a purely pragmatic story on which we reward accuracy only because calibrated forecasts are more useful for downstream decisions, or a consensus-based picture on which the community's aggregate is itself the object of interest? And if we deliberately replaced an accuracy-first interpretation with one of those rivals, what would concretely change in how the platform scores, ranks, and narrates its forecasters? Please say how confident you are in the reconstruction you give, and single out the specific point you are least sure of.
