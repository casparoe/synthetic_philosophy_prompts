I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, currently on a six-month placement with the legal directorate of a national human rights institution. The directorate has been asked by a parliamentary committee drafting a new equality act to produce a short, reusable guidance note on how the committee's own staff should decide whether some newly proposed characteristic deserves to be added to the list of protected grounds. The committee does not want our office to take a position on any pending proposal; it wants a screening procedure that can be run each time a draft bill or amendment proposes protecting a new ground. I have been asked to write the first draft.

The philosophical material feels tractable in the abstract: the hallmark criteria in the literature are something like immutability (or, more carefully, the cost of changing or hiding the trait), salience, a history of subordination or exclusion, political powerlessness, weak relevance to legitimate qualifications, stigmatic or dignitary harm, and identity-centrality — along with the standard objections to each criterion. What I cannot do is turn that list into something a legislative drafter without a theory background can apply in an afternoon, with defensible stopping rules. I need the procedure to say which questions to ask and in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, at which points the analysis stops with a no, and at which points the file must be handed to a person because the residual question is a contest of values rather than an application of the test. I also want it to say which distinctions must survive the simplification: for example, the difference between a ground being genuinely tied to a qualification and its merely being correlated with one, and the difference between a trait being chosen and its being costly or humiliating to abandon.

Please write the procedure as something the directorate could adopt, then run it on three proposed grounds currently being floated: weight or body size, a past criminal conviction, and political affiliation. I suspect the third will produce an answer the institution will not want to give, and I want to be told plainly whether it does. For each worked case, say what the procedure returns, which step is doing most of the work, where the result is most exposed to a reasonable objection, and what specific fact or argument would flip the outcome. Finally, identify the step of the whole procedure where a critic could most fairly say the theory has been smuggled into the checklist, and say what would have to be different — in the case, the law, or the underlying principle — for the procedure's verdict at that step to change.
