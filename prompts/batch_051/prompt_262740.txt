I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've been going down a rabbit hole reading about akrasia for a character I'm working on. She's a recovering alcoholic who pours the bottle down the sink every morning and is at the liquor store by eight every night, and I need to understand what is actually happening in her head so the scenes don't just come out as "she's weak" or "she's self-destructive." A buddy of mine who works in behavioral economics gave me an argument last week that I can't shake. It seems to show that the entire premise of my novel — weakness of will as something real — is a confusion, and I'm pretty sure it proves too much, but I can't locate the bad step. Here it is, as cleanly as I can reconstruct it.

(1) In a genuine case of akrasia, a person judges that X is better, all things considered, and then freely does Y — and the judgment stays in place. She doesn't change her mind; she gets overuled by something in herself, and she knows it while it's happening.

(2) Hyperbolic discounting is a model of exactly this pattern: the value a person assigns to a reward inflates more steeply the nearer the reward is in time. So at the planning stage the far-off good of sobriety beats the drink, but at the moment of temptation the drink, being right there, balloons in value and wins.

(3) Under that model, at the moment of choice the person is maximizing her own current valuation. By her time-of-choice discount function, the drink genuinely ranks as the best option on the table. There's no extra "judgment" left over that the model says she's betraying.

(4) If she is choosing the option that ranks best by her own current valuation, then she isn't acting against her better judgment at the time of the act — so she isn't weak-willed at that time. She's just doing what she now most wants.

(5) Therefore what we call akrasia collapses into ordinary preference reversal across time. The person isn't one agent losing a fight against her own verdict; she's a series of time-slices, each doing exactly what it most wants, and the "weakness" is only the morning self losing a negotiation to the evening self.

(6) But the same reasoning would force me to say that a woman who, while pouring the drink, says "I still want to quit — I'd pay someone to handcuff me to the radiator" is not actually in conflict with herself. The whole drama I'm writing — the divided person, wanting the drink and wanting not to want it, her own verdict scrolling past while her hand does the thing she's condemning — becomes impossible. And that seems to prove too much, because that isn't a hallucination. People do live like that.

I refuse to write her as a relay race of time-slices — I'm pretty sure a single person can be genuinely divided against herself at one moment, wanting the drink and wanting not to want it. But this argument seems to make that impossible, so either I'm wrong to refuse or one of the steps has to go. Which step do I reject, and what does it cost?
