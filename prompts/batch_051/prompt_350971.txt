Over the weekend I got into an argument with some friends about constitutional design, and one of them proposed this clause for a small democracy, half as a joke. The rule: if the top executive office becomes vacant without a constitutionally designated successor (say the president and vice-president are both killed or resign at once), all executive powers are automatically suspended rather than being transferred to anyone. No acting president, no emergency Cabinet with borrowed authority. The country simply has no executive until the independent election commission organizes a fresh election. But here's the catch he tacked on: the election commission's budget must be approved by the Ministry of Finance, whose minister is appointed by the president and serves at the president's pleasure; and the armed forces may act without civilian orders only when a majority of the constitutional court declares an existential emergency, except the court's members' terms are about to expire and their replacements are appointed by—you guessed it—the president.

I'm not asking whether the clause is wise in principle; I want to know how it would actually behave. Walk me through what happens after the double vacancy. What's the first workaround, where do people get stuck, and does suspending the executive make the gap safer or more dangerous in practice? How would the rule get gamed before any vacancy even happens? And is there a fix that keeps the original point of the clause—stopping an ambitious official from hoovering up power in a succession crisis—without permanent paralysis?
