I am a third-year undergraduate in a historiography seminar, and we have been working through the older Rankean ideal that history should present the past as it actually happened, together with the later scepticism of writers such as E. H. Carr and Hayden White, who stress that every historian selects events and arranges them into a story. I still use the word “objective” confidently when I criticise a book, but I am no longer sure what I mean by it.

Please take the role of a careful professional philosopher of history, comfortable with both conceptual analysis and the practical difficulties of historical writing. You may begin with rough brainstorming, but I want you to end with a considered working definition of historical objectivity, understood as a property of a work of history (a monograph, chronicle, memoir, or similar text). Then run that definition against each of the following cases and give a definite verdict for each, with a sentence or two of justification.

(a) A chronicle that lists, in strict chronological order, every verifiable event in a single square kilometre of Lyon during July 1789. Every item is true, and nothing is selected, weighted, or turned into narrative.

(b) Two historians write about the same peasant revolt using the same documents. Both are factually accurate, but one emplots the revolt as a tragedy of failed reform and the other as a comedy of institutional bungling.

(c) A meticulously sourced history of the Atlantic slave trade that states in the text that transatlantic slavery was an atrocity.

(d) A history of the French Revolution that says only true things but never mentions the role of women, the Parisian crowd, or economic pressures on the city.

(e) A first-person memoir of a battle that is accurate about everything the author could observe but omits the enemy’s perspective and the blunders of the memoirist’s own side.

(f) An archive-based monograph that argues a sharp thesis — for instance, that a particular diplomatic decision made war more likely — but also presents and seriously engages the documents that count against the thesis.

(g) A synthesis produced by a binational commission of historians from formerly enemy states, which publishes only carefully negotiated judgments that no individual member considers fully adequate.

My rough starting point is that (a) should count as objective in some minimal way, and (d) should not count as objective despite containing no falsehoods; I have no settled view about (c) or (g). I suspect the word is doing at least two jobs, one about conformity to the evidence and one about even-handedness, and that the cases move differently against those two jobs. If you agree, give me both definitions and say which cases change their verdict between them.

Finally, tell me which of your verdicts is the least secure, and whether your account makes objectivity a matter of not selecting at all or of selecting honestly. Do not write a historiography essay about Ranke, Carr, and White; treat their challenge as something to incorporate into the definition rather than a set of authors to survey.
