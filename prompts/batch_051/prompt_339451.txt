I keep coming back to the argument over whether remembering is something we owe—to other people and to our own past—or whether there is a serious case for letting some memories go. I find Margalit's idea that certain people and events lay an ethical claim on us not to be forgotten persuasive, and I also cannot dismiss Rieff's counterclaim that the duty to remember can curdle into a trap, and that forgetting is sometimes the more merciful or more honest response. The abstract debate seems to leave something out, so I want to see it pushed through a story that makes the choice concrete.

Use Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind as the test case. The film's premise is a clinic that erases targeted memories, and it follows Joel's attempt to hold onto his memories of Clementine while they are being destroyed, alongside Mary's discovery that she has already had an affair and its memory erased. I would like a philosophical reading of the film through the Margalit/Rieff debate, one that treats the film as a sincere contribution to the ethics of memory rather than an illustration of a doctrine. Where does the film stand on whether we have something like a duty to remember even painful relationships? Does it present forgetting as legitimate self-protection, or as a self-deception the ending deliberately refuses? Where does it complicate Margalit, by showing memory held onto out of possessiveness, resentment, or self-torment rather than obligation, and where does it complicate Rieff, by showing that what gets erased was load-bearing for character, agency, and relationship in ways the forgetting party could not see? And what does the film's way of showing memory literally disintegrate add that the philosophical debate alone cannot deliver? Be faithful in both directions: do not bend the film to fit the theory or the theory to fit the film, and say plainly where one resists the other.
