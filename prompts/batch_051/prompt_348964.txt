My argument that an oath never adds any real obligation over and above the promise it decorates:
(1) The force of an oath has to come from one of three sources: the words said, the swearer's intention, or the sanction the oath calls down (a god's punishment, society's contempt, the Styx's curse, whatever).
(2) Words are just sounds or marks, and sounds can't create duties by themselves.
(3) The intention to be bound is just the intention to keep the underlying promise; if the oath binds only because you intend it to, then it adds nothing to the promise itself, and an intention can be withdrawn anyway.
(4) A sanction gives you a reason to keep the oath only if it is real and likely to land; where it's absent the oath is inert, and where it's present it seems to pressure you rather than obligate you.
(5) So an oath can't put you under any new moral requirement.

I find each step hard to deny, but the conclusion says breaking a solemn oath sworn on the Styx is no worse, in itself, than breaking a casual promise with identical content, and I don't believe that. Tell me which step, if any, fails, and what the corrected version of that step would say. If step 4 is the one you'd attack, explain exactly how a sanction can obligate rather than merely threaten.
