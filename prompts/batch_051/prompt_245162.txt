Here's a policy I follow in my own work and when I comment on students' papers, and I'm starting to worry its just a bias with tenure. When I'm doing moral philosophy or decision theory, I treat my immediate reactions to concrete cases as evidence, and I treat general principles—especially structural ones like transitivity or simplicity—as mere summaries of those reactions. So if a principle says my pairwise judgments have to be transitive and mine aren't, I don't fix the judgments; I report the intransitivity as a finding. And I never let "but this principle is simpler" count even a little in favor of a theory when it conflicts with a case. Last term a student smoothed out a messy set of case intuitions by adding a gerrymandered exception to a principle, and I wrote in the margins that the smoothing was the mistake. A colleague said I'm treating my case intuitions as if they were incorrigible, and that transitivity and simplicity are themselves evidence because they're the only way to tell a real pattern from noise. Is my policy defensible, or is it a bias toward cases dressed up as a methodology? If it's partly defensible, what's the version that keeps whatever is good in it without making case intuitions the only evidence in philosophy?
