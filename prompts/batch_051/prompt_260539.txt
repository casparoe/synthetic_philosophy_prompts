Okay so I just spent the whole evening arguing with a friend about what Spinoza would have made of the simulation hypothesis, and we could not settle it, and now I want a proper reconstruction of his position. We agreed on the basics: one infinite substance, God or Nature, no real contingency, free will as ignorance of causes, blessedness consisting in the intellectual love of God. But we split hard. My friend thinks Spinoza would just despise the simulation idea as a warmed-over creation story: a designed world, a designer outside it, a base reality versus a made one, and a god who could have coded the universe otherwise. That is exactly the anthropomorphic, will-driven God the Ethics is written against. I think Spinoza's response would be more interesting than a flat no. He could perfectly well accept that our world is simulated without treating the simulation as anything outside nature: the simulator and the simulated are both finite modes of the one substance, and the difference between base world and simulation is a difference between finite things, not between creator and creation. And the idea that it could have been coded differently—he would have to reject that, because that is straight-up contingency talk. So what I want answered is: what is Spinoza's considered position on the simulation hypothesis, not just yes or no, but through the actual machinery of his system? Specifically, would the hypothesis even be intelligible to him as something that matters, or would it collapse once you have the distinction between adequate and inadequate ideas straight? Would discovering that the world is a simulation make any difference to the intellectual love of God—would understanding that the whole thing is a piece of code and that the code is necessary still be the kind of thing you are supposed to love? And one more thing: my friend threw at me that for Spinoza a world fed by inputs from outside would bring back a kind of transcendence, and I did not have a comeback. How would you answer that? Its purely to win the argument, but I would also like to get Spinoza right in the process.
