I am a retired geologist who has lately turned to philosophy, and I sit on my denomination's committee on worship and doctrine. We have received a formal petition from several congregations asking the denomination to require women to cover their heads whenever they pray or prophesy in public worship, on the strength of Saint Paul's instruction in his First Epistle to the Corinthians. The committee are divided. Some members maintain that an apostolic command binds the church unless one can supply a principled, non-arbitrary test for treating it as culturally limited; others reply that the command's own reasoning is embedded in first-century Mediterranean judgements about shame and shorn hair, so that what binds the church is the underlying concern for decorum in worship rather than the covering itself. I have been asked to draft the short written opinion to circulate before our next meeting. The nub of the disagreement is whether the appeal to shame in verses five and six functions as a culturally local premise or as a stable principle, and the opinion should show how your reading of the passage decides that question. It should also recommend a concrete course of action—adopt the requirement, reject it, or adopt it in some modified form—while honestly engaging the strongest consideration on the losing side. The central text is 1 Corinthians 11:4–6 (King James Version):

"4 Every man praying or prophesying, having his head covered, dishonoureth his head.
5 But every woman that prayeth or prophesieth with her head uncovered dishonoureth her head: for that is even all one as if she were shaven.
6 For if the woman be not covered, let her also be shorn: but if it be a shame for a woman to be shorn or shaven, let her be covered."

Please write the opinion in American English.
