I keep coming back to this passage from David Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section X (Of Miracles): "It will be sufficient to observe, that our assurance in any argument of this kind is derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human testimony, and of the usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses." It sounds right in the abstract, but when I look at my actual judgments, I can't tell what principle I'm using. Here are some of them.

1. A stranger at a train station tells me which platform the next train to Chicago leaves from. I believe her straight away, even though I have no track record with this woman. I wouldn't say I'm reasoning from her past reliability; I'm just using the general fact that people usually answer this kind of question correctly.

2. A different stranger at a party tells me that a mutual acquantance borrowed money from someone and never paid it back. I don't reject it out of hand, but I hold it at maybe 30/70, and I'd want to hear the other side before believing it. I feel like I need a track record here in a way I didn't in the train station case.

3. My friend Rohan, who has never lied to me about anything serious, tells me he's been offered a job in another city. I believe him immediately. I don't weigh base rates of job offers; I just accept it.

4. Dave, who exaggerates about fishing and has told me false stories before, tells me he caught a four-pound bass last weekend. I don't disbelieve him automatically, but I assign it maybe 60% credence and I wouldn't repeat it as fact without hedging. My past experience with Dave is doing real work here.

5. A small child tells me there is a monster in the bedroom closet. I don't believe her at all, even though she sounds sincere. I know children are often sincere but unreliable about exactly this kind of thing.

6. A mechanic tells me my brake pads are worn and need replacing. I trust that the pads are likely worn, but I'm much less confident that they need replacing right now, because I know mechanics can inflate the urgency. I might pay for a second opinion. It's not that I reject his testimony; it's that the content plus the source's interest gives me a specific doubt.

Now I want to know: is there one account of testimonial justification that delivers all six verdicts? On the face of it, Hume's passage says all assurance in testimony is derived from observed regularities about human veracity and the conformity of facts to reports. That handles cases 1, 4, 5, and maybe 6. But case 3 feels like it is not derived from any observation about Rohan in particular, and case 2 feels like I'm treating the exact same type of source (a stranger) differently without having observed anything about that individual. So maybe my verdicts are anti-reductionist in some cases and reductionist in others, and I want to know if that can be made principled or if I'm just being inconsistent.

Please give me the principle or small set of principles that best captures these verdicts, stated precisely, explain how it handles each case, and tell me which of my verdicts, if any, it forces me to drop. Also say what the resulting account predicts about a case I haven't listed: a stranger on a long-distance bus tells me the name of the next town and that the route will be delayed by construction. Please write the answer in American English.
