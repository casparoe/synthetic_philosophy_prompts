You are a philosopher of medicine with a specialty in the metaphysics of death and the foundations of bioethics. Think about the problem carefully before answering. As you work, explicitly entertain several candidate diagnoses of the disagreement — for example, a hidden empirical disagreement, a verbal or conceptual disagreement, a normative disagreement, a disagreement about which cases count as data, or two different questions being run under one heading — then argue for the diagnosis you find most defensible, explain which alternatives you reject and why, and close with what would count as progress given your diagnosis.

For more than fifty years, a debate in medicine and bioethics has persisted beneath an official consensus. The official consensus says that death can be declared by either of two standards: the traditional circulatory-respiratory standard (irreversible cessation of circulation and respiration) or the neurological standard (irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem). A patient who is declared dead on neurological grounds is legally dead even while a ventilator keeps the heart beating, the lungs moving, and the body warm.

The two sides are easy to state. The circulatory-respiratory side holds that a body with a beating heart, flowing blood, and gas exchange is not dead, whatever has happened to the brain. The neurological side holds that irreversible loss of whole-brain function is death, even if machines keep the body metabolically active.

And yet the debate never really ended. Critics of the neurological standard point out that bodies declared brain-dead can be maintained for months or even years: they digest tube feedings, heal wounds, mount immune and fever responses, produce urine, exhibit spinal reflexes, and in documented cases have even gestated a fetus to term. On their view, this is not what death looks like, and calling such patients dead is a legal fiction masking a value judgment. They also argue that the "whole brain" standard is not actually met in practice, because the clinical tests used to declare brain death are insensitive to pockets of residual brain function. Defenders reply that what matters is not the metabolic coordination of organs but the permanent loss of the integrated functioning of the organism as a whole, and in particular the permanent loss of consciousness and of any capacity for conscious life. Critics answer that "integration" and "organism" are doing a great deal of unexamined work, and that treating the brain as the body's "master organ" is assumed rather than argued for. Meanwhile, transplantation policy sits on top of this distinction: the dead-donor rule says that vital organs may be taken only from patients who are dead, and brain-dead patients with otherwise healthy organs are a central source of transplantable organs. That fact makes some people reluctant to abandon the neurological standard and makes others suspect that the standard was reverse-engineered to serve organ procurement.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides mostly agree on the biology, and that the real disagreement is about what the concept of death is supposed to do for us — a biological classification of organisms, a metaphysical line between the living and the non-living, a legal status that extinguishes rights and permits burial or organ removal, or a normative threshold for when someone stops being one of us. Is that diagnosis right? Or is there a substantive empirical or conceptual question buried in the dispute — about whether brain death is reliably irreversible, about which functions constitute organismic integration, or about what makes the brain the relevant organ — that could actually be resolved? Give me a structural diagnosis of the disagreement, grounded in what each side actually commits itself to, not a history or a survey of the literature. Given the diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a better criterion, an empirical finding, a conceptual result, a change of question — or an argument that no progress should be expected.
