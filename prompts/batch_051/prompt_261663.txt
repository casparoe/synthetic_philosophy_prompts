In chapter 21 of The Prince, Machiavelli argues that a prince caught between two more powerful neighbors should not remain neutral: if one side wins, the winner will regard the neutral as easy prey; if the other wins, that side will neither help nor shelter him; and neutrality is typically chosen by irresolute rulers to escape present dangers. He thinks it is better to declare for one side openly, even at some risk, because a committed ally has a claim on the victor and shares the fate of the war.

Machiavelli wrote this for a Renaissance principality in a world of limited wars and shifting alliances. Now consider Switzerland, Sweden, and Finland: three modern states that have built their security around variants of armed neutrality or non-alignment, backed by strong militia systems or conscription and, in some cases, by limited but still real alliance commitments. Their neutrality is not passive; it is armed and intended to make conquest costly.

Does Machiavelli's argument survive the change from his world to this one? Which premise is actually under pressure: the assumption that a neutral can be cheaply punished after the war, the assumption that alliances are flexible and personal, or something else? I want a precise diagnosis. Can a modern armed neutral state be distinguished from Machiavelli's irresolute prince, or is its security ultimately created by conditions Machiavelli did not anticipate, such as nuclear deterrence, economic interdependence, or international institutions that restrain victorious powers? If the argument does not survive in its original form, state the strongest repaired version of it that would still explain why armed neutrality sometimes fails.
