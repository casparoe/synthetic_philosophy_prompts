You are to act as a philosopher of mind who specialises in animal consciousness, writing for an advanced philosophy seminar. I want a specification rather than a survey: before anyone proposes a theory of the distribution of phenomenal consciousness across the animal kingdom — which animals have felt experience, and how much or of what kind — what must such a theory deliver to count as adequate?

Start by thinking the problem through in prose for a few paragraphs. Do not move to the list until you have worked through the cases and distinctions that put pressure on naive answers. Then present the requirements as a numbered list. For each requirement: state it precisely; give the case or consideration that makes it a genuine requirement rather than merely one camp's preference; and say whether it is non-negotiable or contested. Your list must address, in one form or another: the distinction between an animal's being conscious, the character and degree of its consciousness, and its moral status; the fact that behaviour and felt experience can come apart in both directions, since a human can withdraw from a noxious stimulus by a spinal reflex before any pain is felt, and a person can be conscious while producing almost no outward behaviour; the line-drawing problem across species and across degrees, including why a sharp threshold cannot merely be asserted; the disunity of nervous systems, such as the very different brain organisation of cephalopods and vertebrates, so that no single structure like the cortex is assumed necessary without argument; and the contested cases people actually want guidance on, such as fish, insects, crustaceans, and cephalopods. After the list, identify the pairs of requirements you suspect cannot all be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most work to exclude familiar proposals. Do not include a bibliography, citations, or a reading list; the deliverable is the reasoned requirement list itself, and I will supply the literature separately.
