In my graduate seminar on the philosophy of law, a student invoked Bentham’s famous dismissal of natural rights—“Natural rights is simple nonsense: natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense, — nonsense upon stilts”—to dispose of a classmate’s argument that future generations possess a natural right to a stable climate. I told the seminar that the sentence is several arguments compressed into a slogan, and that before it can be used it must be decompressed. Lay out the distinct precise theses this line, and especially its closing phrase “nonsense upon stilts,” could be used to assert—claims about what rights-talk means, about whether non-legal rights exist, about the content of declared natural rights, and about the rhetorical function of such declarations. For each thesis, indicate whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false. Then say which reading or readings would genuinely support dismissing the claim about future generations, and which readings Bentham’s text alone does not supply.
