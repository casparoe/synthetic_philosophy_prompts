I want a decision-tree handout for a small blog I write for LessWrong- and EA-adjacent rationalist readers. It should sort readers by what they actually believe about the torture-vs-dust-specks tradeoff: is it worse for one person to suffer fifty years of torture, or for 3^^^3 people to each receive a single dust speck in the eye? My readers already know the setup, so don't summarize it; use those two outcomes as the anchor case at every branch.

Build the tree from logical space rather than from a list of names. First give the diagnostic questions, phrased as concrete claims or choices about the anchor case that a reader can answer without knowing terms like total utilitarianism, prioritarianism, maximin, or the separateness of persons. Then locate the positions as combinations of answers, and note any unoccupied combinations with a one-line diagnosis: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The branch points must cover: (1) whether the astronomical population size makes the comparison so physically or epistemically exotic that it has no determinate answer; (2) whether within a single life an arbitrarily large number of very mild irritations can sum to outweigh the torture—test this with a same-person analog, such as a staggeringly long series of momentary pinprick-level annoyances across one life versus the fifty-year torture, and do not route readers who refuse to flip through cross-person summing questions as if they accepted it; (3) whether intra-personal summing carries over to sums across lives; (4) whether there is a smallest morally relevant unit of suffering, so that a dust speck counts as zero; (5) whether the evaluation should run ex ante through a veil of ignorance or ex post by adding realized states; and (6) the shape of the aggregation function—additive, concave, or bounded.

Leaves that must appear, as answer-combinations rather than labels-first, are: a deflationary/finitist answer; a separateness-of-persons anti-aggregation answer; a significance-threshold answer; a maximin or worst-off-priority answer; a strict discontinuity or lexical answer; a continuous additive answer; and a concave or bounded aggregation answer. Flag positions the tree cannot separate with the dust-speck anchor alone—I suspect total and average welfare, and ex ante and ex post evaluation, may collapse on this case—and where they do, give the minimal supplementary case that separates them. Give each leaf a two-sentence summary, the second specifying the commitment its defenders find most awkward. Aim for six to eight decision points and roughly eight to twelve leaves.
