I'm drafting a short position statement on the evidential role of elegance in science for a philosophy of science seminar, and before I circulate it I'd like an outside audit of whether my commitments actually hang together. I believe all of the following, but I can't locate where the strain is—colleagues I've shown it to have each pointed at a different pair of claims.

(a) Elegance, understood as simplicity, symmetry, and unifying power, is a genuinely epistemic virtue rather than a merely pragmatic or aesthetic one: other things equal, a more elegant theory is more likely to be true.

(b) Elegance is nonetheless strictly subordinate to the data. A theory that fails empirical tests cannot be rescued by its beauty, and in that situation elegance carries no epistemic weight at all.

(c) What counts as elegant is historically and culturally local. Scientific communities in different eras have found quite different theories beautiful, and there is no obvious reason to think our present standards of elegance are the ones attuned to how nature actually works.

(d) The history of science contains many elegant theories that turned out to be false, and this track record should substantially lower the confidence we place in elegance as a guide to truth.

(e) Even so, I think the mathematical beauty of string theory is a good reason to keep developing it and treating it seriously, despite the absence of direct empirical confirmation.

(f) I also distrust no-alternatives arguments: the move from the fact that a theory is the only elegant one we have found to the conclusion that it is therefore likely true.

Please go through these six commitments and produce a precise audit rather than a general essay. I want to know which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be; rank the genuine tensions by severity; and for each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the least costly revision would be, including what that revision forfeits.
