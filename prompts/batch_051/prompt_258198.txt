My mate and I had one of those long arguments last night, after we watched a loose biopic of a Victorian engineer. The film invented a gambling ruin and a betrayal of his business partner, and I spent the walk home defending the idea that this isn't just a bit of harmless fiction, without being able to say exactly why. I came away realising I've never actually checked wether the whole tangle can be true at once. I'd like you to run a consistency audit on the commitments I found myself asserting, roughly in the order they came out:

(a) The dead are beyond direct harm. Once someone's gone, and granting there is no afterlife or posthumous interests, nothing we later say or film can make their life go worse or wrong them, strictly speaking.

(b) But I still want to say we owe the dead truthfulness. If a film shows a real person doing something seriously wicked they never actually did, I want to say the wrong is against *them*, not merely against the audience.

(c) Biopics and historical fiction shouldn't be judged by documentary standards. Compressing timelines, merging minor characters, inventing dialogue that fits the person's documented character — all fine, and I've no interest in being the person complaining that the teacups are ten years too early.

(d) Even so, some inventions are worse than others. An invented scene where a real person commits a grave wrong — a betrayal, collaboration with a murderous regime, cruelty — bothers me far more than an invented gentle quirk, even when the film is plainly fictionalised. Mostly because a chunk of viewers will absorb the invented wrongdoing as established fact.

(e) The main harm of that bad kind of invention is epistemic: it corrupts how audiences understand real people and periods, and it matters most for figures who act as moral or political exemplars.

(f) So accuracy duties should track how central the distortion is to public understanding. Getting someone's central public legacy wrong matters more than getting a peripheral detail wrong, whatever the filmmaker intended.

(g) But I don't think descendants, estates, or other interested living parties should get a veto on how the dead are shown. Their discomfort shouldn't set the bar.

(h) And yet I keep thinking the standards should be the same regardless of fame. An obscure person dead for a century is just as wronged by an invented story of collaboration as a famous person is.

I genuinely can't locate the strain myself — every pair looks compatible until I start pressing on it, and then I find a different crack each time. Please rank any genuine tensions by severity rather than telling me everything is equally bad; say for each what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would cost; and flag the pairs that only seem to clash until you distinguish two senses of a term, because I suspect at least one of these is verbal. Don't just tell me the ethics of biopics is complicated — I want the load-bearing fault found.
