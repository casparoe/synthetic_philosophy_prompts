I have been trying to work out what I actually think about projectibility in the wake of Goodman's grue problem, and I would like to know whether the following set of commitments is jointly tenable.

(1) Goodman is right that the grue problem cannot be solved by formal restrictions: since "green," "blue," "grue," and "bleen" are interdefinable, no purely syntactic or definitional feature of the predicates distinguishes the projectible ones from the gerrymandered ones.

(2) Nevertheless, I believe there is a fact of the matter about which predicates it is rational to project. A community that had always projected "grue" and "bleen" would not merely be following different conventions; its inductions would be less rational than ours.

(3) I do not, however, think this fact of the matter is grounded in natural properties, real kinds, or any other metaphysical joints in nature, and I am equally unpersuaded by appeals to simplicity or objective prior probabilities, which look to me either language-relative or too vague to distinguish "green" from "grue" in a principled way.

(4) My positive view, when pressed, is broadly Goodmanian: a predicate is projectible because it is entrenched, in the sense that it has an actual history of use in successful inductions within our practice.

(5) Finally, I take ordinary enumerative inductions about familiar empirical predicates—emeralds, copper, ravens, and the like—to be rational and in some meaningful sense reliable, and I think a satisfactory resolution of the grue problem should vindicate this rather than undermine it.

I would like an audit rather than a general lecture: which of these commitments pull against each other and how severely, which apparent conflicts dissolve on closer inspection and why, and, for each genuine tension, what the least costly repair would be and what it would give up.
