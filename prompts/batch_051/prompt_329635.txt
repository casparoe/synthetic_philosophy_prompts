I'm organizing a one-day adversarial collaboration between two readers of C. S. Peirce's 1877 essay The Fixation of Belief. The focus is the passage near the start of Part V where Peirce says that the method of science rests on a fundamental hypothesis: that there are real things whose characters are entirely independent of what we think about them, that those realities affect our senses according to regular laws, and that by taking advantage of the laws of perception any sufficiently experienced reasoner will be led to the one true conclusion.

Two camps have emerged. One reads the passage as an empirical bet about what inquiry will do: Peirce is claiming that the method of science tends to produce convergent belief under specified conditions, and that claim itself is fallible and revisable. The other reads it as a constitutive claim about what it is to talk about real objects at all: the hypothesis cannot be tested by ordinary scientific results, because any such test would already assume that there is a shared external world to which results are answerable.

Write the document this adversarial collaboration should produce. It should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can sign about what Peirce is doing in the passage; (2) a table where the two readings are stated in language each camp would accept for its own side; (3) the crux or cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence both camps agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance. Do not adjudicate between them; the point is to make the residual disagreement exact.
