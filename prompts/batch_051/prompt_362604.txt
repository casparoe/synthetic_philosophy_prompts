I cover science and ideas for a living, and I have been turning over a conflict that keeps reappearing in my reporting. On one side is a research program I find genuinely impressive: the structural-demographic, or secular-cycles, approach to macrohistory associated with Peter Turchin and his collaborators. As I understand it, the theory holds that large agrarian and early modern societies oscillate through recurrent phases driven by a small set of measurable pressures: population growth pressing against the productive capacity of the land, the overproduction of elites relative to the positions and livelihoods available to them, declining real incomes and living standards for ordinary people, and a state whose spending obligations expand while its revenues stagnate. When these pressures peak together, societies become statistically prone to political instability — popular revolt, intra-elite conflict, fiscal crisis, state breakdown — and slip into a disintegrative phase, after which the cycle resets. The appeal of this picture is exactly what I want from a science: many apparently distinct crises unified under a single causal mechanism, variables that can be compared across centuries and continents, and a procedure that at least claims to say when instability is more or less likely rather than merely explaining it after the fact.

On the other side is an intuition I cannot talk myself out of. When I think of the French Revolution, I do not think of a society crossing a threshold on a composite index. I think of a specific fiscal crisis, specific harvest failures, specific pamphlets, and particular ministers and assemblies making particular decisions in 1788 and 1789. The difference between reform, constitutional monarchy, terror, and military dictatorship seems to lie exactly in those particulars — in beliefs, personalities, accidents, and the meanings people attached to their situation. A model built from population, prices, and elite numbers would not have told you which of those futures France would take, and the choice among them is precisely what matters for actual human lives. I therefore feel as though the theory, however elegant, abstracts away the very thing history is about.

What I want from you is the strongest version of both positions, followed by a negotiated verdict. Does the theory actually contradict the intuition, or does it contradict only a philosophical gloss on the theory — the claim that structure explains everything? Is the intuition a product of narrative bias and hindsight, tracking details that are individually real but causally overrated? Should structure instead be understood as setting the conditions under which contingent choices operate, or is that a face-saving compromise that concedes everything to the intuition anyway? Tell me what would justify each move — debunking the intuition, amending the theory, abandoning the theory, or accepting a compatibility reading — and which move you would make.
