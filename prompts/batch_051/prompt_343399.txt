So I had this long, slightly heated argument with a friend last night and I still can't get it to sit right. She's vegan and normally pretty uncompromising, but even she admitted she wouldn't know what to say to, say, a goat herder in a place where the nearest market is a day away and the land will only really support grazing, not lentils or chickpeas. Yet she'll flat-out say that someone in a rich city who buys a factory-farmed ham sandwich on the way to work, when there's a perfectly fine chickpea thing in the same shop at the same price, is doing something wrong. I kept pushing her on whether those two judgments are consistent. The goat may have suffered just as much as the pig, maybe more, so the difference in judgment can't just be about how much harm is on the plate. So what is doing the work in treating the two eaters so differently?

The obvious candidate is some version of ought-implies-can or reasonable alternatives: the commuter could easily have chosen the plant option, while the herder couldn't, or not without a real sacrifice. But I'm not sure where that line is. It can't be that any passing preference counts as a sacrifice, because then the commuter could always say the chickpea wrap wasn't what she was in the mood for. And it can't be that only literal survival excuses, because then we'd have to excuse a dairy farmer in a rich country who insists there's no other way to keep the family afloat, and I suspect my friend would not want to excuse him, though maybe she should. Another possibility my friend floated is that the commuter is paying into an industrial system that produces misery at scale, while the herder is just living inside a subsistence economy he didn't design. But the goat presumably doesn't care whether its death was industrial or artisanal, so I'm not sure why that would make the moral difference for the eater. I also worry we might just be going easier on the herder because he's poor and far away and it feels ugly to moralize at him, which is a psychological explanation, not a justification.

So I want to know: is the asymmetry justified, and if so by what exactly, a line about available options, the cost of changing your life, something about participation in industrial systems, or something else? Give me the strongest version of the distinction you can find, test it against a few nearby cases like a well-off tourist in the herder's region or a broke person in the rich city who hates beans, and then say whether the asymmetry should be kept or whether one of the two judgments should be given up. And please just write it as continuous prose, like an email or a letter, with no headings, bullets, bold text, or tables.
