I direct the distributional-analysis unit at a state public utilities commission. My analysts prepare recommendations in rate cases, grid-hardening plans, and infrastructure siting decisions, and nearly every docket creates winners and losers: a reliability project may prevent medically serious blackouts for a few households while raising bills for everyone; a rooftop-solar compensation rule helps wealthier early adopters and costs renters; a wildfire-prevention shutdown protects lives but leaves low-income workers without pay. For years we have oscillated between monetizing everything — which quietly assumes that a dollar lost or gained has the same welfare significance for every household — and refusing to aggregate at all, which just dumps an impact table on the commissioners and lets the decisive comparison get made later, unstated and unreviewed.

Write me the decision procedure that settles this. It should translate what decision theory and social choice theory actually establish about interpersonal utility comparisons into a checklist my analysts can run in about two days per case: the distinction between comparing levels of welfare and comparing units of welfare, what a zero-one normalization of each person's utility scale does and does not license, when we need something like an extended-preference judgment — one person assessing another's situation — and what that move costs, and why Arrow-type results mean not every aggregation question has a defensible answer. The analysts have policy degrees but no philosophical training, so every step must be executable without the underlying theory, and the procedure must state what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where it stops with a formal finding that no comparison is warranted, and which steps are value judgments masquerading as method. Then run the procedure on the three docket items above, ensure at least one of the applications returns an answer my office will dislike — for instance, forbidding a comparison we now make routinely — and close with a candid statement of what the procedure cannot do.
