I teach a third-year undergrad subject called "Formal Systems and Their Discontents", and I'm setting the final. I want one essay question on Gödel's incompleteness theorems and how they get used outside mathematics—specifically the claim that they refute computationalism or show the mind can't be a machine. The question has to be one students can't ace by reciting the two theorems; it has to make them pick apart a specific bad argument. So I'd like the question to include a short invented passage where someone argues: Gödel showed that in any consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic there's a true sentence that system can't prove, and since mathematicians can see that the Gödel sentence is true, human mathematical reasoning can't be captured by any formal system or algorithm. Please write the exam question (with that passage), a model answer of around 700 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B. The marking scheme should name the specific moves: stating the theorem with its quantifiers and conditions (recursive axiomatizability, enough arithmetic, consistency), explaining why "true but unprovable" is relative to a system and not unprovable full stop, identifying the hidden premise in the anti-mechanist inference (that the mind is a sound formal system whose consistency we can know), and saying what extending a system to prove its own Gödel sentence does and doesn't show. The model answer should also be clear about what the theorems do license: no single formal system of that kind settles all arithmetical truth, and a sufficiently strong consistent system can't prove its own consistency, but they don't show there are truths no possible system can prove or that human reasoning has some non-algorithmic magic. One trap I want built into the passage: it should subtly conflate provable-in-the-system with provable simpliciter, because catching that is what I think seperates the top answers from the rest.
