I'm preparing a unit on social epistemology for a second-year undergraduate course of about 24 students, and I have one 80-minute session in which I want them to feel, rather than merely hear, the difference between an epistemic bubble and an echo chamber. The specific idea I want the activity to force on them is this: a bubble is fundamentally a matter of missing information, and it can often be fixed by adding information, whereas an echo chamber is fundamentally a matter of trust, and it is often hardened by adding information, because the new information arrives through sources that members have been trained in advance to dismiss. Please design a runnable classroom game or structured role-play, with clear rules, roles, scripts or packets where needed, and an 80-minute timeline. It should use a low-stakes fictional topic so students do not bring partisan baggage into it. The sequence should first let students form confident judgments on the basis of deliberately partial information; then give them the information that ought, by the activity's own lights, to unsettle those judgments; and then reveal that some groups remain unmoved — not because the correcting information is weak, but because an earlier stage gave them a standing reason to distrust its source. The distinction between not having heard the other side and having been taught not to listen should surface in the flow of the activity before either term is named.

Then write the debrief in detail. It should map concrete moments from the activity onto the two concepts — where information was merely absent, where trust was shaped, and why additional information repairs one but not the other. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (for example, students performing the distrust role may collapse it into belligerence, or the group may slide into debating the content of the fictional case instead of attending to the structure of who is believed and why), and how to handle each failure. Be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: real echo chambers are typically built slowly through identity, community, and repeated encounters with putatively corrupt outsiders, and the facilitator will have to supply that caveat. Finally, in the facilitator's notes, please separate what is reasonably settled about this distinction from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it — for instance, whether echo chambers require a deliberate manipulator, how cleanly the bubble/chamber distinction applies to real online environments, and whether the distinction describes two kinds of structures or two aspects of a single continuum. Do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
