I'm doing a module on legal philosophy and we've spent a week on desuetude — laws that sit on the books but are never enforced, never pleaded in court, and never repealed. A mate on the course reckons he's found a way to show that most of us who say we believe both in the rule of law and in the ordinary positivist picture (law is a matter of enactment, not morality) are commiting to an inconsistent pair. His argument, as he wrote it on the whiteboard:

(1) In any mature legal system there are valid statutes that are dead letter in exactly this way — say, some minor offence nobody remembers passing. It would be odd to insist these are not laws at all: they were enacted by the proper body and never repealed.

(2) If such a rule is law even though it never guides anyone's behaviour, then being a law cannot consist in actually guiding officials or citizens; it must consist in coming from the proper source.

(3) But then any rule from the proper source counts as law, no matter how obscure, contradictory, pointless, or impossible to obey it is.

(4) Now scale up. Imagine a legal system consisting only of these dead-letter rules: every one was enacted correctly, but none is enforced, applied, or relied on by anyone. By (2) and (3) this system is full of law. Yet it's a system in which no law actually governs anyone, which is exactly what the rule of law is supposed to rule out — the familiar idea that law should be public, clear, stable, prospective, and actually applied so people can plan their lives.

(5) So either we deny that the original dead-letter statute is a law — which means a rule loses legal status simply because officials stop enforcing it, a pretty radical conclusion — or we admit that the rule of law has nothing to do with what counts as law, in which case it isn't a real constraint on legal systems at all, just a compliment we pay to systems we like.

(6) Therefore the positivist who says they care about the rule of law has to surrender one of two things: the existence of valid-but-unenforced laws, or the idea that the rule of law sets a genuine standard for legal systems.

He says the argument is sound and the dilemma is inescapable; I'd like to hang on to both of the things he's making me choose between (dead-letter laws are laws, and the rule of law is a real standard rather than a slogan). So: which premise(s) or inference(s) do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
