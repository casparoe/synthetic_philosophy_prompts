I'm teaching a unit on what it is to be alive, and I'd like a philosophical reading of Mary Shelley's Frankenstein (the novel, not the film adaptations). The creature is an engineered borderline case: assembled from dead human and animal tissue and animated by a method the novel leaves scientifically vague, yet it eats, moves, learns language, reasons, suffers, and demands recognition as a fellow being. Work through which of the standard candidate criteria for life — metabolism and self-maintenance, bounded individuality, reproduction with variation and membership in an evolving lineage, and cognitive or informational criteria if you think they apply — the creature satisfies, fails, or leaves ambiguous, and consider whether the novel ends up committed to a different criterion (something like self-awareness, the capacity to suffer, or a residual vital principle) that the biological criteria miss. I also want an honest account of where the novel resists this reading: where the creature is treated as alive, or as subhuman, because of how it looks and where it came from rather than because of any criterion, and where the text stays inside early-nineteenth-century assumptions about animation. Please do not reduce the novel to an illustration of a checklist; say what it dramatizes about the concept of life that a definition alone doesn't capture. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers of biology (for instance, whether viruses count as alive, or whether life is a natural kind rather than a cluster concept), and don't present a contested definition as the consensus. Reply as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
