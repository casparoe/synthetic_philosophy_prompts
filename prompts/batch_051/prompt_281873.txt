A friend and I got stuck on a Schopenhauer argument that I can't shake. In The World as Will and Idea he says the will is the inner nature of everything, and that space and time are just forms of representation, so individuation—the separateness of things—is something the intellect imposes, not a feature of the will as thing-in-itself. My friend tightened this into a numbered argument:

(1) The will is the inner nature of each individual.
(2) The will as thing-in-itself is outside space, time, and causality, because those belong only to representation.
(3) There can be plurality only in space and time, so the will as thing-in-itself is not divided into distinct wills.
(4) Thus the very same single will is present, whole and undivided, in me and in the person next to me.
(5) If the same will is the inner nature of both of us, then when I harm another person I am harming the very same subject that I am.
(6) So there is no difference in kind between harming myself and harming another.

He then pointed out that Schopenhauer uses something like this to explain compassion: once someone sees through the principium individuationis, they recognize that the sufferer and the one who acts are the same will, and so they feel the other's pain as their own. But the argument seems to prove too much, because if (5) and (6) go through, then cruelty is also just self-harm, and compassion is no less self-regarding than selfishness. The distinction Schopenhauer wants between egoism and genuine compassion would collapse, since everything an agent does would be, at bottom, an act on the same single will.

I suspect something slides between (4) and (5) with the words same subject or the I doing the harming, but I can't make it precise. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost Schopenhauer's account of compassion? Or does the argument not actually prove too much?
