I've been going down a rabbit hole lately about pieces of territory that function like countries but aren't officially listed as countries: they run schools and courts, issue passports, sometimes they've been doing it for decades, but the UN or the major powers have never formally accepted them. The old position I keep circling back to is the idea that a country doesn't simply become one by controlling land and acting like a state; it needs other countries to acknowledge it, and until they do it's something less than a state. I can see why someone would say that: if nobody will sit down with your diplomats, there's a sense in which you aren't really in the international system at all.

But here's the wrinkle I can't shake. Acknowledgement these days isn't only a matter of an embassy and a vote in the General Assembly. Suppose a place like that gets plugged into the machinery in bits and pieces: its passports get accepted by airline systems because someone added the code to a database, it appears as a separate territory in the software that payment networks use, banks wire money there under its own name, a sports body lets its teams play, a phone or internet registry gives it its own prefix. None of these are other countries formally recognising it, but together they look a lot like the world treating it as one. So does the old argument survive that development, or was it relying on the assumption that recognition is something only states can do, and that it happens all at once? If it survives, what is the defence for saying the piecemeal stuff counts for nothing? If it doesn't, what should the argument be replaced with: recognition as a bundle that can be partially met, or something else entirely?

I'm not asking for the practical policy or who deserves it. I want to know what happens to the argument itself. Please commit to a definite answer rather than laying out both sides and leaving me to pick: which premise is under the most pressure, and is there a repaired version that still captures the original worry that being a state is somehow social rather than just factual? Also, could you write the answer in British English?
