So my friend has been pushing this line that the Dutch book argument proves way too much, and I cant find the flaw in it, but the conclusion is obviously insane. Here's the argument as he wrote it out:

(1) A set of credences is rationally permissible only if it can't be Dutch booked — meaning there's no collection of bets, each of which you'd take at the odds your own credences set, that together guarantee you a net loss no matter what happens.

(2) The Dutch Book Theorem says a credence function is Dutch-bookable iff it violates the probability axioms.

(3) The probability axioms require credence 1 in every tautology and credence 0 in every contradiction.

(4) But you don't have credence 1 in every tautology. Some logical truths are far too complicated for you to ever recognize as logical truths, and plenty you've never even entertained. Same goes for contradictions.

(5) So your credence function violates the probability axioms.

(6) So by (2), you're Dutch-bookable.

(7) So by (1), your actual credences are irrational. And since no finite reasoner can recognize every tautology, every finite reasoner is irrational.

(8) But a standard of rationality that no finite reasoner could possibly meet can't be what we mean by rationality.

He thinks the right move is to toss out the Dutch book argument for probabilism entirely. I'd rather keep the theorem — but I keep worrying that's just snobbery, and the real problem is somewhere earlier. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
