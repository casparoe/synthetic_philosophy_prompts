Right, here's the thing I keep going round in circles on. We're doing imaginative resistance in my aesthetics module, and I've spent the last week writing tiny made-up stories and just noting what my gut does. My seminar tutor keeps saying to test any principle against scruffy cases rather than the nice clean ones from the lectures, so here are mine. (All the one-sentence stories are invented, so no need for any actual texts.)

1. A literary story says, in a neutral authorial voice, that a mother who left her newborn in a freezing stairwell so she could keep her job 'did the right thing'. My reaction: I can imagine the act, her exhaustion, even her relief afterwards, but I can't make 'right' stick to it. I find myself rewriting the sentence as 'she did what she believed was right.' That's the clearest case of resistance I have.

2. A high-fantasy novel is set in a city where the kindest citizens throw one child a year into the volcano so the sun keeps burning. The narrator says the sacrifice is 'the most loving act a citizen can perform.' My reaction: same as before. The alien setting doesn't dissolve it; I can track their practice and understand how it hangs together, but the moment I'm asked to share the narrator's evaluation I'm out.

3. A crime novel is in third-person limited to a corrupt cop who has just killed an informant, and the prose goes: 'He wasn't wrong to kill him. It was the only clean way out.' My reaction: wierdly, I don't resist. I read both sentences as the cop's thought, not the book's verdict, and I can occupy that perspective while keeping my own judgement running on a seperate screen.

4. Same two sentences, same story, but this time the narrator says them on the book's behalf, not as the cop's thought. My reaction: full resistance. This is the pair that bothers me most: identical words, identical moral content, but I accept one and fight the other. So something about the source or framing is doing a lot of work.

5. A surreal story says: 'The tower collapsed east and west at the same time, and afterwards it was neither standing nor fallen.' My reaction: I notice the contradiction and can't picture it, but it doesn't feel like refusal. The image glitches and I move on. So my resistance isn't just to impossible things.

6. A comic novel of manners says a man wearing brown shoes with a dinner jacket has committed 'a serious moral wrong, of the same kind as lying to a business partner.' My reaction: I went along with it completely, even happily. No resistance at all. This is the case that keeps wrecking the obvious theories.

7. A first-person piece describes a woman slowly and calmly poisoning her sister out of envy; the narration never says she was right, it just shows her doing it and settling into a calm satisfaction afterwards. My reaction: no resistance. I can imagine being her, including the calm. So it's not that I can't imagine doing evil or taking satisfaction in it.

My current guess is something like: I resist when the fiction invites me to use my own evaluative capacities 'for real' inside the fiction, rather than just to represent a character using theirs. But that's hopelessly vague, and case 6 seems to contradict the bit about evaluative capacities (I happily let a daft norm go global there), and case 5 contradicts the bit about being unable to represent. I also thought maybe I resist only when the norm is one I actually care about, but that makes the whole thing look parochial, and I don't want to just define resistance as 'the stuff I don't like.'

So my question is: is there one principle that gets all seven of these, or all but one, and if one verdict has to go, which should it be and why? And what does the principle predict about these: a historical novel that treats slavery as good; a story that treats an act I find wrong as funny rather than as right; a near-future story saying 'the happiest people are the ones who've given up all loyalty to family'; and a fantasy world where the word 'murder' just means greeting someone? I'm not after a reading list, I want the principle stated sharply enough that it actually earns its keep.
