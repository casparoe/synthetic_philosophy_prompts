I keep seeing a parallel that I cant decide about. Popper's poverty of historicism argument says the course of human history is strongly shaped by the growth of knowledge, and since we can't predict future knowledge now, there can be no laws of history: any would-be law would have to include knowledge we don't yet have. In macroeconomics, the Lucas critique says that when policy changes, people's expectations and behavior change in response, so estimated relationships from past data won't stay stable under the new policy. Both seem to be saying that predictions about social systems are undermined because the thing being predicted includes agents who know things, or will know things, that alter their behavior in ways the predictor can't fully anticipate. But I'm not sure whether these are the same point or just superficially alike. Popper's version is about the open-ended growth of knowledge making historical prediction impossible in principle, while Lucas's seems more limited: it's about policy evaluation and rational expectations, not about the impossibility of any social science. Is there a deep structural identity here—are both instances of a reflexive limit on prediction—or does the analogy break down because Lucas leaves room for well-specified conditional forecasts while Popper rules out historical laws altogether?
