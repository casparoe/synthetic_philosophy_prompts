Take the knowledge argument in its standard Mary's-room form, and change one feature. As in the original, Mary has lived from birth in a black-and-white environment and has mastered every physical fact about human color vision. Unlike in the original, Mary has not been without color experiences: a device in the room directly induces in her the very experiences that normal perceivers have when they see red, green, blue, and so on, in the sense that the induced states are stipulated to be subjectively identical to ordinary color experiences. The device bypasses her eyes entirely, and Mary has never seen anything colored with her own eyes. She knows, as part of her exhaustive physical knowledge, which setting of the device produces the state ordinarily correlated with seeing red, and she has used the device often.

When Mary finally leaves the room and looks with her own eyes at a large uniformly red surface for the first time, does she acquire a new fact, a new ability, or neither? If she acquires something, state as precisely as you can what it is, given that she has already had the red experience and knows every physical fact about both her induced state and ordinary seeing. If she acquires nothing, explain what this implies for the unmodified knowledge argument: does the variant merely remove a premise the argument needs, or does it expose a conflation in the original argument between having an experience, acquiring an ability, and learning a fact? Then work through how the ability hypothesis, the acquaintance view, and the phenomenal-concept strategy would each handle this case, and say which of the three is most changed by the modification and which survives it best.
