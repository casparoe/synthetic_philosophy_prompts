I am a decision theorist and behavioural economist. My working vocabulary centres on preferences over streams of outcomes and states of the world: utility functions, discount factors and pure time preference, stationary and non-stationary preferences, infinite-horizon aggregation, satiation, habit formation, and the distinction between utility defined over experiences and utility defined over the satisfaction of aims or projects. I am at ease with formal models and with trading off different kinds of value; what I lack is patience for a philosophical argument that has not actually been translated into those terms.

I repeatedly encounter the claim, associated with Bernard Williams's discussion of the Makropulos case, that an indefinitely extended life would in the end become tedious and meaningless, so that immortality is not something a reflective person should want. As I understand it, the argument runs roughly as follows. A given human character can sustain only a finite stock of categorical desires, where a categorical desire is a desire that can give a person a reason to continue living, as opposed to a conditional desire that operates only once one is alive and intends to remain so. In an endless life, the categorical desires belonging to any fixed character would eventually be exhausted, and what would remain is repetition, boredom, and indifference. The value of continued life is therefore not a simple increasing function of time; at the limit, more life is not better and may be worse.

Please restate this argument in the vocabulary of decision theory and behavioural economics as faithfully as you can, and then report what does not survive the translation. Specifically:

1. Translate each move — the categorical/conditional distinction, the finitude of a character's motivating concerns, the alleged inevitability of repetition and boredom, and the final claim about the value of endless life — into the apparatus of preferences, utility, discounting, satiation, habits, and infinite horizons. Keep two readings apart throughout: one in which utility is experienced utility concerning the quality of mental states over time, and another in which utility concerns the satisfaction of aims, projects, or commitments. Do not slide between them.

2. Mark every place where the translation distorts. Which distinctions have no counterpart in the decision-theoretic vocabulary? Which claims get forced into a shape the original argument does not commit to, such as additively separable utility, constant discounting, stationarity, or the treatment of boredom as merely declining marginal utility? State what weaker or different formal assumption would be needed if the original claim is to be represented honestly.

3. Where a version of the argument becomes a theorem once certain assumptions are imposed, identify precisely which assumptions do the work and say whether the formalisation has smuggled them in or whether the original argument had in fact been relying on them all along.

4. Finish with a residue report: is what failed to translate a defect in the decision-theoretic framework, expressive resources it lacks, or a sign that the philosophical argument is less precise than it looks? Be even-handed, and do not assume in advance that the framework is adequate or that Williams's conclusion is correct.

The deliverable is the translation and the residue, not a defence or refutation of immortality. Please write the entire answer in British English.
