For the past few months I have been trying to decide whether to leave my job in climate technology for a role in pandemic preparedness, and what keeps stalling me is how little I can see of the downstream consequences of either choice. I have come to realize that the problem is a general one: a person can know the immediate effects of an action and still be almost completely ignorant of the long-run causal ripples, which is why I have been reading about consequentialist cluelessness. Here is the question I would like you to answer. Suppose that, in a world otherwise like ours, every person could consult, before any significant decision, a costless oracle that would accurately report all the causal consequences of each available option over the next two hundred years, described at the level of individual events, but would say nothing about how good or bad those consequences are. Give me your best reasoned prediction of how the actual practice of consequentialist moral deliberation would differ in that world from ours. I want the chain of reasoning made explicit: which changes follow robustly from removing cluelessness, which changes are guesses, and what would have to be true about human psychology and about the structure of the consequences themselves for your prediction to be wrong. Please answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write an email or a letter.
