In the policy debates I follow, advocates of strong transparency requirements lean on a particular analogy. Access to information, they argue, is like holding an option: the holder is never worse off for possessing it, because she is always free not to act on it. They give the analogy formal backing by appealing to Good's theorem — the result that a rational Bayesian decision-maker cannot be made worse off in expected utility by costless information, since ignoring the information is always an available strategy — and they use this to conclude that mandatory disclosure of institutional performance or risk data cannot harm a rational recipient. Anyone who resists such transparency must therefore be irrational, or hiding something. I want an audit of how far this analogy really goes.

Please give, first, a correspondence table: what maps to the option holder, the option itself, the act of exercising it, the strike price or premium, and the option's expiration? Then identify what the analogy quietly imports from options — for example, that a right can be left unexercised at no cost, or that acquiring an option leaves the holder otherwise unchanged — and specify where those mappings break. Give particular attention to cases in which information cannot simply be left unexercised: cases where it alters preferences, emotions, or self-conception; cases where it changes what other agents know about what one knows; cases where ignoring it is itself costly, impossible, or socially consequential; and cases where the conclusion is applied to groups rather than to a single Bayesian decision-maker. For each disanalogy, say whether it is decorative or decisive for the intended transparency conclusion. Then tell me whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the option analogy was carrying it the whole time, and what a better analogy would be if there is one.

End with exactly one line of the form \answer{...}, where ... is one of: the conclusion survives; it survives only with qualifications; the analogy fails its intended use.
