In my philosophy club we've been doing formal debates, and I've picked up a habit that's starting to annoy everyone. When someone lays out an argument, I'll happily grant every premise they want. But then when they say, so you have to accept the conclusion, I refuse and ask them to justify the move from exactly those premises to exactly that conclusion. If they say that's just modus ponens, or that if the premises are true the conclusion must be true, I say fine, I'll add that as a new premise. Then I ask what justifies the move from the original premises plus that new premise to the conclusion. And so on. I've watched people get genuinly angry because they say at some point you just have to accept the inference or no one could ever argue at all.

I'm not doing this to be a jerk—I honestly don't see why accepting the premises forces me to accept the conclusion unless there's permission to take the step, and every time someone gives me the permission as a sentence, I seem to need a second permission to use the sentence. Last week I did it in a debate about whether free will is compatible with determinism, and afterwards my friend said I was being exactly like the tortoise in the Lewis Carroll story, just refusing to be convinced by anything. That stung. So my question is whether my policy is defensible. Is there a level at which demanding a justification for an inference is rational, and a level at which it becomes a shield against ever having to update? And where should I draw the line—between premises and rules of inference, between rules and the practice of following rules, something else? Could the habit be restated so it keeps whatever is good about it without the infinite regress?
