I recently read a confident essay arguing that the universalizability tradition in ethics boils down to the question "what if everyone did that?" — and that the question, on inspection, does no real work. As I understood it, the author's summary ran roughly as follows. Kant's categorical imperative is best read as a formalized version of that question: one asks whether one could will one's maxim as a universal law, and the test draws its real force from the thought that if everyone lied, cheated, or broke promises, trust would collapse and the world would be worse. R. M. Hare and Marcus Singer appear as the modern heirs who keep the universalizability idea while stripping away Kant's metaphysical machinery — Hare by using universalization to check the consistency of one's prescriptions, Singer by converting the basic question into a rule-utilitarian generalization test. The author then argues that the whole family of views fails twice over: any action can be made to pass the test by redescribing its maxim narrowly enough ("I may break this promise to my uncle at 4 p.m. on a Tuesday in order to save an innocent person"), and whenever the test does seem to exclude something, it does so only by smuggling in consequentialist considerations about bad outcomes. So, he concludes, universalizability either excludes nothing or collapses into rule-utilitarianism, and "what if everyone did that?" is a rhetorical gesture rather than a genuine test of moral permissibility.

I found the argument suspiciously neat, but I could not locate the slide; I kept sensing that several different positions were being run together, yet each time I tried to state the difference it slipped away. I would therefore like an accuracy audit of the author's summary: which parts would a careful reader of Kant, R. M. Hare, and Marcus Singer respectively accept, where does the essay run distinct positions together, and does the final conclusion survive once the distinctions are restored? In particular, I want to know how seriously to take the maxim-redescription objection against each of the three views, and whether "covert appeal to consequences" is a fair description of what Kant's universalization procedure is actually doing.
