So here's a thing that keeps coming up in my reporting and I still cant tell wether it's a real distinction or just a feeling people have. Ask someone to put a probability on another person's open choice — the chance their sister takes the job in Seattle, the chance their boss quits by summer — and they'll happily give you a number, argue about base rates, update it over lunch. But ask them the matching question about themselves — the chance they'll take the job they're weighing right now, or quit their own job this year — and they flinch. It feels out of bounds. They say it's up to them, or that any number they give would either be a decision in disguise ('if I say 70, I'm half committing to it') or a prediction that doesn't bind them at all, which they find a bit creepy. Some go further and say there's no fact of the matter about an open choice until they settle it themselves. But that can't be right, because other people seem perfectly able to predict their open choice: I can put my producer at 60 percent to quit and she can put me at 50, and one of us can turn out correct without anybody being inconsistent. So what is the asymmetry tracking? Is it just that predicting yourself gets tangled up with causing, or is there something deeper about deliberation crowding prediction out? And what should I make of the case where the prediction comes from outside while the choice is still open for the person being predicted — a parole board assigning someone a recidivism risk score when that person hasn't yet decided whether to offend again. Is the board doing something to them that they can't coherently do for themselves, and does that make it objectionable or just a division of labor? Give me the strongest justification you can build for the self/other asymmetry, test it against the uncomfortable cases — just after I've decided but before I've told anyone, New Year's resolutions, predictions about my own choices years from now — and then tell me honestly whether I should keep the asymmetry or give it up.
