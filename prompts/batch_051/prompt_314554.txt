Lon Fuller, in The Morality of Law, develops his account through a parable about a king who fails at law-making in a number of ways. One of those failures is that the king makes rules but does not publish them; his subjects have to guess what is expected of them, and Fuller treats the non-publication as a defect internal to law, not merely a problem about fairness or convenience. This suggests that there is something special about legal rules being public, as opposed to rules generally being well-run. Yet in practice we seem not to apply that demand outside the state. A platform can change its content-moderation standards without publishing them; a credit bureau can score people using an unpublished model; a firm often governs its employees through internal policies they never see; and we rarely call these institutions lawless in the way we would call an unpublished penal code lawless. Some of them affect people more often than many statutes do. I want the strongest principle that both condemns the unpublished royal rules as lawless and permits the unpublished private or administrative rules as legitimate, or the strongest argument that the ordinary practice is simply inconsistent. Then give an honest verdict: should the publicity demand be extended, narrowed, or left as it is?
