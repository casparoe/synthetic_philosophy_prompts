i've been trying to pin down the difference between manipulation and legitimate persuasion, and I keep coming back to Iago in Othello. the standard story says manipulation is wrong because it either tricks the target or bypasses their rational capacities, while persuasion gives reasons and lets the person decide. but Iago makes that distinction weird. he rarely lies outright — mostly he insinuates, asks loaded questions, pretends to be reluctant to speak, and lets Othello draw the conclusion himself. Othello does reason his way to the murder, just from poisoned premises that Iago planted. so is Iago manipulating through deception, through bypassing Othello's rationality, or through something else entirely? i'd like a reading of the act 3 temptation scenes through the manipulation vs persuasion debate. where does the play fit the standard account, and where does it put pressure on it? does Othello count as making his own (terrible) decision, and does the play think that matters for wether he's responsible? and what would the play say to someone who thinks manipulation is just persuasion that works by non-rational means? i'm writing an essay on the ethics of influence and want the play to do more than be a stock example.
