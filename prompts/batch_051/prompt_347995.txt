Currently doing a module on archives and power (it's actually called something like Archives, Records and Silences) and in seminar last week somebody ended a discussion with 'history is written by the victors' in that tone where the point is supposed to be settled. The thing I'd asked was whether the perspective of enslaved people is recoverable from plantation records at all, or whether the records are so organised around the planters' categories that we can only ever see the enslaved through the planters' eyes. And this person said, well, history is written by the victors, so we can't. But I've been turning it over and I'm sure that sentence could be making a whole range of different claims: a boringly true one about whose documents happen to survive, a more interesting one about whose categories shape the archive, a much stronger one that reading against the grain just reconstructs the winners' version anyway, and maybe even the claim that all history-writing is just an exercise of power. Some of those are platitudes, some are probably false, and only one or two would actually support the conclusion he drew about the archive question. Could you seperate out the distinct precise claims the slogan might be making, from trivial to radical, flag which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false, and then say which reading was doing the actual work when it got used to shut down my question? I'd like it as a menu I can bring to my tutor rather than one settled interpretation.
