I'm not an academic; I've just been working my way through Daniel Dennett's description of the different kinds of minds, and I've been trying to apply it to large language models. The framework distinguishes four kinds of creatures by where their trial and error happens. A Darwinian creature lets natural selection do the trying: variations arise across generations, and the bad ones are eliminated by death or failure to reproduce. A Skinnerian creature can do some of that trying within its own lifetime, producing behaviors blindly and keeping the ones that get reinforced. A Popperian creature imports some of the testing into an internal model of the world, so a risky move can be tried out in the model and rejected before it has to be lived out. A Gregorian creature makes its environment smarter by using the mental tools inherited from culture — language above all — so that ideas can be tested and refined by other minds and across generations.

Now consider a large language model. It doesn't appear to work any of those four ways. During training, gradient descent adjusts a huge number of parameters on the basis of prediction error over a large corpus of human text; after training, the model simply runs forward and produces tokens. There is no natural selection over generations, no reinforcement of the model's own actions, no internal model being consulted before answering, and no culture it participates in — or at least not in any obvious way.

The question I want answered is whether this framework survives the existence of LLMs. Is gradient descent on a corpus a version of the same generate-and-test logic, just applied at the level of parameters rather than organisms, so that an LLM counts as a Darwinian creature in some extended sense? Or is it a genuinely new rung, and if so, what should that rung be called and what marks it off from the other four? Or is the better conclusion that LLMs are not creatures in the sense the framework is about, so the framework is not refuted but its limits become visible?

Please work through this carefully: say what the ladder is fundamentally a theory of, locate an LLM at each of the four rungs, identify which premise of the generate-and-test framework gradient descent puts the most pressure on and whether a weakened version of that premise still does useful work, and then give your verdict — does the classification hold up unchanged, hold up with a repair, or need to be abandoned or restricted?
