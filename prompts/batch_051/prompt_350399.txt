I've been turning over a question for a while now, and I'd like someone to think through it with me rather than settle it too quickly. The question is whether a human language could do without pronouns and the words that pick out people, places, and times only relative to the speaker's situation — words like I, you, here, now, this, tomorrow. A friend of mine insists it couldn't, and his favorite argument is an analogy. He says such a language would be like a map with no "you are here" marker. A map without that marker can still show every road, building, and river, but it cannot show the person reading it where she is in the picture, and so it is useless for getting anywhere. In the same way, he says, a language without those pointing words could describe the world in as much detail as one likes and still leave every speaker unable to locate herself, her audience, or her moment in what is being said — so it would be useless for actually doing things with words.

I want an audit of this analogy, not a verdict delivered by fiat. Lay out the correspondences explicitly: what is doing the work of the map, the territory, the marker, and the lost reader when we move over to language? Where does the mapping hold, and where does it begin to break? I suspect the analogy quietly imports a few assumptions: that the main job of such words is self-location; that a speaker has to locate herself in a described situation before she can speak or act; and that a language is a static representation that can be read off independently of the person using it. Are those imports fair? Then give me the judgment I actually care about: does my friend's conclusion — that a language without pronouns and their relatives would be impossible, or at least unusable for real communication — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or has the analogy been carrying the argument? If it has been, tell me what a better analogy would be, if you think there is one.
