For my graduate seminar on the philosophy of intellectual property, I want the class to begin from a specification rather than from the usual slogans. Let the target be an adequate theory of what it is to own an idea and why such ownership is justified at all — adequate for judging existing legal arrangements and proposed reforms in a digital, networked economy, not merely for winning a classroom exchange. Write the list of desiderata that any such theory must meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that earns it a place on the list: independent creation (two researchers arrive at the same proof in the same month — does each have a claim, and a claim against whom?), non-rivalrousness and extraction without deprivation (taking a digital text does not remove the original), the distinction between owning the token and owning the type, cumulative authorship (every new work incorporates the work of predecessors, so at what point does fresh labour generate a fresh right?), the eventual return of works to a common stock, collective and traditional knowledge, and the standing conflict between incentivizing creation and securing public access. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for any adequate theory and which are merely the preferences of a particular camp, such as the desert, personality, or incentive camps. Then tell me which pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and which single requirement, if treated as non-negotiable, does the most work to exclude the standard contenders. End with a brief scorecard of Locke's labour-desert view, Hegel's personality view, and a utilitarian incentive view against the list — brief, because the desiderata themselves, and the reasoning behind each, are the real deliverable.
