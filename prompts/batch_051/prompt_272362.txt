For a political philosophy seminar I have to articulate my own position, and I want a consistency check before I present it. I have ended up holding all of the following:

(1) There is no general moral obligation to obey the law as such. Whatever reasons we have to comply with particular laws are the ordinary content-based reasons we would have even if the state had not enacted them.

(2) In a broadly democratic and mostly just society, civil disobedience is often a positive civic contribution rather than a merely tolerated breach: it can correct democratic deficits that the normal political process systematically ignores.

(3) Civil disobedience, to count as such, must be public, nonviolent, and performed with a willingness to accept the legal penalty; these features are not merely conventional but are part of what makes the practice morally distinctive.

(4) Some covert, anonymity-preserving, punishment-evading illegal political acts — for example, leaking internal documents or covertly disrupting a harmful policy — are sometimes all-things-considered justified, and they are not best described as civil disobedience.

(5) A satisfactory account of justified political law-breaking should be able to draw a principled, non-stipulative distinction between justified civil disobedience and the justified covert acts in (4).

Do these commitments hang together? Please give me an audit rather than a lecture: identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say which commitment would have to give and what the least costly revision would involve.
