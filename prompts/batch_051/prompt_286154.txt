I'm putting together a syllabus for a philosophy of games and leisure course, and before I write the week on definitions I figured I should pin down what I actually think, because I have a tendency to hold all the positions at once and then get caught out in discussion. Here's the set, roughly in order of how strongly I'd defend them: (1) Play is autotelic — you do it for its own sake, and if you're playing chess to network or train your concentration you're still playing in some sense, but you're missing the thing that makes play play. (2) I accept a form of the magic circle: during play the ordinary stakes are bracketed, and what counts as a good move or fair play is set by the game's rules, not by its consequences outside the game. (3) Professional athletes are genuinely playing, not just doing work that happens to look playful, and kids are genuinely playing when they pretend the doll's parents are divorcing. (4) A big part of what makes play valuable — maybe the part I actually build the course around — is that it lets people rehearse identities, conflicts, and scary possibilities at lower stakes and then carry something back into ordinary life. (5) But I also don't think play should be defined by that function: rehearsal, restoration, skill-building accounts get the normativity wrong, because the reasons inside play aren't instrumental reasons, they're the game's own reasons. (6) Last, I'm attached to the idea that Huizinga's active, rule-bound, agonistic play and Pieper's receptive, contemplative leisure aren't rivals; they're two angles on the same thing, and any story about the origin of culture has to make room for both.

Does this lot actually hang together? My guess is some pairs are only superficially in tension and one or two are the real problem, but I can't locate them. Please audit it: go through the pairs, say which tensions are real and which dissolve on a better distinction, rank the real ones by severity, and tell me for each what I'd have to give up and what the cheapest fix costs. And when you seperate the settled from the contested, don't present a contested reading of Huizinga or Pieper as the consensus — flag where you're choosing one interpretation over another.
