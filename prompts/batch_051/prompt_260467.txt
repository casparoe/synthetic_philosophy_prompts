So over the holidays I made the mistake of bringing up the stimulus checks, and I ended up three hours deep with my brother-in-law over wether a government can actually create wealth by printing money. His side was the obvious one: printing money doesn't grow a single potato, it just changes the numbers on price tags, and if it did create wealth then Zimbabwe or Weimar would have been rich. I found myself pushing back with the stuff I'd been reading about how a currency-issuing government's budget isn't like a household budget, and how it can never literally run out of dollars, so the real limit is idle labor and machines, not money. By the end of the night I'd argued my way into the view that when the economy has slack, the printing isn't just nominal — it mobilizes real resources that were sitting there doing nothing, so in some sense we really do end up wealthier. But I cant shake the no-free-lunch feeling: the new money always lands on somebody's balance sheet, and a balance sheet entry is not a warehouse full of stuff.

I don't want to be told which side wins. I want the strongest version of both pushed as far as they can go. What exactly has to be true for "printing money creates wealth" to be substantially false rather than trivially true — something about full employment, or how fast people spend, or whether the new money buys real resources or just reprices existing claims? And what has to be true for it to be substantially true, not just an accounting identity? Is the whole argument actually a disagreement about the word "wealth," with one of us meaning financial claims and the other meaning real stuff, or does one of us have a genuine factual mistake buried in there? Tell me which piece should give way — the theory or the intuition — and what evidence would force each one.
