I keep a running list of objections to the gene's-eye view of evolution — the idea, mainly associated with Dawkins and G. C. Williams, that natural selection is most clearly understood as acting on genes, with organisms as the vehicles those replicators build. The objections I already know are below, so don't rephrase or recombine them:

- the multilevel-selection objection: selection also acts at the level of groups and species, so "the gene is the unit of selection" is at best half the story;
- the pluralist/bookkeeping objection: genes-vs-organisms is a choice of notation rather than a substantive empirical claim about the world;
- the replicator/interactor objection: the view mixes up what gets copied with where the causal work is done — selection acts on whole organisms (interactors), while genes are just replicators;
- the additivity objection: genes only have well-defined "effects of their own" under an additive model, and once you allow for epistasis and background dependence the accounting falls apart;
- the developmental-systems objection: genes are one resource among many in development, so singling them out as the privileged unit is arbitrary;
- the extended-phenotype objection: once a beaver dam counts as part of a gene's phenotype, "phenotype" has been stretched until it no longer cuts nature at a real joint.

I want an objection that isn't on this list and isn't a rewording, special case, or combination of any of them — ideally one that takes aim at the view's explanatory ambitions rather than re-fighting the units-of-selection debate, though I'll take anything genuinely new. After you propose it, tell me honestly wether it's any good and how it relates to the six I've got.
