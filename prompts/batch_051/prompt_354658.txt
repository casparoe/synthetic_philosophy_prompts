After a career in seismology, I have recently taken up philosophy, and for the past several months I have been working through Peirce's essays on inquiry with a small reading group. One member of the group keeps compressing Peirce's position into the slogan "truth is what works." When pressed, he uses the phrase to do several jobs at once: to explain why the scientific method is superior to tenacity or authority, to deny that there is any meaningful question about a belief's correspondence to reality beyond its practical upshot, and to deflect the objection that a belief could be useful yet false. I suspect these different argumentative jobs trade on different readings of that little sentence, and that some readings are fairly harmless while others are false or beg the very question he is trying to settle.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that "truth is what works" might be expressing, distinguishing in particular what "works" could mean (predictive success, satisfaction of desire, survival or biological usefulness, convergence of inquiry in the long run, something else) and whose purposes or timescale are relevant? For each reading, I would like a verdict on whether it is platitudinous, substantive but defensible, or false, and whether it would actually do the work he was assigning to it in our discussion. I would prefer the readings presented as a menu rather than an argument for a single interpretation, with a clear note on which reading he needed for each of his three moves. Finally, since I am still training my philosophical instincts, I would value an epistemically cautious answer: please indicate, for the main verdicts you reach, what consideration or argument would lead you to revise them, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
