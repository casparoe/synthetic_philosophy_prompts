I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am working on a piece about how to explain to general readers where science's authority comes from. A source — a historian of science with a polemical streak — pressed a version of Feyerabend's anything-goes line on me, and it has been bothering me because, while I find the conclusion absurd, I cannot locate the faulty step. As best I can reconstruct it, the argument runs as follows.

(1) If science has a method, that method must be a set of rules that separates genuinely scientific work from non-science and explains why the former deserves more trust.
(2) Every candidate rule that has actually been proposed — falsifiability, testability, controlled experiment, peer review, reproducibility — has been violated by at least one episode that working scientists now count as good science, or has been satisfied by at least one episode they now count as pseudoscience.
(3) So no proposed rule is either necessary or sufficient for a practice's being scientific.
(4) Therefore science has no method.
(5) If science has no method, then science possesses no special kind of reliability that non-scientific practices lack.
(6) If science possesses no special reliability of that sort, then preferring, for example, epidemiology to astrology is ultimately a matter of institutional power or convention rather than reason.
(7) But the preference for epidemiology over astrology is not merely a matter of power or convention.

I would give up almost any premise before I give up (7), but I cannot tell whether the trouble is a false premise or an invalid inference. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I would also like to know why that step looks so tempting, and what a defender of the legitimate core of Feyerabend's point must hold onto once the overreach is removed. I need the diagnosis precise enough that I can present it fairly in the piece.
