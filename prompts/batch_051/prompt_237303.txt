Last Sunday two of my friends, Nina and Ray, got into a long fight over a decision our friend Sam has to make, and I want someone to break the whole thing down.

Sam promised a friend he'd spend Saturday helping him move. Then he got asked to spend that same day helping at a free clinic that's short-staffed and may have to turn people away if he doesn't show. Sam genuinely doesn't know whether keeping a promise is the sort of thing you just aren't allowed to break, or whether promises are just one factor among others and you should usually do what helps more. He thinks there's a real chance promises are absolute and a real chance they aren't.

Nina said he should keep the promise. Her reasoning was: if promises are absolute and he breaks one, he's done something wrong in a way that can't be undone. If he keeps it and promises turn out to be just one factor, then the worst is he didn't help as many people as he could've—bad, but not the same kind of bad as breaking a rule that might be absolute. Since one mistake is way worse than the other, the cautious move under uncertainty is to keep the promise.

Ray said that's backwards. He said Nina is using the absolute view's own scale to measure how bad the mistakes are. If promises are just one factor, there's no special category of "wrong in a way that can't be undone"—the only thing that matters is whether people get helped. On that view, ditching the clinic to carry boxes is the bigger moral failure, because people actually get turned away. So the so-called asymmetry is just one side's view pretending to be neutral.

Nina shot back that Ray was deliberately flattening things. She said if the "just one factor" view is true, choosing to help fewer people is still less serious than knowingly doing something that, on another live view, is forbidden. There's a real difference between picking something that turns out suboptimal and picking something that turns out prohibited. Even if you haven't figured out which theory is true, you can care more about the mistake that can't be fixed in a moral sense.

Ray said that's circular. "Forbidden," "prohibited," "wrong in a special way"—those are all terms the absolute view supplies. If you don't assume that view, a wrong act is just an act that led to less good. There's no neutral unit of moral badness both sides can read off the same ruler, so any rule that says one risk is worse than the other quietly takes a side. He said under real uncertainty the best you can do is decide which view is more likely and act on it, because every "safe" rule is itself a moral bet.

Nina said that doesn't help Sam, since he's genuinely torn, and "go with the more likely view" is empty until you've already decided how to weigh the two kinds of wrong. And if the two kinds of wrong can't be compared, then the only sane policy is to be cautious about the one that looks irreversible.

They went in circles for a while after that and never settled it. I was just listening and I honestly can't tell which of them is closer. Can you go through their argument step by step and adjudicate it: which specific points hold, which don't, what both of them are missing, and who's more right overall about how Sam should think about this kind of uncertainty?
