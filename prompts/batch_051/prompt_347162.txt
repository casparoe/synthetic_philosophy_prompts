You are grading an upper-division epistemology exam. The question you set was:

"The 'epistemic backstop' thesis says that some body of evidence can serve as a regress-stopper: a point at which inquiry may end without the stopping point itself needing further justification. Explain how photographic and video evidence has been cast in this role, and assess exactly what the availability of deepfakes does—and does not—show about that backstop."

One student wrote:

"The backstop thesis holds that recordings are not just more testimony. A photograph or video is caused mechanically by the scene it depicts, so it does not pass through a narrator's beliefs and biases the way an eyewitness report does. That is why a bystander's video can settle disputes that conflicting witness reports cannot: it is a place where inquiry can stop. Deepfakes destroy this. If a video can be produced without the scene it appears to depict, then the viewer can no longer take the causal link between recording and world for granted. So once deepfakes are widespread, no video can serve as a backstop, because any video might be fake and the viewer cannot tell from the video alone. And since any method we might use to rule out a fake—provenance records, forensic analysis, corroborating footage—is itself the kind of record that could be forged, there is no way to re-establish a stopping point. The backstop role played by recordings is therefore not merely damaged but removed, and video evidence should be treated as just one more fallible report among others."

Grade this answer out of 10, then justify the grade with point-by-point comments: identify what the answer gets right, where it goes wrong, and whether each error is conceptual or a matter of overstatement. Make the comments specific enough that the student could revise from them rather than from a general impression.
