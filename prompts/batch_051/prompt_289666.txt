Consider an asymmetry in ordinary moral practice. When an acquaintance acts cruelly or dishonestly in a single episode, we readily explain the conduct in terms of their character: they are cruel, or they are a liar. When we ourselves act in the same way, however, we reach for the situation: we were exhausted, cornered, under pressure, or following orders. Virtue ethics seems to depend on the first sort of explanation being frequently correct, since it explains morally excellent conduct partly by appeal to settled traits of character. The situationist critique of virtue ethics maintains that such robust traits are rarer than ordinary moral vocabulary assumes and that much conduct is better explained by situational pressures; the trait attributions this asymmetry protects are therefore the very attributions the critique targets. If the situationists are even partly right, the asymmetry comes under pressure: we may be attributing to others stable traits that few people possess and that we would not attribute to ourselves. Is there any principled justification for this asymmetry? For instance, could first-person access to our own motives and constraints justify treating our own case differently, or is the asymmetry best understood as a self-serving bias that an ethics of virtue ought to correct rather than ratify? Please defend your judgement against the nearby case in which the observer knows as much about the other person's situation and history as the person concerned does. Please answer in British English.
