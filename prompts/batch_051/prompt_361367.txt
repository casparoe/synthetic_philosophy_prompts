I'm a novelist, and I'm drafting something built around a Hari Seldon type, so lately I've been going back and forth between Asimov's Foundation and Popper's The Poverty of Historicism. I want a reading of Foundation through Popper's critique of historicism — but the kind of reading that would actually help me plot a novel, not a paragraph that just says Asimov illustrates Popper.

Rough version of the philosophical side as I understand it: Popper argues there can't be scientific laws of history, because the future course of history depends on the growth of knowledge, and we can't predict the future growth of our own knowledge. Asimov's psychohistory looks like it walks straight into that problem and then tries to build a door around it: it predicts the Empire will fall and a dark age will follow, and Seldon sets up the Foundation to shorten that dark age. So first question: does Asimov just dodge Popper, or does he actually answer him? It seems load-bearing that psychohistory only works on huge populations, averages out individual choices, and only holds while the mass of people don't know its predictions — which sounds suspiciously like the point that a prediction, once it's known, changes the thing predicted. Is that Asimov anticipating the objection, or is it a fudge? Then there's the Mule, the individual mutant who breaks the whole statistical model. I want your view on what the Mule is doing philosophically: is he Popper's unpredictable individual, or is he the book quietly admitting that the thing psychohistory says doesn't matter is the thing that keeps ruining predictions?

I also want the reading to notice where the novel resists it. The stories mostly treat psychohistory as a working science and the premise gets vindicated over and over; the idea of the Seldon Plan does a lot of metaphysical heavy lifting and the book generally wants us to trust it. I don't want a reading that flattens that away. Tell me where Asimov is being a genuine historicist and where he's being sneakily anti-historicist, and wether his middle position — a science that predicts large-scale social structure while leaving individual action open — is actually coherent, or just good plotting. Engage the actual beats: the setup of psychohistory, the secrecy around the predictions, the Seldon Crises, the Mule, the Second Foundation's work keeping the plan on track.

No bibliography and no verbatim quotes, please — I couldn't check them and you'd have to guess. I want the reading to stand on the plot points themselves. End by saying what Foundation as a piece of fiction adds to the philosophy that Popper's argument alone doesn't, or what it gets wrong that a novelist should be careful not to smuggle in.

Since I'm going to lean on this for a novel, tell me how confident you are in the reading overall, and name the single point you're least sure about — the move you could see a Popper specialist pushing back on hardest.
