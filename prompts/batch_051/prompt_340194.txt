I am a PhD student in human-computer interaction, and I am embedded in a public defender's office that is preparing to deploy machine-learning scores to decide which eviction-defense cases it accepts and how much time each case gets. My job is to produce a reusable review procedure — one I will run alongside the attorneys — that any attorney or caseworker in the office, none of whom has a background in machine learning, can run on a proposed model before it touches a real client's file. Reviews will happen every few months, will usually have to be completed in a single working day, and will rarely have access to the model's internals, the training code, or a clean external audit dataset. The office's stated standard is that a model must be understandable rather than merely correct: high average accuracy is not enough if the person relying on the model cannot tell where it is right and where it is wrong.

The principle I want the procedure to operationalize is this. Understanding a model is not the same as receiving an explanation for a particular output. A model is legible for a given user to the extent that the user can form accurate expectations about the model's failures in advance and can recognize, in time, when a specific output should not be followed. What the user needs is not the model's mechanism but its boundary: the conditions under which relying on the output would be a mistake. A long post-hoc explanation can fail to provide this, and a short, honest statement of documented failure modes can succeed at it. The procedure should therefore test the model-and-user pair, not the explanation alone.

Please write the procedure as an ordered checklist with decision points, and include:
(i) the questions to ask in order;
(ii) for each question, what counts as passing, what counts as failing, and what evidence the reviewer is allowed to use;
(iii) where the procedure stops with a no, where it permits a conditional yes, and what conditions attach;
(iv) which steps are genuine judgments rather than mechanical checks, and who in the office owns them — the intake worker, the supervising attorney, or the model steward.

Then run the procedure on the following two systems currently under consideration.

Case A is a gradient-boosted model that predicts, for each incoming eviction case, whether the tenant has a realistic chance of retaining possession if the office takes the case. In back-testing it is substantially more accurate than the office's current intake heuristic. The only legibility materials available are a list of the ten most important features and a short natural-language sentence generated for each prediction. The feature ranking shifts across neighborhoods in ways no one in the office can explain, and the per-prediction sentences are generic. The vendor states that the model is fair because protected attributes were not used as features.

Case B is a points-based scoring sheet, hand-coded by the office's own attorneys, that predicts the same thing with lower measured accuracy. The inputs are entered by the intake worker, every scoring rule is stated on the form, and the office has documented the two fact patterns in which the score is known to be misleading. The office is reluctant to use it because it is measurably worse on average.

Do not adjust the procedure so that the higher-accuracy system passes; if the principle says a system is not legible enough, say so and explain what would have to change. Close by telling me what the procedure cannot settle: the kinds of cases where it will still leave the office without a decision, and the least-bad default for those cases.
