I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've been asked to give a five-minute toast at the retirement dinner for my old writing teacher, Elena. For thirty years she taught fiction workshops, and her whole method was basically wu-wei: she'd say that the more you strain after a great sentence, the more it clams up on you, and that the best revision feels like an accident. I've been trying to write this thing for a week and its not coming, which I guess proves her point. I want the toast to be warm and personal, but I also want it to have some philosophical spine, because about a third of the room will be her old colleagues from the philosophy department and they will know the Chinese texts. The rest will be family and writers, so it can't turn into a seminar. I'd like to hang it on a passage from Zhuangzi, the "Da Sheng" or "Full Understanding of Life" chapter, in the old James Legge translation. This is the part I keep returning to:

"Take the case of a drunken man falling from his carriage - though he may suffer injury, he will not die. His bones and joints are the same as those of other men, but the injury which he receives is different: his spirit is entire. He knew nothing about his getting into the carriage, and knew nothing about his falling from it. The thought of death or life, or of any alarm or affright, does not enter his breast; and therefore he encounters danger without any shrinking from it. Completely under the influence of the liquor he has drunk, it is thus with him - how much more would it be so, if he were under the influence of his Heavenly constitution!"

You are a scholar of classical Chinese philosophy and an experienced speechwriter. Start by brainstorming what the passage is really doing—especially the difference between the drunkard's unthinking luck and the "Heavenly constitution" the warden Yin says the sage possesses—then write me the toast. It should use the passage as more than a decorative quote: it should shape what I can and cannot say about Elena's teaching. I don't want to pretend she was a sage, and I don't want to suggest that just not caring is the secret to writing or to anything else. After the speech, give me a short note on where your interpretation of the passage, or the way you've used it in the toast, is most likely to be wrong or one-sided, and what consideration would most likely change it.
