I've been toying with something I call the "parsimony budget" model of ethical theories. The idea is that instead of treating simplicity and fit with intuitions as two separate theoretical virtues, we treat them as two entries in a single cost function. Every fundamental posit in a theory—a principle, a right, a kind of value, a veil-of-ignorance clause, whatever—costs one parsimony unit. Every clear, widely shared intuition the theory can't accommodate also costs one fit unit. We then compare theories by total cost. The punchline is that simplicity stops looking like a "virtue" and starts looking like a budget: a theory with two principles and eight violated intuitions is on a par with a theory with ten principles and zero violated intuitions, at least until we've got reason to weight the units differently. I know this resembles reflective equilibrium and maybe Scanlon's method, and it also sounds a bit like formal epistemology's tradeoffs between simplicity and empirical adequacy. But I think the budget framing changes something: it forces you to state, upfront, how many intuitions a single posit is worth, and it suggests that being "simple but counterintuitive" is just a way of spending your parsimony savings on fit violations, not a separate theoretical achievement. My question is whether this is a real model or just a metaphor dressed up with numbers. Where does it break? Specifically, what happens when the posits and the intuitions aren't independent—when the same simplifying move both reduces the number of principles and causes the violations, so the two costs are double-counting or cancelling? And what would a defender of simplicity as an intrinsic virtue say is lost by turning it into a budget? Be candid.
