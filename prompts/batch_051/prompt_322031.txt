I've been developing a distinction I want pressure-tested before I build an essay around it. I call it "normative carrying capacity." The starting observation is that every society imposes a total prescriptive load on its members — traffic laws, workplace protocols, tax forms, etiquette, platform permissions, informal norms about phrasing and timing — and that this load is paid for in a fixed currency: attention, memory, self-monitoring, and the effort of making compliance visible to whoever might be watching. My claim is that a society has a finite capacity to absorb that load, determined by three things I think are doing the real work: how badly the rules conflict with one another, how predictable enforcement is, and how much of compliance is inherently observable, such that doing the thing and being seen to do the thing are the same act. When total demand exceeds capacity, you don't simply get noncompliance; you get compliance theater — performative rule-following that diverts effort from the substance of the rule toward its appearance. And once theater is the equilibrium, I want to say, two things predictably follow: enforcement goes selective, because no enforcer can process the full rule stock and so they triage by whom they happen to be watching; and norm entrepreneurs gain a market, promising to clear the clutter by cutting rules — with the cuts landing, in practice, on the rules that protect whoever cannot make their compliance visible.

My normative upshot is that the standing question in the tight–loose literature — how strictly should a society enforce a given stock of rules? — is the wrong first question. Strictness is what enforcement does; carrying capacity is what the rule stock itself demands. The same strictness can be cheap in a coherent, low-load society and brutal in a high-load, contradictory one, so "how much rule-following should a society demand?" ought to be constrained by a sustainability condition before it is answered by a coordination calculation. I know the distinction overlaps with the tight–loose line of research and probably with work on administrative burden, and I'm aware "carrying capacity" sounds like an ecological metaphor dressed up as a concept. Before I invest further, I'd like an honest assessment. Is the concept doing anything that tightness-plus-rule-volume doesn't already do, or is the load–strictness interaction a genuine addition? Where does it break? Give me the strongest counterexample you can — real or constructed — of a society that ran far above what my three factors would predict as carrying capacity, or far below it while still producing compliance theater. Finally, tell me whether the normative jump is licensed at all, or whether I'm smuggling in a limit-to-state-demand conclusion under the cover of an empirical claim about overload.
