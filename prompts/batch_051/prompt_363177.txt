Wolfram's principle of computational equivalence, from A New Kind of Science, says roughly that almost any process that isn't obviously simple is already as computationally sophisticated as it could be: once you're past a low threshold, you're doing universal computation. He presents it as an empirical claim about nature, not a theorem, and uses it to argue that things like intelligence or complexity don't admit of higher degrees. I'm not sure it's really testable rather than a definitional choice about what counts as sophisticated. So: what is the strongest experiment or observation one could actually run to test PCE? Design it concretely — what kind of systems you'd sample, what measurement or proof task you'd run, what result would push toward the principle being true, and what result would count against it. Then mark exactly where the test gives out: which parts of the claim are genuinely empirical, and which parts are untouched by any observation because theyre decisions about words or about what to bother grading. Is a clean counterexample even possible in principle?
