A clinical ethics training program proposes to grade students on moral worth rather than outcomes. Before taking any action that might later be reviewed, a student must write down the feature of the situation that they take to make the action right. Reviewers compare the stated feature against an official list of right-making features, revised annually by senior clinicians. If the action turns out well but the stated feature is not on the list, it is recorded as correct-but-worthless; if the action turns out badly but the stated feature is on the list, it is recorded as well-motivated-but-misapplied. Analyze what this scheme does to which actions students choose, to how they describe their own reasons, to what the official list becomes, and to the gap between being certified as morally worthy and actually acting for the right reasons. Where does it get gamed first, and can its failure modes be fixed without turning the assessment back into an outcome audit?
