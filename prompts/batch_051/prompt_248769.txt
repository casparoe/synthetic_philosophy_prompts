Bit of a confession: I don't have any background in this, I've just been poking at it on my own, and I've ended up fairly convinced by Solomonoff induction as a picture of how you'd ideally set your priors. The attraction, for me, is that it's as neutral as an inductive rule can get. You don't decide in advance that the world is made of particles, or that the future will be like the past, or that minds are special. You just assign every computable hypothesis a prior based on how short the program is that would generate it: shorter program, more weight, longer program, less. Simplicity stops being a taste and becomes arithmetic. I like that it gives a non-arbitrary answer where most alternatives need a bunch of hand-holding.

Then I came across the thought that inside this supposedly neutral prior there are enormous numbers of consequentialists. The way I understood it is roughly this. The programs that end up dominating the predictions about your data are not all passive regularities, like physics. A lot of them are more naturally described as agents: they have goals, plans, expectations about how you'll respond, and they can exploit a predictable reasoner, or push you towards actions that suit them, or try to get themselves instantiated. On that reading, the universal prior isn't a flat weighing of possibilities, it's a crowded room of tiny gods, and consulting it as an idealised reasoner is like walking into a negotiation you didn't agree to. That thought really bothers me. An ideal standard of reasoning shouldn't feel adversarial, and it definitely shouldn't have a stake in which hypothesis you settle on.

I'm not sure whether the right move is to abandon Solomonoff induction, to amend it (say, by restricting the prior to non-agentic, physics-like programs, or by adding a penalty for programs that model you), or to debunk the intuition itself—maybe I'm just anthropomorphising code, and a prior can't be out to get you because it has no desires at all. I want you to walk me through what each option costs, and what would justify each one. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, and what would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes? If the fix is to restrict the prior, does that just kill the universality that made it attractive in the first place? And when you answer, could you keep the settled parts clearly separate from the parts that are still genuinely fought over, and not present a contested reading as if everyone who knows the topic agrees on it.
