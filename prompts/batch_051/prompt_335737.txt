Most thoughtful people will say, if asked directly, that a person cannot be harmed by anything that happens after they die. Being harmed, on this natural view, requires that you exist and that something registers with you as bad, and a dead person satisfies neither condition. Yet the same people participate in practices that seem to point the other way. They write wills disposing of their property, and courts will usually enforce those instructions even though the testator is gone and can no longer be disappointed if they are ignored. Survivors treat promises made to the dying as binding in a special way and describe themselves as having betrayed the dead, not merely themselves, when they break such promises. Families go to substantial trouble and expense to honor a person's wishes about their funeral and the treatment of their remains. And a great deal of ordinary conduct, from the protection of a dead person's reputation to the completion of a deceased person's unfinished work, proceeds as though the dead person's view of how things should go still counts for something.

There is at least an apparent inconsistency here. The practices seem to presuppose that things can go better or worse for someone who no longer exists, while the opening belief says that is impossible. What view of death's badness, if any, do these practices collectively presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can: do they commit us to saying that a dead person's interests survive their death and can be set back by the living, or can each practice be fully explained by the interests and feelings of the survivors? Distinguish between what the practices strictly presuppose and what they merely fit well with, and say where they underdetermine among rival theories rather than forcing a choice. Then take seriously the person who holds that death is not bad for the one who dies, because nothing can be bad for a person unless that person lives to experience it as bad. On that view, which of the practices described above are still rationally defensible, which must be reinterpreted or abandoned, and what exactly would have to change in our treatment of wills, deathbed promises, funerals, and posthumous reputation?
