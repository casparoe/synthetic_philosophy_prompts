I'm a novelist, and I keep banging my head against a scene I can't resolve, and I think it maps onto something I'd like your actual view on. The protagonist is a cellist who was luminous as a child because she didn't know she was working—she just played. Now she's in her twenties, technically superb and self-conscious about all of it, and every performance comes out a little forced. The advice everybody in the book gives her is that she's trying too hard, that she needs to stop controlling the arc of every phrase and let the piece play itself: wu-wei, basically, though they never use the word. But here's the thing. As a description, I'm fairly convinced: aiming at spontaneity kills spontaneity, and the more she tries to play the way she played at nine, the further she gets from it. Trying not to try just seems to be more trying. Yet I also have a stubborn intuition that effort is the only lever a person actually has. Tell someone 'stop trying,' and they either do nothing or they try to stop trying. Any advice that means anything seems to smuggle effort back in under another name—'notice the tension in your shoulder and release it,' 'focus on the sound, not yourself'—which just gives her new things to try, and a new way to fail at them.

So I want to know which one should give. Give me the strongest version of the theory—that there is a real, trainable mode of unforced, unselfconscious action, and that instruction in non-doing is coherent despite the paradox—and the strongest version of the intuition—that you cannot intend your way into unintentional action, and any advice that cant be operationalized as an effortful instruction is empty. Then tell me what evidence or argument would make the intuition the thing I abandon, what would make the theory the thing I abandon, and whether there's a reading on which they're actually compatible. And don't leave it abstract: under each resolution, what should the cellist do differently in the next chapter, concretely?
