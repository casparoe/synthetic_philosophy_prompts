In the self-deception reading group I'm in, we've been stuck in the same loop all year. One camp says self-deception as described is flatly impossible: to be the deceiver you have to know not-P, to be the deceived you have to believe P, and a single mind can't occupy both roles at once without secretly splitting into two agents. The other camp says that's a philosopher's hang-up: all you need is a person whose desire for a certain conclusion biases how they process the evidence, and they end up with a belief the evidence doesn't license — no paradox, no hidden knowledge, just motivated cognition. We all agree about the examples; the man misreading the texts in front of him, the failing student, the gambler on a hot streak. We just can't agree about what the examples are examples of. I keep wondering whether this is an empirical question about how minds work, a verbal fight over the word "deception," or two groups answering different questions under one name. I'd rather have a diagnosis of the stall itself than another summary of the two views, and an honest answer about whether anything — an experiment, a distinction, a change of question — could actually break it.
