i've been chewing on an idea about state apologies and i want you to actually stress-test it rather than just tell me it's interesting. i've been calling it the two we's. when a government apologizes for something the state did in, say, the 1880s, the 'we' in 'we are sorry' has to be doing one of two jobs. first, it could mean the same continuing agent that did the wrong, the way i can apologize at forty for what i did at nineteen, scaled up to an institution; on that reading the apology is really a confession, and today's citizens get folded into a single temporally stretched perpetrator, which makes the wrong ongoing rather than over. second, it could just mean the present population of the state, in which case nobody speaking actually did the thing, and the apology is third-party regret wearing a first-person costume. so my claim, if i had to write it down, is that a state apology is either the corporate confession of a continuing agent or it's not really an apology at all, just a statement of regret by bystanders with the right passport. i realize this assumes the state has to be a full-blown person for the first reading to work, and i know people in collective responsibility debates talk about group liability without group personhood, so maybe my fork is a false one and a state apology is some third kind of speech act that isnt either. is the distinction real or just a restatement of the obvious? has someone made this point better and under what name? and where does it break, what kind of wrong or apology does it force into the wrong box? and when you write back please just use plain paragraphs, no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, the way youd write an email.
