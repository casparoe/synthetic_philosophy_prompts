So in AP Environmental Science we just did a unit on Malthus, and ever since I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a word for, and its sort of driving me crazy. Malthus's argument was that population grows exponentially while food grows linearly, so eventually there has to be a crash. But here we are like two centuries later and technology kept boosting food production, so the crash keeps not happening, and the usual response isn't "Malthus was wrong" but "the logic is still valid, it just got delayed." Then I started noticing the same move everywhere: the people who warned about peak oil decades ago now say the original prediction was right but fracking and new discoveries postponed it; the people who said automation would create huge permanent unemployment by now say it's still coming, just slower. Each time the thing fails to happen on time, the theory absorbs the delay and somehow the failure counts as evidence for the theory.

I know about moving the goalposts, but that seems slightly off, becuase here nobody is changing the standard of success after the fact—they're changing an assumption about background conditions, and the original logic really is sound as far as it goes. Is there an established name for this specific configuration, where a theory can always be "right but early" and no deadline could ever clearly refute it? What would actually count as evidence against it, and at what point does it stop being a normal scientific prediction that's merely early and start being unfalsifiable? Also how is this different from a regular auxiliary hypothesis, which I think is supposed to be an okay part of science? I'm not looking for just a label; I want to know how to carve this apart from a prediction that's legitimately early and from actual goalpost-moving.
