I am drafting a novel set during an interregnum in an invented state — call it Velatha — and I find I cannot proceed until I have a working definition of a phrase I thought I understood: "the legitimate government." The constitution under which Velatha has lived for a century has just run out: the head of state has died, the rules of succession are silent, and no institution any longer has an uncontested legal warrant to rule. I want a definition that tells me, chapter by chapter, which of my claimants is not merely in power but has the right to be.

Here are the cases I need the definition to classify. Treat each as a way the first weeks of the gap might run, not as seven simultaneous governments.

1. The Council of Ministers, appointed under the old constitution three years before the gap opened, stays in office. It levies taxes, staffs the courts, and commands the obedience of the civil service and the army; no other claimant controls the capital. I take this one to be legitimate, and a definition that denies it is wrong.

2. A provisional governor installed by a neighboring power with an expeditionary force. He controls the port, the customs houses, and the gunboats, and he has the recognition of two foreign governments, but nothing in Velathan law authorizes him. I take this one not to be legitimate, however effectively he governs.

3. The dead head of state's adult son, who proclaims himself sovereign on the strength of blood alone. The palace guard salutes him, but the constitution nowhere provides for hereditary succession.

4. The ministers resign on the third day, and a caretaker committee of senior civil servants takes over the ministries to keep roads, schools, and courts running. It announces that it will administer existing law but will not legislate, sign treaties, or commute sentences, because its mandate is purely custodial.

5. The Constitutional Court announces that in the interregnum it alone holds custody of the constitutional order and will certify as valid any decree issued in the state's name.

6. The last elected assembly, whose five-year term expired a week before the gap opened, convenes again and claims to carry the people's final recorded mandate.

7. A clerk finds a forgotten statute, never repealed, providing that when there is neither head of state nor ministry, the senior officer of the army shall exercise the powers of state; the senior officer now claims office under it.

Cases 1 and 2 anchor the two ends of my intuitions. Cases 3 through 7 pull in different directions: some look like legal continuity without popular warrant, some like popular warrant without legal continuity, and the last is a legal warrant discovered only after the fact.

Propose a definition of legitimate government that gets cases 1 and 2 right, run it through all seven, and tell me honestly where it yields the wrong verdict or no verdict at all. Then revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. Finally, tell me which of cases 3 through 7 you find least secure under your definition, and what would have to change in that case to flip your answer.
