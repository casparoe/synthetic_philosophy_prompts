Here is a parallel I keep noticing between my craft and the philosophy of science, and I cannot tell whether it is a real identity of structure or flattering pattern-matching. When I revise a novel, I choose among plot fixes by asking which one best explains the passages I have already committed to keeping—their dialogue, pacing, and emotional logic—while adding the fewest ad hoc contrivances; when a character's behavior sits oddly alongside everything else, I posit an offstage motive as the best explanation of the behavior. This is, almost term for term, what inference to the best explanation says about belief in unobservables such as electrons or genes: accept the hypothesis that best explains the data, where best trades on conservatism, scope, simplicity, and fertility. Is the parallel deep? Two asymmetries worry me. In fiction there is no mind-independent fact about a character's hidden motive—often I have not determined one myself—whereas in science the unobservables are answerable to the world; and in fiction several hidden stories can explain the same visible text equally well, so the best explanation is underdetermined by the text. Do these asymmetries merely limit how far the analogy can run, or do they show it rests on a confusion? And if anything does transfer, what should the underdetermination worry imply for a novelist thinking about how readers reconstruct motive?
