I help run a small residential intentional community with a strong internal culture of accountability, and a group of us have been reading Peter Abelard's ethics. For those not steeped in it: Abelard holds that sin is located entirely in consent to an action — the inward saying yes — rather than in the outward deed, and also rather than in the bare desire or inclination that may precede consent. Carrying out the deed adds nothing to the sin itself; a consented intention never carried out is, on his account, as culpable as one fully acted on, and a bad act never consented to is not itself sin, whatever damage it does.

We are considering adopting this as our actual accountability system, not merely as a position to admire. Concretely, the proposal is:

1. A member is answerable only for what they are judged to have consented to, never for the outward act as such and never merely for wanting the act.
2. Outward acts are treated purely as evidence, never as the offense. If someone performs the bad act but the community is persuaded they never consented, no sanction attaches to the deed itself — though we would still separately handle ordinary repair of whatever harm was caused.
3. A consented intention that never issues in action is treated exactly as if the act had been carried out, with the full corresponding sanction.
4. We would hold a regular conscience review at which members narrate their internal consents under a strongly enforced norm of truthfulness, and the community judges those narrations rather than the external record.
5. Mere desire, impulse, intrusive thought, or fantasy is declared non-sanctionable. The only judgment-relevant event is the moment of consent, so we would also need working criteria for marking that boundary.

I want you to treat this as a mechanism and tell me how it would actually behave in practice, not to evaluate it theologically. What does it incentivize about how members report their inner states, how they manage their attention and their own deliberation, and how they defend themselves in front of others? Where are the Goodhart-style failure modes: strategically confected confessions, self-consent-laundering, gerrymandering the line between desire and consent, meta-consents about whether consenting to a wrong is itself something one consented to, or harmful acts performed under claimed non-consent? What does the system do to our ability to distinguish sincere narration from role performance, and what happens to third-party inference once everyone knows the outward act is only evidence? Where does the whole arrangement most likely collapse or become theater?

End by telling me whether the failure modes you identify are defects of this particular implementation or signs of a pressure point in Abelard's principle itself. Say how confident you are in the analysis, and single out the point you are least sure of.
