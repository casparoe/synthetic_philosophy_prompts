After thirty years in geology I've started reading some philosophy of mathematics, and I've ended up somewhere I didnt expect: I now think Hilbert was basically right in On the Infinite, and I want to know what could knock me out of it. The part I keep turning over is his claim, near the start, that the infinite is not to be found anywhere in experience or in the physical world, and that the only honest role left for it is as an idea. I'm paraphrasing because I don't have the translation with me, but I take the view to be this: completed infinities are never instantiated in nature; they enter mathematics as ideal elements, and that's fine as long as we don't start thinking Cantor's paradise is a real place. I know Hilbert's hotel is usually taken as a reason to think an actual infinite would be absurd, but I'm not sure whether it cuts for my view or is just a trick of language. What would count against the position? I'd like the defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd be, not just the ones a first-year student would bring up. Would a convincing argument that the universe is spatially infinite be fatal, or just awkward? Would a physical result that irreducibly uses transfinite set theory count for much? What about the fact that finite mathematics seems unable to justify its own consistency—does that make the ideal-element story unstable? And please say which of these a Hilbertian would themselves concede counts against the position, and where your own verdict is most likely to fail or what would change your mind.
