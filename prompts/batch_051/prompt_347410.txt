Over the past several decades, the Frege–Geach debate in metaethics seems to me to have settled into a fixed and somewhat ritualized pattern. Expressivists claim that moral utterances function primarily to express noncognitive attitudes, such as desires, plans, or acceptances of norms, rather than to represent evaluative states of affairs. Their critics point out that such attitudes, unlike ordinary beliefs, do not obviously enter into the logical relations needed to explain conditionals, negations, and modus ponens arguments containing moral sentences. Expressivists respond with increasingly elaborate machinery: for example, treating the meaning of a conditional with a moral consequent as the expression of a higher-order attitude toward combinations of attitudes, and adopting a quasi-realist program to explain why moral discourse nevertheless displays truth-like and belief-like surface features. Critics reply that these constructions either presuppose exactly the representational content they were introduced to avoid or fail to provide a genuine compositional semantics. After decades of this back-and-forth, neither side seems to have moved, and I am less interested in another round of adjudication than in a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is this a substantive dispute about the psychology of moral judgment, a partly verbal dispute about the relevant notions of belief, content, or meaning, or a dispute about what a semantic theory must accomplish? Is there a hidden formal or empirical question that could settle the matter, such as whether there can be a workable logic of attitudes that supports embedding and inference without covert representational content? Or is the impasse evidence that the original expressivist question was ill-posed? Given your diagnosis, what would genuine progress look like, and what kind of evidence or distinction could actually move the debate forward?
