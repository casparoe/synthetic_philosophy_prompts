I have recently been helping my father digitize a box of old stereographic cards that belonged to his great-uncle, and I have become mildly obsessed with the question of whether, when I look at these images, I am seeing the photographed people or only representations of them. While trying to think clearly about this, I came across the opening of an 1859 essay by Oliver Wendell Holmes called "The Stereoscope and the Stereograph." It begins:

"Form is henceforth divorced from matter. In fact, matter as a visible object is of no great use any longer, except as the mould on which form is shaped. Give us a few negatives of a thing worth seeing, taken from different points of view, and that is all we want of it"

I am having trouble making precise what Holmes means here, and whether the essay is really siding with what I have come to think of as the transparency thesis: the idea that we literally see through photographs to the objects themselves. Is "form is divorced from matter" a thesis about perception or about the economy of images? Is a stereoscopic pair especially important because it seems to restore something a single photograph lacks, and, if so, does that actually undermine the claim that we see through the photograph? And how should a reader take the closing phrase "that is all we want of it": as the claim that the material original is no longer needed once our visual access to its form has been secured, or as a looser boast about photography's power?

I would like help with the passage itself and with its relation to the transparency idea: can the opening claim be given both a strong and a weak reading, and which reading is better supported by what follows in the essay?
