I'm in my final year of a philosophy degree and taking a module on respect and recognition (Kant, Darwall, a bit of Honneth), and I also work as a paid peer tutor at the uni's academic skills centre. I see first-year students one-to-one and write them feedback sheets on their essays, about six a week. I get roughly thirty minutes per sheet, the senior tutor has to sign off before anything goes to the student, and the other tutors get one afternoon of training, so whatever I hand them has to work without the theory. The students can appeal the feedback to the course convenor, so the stakes are higher than they look.

The module keeps pressing on a distinction between recognition respect—what you owe everyone just as a person—and appraisal respect, which is the credit you earn for being good at something. And there's a related idea that respecting someone isn't only about what you say or do, but whether you treat them as an equal who can call you to account for your judgement, rather than as a problem to be managed. I can write a feedback sheet thats technically fine on every rubric line and still get that attitude wrong.

Please can you write me a decision procedure my fellow tutors can actually run on a draft feedback sheet before we send it. It should be a checklist with the questions in order, what counts as a pass on each, where the procedure stops with a no and says rewrite, and where it hands off to the senior tutor because it's a values call. Keep the conceptual distinction in it—don't let it collapse into "be nice"—but no jargon. One thing I especially want it to catch is when a comment doesn't just correct the claim but undercuts the student's standing to make it at all (comments like "as a first-year you can't expect to weigh in on this").

Then run the procedure on three draft sheets: (a) an essay defending a standard position competently but in language mostly borrowed from the lecture slides, where the draft just corrects the referencing and gives a grade; (b) an essay with a genuinely original but under-argued idea, where the draft says "unclear, this won't fly at university level"; and (c) an essay arguing something I find honestly offensive, where I'd rather just write "this is unacceptable"—I suspect the procedure is going to tell me something I won't like.

Finally, be honest about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure, and say what the checklist can't tell us (like whether a particular student will actually feel recognised). And write the whole thing in British English, my tutor docks marks for American spelling.
