Two members of my department spent the later part of a faculty seminar arguing about promising, and I could not tell exactly where the argument turned. The case on the table was this. A promises B that A will provide comments on B's draft by Friday. B accepts and thanks A, but knowing A's record, privately forms no expectation that the comments will arrive. A meant it at the time, then simply never got to it. Friday passes; B has meanwhile arranged for comments from someone else and is not at all put out.

The first colleague argued that A has not wronged B. Perhaps A has shown weakness of will, she said, but there is no unsatisfied duty owed to B. The binding force of a promise lies in the assurance it gives the promisee, and on these facts B was given no assurance and relied on none; the promise misfired, much as if B had declined it. The second colleague answered that this makes promissory obligation hostage to the promisee's credulity: the very same words, intention, and circumstances would bind A if B were trusting and not if B were cynical, and that is the wrong way around. A binds herself by exercising a normative power; B's entitlement is created by that act, not constituted by B's expectation of performance. The first replied that B could release A from the promise, and what B can waive, B can also decline to take up from the start. The second replied that promises create directed duties, and a directed duty can be waived by its holder without being optional; B's silent lack of expectation is not a release. The first pressed: if the wrong consists neither in disappointment nor in harm, what exactly does B have a claim to? The second responded that B has a claim to A's compliance as a matter of right, in the way a creditor has a claim to repayment even if she is too proud to expect it and will never collect.

Could you adjudicate the exchange as it stands? I am not asking for a verdict on the better general theory of promising. I would like you to go through the moves one by one: for each contested step, state who is right about that specific point and why; flag any place where one of them mischaracterizes the other's position; and say what, if anything, both parties are missing.
