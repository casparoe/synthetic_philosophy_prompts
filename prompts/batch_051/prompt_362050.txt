One of the standard moves in arguments about whether democracies or markets can be said to know things is the Condorcet jury theorem: for a binary question with an objective answer, if each voter is independently more likely right than wrong (p > 0.5), then as the number of voters increases the probability that a simple majority is correct goes to 1. From the theorem and its usual caveats I have extracted five working criteria for calling a real aggregative procedure Condorcet-style truth tracking:

(1) the underlying question is genuinely binary, with an objective answer that the two options partition;
(2) each judge has better-than-chance reliability on questions of this type;
(3) the judges' errors are conditionally independent — once the truth is fixed, no shared mistake source is driving them all;
(4) judges sincerely report their own judgment rather than voting strategically;
(5) aggregation is unweighted simple majority.

Now consider a particular real-world case: a liquid prediction market on whether a specific piece of legislation will pass in an upcoming national referendum. The contract pays $1 if the measure passes and $0 otherwise. Trading is a continuous double auction open to anyone, with order sizes limited only by what participants are willing to put up. There are hundreds of active traders: some have small pieces of genuinely private information (local canvassing data, connections to party staff, polls run privately), many trade on widely shared public information, and a few trade largely on guesses about what the rest of the market will do. A handful of well-capitalized traders take positions large enough to move the price more than all the small traders combined. No trader has inside access to the vote count, and no major new information arrives during the final day of trading. The price ends the day at 73 cents.

This market violates, or at least strains, several of my five criteria on the obvious reading. Yet I keep wanting to say it is one of the better truth-tracking institutions available, and I have seen people cite the jury theorem when defending that intuition.

For each, say whether the market clearly satisfies it, clearly fails it, or leaves the criterion underdetermined, and where the criterion is underdetermined, what further fact about the market would settle it. Then answer the real question: is this market doing the same epistemic work as Condorcet's jury, or is it doing something different — price discovery through capital-weighted bets, information aggregation under strategic trading, a Keynesian beauty contest that happens to track the truth — that the five criteria were never designed to capture?
