I am an undergraduate in a philosophy seminar on aggregation in moral reasoning, and next week I have to defend a paper before the seminar. My thesis is a limited Taurekian one. I take a rescue case in which I can save exactly one stranger or exactly five strangers, with no morally relevant differences among the six except the numbers. I argue that the fact that five is greater than one is not by itself a reason to save the five; the one person's claim not to be left to die is exactly as strong as any of the five's claims, those five claims do not fuse into a single fivefold claim, and so a procedure that gives each side an equal chance—a coin toss between saving the one and saving the five—is at least as defensible as simply saving the larger group. My argument rests on a pairwise comparison of complaints: in each one-to-one comparison the complaint of being left to die is of the same magnitude; the only question is whether several equal complaints collectively outweigh one equal complaint, and I deny that they do unless some further story about the separateness of persons is told. I am not denying all aggregation: I grant that when the harms differ in magnitude, or when there is an established practice whose justification does not depend on the disputed summing, numbers can matter. What I deny is that bare cardinality among equivalent life-or-death claims does the work.

The audience matters. The seminar includes two committed act-utilitarians who will immediately translate the case into expected-value terms and will treat my refusal to save the five as a failure to maximize expected lives saved; a deontologist who sympathizes with the separateness-of-persons idea but will press me on whether my view paralyzes triage and disaster response; a graduate student who works on social choice and will argue that pairwise comparisons are formally underdetermined once there are more than two parties; the professor, whose own work is critical of aggregation for non-fatal goods but who has argued that when lives are at stake the shape of the choice changes; and several agnostics. Please give me a question-period prep sheet rather than a further defense of the view. Order the objections by how likely and how damaging they are; phrase each one in the way the relevant member of the seminar would actually put it; and for each tell me what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. The question period is short, so also indicate which replies can be compressed into two or three sentences and which ones I should slow down for. Be sure to include the objection I am hoping nobody asks and explain how I should handle it if it comes.
