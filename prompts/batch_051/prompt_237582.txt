I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but I've been trying to reverse-engineer a former coworker's rules for saying when someone knew something in engineering contexts. Over the past year I've written down how he rules in postmortems and design reviews, and I can't quite tell what principle he's applying. In each case the person's belief turned out true; the only question was whether they knew it at the time. I'd like your help extracting the principle and applying it to a new case he and I haven't discussed.

Here are his earlier rulings, compressed to the details that matter:

1. Deploy flag. An engineer checked a dashboard that displayed a cached 'deploy successful' badge from the previous run; the badge would have displayed 'success' regardless of the current run. The current run had in fact succeeded. He ruled: she did not know it had succeeded.
2. Two bugs canceling. A test suite passed, but the two configuration errors that caused the pass made the suite pass regardless of whether the feature worked; with either error fixed, it would have failed. The engineer concluded from the green suite that the feature worked, and it did. He ruled: he did not know the feature worked.
3. Flaky test. An engineer ran a test known to pass most of the time even when the relevant code path is broken; it passed, and the code was in fact sound. He ruled: she did not know the code was sound.
4. Reliable reviewer. An engineer asked the one person on the team who is, in practice, always right about this subsystem; that person reviewed the change and said it was safe. He ruled: the engineer knew it was safe.
5. Calibrated metric. An engineer relied on a well-calibrated service metric that has reliably indicated healthy rollouts in every past case; the metric crossed the healthy threshold, and the rollout was healthy. He ruled: she knew it was healthy.
6. Hunch. An engineer had a strong hunch that one particular service was the failure point but no diagnostic evidence at the time. He ruled: she did not know, even though the hunch turned out right.

The new case is this. Mira believes a migration was safe because an automated dry-run verifier certified it 'safe.' The verifier is extremely reliable: in every nearby situation in which it emits a 'safe' certificate, the migration really is safe; there is no realistic near-miss in which it produces a false certificate. However, the verifier has a latent bug that only fires in an exotic, far-fetched failure mode: in the nearest situations in which the migration failed, the verifier would nevertheless emit a 'safe' certificate because of this bug, and Mira would trust it and believe the migration was safe. In the actual situation, the migration was safe, and she believed it was safe on the basis of the certificate.

I have two things I'd like from you. First, a prediction: based on his record, do you think he would say Mira knew the migration was safe? Second, a diagnosis: what principle, or what two or three candidate principles, would make his record hang together? I know enough to have heard the labels 'safety' and 'sensitivity,' and enough to know this is the neighborhood of the Gettier problem, but I can't do the mapping myself. Tell me which of my six cases is carrying the weight, and give me one further case in plain engineering terms that would cleanly split the candidate principles.

Please attach a rough confidence to the prediction and to the diagnosis separately. If six cases is too small a sample to support any principle, or if my new case is ill-posed, or if the whole idea of a single underlying rule is itself the mistake, I'd rather hear that directly than get a tidy answer that pretends otherwise.
