Below are a short-answer question from an undergraduate metaphysics exam and a student's answer. Grade the answer as an instructor: assign a letter grade and then give point-by-point comments justifying the grade, identifying both the inferences that are correct and any that are unsupported or fallacious; do not substitute a general impression for the assessment.

Question: Explain the thesis that a person could not have originated from a different sperm and egg than the ones from which they actually originated. Present the strongest argument for the thesis, discuss what monozygotic twinning shows about the relation between origin and identity, and state what the thesis implies, if anything, about when a person begins to exist.

Student's answer: The thesis holds that every person has their origin essentially. If a different sperm had fertilized the same egg, the resulting individual would not have been me, even if qualitatively very similar; it would be a sibling of mine rather than a different version of me. The strongest argument is a Kripke-style argument from cross-world identification: when we suppose my parents had produced a child from a different gamete, we are not imagining me with a different start but imagining a numerically distinct person. Persons are individuated across worlds by their causal-historical origin, and origin is therefore necessary.

Monozygotic twinning is important because it shows that origin is not sufficient for identity. The two twins trace back to a single zygote formed from one sperm and one egg, yet they are two persons, not one, so a complete shared origin does not guarantee being the same person. It also shows that the pre-division zygote cannot be identical with either twin, for otherwise the twins would be identical with each other. But from the fact that neither twin is identical with the zygote it follows that the twins are not tied to the zygote's sperm and egg as the necessity-of-origin thesis requires. The zygote is a developmental ancestor, and what matters for a twin's identity is the particular cell from which the twin buds after division, not the gametes that formed the original zygote. The necessity-of-origin thesis, properly applied, is therefore a thesis about the zygote, and it leaves open that a twin could have originated from a different sperm and egg.

This in turn tells us when a person begins to exist. Necessity of origin implies that in an ordinary pregnancy the zygote is already the person, since the zygote is the first thing to possess that person's complete origin. In a twinning pregnancy, by contrast, the person does not begin until division has produced a distinct embryo, since neither twin is the earlier zygote. Personhood therefore begins at the zygote stage in ordinary cases and only after division in twinning cases.
