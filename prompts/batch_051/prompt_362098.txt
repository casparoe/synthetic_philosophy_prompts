Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether we are entitled to apply our present moral convictions to people who lived before those convictions took hold. I found myself insisting that a literate Englishman in 1780 did not deserve a pass for slaveholding merely because the practice was normal around him; the wrongness was not a secret that had to wait for later decades to be uncovered, and appealing to the standards of his time struck me as mostly an evasion. But then my friend turned the lens around. Someone in 2280, I imagine, will look back at some practice I currently find unremarkable and regard it as plainly abhorrent, and I would not accept that their hindsight gives them jurisdiction over my conscience. So I appear to be holding an asymmetry: I am willing to sit in judgment on the past from my present convictions, while treating future convictions as irrelevant to what I can now be blamed for. I judge backward but not forward.

I suspect this asymmetry is a cousin of the habit of writing history as a story of progress toward the present, with the present serving as both destination and judge. But I would like to know whether the asymmetry can actually be defended. The natural defense is that I apply my standards to the past not because they are present but because I believe they are true, and I refuse to be judged by future standards because I do not yet hold them and might reject them once I encounter them. Yet the Englishman of 1780 could have said exactly the same in his own defense, and I want to deny him that defense. What separates my position from his? If the only difference is that I happen to stand at the later point in time, then my confidence in judging him looks like the same temporal chauvinism I accused him of, pointed the other way. Is there a coherent view of moral progress that licenses backward judgment but not forward judgment, or must one of those two attitudes be given up?
