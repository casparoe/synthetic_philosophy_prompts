In a recent seminar on international political economy, the question was whether a modern state should deliberately pursue a greater degree of autarky — shortening its supply chains and securing domestic sources of food, energy, medicines, and critical components so that it depends as little as possible on other states. One participant repeatedly cut off the case for self-sufficiency by saying, "No man is an island, entire of itself." The proverb was made to carry the conclusion that the ideal of self-sufficiency is a mistake, but it seemed to shift sense as the discussion went on. At some points it expressed only the plain fact that every human being depends on others for material needs; at other points it expressed the thicker claim that a life organized around avoiding dependence would be a worse human life, even if it could be approximated; at still other points it expressed the much stronger claim that self-sufficiency is an incoherent or vicious ideal even for a political community. I want help separating these readings. What are the distinct precise claims that the proverb could be used to assert? For each, is it a near-platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something false or dubious? And which of them, if any, actually support the inference from the proverb to the conclusion that a state's pursuit of self-sufficiency is misguided? Please be explicit about the step from the individual to the state. A person and a state are very different sorts of things: a person can depend on others in emotional, social, and economic ways at once, while a state's dependence is something more like vulnerability to coercion or supply disruption. Is self-sufficiency the same kind of ideal in the two cases, or does the analogy that gives the proverb its rhetorical force break down somewhere? I am not looking for a single interpretive answer; lay the readings out as a list and say for each what work it can and cannot do in that debate.
