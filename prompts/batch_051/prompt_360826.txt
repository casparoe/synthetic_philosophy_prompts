I've been wading through the peer disagreement debate and it honestly feels like it's been circling the same drain since the mid-2000s. The setup is always the same: someone who looks just as smart, honest, and well-informed as you reviews the same evidence and reaches the opposite conclusion. Conciliationists say that by itself should make you drop your confidence a lot, maybe even split the difference. Steadfast types say no, you can rationally hold your ground, because you're responding to the first-order evidence as you see it, not counting heads. And the exchanges never seem to move anyone. Conciliationists accuse the steadfast view of licensing dogmatism and epistemic arrogance; steadfasters retort that conciliationism is self-undermining and leads to a skeptical regress where nobody can ever rationally keep a belief. My working theory is that what's really going on is a dispute over what counts as evidence in the first place—whether your own private take on the shared evidence is itself something you have and your peer lacks—and if that's right, the two sides just have different bedrock intuitions about evidence, not a normal factual disagreement. Is that the right diagnosis? If so, what would actually count as progress here, or is the debate set up in a way that guarantees a stalemate?
