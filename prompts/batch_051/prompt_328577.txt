I was a neurophysiologist before I retired (mostly recording from motor and premotor cortex in monkeys), and I've fallen hard into philosophy of law and technology. Here's a distinction I keep reaching for, and I want someone to check whether it's doing real work or whether I've just reinvented a wheel badly.

I've been calling it upstream vs downstream privacy. Downstream privacy covers what you've already put out into the world: a photograph, a conversation, a search query, walking through a public square. Upstream privacy covers what comes before any of that: inner speech, a half-formed intention, an emotional response you'd never voice, the neural precursors of a decision. Most privacy law (and, I think, most privacy theory) is downstream: it regulates who may observe, record, or publish traces and expressions. A good brain–computer interface records the neural state itself, before there is any outward trace to observe. That feels like a change in kind, not degree.

The passage that got me going is from Warren and Brandeis, The Right to Privacy (1890):

"This development of the law was inevitable. The intense intellectual and emotional life, and the heightening of sensations which came with the advance of civilization, made it clear to men that only a part of the pain, pleasure, and profit of life lay in physical things. Thoughts, emotions, and sensations demanded legal recognition, and the beautiful capacity for growth which characterizes the common law enabled the judges to afford the requisite protection, without the interposition of the legislature."

I read that as them already claiming that thoughts, emotions, and sensations are the real objects of privacy protection, with the downstream stuff (letters, portraits, gossip) being just the measurable proxies they had available. Am I reading them right, or is "thoughts and sensations" doing broader, vaguer work for them? Is the upstream/downstream distinction already in the literature under some other name? And where does it break: a BCI that decodes inner speech into text is producing text, so is there actually any privacy interest that isn't downstream once a decoder spits out a shareable output? Please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested reading of privacy law as if it were the standard one.
