In a seminar on natural kinds, we read Book IV, Chapter VII of Mill’s A System of Logic (1843), where he argues, if I understand him rightly, that natural classifications are distinguished from artificial ones by the fact that they group things according to the greatest number of shared attributes, whereas artificial classifications rest on a single chosen mark. That looked intuitive until a classmate produced the following argument.

(1) Mill’s criterion: a kind is natural only if its members share a large number of properties, and a classification is real only if the shared properties are sufficiently numerous and important. (2) The members of any acknowledged natural kind share only a finite, comparatively small set of properties: gold atoms share atomic number 79, but differ in mass number, thermal history, position, degree of purity, and indefinitely many relational properties. (3) If we keep adding properties to preserve the criterion, every sample will eventually diverge from every other; no two pieces of gold share all their properties. (4) So Mill’s criterion either yields no natural kinds at all, or, if relaxed far enough to include gold, yields as many natural kinds as there are individuals. (5) But there are natural kinds; gold is one. So something has to go.

I would prefer to keep (5) and abandon Mill’s criterion, but I cannot see whether the pressure is really on (1), on a quantifier shift in (3), or on an ambiguity in the notion of a shared attribute between (1) and (2). Where does the argument go wrong? If it does not go wrong, say which premise is least costly to give up and what the resulting view of natural kinds must look like.
