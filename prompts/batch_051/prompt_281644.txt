A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether "what if everyone did that?" can serve as a genuine non-consequentialist moral test, or whether universalization looks Kantian only until one sees that consequentialist or formal-consistency reasoning is doing all the real work. Write a dialogue between two equally sophisticated characters on opposite sides of this. Character A should be a neo-Kantian who thinks the Formula of Universal Law yields substantive duties without summing consequences, and who can defend the distinction between contradictions in conception and contradictions in will rather than just restating it. Character B should be a critic in the spirit of Hare's universal prescriptivism, arguing that universalizability is at best a formal constraint of impartial consistency: purely formal versions of the test are empty, and any version substantive enough to yield a verdict borrows from consequentialism. Have them go several rounds over the lying-promise case, the problem of how finely a maxim should be specified, and a threshold case such as an individually harmless act that would be disastrous if everyone did it. Each should respond to the other's actual points, and neither should be a strawman. After the dialogue, add a brief note saying how confident you are that both positions are represented faithfully, and identify the single move in the dialogue you are least sure you have attributed correctly.
