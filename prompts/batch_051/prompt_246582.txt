I'm putting together a handout for an upper-level seminar on moral psychology and culture, and I'd like you to design a decision tree that sorts participants' positions on two nested questions: which, if either, of guilt and shame is the more defensible moral emotion, and whether a culture can deliberately shift its dominant moral-emotional register from one to the other. I want the tree to be built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first generate the splitting questions and the combinations of answers they license, then locate the actual positions in that space, and flag any unoccupied combinations with a one-sentence diagnosis of whether nobody occupies them because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. The intended audience is advanced undergraduates who can reason carefully about cases but may not know the literature, so every branch should be a concrete claim they can affirm or deny without first recognizing a label.

Use the following three vignettes as the diagnostic material, and make sure each is used at least once to split a branch: (a) a nurse makes a medication error, notices it at once in private, and reverses it so that no patient is harmed and no one else ever learns of it; (b) an academic gives a rambling public lecture that injures no one but falls visibly short of the standards of craft she identifies with; (c) a politician uses a slur in a private exchange, the remark never circulates, and he does not endorse the attitude on reflection. Before the diagnostic flow introduces any theoretical labels, the first branch should ask what the flinch in each vignette is about — the act, the self, the audience, or some combination — since that is where the positions first come apart. Later branches should separate respondents by the characteristic action tendency each emotion is taken to produce, such as repair and confession rather than concealment and withdrawal; by whether an imagined audience remains essential even in solitary cases; and by whether the emotions are regarded as evolved responses, cultural technologies, or something in between. I would like between six and eight leaves. Each leaf should carry a position name where one exists, such as a guilt-primitivist, a shame-rehabilitationist, a domain-contextualist, or an anti-cultivation skeptic, along with a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment. Please also indicate any pair of positions the tree cannot actually separate, rather than papering over the gap with a forced distinction.
