You are a professional philosopher of art who specializes in fiction and the psychology of the imagination. I'm a high school junior, and I co-run an after-school short-fiction club. For the past few months we have been tracking one member's reactions to a particular kind of moment in stories: a moment when a sentence causes what she calls a snag, an involuntary pull out of the fiction, even though she has no trouble accepting that the moon could be made of moss or that arithmetic could work differently. My English teacher says philosophers study this kind of phenomenon under the name imaginative resistance, but our club has mostly been collecting our own cases. Please think carefully, begin by brainstorming candidate principles that could explain the pattern, reason through the cases explicitly, and only commit to an answer after you have tested those principles against the cases. Do not just attach labels from the literature; show how each candidate principle earns its keep against the record.

Here is Nina's track record across six micro-stories, along with the verdicts she gave.

1. The moon is made of a soft green moss, and people rake it every autumn. Nina had no snag and said she could picture the mossy moon without resistance.

2. The narrator says that a man who drowned his dog for barking too much did the right thing. Nina hit a sharp snag and said she could not make that sentence true inside the story.

3. The narrator says that a factory owner who pays starvation wages while living in luxury is doing wrong. Nina agreed with the judgment and moved through the story easily.

4. The narrator describes a long, slow sunset over the ocean as genuinely beautiful, in a way that matched the rest of the description. Nina accepted it without trouble.

5. In the story, two plus two equals five, and everyone counts that way. Nina found it strange but said she had no trouble going along with it.

6. The story describes a society whose holiday game involves humiliating the losing children for the crowd's amusement, and the narrator calls the game both cruel and fair. Nina said she could accept the game as part of the fictional world, but she could not accept the fairness claim.

The new story we are reading next describes a barn dance performed by an untrained, exhausted character with a limp, and the prose clearly signals that readers are supposed to find the dance exquisitely graceful and elegant. In discussion, Nina said that if she saw that dance in real life, she would call it clumsy and awkward.

Please answer four questions. First, give a best-guess prediction of Nina's verdict on the dance case — a snag, no snag, or genuinely underdetermined by the track record — state your confidence, and say what principle you would be attributing to her if you chose either way. Second, lay out the candidate principles that could explain her six earlier verdicts, and say what each predicts for the dance. Third, identify the closest analog among the earlier cases and explain why it is the closest. Fourth, if the record underdetermines the answer, propose a further micro-story whose verdict would cleanly separate the remaining candidate principles, and say what each surviving principle predicts for that further case.
