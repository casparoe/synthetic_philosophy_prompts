I keep noticing a pattern in how people evaluate risky choices, and I wonder whether it has a name. Consider a bet that looks favorable when you average the outcomes across a large group of people who each take it once. The same bet, taken repeatedly by a single person who reinvests the proceeds each time, can drive that person's wealth down toward zero almost surely, even though each round looks good on its own. I have seen the same shape elsewhere: a startup investment with a high average return because a few companies win enormously, which is still ruinous for someone who keeps staking everything on one startup after another; and a policy that improves outcomes for the average person in any given year but makes nearly every person who follows it worse off over time. It seems there are two different averages at work, one across many people at a single moment and one across many moments for a single person, and that people rarely notice when they diverge. Is there an established concept for this divergence, and for the conditions under which the two averages coincide or come apart? I am less interested in a bare label than in the precise distinction and its edges: which kinds of situations it matters for, and how it differs from the older puzzle that a gamble can have an unlimited average payout yet almost no one would pay much to play it.
