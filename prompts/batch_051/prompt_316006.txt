I'm a reporter who covers science and ideas, and I've been reading around on famine for a piece — Amartya Sen plus the people who argue with him — and I've ended up fairly convinced of a line you could sum up as: modern famines are political crimes, not natural disasters. The canonical cases — Bengal in 1943, Ireland in the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward famine in China — apperently all have the same shape: food was available, or at least not catastrophically unavailable, but large groups lost access to it because of prices, entitlements, exports, procurement quotas, or state ideology. Weather and blight set the stage; they don't produce the body count.

I'm writing this up, and before I do I want to know how exposed the position actually is. Play it straight for me. What would the historical record have to look like for this view to be wrong? What's the strongest version of the "it really was a shortage after all" objection in each of those three cases, and what would the data have to show to make it decisive rather than just awkward? Are there things a defender of this line has to concede — for instance, that the entitlement story explains the mechanism but not the politics that produced it, or that some famines genuinely are supply-caused — that quietly open the door to the other side? And is the position falsifiable at all, or has it been padded with enough "it was still avoidable" qualifiers that nothing could count against it? I'd personallly find one clean counterexample — a bad harvest that would have killed people no matter how competent the government was — more convincing than econometric disputes over the Bengal rice numbers, but I want to know if the record actually offers one.
