For a history paper on whether the famous cases usually called collapses—the Classic Maya, Easter Island (Rapa Nui), the Late Bronze Age Aegean—were really endings or just reorganizations, I tried to formalize an argument I kept running into, and I think something must be wrong with it, but I cannot locate the exact fault. The argument is this:

(1) If "collapse" is a useful factual concept, then there must be a principled difference between a society's collapsing and its merely changing or transforming.
(2) A principled difference of that kind requires a criterion of identity over time: a principled way of saying when a society stops being the same society.
(3) Every criterion of societal identity that the collapse literature actually uses is vague. Political authority, writing, monumental construction, urban density, and long-distance trade can each be lost partially, in some places but not others, or over several generations; the population may persist while institutions do not, or vice versa.
(4) A vague criterion cannot underwrite a principled difference between two outcomes.
(5) Therefore no criterion of societal identity can underwrite a principled difference between collapse and mere change.
(6) So "collapse" is not a useful factual concept; it is only a loose, interest-relative label.

I hesitate because plenty of respectable concepts, from species to adulthood, have fuzzy edges and still do factual work. But if the argument is valid, then I must reject one of the premises, and I cannot decide whether the weak point is (1), with its demand for a principled difference in the first place, or (4), with its leap from vagueness to being unprincipled. Is the argument valid? If it is valid but unsound, identify the exact premise that fails and state the strongest available replacement for it. If it is invalid, identify the exact step at which the inference breaks. Please also say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
