Counterfactual. One morning in early 2027, every adult on Earth wakes up with their pure time preference set to zero. That doesn't mean they become long-termists or stop caring about the present; plenty of people will still blow their paycheck tonight. And it doesn't mean their risk-aversion disappears. They still discount outcomes that might not happen because the future is contingent; they still partly discount benefits that would land on strangers rather than their own kids; a forty-year-old still weighs a benefit at age ninety partly by whether he'll still be around. But the bare preference for sooner things just because they're sooner is gone: once you've corrected for uncertainty and for who receives something, a tonne of carbon emitted now and a tonne of climate damage in 2150 trade off one for one in everybody's head.

I don't want a story about how nice the world would be, and I'm not asking whether zero pure time preference is ethically correct. I want the argued prediction, link by link. What happens to household saving and interest rates over the first decade? Does capital get cheaper and shift toward long-horizon projects, or do other frictions keep short-termism in place? What happens in politics — does a median voter who now experiences future climate damages at full weight push climate policy up, or do free-riding, lobbying, and uncertainty about whose country pays still dominate? Then the climate part: how much extra mitigation actually happens by 2050, and through which mechanism — carbon pricing, R&D subsidies, public investment, something else? For each link in the causal chain, state your confidence and say which background assumption is doing the work, and what would have to be false about people or institutions for the prediction to fail. The thing I most want pinned down is the bottleneck: where does this psychological change, powerful as it sounds, actually get stuck?
