I'm a philosophy major serving as the opinion editor of my university's student paper, and I'm currently in a seminar on Kierkegaard's criticism of the press. We've been working through his account of how the daily press manufactures a phantom 'public' that levels qualitative distinctions, replaces personal decision with anonymous chatter, and turns readers into spectators who hold opinions about everything and commit to nothing. I want to put that account to use. Each week our editorial board makes three recurring decisions: whether to run anonymous or pseudonymous op-eds, whether to keep an open and often vicious anonymous comment section, and whether to publish 'students are saying' pieces built on unattributed campus sentiment. Write me a standing rubric, grounded in Kierkegaard's actual categories — the public, leveling, the crowd, the single individual — that the other editors, who have no background in Kierkegaard and only fifteen minutes per meeting, can apply as an ordered checklist. Include two or three worked applications, at least one of which yields a decision we will not like, and mark explicitly where the rubric genuinely follows from Kierkegaard's diagnosis, where it is silent, and where turning that diagnosis into an editorial policy becomes unfaithful to what he was doing. The stakes are moderate: the paper reaches a few thousand students, and these choices generally determine whether we inflame or calm the campus.
