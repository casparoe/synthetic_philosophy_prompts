Gödel's first incompleteness theorem says that any consistent formal system strong enough to do basic arithmetic contains a statement that the system can neither prove nor disprove. I'd always assumed this was a pure math thing, but a legal code seems to be a formal system in exactly that sense: a finite list of rules and a procedure for deriveing verdicts from facts. Does it follow that every nontrivial legal system has cases that are neither provably legal nor provably illegal, so a judge who lands on one isn't really applying law but making it? If thats right, the usual talk about hard cases and judicial lawmaking would just be Gödel in disguise. Where does the analogy break down, if it does?
