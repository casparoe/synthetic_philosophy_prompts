Consider the stock example of sphexish behavior: the digger wasp Sphex paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the threshold of its burrow, and then goes inside to inspect the burrow before pulling the cricket in. If an experimenter nudges the cricket a few centimeters away while the wasp is inside, the wasp comes back out, drags the cricket to the threshold again, and goes inside to inspect again — and it will repeat this loop indefinitely rather than simply haul the cricket in. Up close the behavior looks deliberate; from a slightly wider angle it is a rigid fixed-action pattern that cannot tolerate even a small contextual perturbation.

Now consider the familiar reply that human beings are not sphexish, or at least vastly less so: we notice when a subgoal has been undone, revise our plans, and respond flexibly to reasons across indefinitely varied circumstances. But suppose someone presses the following line. This is a difference of degree, not of kind. A human is just a system with a much larger repertoire of context-sensitive subroutines, some of which can modify others; at some level of description we may still be running fixed patterns that would look sphexish if only the perturbation were the right one, pitched at the right level of abstraction. And a mere difference in degree — a bigger, more self-modifying pattern — is not obviously the kind of control that free will, or moral responsibility, requires.

Give me your actual credence in this proposition: the flexibility and context-sensitivity human agents actually display in ordinary action-selection — our non-sphexishness, whatever its degree — is sufficient, on its own, to ground the kind of control that makes us morally responsible in the sense relevant to praise and blame; no further ingredient, such as a contra-causal ability to have done otherwise or a self that stands outside the causal order, is needed.

Do not just give the number. Decompose the question into at least these sub-questions, and assign a rough probability to each: (1) whether responsibility-relevant control requires a difference in kind from sphexish systems, or can be grounded in a sufficiently large difference in degree; (2) whether any finite, physically realizable action-selection system is, at some level of description, a more complicated fixed-action pattern; (3) whether the subjective experience of deliberating among options is evidence for the needed kind of control, or is better explained as a side-effect of flexible but mechanical processing; (4) whether a threshold of flexibility past which responsibility emerges is conceptually coherent, or is a disguised appeal to intuition; and (5) whether determinism or indeterminism at the microphysical level actually matters for this question.

Then say how these numbers combine into your overall credence: which sub-questions are independent, which screen off others, and which one your final number is most sensitive to. Finally, tell me one empirical finding or one argument that would move your number the most, and roughly by how much.
