I work for an organization that is asked, year after year, to say which countries are undergoing democratic backsliding, and I have developed a personal rule for making that judgment. I will describe a case as backsliding only when there has been a codified change — in the constitution, the electoral law, the jurisdiction of the courts, or some surrounding set of formal rules — that makes it harder for voters in future elections to remove the current leadership. I deliberately set aside as insufficient the informal indicators on which many of my colleagues rely: the tone of political rhetoric, the harassment of independent media through tax audits or licensing, the staffing of the public broadcaster, the criminal prosecution of protesters, and changes in media ownership. I do this because those indicators are contested in exactly the way the concept itself is contested; they usually reach us from the government's domestic opponents or from outside observers with their own picture of a healthy democracy, and relying on them feels like letting the term become a coded way of saying that one dislikes a government's policy choices and political culture.

My colleagues reply that this rule makes me blind to the early stages of the very cases all of us care about, since backsliding often operates through informal pressure long before anyone amends a statute, and that I have narrowed the concept until only a textbook coup would count. I think I am merely demanding a fixed, checkable referent before we attach a serious institutional charge to a democratically elected government. Is this rule a defensible epistemic discipline for an organization with limited investigatory capacity and an obligation to be able to stand behind its classifications, or is it a bias toward legal formalism and toward incumbents, dressed up as methodological caution? If it is partly right, what would a corrected version of the rule look like — one that can catch genuinely informal erosion without dissolving the concept back into partisanship?
