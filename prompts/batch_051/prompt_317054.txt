I’m in a reading group on Dennett and Kinsbourne’s jointly authored paper *Time and the Observer*, along with the later development of the view in *Consciousness Explained*. One passage keeps nagging at me. As I understand it, the authors argue that conscious experience is not a single stream of frames being projected in a private theater; instead, content is processed in parallel and continuously edited, so there is no privileged moment at which one version becomes *the* conscious one. What we mistake for a canonical conscious experience is, they say, the result of a probe: when the system is asked to respond, report, or remember, one among several drafts gets consolidated into an official story, and we then retrospectively read that story back as if it had been present all along.

When I tried to explain this to a friend who works on distributed systems, he immediately said the same structure appears in event-sourced applications. An event-sourced system does not maintain a single authoritative current state; it maintains an append-only log of events, and any query constructs a projection or view at query time. There is no canonical current state sitting in the log itself—what you get depends on which query is posed and when, just as the “final” draft depends on the probe. I cannot tell whether this is a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or something I am merely pattern-matching. I would like you to audit the parallel: state the mapping precisely, say what would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer from one domain to the other, and identify at least one thing the analogy appears to predict that is false or misleading. Please give exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
