I am a third-year student in a course on political philosophy and public reason, and we have just finished Hannah Arendt’s essay Lying in Politics: Reflections on the Pentagon Papers, from her collection Crises of the Republic. In one passage that occupied most of our seminar, Arendt distinguishes between the traditional political lie, which conceals a particular fact such as a planned invasion or a bombing, and a more radical form of official deception that tries to replace the factual record with a coherent, internally plausible story. Her suggestion, as I understood it, is that the newer form does not merely mislead; it attacks the shared public sense of reality, so that even after the facts are exposed, the harm has already occurred because people no longer treat factual truth as the common ground for judgment.

I keep wondering whether this is a defensible conceptual distinction or something closer to a persuasive description of a mood. How would one test the claim that there is a distinct kind of political lying, one aimed at manufacturing a substitute reality rather than hiding a particular fact? Design the strongest empirical study you can, using historical cases, contemporary political communications, or both. Say what outcome would support Arendt’s distinction, what outcome would reduce the distinction to a mere difference in scale or brazenness, and what part of her claim about the common factual world would remain beyond any such test. More specifically, could a study ever distinguish between a liar who hopes people will accept a false story and a liar who is trying to make facts themselves irrelevant, or does that boundary depend on the interpreter’s standpoint rather than on anything observable in the lying?
