I keep coming back to an argument I had with my older brother, and I'd like a clear-eyed assessment of it rather than a referee's ruling.

A few years ago my brother became seriously ill. Alongside conventional medical treatment, he adopted an elaborate wellness regimen — energy fields, detox protocols, that sort of thing — which I regarded, and still regard, as pseudoscience. He recovered. He now says the regimen is "true for him."

When I challenged him, he made two moves. First, he said the belief had done real work: it gave him a sense of agency, helped him comply with the actual medical treatment, and pulled him through a period when he might otherwise have given up. Second, he said a belief that lets you go on living, that renders your experience intelligible and your actions effective, is as true as a belief ever gets; "truth" beyond that is a word for something no one has ever touched.

I replied that millions of people have believed mutually incompatible energy-field theories, and they cannot all be true just because each one helped somebody. He said that is exactly what one should expect: truth is plural because lives and purposes are plural, and the demand for a single, detached truth is a leftover habit of monotheism.

I agree his belief was useful, and I can even grant that it may have contributed to his recovery. But I do not think usefulness is the same thing as truth, and I do not think "true for him" is a real kind of truth. Yet when I try to say what truth is instead, I either find myself gesturing at a God's-eye view I do not believe in, or simply insisting that truth is "out there."

Who is right? Is there a way to keep what I find attractive in his position — that a belief must earn its keep in practice — while rejecting the conclusion that whatever works for someone is true? Or has my brother put his finger on something I am refusing to see?
