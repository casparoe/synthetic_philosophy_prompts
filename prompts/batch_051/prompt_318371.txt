In a historiography seminar I keep noticing an asymmetry in the way people treat historical claims, and I want to know whether it is defensible. Claims about localized, datable events are treated as though they have a determinate answer and can simply be got right or wrong: that the Treaty of Versailles was signed on 28 June 1919, that the Bastille was stormed on 14 July 1789, that Caesar crossed the Rubicon with a legion. But claims one step up in scale or abstraction are treated as contestable, subjective, or merely a matter of interpretation: that the Treaty of Versailles helped bring about the rise of Nazism, that the French Revolution was a bourgeois revolution, that the fall of Rome was a long decline rather than an event. Yet both kinds of claim are put together from the same fragmentary, selected evidence, and the event-claims themselves already involve choices of description—what counts as the treaty being signed, or as a crossing of the Rubicon as an act of war rather than a movement of men across a river. So the line between them does not seem to be simply given by the past.

The historiography makes this harder. One tradition, associated with Ranke, holds that the historian's task is to show the past as it actually happened, which would make the event-claims epistemically secure. Another tradition, associated with E.H. Carr and Hayden White, replies that the historian selects and arranges fragments from the standpoint of the present, and that even the apparently neutral event is narratively shaped; on this view, the fact/interpretation distinction begins to look like a difference of confidence, literary convention, or professional courtesy rather than a difference in kind. If the second tradition is pushed, it threatens to flatten the asymmetry: once the date and the causal claim are both constructions, why should we treat the date as settled and the causal claim as open?

I do not want to give up the asymmetry entirely, because it seems practically sane to treat the signing date as something to look up and the causal claim as something to argue about. But I want to know whether anything principled justifies it. What is the strongest principle that could generate the asymmetry? Does it survive the fact that even the event-claims are selected and described under interpretations? And should the asymmetry ultimately be kept, revised, or given up?
