I am a novelist drafting a book set in a city that is living through the end of its own political order: the palaces are shuttered, the scribes stop cutting records into stone, outlying districts thin out, and a generation later the population is a fraction of what it had been, though the fields around the ruins are still planted and the spoken language survives. In researching this setting I keep meeting two incompatible descriptions of such periods. One archaeologist friend treats the episode as a collapse: a measurable fall in population, political hierarchy, specialized production, and long-distance exchange. A historian friend answers that collapse is a value-laden metaphor, that societies do not fall so much as reorganize, and that the farmers, potters, and descendants simply continue under new arrangements. I am sympathetic to her caution, but I do not want the novel to turn a contested interpretive fashion into established fact.

I want to know whether this disagreement can be disciplined rather than merely asserted. Consider the southern Maya lowlands in the ninth century, the Aegean around 1200 BCE, or Rapa Nui around first European contact: all cases in which the label collapse has been contested. Set aside, for the moment, the later stories told about these places. How would one test whether such a society collapsed rather than merely changed? Please be concrete: specify the measurements or proxies you would use, such as estimated population, urban abandonment, monumental construction, writing, diet, long-distance trade, or inequality, and state the results that would favor collapse over transformation and the results that would favor transformation over collapse. Then be honest about what the tests would not settle: which part of the word collapse is not an empirical finding at all but a choice about scale, values, or whose continuity counts. I suspect I am asking for a test that can only be partly constructed, and I would like to know precisely where the testable portion ends.
