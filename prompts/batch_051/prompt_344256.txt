I'm a retired physiologist — spent my career on thermoregulation in insects and other ectotherms, so I've built more thermostats and watched more tiny animals overheat than I care to count — and I've recently fallen into philosophy of mind and animal ethics. The move I keep tripping over goes like this. Sentience isn't an on/off property; its a matter of degree. The standard picture is a dimmer switch (some people prefer temperature, but its the same idea): the dog's light is bright, the insect's is a faint glow, and a thermostat sits either at zero or just a hair above it depending on who you're reading. The point people put on this is that moral status should track the dimmer, so we should have graded moral concern instead of a sharp line at, say, can-suffer or mammals. The thermostat bit gets to me, because I know exactly what's inside one, and I also know an insect nervous system isn't just a cruder thermostat.

I'd like you to audit that analogy properly, the way I'd audit a model in a physiology paper. Pretend you're a philosopher of mind who also knows the animal cognition and affective neuroscience literature. Start by building the correspondence table: what maps to the dial position, the current, the light output, the zero point, the wall socket. Then show me where the mapping strains or breaks. In particular: is sentience really one scalar, like brightness or temperature, or is it a bunch of dimensions — sensory intensity, valence, arousal, self-awareness, metacognition — that can come apart from each other? Does pain specifically scale smoothly, or are there qualitative thresholds in there, like nociception without felt pain or reflex withdrawal, which is what a lot of us suspect insects are doing? Does the analogy sneak in the assumption that there's a single underlying quantity doing the scaling, and is that assumption doing the real argumentative work? And what about the thermostat: it has a set point, negative feedback, can act for a goal; does that buy it any moral status, even a tiny bit, or is that a category mistake?

Then give me the verdict: does the conclusion — that moral concern should be graded all the way down to insects and maybe simple devices — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the dimmer carrying the whole argument? If the analogy fails, tell me what a better one would be, or whether any single scalar analogy will mislead here.

Take your time and brainstorm the mapping before you commit; don't just write the standard essay. And be explicit about your confidence: say how sure you are about the key claims and name the single point you're least sure of, even if it's just this is an empirical question we haven't settled.
