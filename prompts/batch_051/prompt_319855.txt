I'm trying to get clear on what I actually think about eating animal products when plant-based alternatives are scarce, so I've been collecting my own verdicts on cases. I don't want sources or a reading list; just reason with the cases. Go with my gut and see if there's a principle behind it, because on my own I can't find one that doesn't wobble.

Here are my verdicts.

1. A wealthy Londoner who could easily afford a healthy plant-based diet but eats meat every day because he likes it: not fine, that one's easy.
2. A herding family in rural Mongolia whose diet is mostly mutton and dairy because little grows there and imported vegetables are expenisve: fine, or at least I can't call it wrong.
3. A fishing family on a small island in the Philippines where fish is the main cheap protein and tofu or lentils cost more than a day's wages: fine to eat the fish.
4. A broke student in London who's mostly plant-based but sometimes grabs a cheap cheese sandwich between shifts because she's hungry, exhausted and can't face cooking: I don't think it's fine exactly, but it's much less bad than the rich Londoner.
5. A guest at a funeral meal in rural Ghana, where refusing the chicken stew would read as refusing hospitality, even though there's a market with plant food nearby: fine.
6. A bodybuilder in a low-income country who wants cheap complete protein and can't afford imported vegan protein powders, so he eats eggs and chicken even though beans would cover his calories but not his macros: I'm uneasy and not sure why.
7. Eating mussels and oysters in a rich country: fine, because I doubt they're sentient in the way fish or pigs are, though I know that's disputed.
8. A middle-class person in a village in India who's vegetarian and drinks a lot of milk for protein, when the only realistic vegan alternatives are expensive imported products: fine, but less comfortable than with the Mongolian herders.
9. A tourist from a rich country staying with the Mongolian herders who refuses the mutton and asks them to make a seperate plant-based meal: I don't think the refusal is wrong, but I don't think it's admirable either; something else is off.

I know 4 and 5 look alike — plant food was available in both, and the person ate animal products anyway — but I feel differently about them and I'm not sure I can defend that.

My working principle is something like: it's permissible when going plant-based would cost you something substantial in money, nutrition or social standing, and wrong when it wouldn't. But it already wobbles. The broke student avoids a real cost (some money and a lot of energy) yet I still want to call her sandwich a mild wrong; the bodybuilder is choosing a hobby over a harm; the India case seems more about what counts as 'realistic' than about cost.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? State it up front — or maybe two candidate principles — then run it through the cases, and tell me straight which of my verdicts it can't accommodate. In particular, test it on two cases I keep flip-flopping on: a poor rural family that keeps a few hens for eggs and eats them when they stop laying; and a vegan in London who buys air-freighted asparagus and cashews with a huge water footprint while lecturing the broke student. Write in British English, and keep the whole thing under 800 words.
