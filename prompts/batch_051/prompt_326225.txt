I'm giving a workshop talk at a law school in a couple months and the part I most need help with is the Q&A, which I tend to flub. The talk defends the claim that the law's refusal to rest liability on naked statistical evidence is an epistemic feature of legal proof rather than a probabilistic mistake. I'm thinking of the standard cases: the blue bus case, where the plaintiff knows the Blue Bus Company operates 80 percent of the buses on that route but has nothing that singles out the particular bus that hit her; and a version of the gatecrasher case, where just over half of a rodeo crowd sneaked in without paying and the organizer can point only to a randomly selected attendee. My thesis is that even under the preponderance standard, more likely than not, these plaintiffs should still lose, and that the real explanation isnt just incentives, jury manageability, or error distribution. It's that a verdict makes a singular claim about this defendant, and a bare base rate about a class the defendant happens to fall into doesn't support that kind of claim no matter how high the probability gets. My rough argument runs: courts and juries systematically resist these verdicts even when the numerical threshold is met; this shows up across civil and criminal settings so it can't be explained only by the higher criminal standard; the best unifying account is an epistemic requirement of individualized, discriminating evidence rather than a merely moral or pragmatic constraint; and that requirement can be defended on broadly epistemic grounds. The audience is mostly evidence law professors with a law-and-economics bent, plus two formal epistemologists who think in Bayesian confirmation-theoretic terms, and a criminal law theorist who will push hard on the civil/criminal split. I need the objections I will actually get from this crowd, not the ones I'd get from a philosophy of science department or an online rationalist forum. Give them in the sequence I should expect them, ordered by how much trouble they will cause, phrased the way these particular people would phrase them, and for each one say where I should give ground, where I should dig in, what a two-minute reply sounds like, and where that reply is shaky. And tell me the question I'm most hoping nobody asks.
