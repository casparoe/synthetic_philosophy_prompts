I've been trying to settle a disagreement with myself brought on by a very ordinary practical question: whether a donor who wants to do the most good should fund interventions that reliably help a small number of people now, or an intervention with a vanishingly small probability of preventing a catastrophe that would end all future lives. The dispute has pushed me toward two convictions that don't sit well together.

The first is that expected-value reasoning is close to compulsory. If I prefer one act to another in every possible state of the world, I should prefer it simpliciter; and if I allow tradeoffs across states at all, it is hard to see how to weigh them except by some form of probability-weighted value, without drawing an arbitrary line between probabilities I count and probabilities I ignore. So I find myself committed to the thought that a sufficiently small probability of a sufficiently large amount of value swamps any finite stack of ordinary good on the other side, and from there fanaticism seems to follow: you shouldn't spend resources on the certain, modest good, because no amount of it is ever tall enough to beat the lottery ticket for the astronomically valuable outcome. The second conviction is blunt, and I think firm rather than a prejudice I'd be glad to drop: there are probabilities so small that a reasonable person may, and perhaps must, treat the prospect as not counting, whatever the prize. When I raise this, I get told to bite the bullet and be a fanatic, to adopt a bounded or risk-averse utility function, or that I'm just bad at small probabilities. The first strikes me as a reductio, the second as giving up something I wasn't prepared to give up, and the third as explaining the person away rather than answering him.

What I want is not a forced choice between fanaticism and an arbitrary cut-off. I want to know what it would take for the anti-fanatic intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for expected-value theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a stable middle that keeps the structural virtues of expected-value reasoning — dominance, state-wise weighing, no arbitrary thresholds — without licensing fanatical conclusions. Rather than just picking a side, say what would justify each of these moves: debunking the intuition, amending or abandoning the theory, or showing the two are compatible after all. And tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to fail, and which argument or consideration would most likely change your verdict.
