I chair a committee that awards one small grant per year. Proposals are read and scored as they arrive, and once we pass on one we cannot go back to it. The committee is split between two procedures. One member wants us to set a fixed quality threshold before reading anything, fund the first proposal that clears it, and only if we get through twenty proposals without a winner, lower the threshold a notch and continue; she says this is the only workable approach when we cannot know the full pool in advance or rank proposals precisely. Another member says that procedure will make us fund a respectable early proposal while a much stronger one sits later in the stack. He wants us to read some initial batch purely to learn what a good proposal looks like, then fund the next proposal that beats every one in that batch, and, if none ever does, take the final proposal rather than lower the threshold. His procedure seems to assume we can learn the shape of the pool quickly and that the harm of passing over a strong early proposal is the same kind of mistake as funding a weak one, which I doubt. But I cannot shake his worry that the first procedure leaves real value on the table. I have to decide which procedure to impose. Walk me through what each procedure is assuming about the pool of proposals, about what we are trying to achieve, and about what reviewing costs us; tell me which assumptions are doing the real work, and give me a decision rule with reasons rather than generic advice about balancing speed and quality.
