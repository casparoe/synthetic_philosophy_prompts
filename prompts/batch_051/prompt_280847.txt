Question: "It's often said that philosophy, unlike science, never converges on settled answers, and that this shows philosophy makes no real progress. Is this a good argument? Discuss how 'progress' should be understood in philosophy, and defend a view about whether philosophy makes progress."

Student answer:

"There's a familiar challenge: after two thousand years philosophers still disagree about almost every big question, while science settles debates and moves on. You might think that shows philosophy can't make progress. But that's to quick. First, it conflates 'not yet settled' with 'not making progress': a field can move closer to an answer without having reached it. Second, science has its own frontier disagreements; what looks like consensus is mostly consensus about the settled core, not about current research. The real difference is that philosophy keeps its hard problems in view instead of handing them off once a technique works.

Progress in philosophy shouldn't be measured by consensus. It can be measured by the options that get ruled out or made more precise. For example, the Gettier cases killed the simple JTB analysis of knowledge, and nobody seriously holds that analysis anymore. Modal logic, decision theory, and probability theory grew out of philosophical work and are now tools used everywhere. Each time philosophy successfully formalizes an area, that area becomes a new science: physics came out of natural philosophy, psychology and linguistics came out of philosophy of mind and language, and computer science came out of logic. That shows the real pattern: philosophy only makes genuine progress when it turns into a discipline that no longer needs philosophy.

So here's my view. Philosophy does make progress, but the progress is mostly negative and indirect: it rules out bad theories and generates new fields. Since the deepest philosophical questions are the ones that never become sciences, we should expect no convergence on them. The persistence of disagreement is therefore exactly what a successful philosophical division of labor looks like. And if a philosophical debate actually starts to converge, that's just a sign it's about to stop being philosophy. So the objection from lack of convergence fails: lack of consensus isn't a sign of stagnation, it's a sign philosophy is doing its job."
