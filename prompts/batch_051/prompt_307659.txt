We've been arguing in my political philosophy group about whether rights are just the kind of consideration that beats the others, and one member keeps falling back on the card-game picture: ordinary considerations — aggregate welfare, efficiency, public convenience, coordination — are the plain suits, while a rights claim is a trump. Once a trump is led, he says, it takes the trick even if the other side played a numerically higher card in a non-trump suit. So when a right genuinely applies, we don't weigh it against the general good at all; the only real argument is about whether this is realy a rights case in the first place. He used it the other week to settle, rather too quickly, a dispute about whether a landlord's property right gets to ignore a city's energy-efficiency code.

I can't shake the sense that the card game is doing more work than he admits. I'd like you to audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence table: what in the rights picture is supposed to be the deck, the suits, the trump suit, the bid or agreement that makes one suit trumps rather than another, following suit, overtrumping, the tricks, and the score. Then tell me where the mapping breaks and which of those disanalogies actually matter for his conclusion, as opposed to being incidental to cards. Two things specifically bother me. In most trump-taking games, a higher trump beats a lower trump, and the trump suit isn't written into the cards themselves — it's fixed by a prior agreement among the players in the bidding, which is exactly the kind of convention you might think rights are supposed to stand outside of. What do those two features do to the claim that rights outrank welfare without being weighed against it? And is there a better analogy — a side constraint, a fence, a veto, a wall — and if so, would it leave his conclusion standing, or has the conclusion been carried by the card game the whole time?
