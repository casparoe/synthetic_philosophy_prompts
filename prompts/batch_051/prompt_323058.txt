I'm a software engineer working on a math engine that has to parse and evaluate expressions, and we've been going back and forth about whether our internal representation of numbers should include set-theoretic facts. The thing is, I just read this old paper by Paul Benacerraf, "What Numbers Could Not Be," and it kind of messed with my head. He points out that if you pick one standard set-theoretic reduction of arithmetic, you get one answer to whether 2 is a member of 4, and if you pick another equally standard reduction, you get the opposite answer. His argument, as I understood it, is that numbers don't secretly *just are* one of these set-theoretic constructions, and any choice of reduction encodes arbitrary information that arithmetic itself doesn't care about. So now I'm stuck on a practical design decision: our parser gets asked questions like "is 2 an element of 4?" and my coworker thinks we should just pick the von Neumann encoding and make it return true. But if I've understood Benacerraf right, that's the wrong lesson—it would be baking an arbitrary implementation detail into what we treat as a semantic fact about the numbers themselves. So how should I actually think about this? Should the engine treat "is 2 ∈ 4?" as a genuine question with a right answer, a question that only has an answer relative to an encoding we happen to choose, or a question our system should refuse to answer because it's not well-formed at the level of arithmetic itself? And more concretely: if we do need to expose some answer to the query (the system has to do *something*), what's the principled way to make that call without fooling ourselves or our users into thinking we've discovered a fact about numbers rather than made a modeling choice? I'd like a way to think about this that helps me decide where in the codebase the encoding should live, what the public API should claim, and how to handle expressions that mix set-theoretic and arithmetic vocabulary.
