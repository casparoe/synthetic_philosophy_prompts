I have been taking a graduate seminar on nuclear strategy this semester, and the professor has been grading a series of one-paragraph crisis scenarios with a single verdict: whether a state's threatened retaliation would be credible to a rational adversary. Her record so far is as follows.

1. A threatens to use nuclear weapons in response to a border incursion into remote, near-worthless disputed territory where A has no forces deployed, has made no public statements, and has no meaningful stake. Verdict: not credible. Her comment: nothing is at stake, and nothing would force A to follow through.

2. A threatens nuclear retaliation against a full-scale conventional invasion of its own industrial heartland. A has no prior reputation for resolve and has made no public commitment, but the stakes are existential. Verdict: credible. Her comment: no rational government would prefer the loss of its heartland to fighting, so the threat enforces itself.

3. A threatens to retaliate on behalf of a small ally whose defense is not itself vital to A, but A has stationed tripwire forces on the ally's territory and has ratified a formal public alliance. Verdict: credible. Her comment: the public commitment and the tripwires mean A cannot walk away without destroying its reputation with every other ally, and the adversary knows it.

4. A threatens military escalation over a minor maritime boundary dispute in which it has a modest economic interest, but it has no forces in the zone, no public pledge, and no relevant record of following through. Verdict: not credible. Her comment: the stakes are too small to make escalation rational by itself, and there is no audience cost or tripwire to lock A in.

Next week's scenario is this. A has no vital interest in a contested buffer zone and has made no public pledge about it. Instead, A has deliberately arranged its posture so that the final decision to escalate will not be entirely under its leaders' control: it has pre-delegated release authority to regional commanders, written deliberately ambiguous rules of engagement, and declined to install command-and-control arrangements that would guarantee a central halt. A's leaders privately prefer to avoid a nuclear exchange, but the challenger knows that a probe of the zone could trigger escalation through local initiative or misperception even if the leaders would rather stand down.

Predict her verdict on this new scenario. Distinguish the candidate theories of credibility that fit her record — a pure interests account, a pure commitment or audience-cost account, a disjunctive account on which either interest or commitment suffices, and a version of that account that also admits a third source of credibility, namely deliberately leaving part of the decision to chance or subordinate initiative. Say which theory you think she is actually running, what each candidate predicts about the new scenario, and what the best-supported prediction is. Then give me the one further scenario you would put to her next to settle whatever ambiguity remains.
