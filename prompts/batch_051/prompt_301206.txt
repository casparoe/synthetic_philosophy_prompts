I'm a science and ideas journalist. My desk keeps landing on stories where an ordinary term is starting to buckle: people stretch 'addiction' to cover short-video binges, call an LLM 'intelligent', or fight over whether a de-extinct animal belongs to the original 'species'. Our current policy is whatever the assigning editor felt that week, and it's starting to cause consistency rows. I'd like a reusable editorial procedure for deciding when to keep using such a word in its recieved sense, when to quietly shift to a revised or ameliorated version, and when to flag it as contested. Philosophers, I'm told, would describe some of this as conceptual analysis and some as conceptual engineering or ameliorative analysis — asking what a concept actually does versus what it should do for our purposes. Turn that into a checklist a non-specialist editor could run in 20 minutes on deadline: the order of questions, the distinctions that have to survive being simplified, the points where it hands over to human judgement, and what it can't settle. Then run it through three cases: 'addiction' for compulsive short-video use, 'intelligence' for an AI system, and 'species' for a de-extinction story; make sure at least one lands somewhere we won't like. No reading lists.
