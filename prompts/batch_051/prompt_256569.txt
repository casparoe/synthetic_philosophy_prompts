A defender of the debt view of filial obligation and a defender of the friendship view associated with Jane English have spent a term arguing past one another. The debt view holds that adult children owe their parents something — paradigmatically care, support, or special consideration in old age — in virtue of what their parents gave or sacrificed for them, and that this is a duty, not merely a matter of affection. The friendship view holds that once the child is an adult, the relationship carries no special obligations over and above those generated by an ongoing voluntary relationship of friendship or love: whatever a grown child owes a parent is roughly what one friend would owe another, so that an adult child who does not stand in any such relationship may owe nothing beyond what is owed to any person.

They have agreed to produce an adversarial-collaboration document. Write it. It should contain (1) a numbered section of claims both sides can sign, substantive enough to be worth signing; (2) a disagreements table whose columns are the issue, the debt view's statement of its own position (worded as its advocates would endorse it), the friendship view's statement of its own position (worded as its advocates would endorse it), and a verdict on whether that row records a substantive disagreement or a merely verbal one; and (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side — together with, for each crux, a concrete case or argument that both sides accept bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I suspect, without prejudging, that one crux will be whether unsolicited benefits can bind a recipient who never consented to them, and another whether anything about the parent-child relationship as such has normative force once the child is an adult; the document should earn whatever cruxes it settles on rather than assert them.

Do not let either side rest in a slogan. The document must force both positions to take definite stands on at least these cases: a parent who made severe, unreciprocated sacrifices for a child who now feels no warmth toward them; a parent who was neglectful or abusive and now demands care; an adult child who enjoys a warm, friendship-like relationship with a parent but resists demands that go beyond ordinary friendship norms; a parent with advanced dementia who no longer recognizes the child; and a grown child who received large unsolicited benefits from a parent and is now asked for repayment or support. Where a view yields a counterintuitive answer, the document should either defend that answer or revise the view, rather than restating it in vaguer terms.
