I keep returning to a debate about strict liability that has stalled in a familiar way. One side says that criminal punishment without mens rea is never legitimate because it punishes someone who did not choose or foresee the harm; the other side says strict liability offenses are acceptable regulatory tools, and points out that the law already holds people to objective standards of care it does not tailor to their capacities. Holmes in The Common Law, Lecture III, gives the classic statement of that second point: roughly, that a person born hasty and awkward may deserve allowance in heaven, but his slips burden his neighbors just the same, so the law insists he meet the community standard at his own peril rather than discounting for his personal equation. My question is not which side is right, but what kind of disagreement this is. Is one side making a hidden empirical claim about deterrence or risk-spreading? Is the other making a claim about the expressive meaning of punishment that the first side can accept without changing its view? Or are the two sides using liability and punishment to mean different things? My working hypothesis is that the impasse is mostly a disagreement over whether legal liability is a form of moral blame, and that both sides actually share a theory of individual desert but apply it to different institutions. Diagnose the structure of this standing dispute, drawing on what each side actually says, and say what would count as progress given the diagnosis—or whether we should expect none.
