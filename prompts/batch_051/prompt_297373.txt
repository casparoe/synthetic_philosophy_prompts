Lately I've been going around in circles about Dennett's sphex wasp and free will, and I want a case built to order. The sphex idea, as I understand it, is that the wasp's rigidity shows why freedom shouldn't be identified with pure spontaneity or being uncaused. The wasp brings a cricket to the burrow threshold, goes inside to inspect, comes back out, and drags the cricket in; if you nudge the cricket away while the wasp is inside, the wasp just repeats the whole fixed routine. So Dennett-style views treat an agent as free-ish when it is non-sphexish: it can notice a disruption and alter its plan, rather than reruning the same subroutine no matter what. Frankfurt-style hierarchical views, by contrast, want something more internal: the agent has to be capable of stepping back and reflecting on the desire it acts on and either endorsing or revising it, so the relevant capacity is about second-order attitudes, not just behavioral flexibility.

Build me a case where those two come apart in the direction that feels a bit unusual: a Dennett-style criterion says the agent counts as free because it is non-sphexish, while a Frankfurt-style criterion says the agent is not free. Constraints: one ordinary realistic scenario — a gadet, an animal, a person, or a simple AI, your pick — but no exotic physics, no perfect predictors, and nothing that just stipulates "it has no free will" as a premise. The scenario should be no more than a couple of paragraphs. After you give it, do the check: state explicitly what each theory's verdict is and why, identify the structural feature that makes them peel apart, say what happens if that feature is removed (does it collapse back into a standard case where Dennett and Frankfurt agree?), and then say how a defender of the Dennett-style view would attack the case as setting an unfair or question-begging standard.
