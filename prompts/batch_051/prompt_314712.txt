A good part of my job as a journalist covering science and ideas is noticing which standards a field uses when it says that one thing caused another, and I have never found those standards harder to pin down than in historical explanation. The criteria I carry around for calling something a cause of a historical event are these. First, counterfactual dependence: had the factor been absent or materially different, the event would not have occurred, or would have unfolded in a materially different way. Second, a concrete connecting story: there should be an intelligible chain of decisions, institutions, or pressures leading from factor to outcome, not merely correlation or an unchanging background condition. Third, explanatory nontriviality: the factor must not be so pervasive that it explains everything and thereby explains nothing.

Consider the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand at Sarajevo in 1914 and the outbreak of the First World War. The assassination plainly satisfies the second criterion: it set off the July Crisis, which then produced ultimatums, mobilizations, and declarations of war through a well-documented sequence of decisions. But it is far less clear that it satisfies the first. Many historians argue that the war was overdetermined by deeper conditions—the alliance system, militarized planning, great-power rivalry, and Balkan instability—so that if the Sarajevo assassination had not occurred, some other incident would probably have lit the same fuse. If that view is right, the assassination may fail the counterfactual test while still being the thing everyone points to as the start.

Please assess, criterion by criterion, whether the assassination qualifies as a cause of the war under these criteria, and say where the criteria themselves underdetermine the answer. Then address whether this borderline case reveals a flaw in the criteria—for instance, whether they need a separate account of overdetermination, or of the difference between a trigger and a structural cause, or of what it is to be "the" cause rather than merely one contributing factor. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which historical claim or argument, if established, would lead you to the opposite verdict, and where in your reasoning the failure is most likely to occur.
