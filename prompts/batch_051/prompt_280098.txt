In a political philosophy reading group, a colleague keeps appealing to a picture of fairness: distributive justice is simply cutting a cake into equal slices. The cake stands for society's stock of distributable goods, the equal slices stand for what justice demands, and any proposal to give some people more than an equal slice is by definition unfair. She uses this picture to argue against spending extra on disability accommodations, remedial education, and mental-health support, on the grounds that such spending tops up some people's slices at everyone else's expense. I suspect the analogy is doing more argumentative work than it appears to, but I want a precise audit rather than a rival slogan.

Walk me through how far the cake-cutting analogy actually holds. In doing so, please do the following.

First, give an explicit table of correspondences: what in the distributive situation plays the role of the cake, the slices, the knife, the baker, the table, the guests, differing appetites, and differing abilities to reach, hold, and digest the cake.

Second, identify the main disanalogies between dividing a cake and distributing the benefits and burdens of social cooperation. For each disanalogy, say whether it is a harmless simplification or one that is doing real work in the colleague's argument, with a sentence explaining why.

Third, use the load-bearing disanalogies to show what the picture quietly imports. In particular, address at least these: the homogeneity and divisibility of the cake; the difference between possessing a good and being able to convert it into something a person can actually do or be; fixed preferences versus adaptive preferences or expensive tastes; the fact that real goods are heterogeneous and some people need different goods, not more of the same one; and the entitlements, contributions, and productive arrangements that already exist before any slicing begins. Also distinguish two readings of the picture: one in which the cake is a windfall with no history of production, and one in which the cake is the ongoing product of cooperation among the very people who will receive slices. Indicate which reading the colleague's argument silently requires.

Fourth, give a verdict on each of these claims the colleague wants the analogy to support: (a) equal shares of distributable resources are the default demand of justice; (b) spending more on disability accommodation or remedial education than is spent on others is unfair; (c) any distribution other than equal shares bears a heavy burden of justification. Say which of these survive the audit and which depended on the analogy's silent assumptions.

Finally, propose a better analogy for whichever conclusion you think does survive, and state explicitly what that improved analogy still cannot capture and what a full theory would have to supply without the aid of any analogy.
