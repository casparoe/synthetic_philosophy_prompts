i'm working on a piece about blame and moral luck, and in interviews I keep hearing the same compression of the basic argument people associate with Galen Strawson. the move is: you do what you do because of the way you are, and to be truly responsible for what you do you'd have to be responsible for the way you are; but you didn't choose your nature, and to have chosen it you'd have needed a prior nature to choose with, and so on back. so being ultimately responsible for yourself would require you to be self-caused, and that, they say, is no more coherent than pulling yourself up by your own bootstraps — you'd have to already be there, fully formed, to do the pulling. end of story, so the claim goes: no one is ultimately morally responsible, and praise and blame are built on a mistake.

the bootstraps image gets used so often that i suspect its doing argumentative work no one has actually checked. audit the analogy for me. give me the correspondence table: in the bootstraps picture, what stands for your initial nature, your later choices or acts of trying to reshape yourself, the causes or circumstances that formed you, whatever plays the role of the ground the person is standing on, and the 'lift.' then sort the disanalogies into ones that matter for the no-responsibility conclusion and ones that are just vivid but irrelevant. i specifically want to know what the picture quietly imports — a fully formed self already there to pull, self-creation happening in one go, maybe other things — and whether those imported features are actually part of what responsibility would have to be. does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the bootstraps image carrying the argument? and if there's a better analogy for what the basic argument needs, or for what the other side thinks responsibility actually requires, give that too.
