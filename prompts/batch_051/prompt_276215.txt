I'm putting together a handout for a cultural evolution seminar, and I want a dialogue I can give students that shows two people who really know the issue disagreeing about whether the "meme" idea is more than a metaphor — that is, whether cultural variants genuinely replicate in something like the way genes or viruses do. Make it a real back-and-forth, not alternating position statements: each character should have to respond to the other's best point, and neither should be a strawman. The setting can be two colleagues leaving a conference talk on a transmission-chain experiment of the sort where someone is shown a story or a line drawing, the next person sees only that person's reproduction, and so on down a chain. Over generations the material shrinks, gets more conventional, and gets pulled toward familiar schemas. One character (the replicationist) argues that this is still Darwinian selection: each retelling is a token copied from the previous public version, with copying error, and the systematic changes are selection plus mutation acting on variants. The other (the attractor or epidemiological view) argues that most of the change is reconstruction rather than copying: people extract a gist and regenerate a new performance, so the "variants" aren't really heritable modifications of a token — they're partly independent draws from a shared cultural attractor. Have the attractor character press the replicationist on how much copying fidelity selection actually needs before it can do work, and point out that the same systematic distortions show up in unrelated chains. Have the replicationist push back hard: a reconstructed gist can still carry heritable variation, and without some copying mechanism you lose the genealogical dependence that makes later versions resemble there immediate predecessors rather than just the shared schema. At some point they should also get into what "the variant is copied" could even mean when the thing being transmitted is a mental representation versus a public performance.

Please write the dialogue first, and then a short note (around 200 words) separating what is genuinely settled in cultural evolution from what remains contested among people who work on this; don't present one side as if it were the consensus. Keep everything self-contained — no citations, no reading list, define technical terms as you go. Approach this as a philosopher of biology who knows the Dawkins-style and the Sperber-style literatures well, but the dialogue itself should carry the arguments rather than namedrop. Start by sketching each character's strongest case and a couple of points where their arguments actually engage each other, then write the dialogue.
