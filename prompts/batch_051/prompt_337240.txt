For the past few months I have been helping design a longitudinal survey of civic attitudes, and the team has repeatedly deadlocked over whether to organize respondents by standard generational cohorts. To sort out my own view before our next meeting, I have been recording my gut verdicts on particular cases, and I would like help turning those verdicts into a principle.

Here is where I currently stand. I do think the cohort that came of age during the Depression and the Second World War — the so-called Greatest Generation, roughly born from 1901 to 1927 — is a real grouping in some substantive sense: shared public events during formative years did the work. I think much the same about the smaller, unofficial cohort born roughly from 1977 to 1983, often called Xennials, who had an analog childhood and a digital early adulthood; that grouping feels like a real kind even though, or precisely because, it cuts across the official Gen X–Millennial boundary. By contrast, the official endpoint of the Baby Boom bothers me: grouping someone born in 1964, who has no lived memory of the 1960s, with someone who came of age in the mid-1960s strikes me as a merely administrative date range. I am also reluctant to say that two people born in 1995, one in Warsaw and one in Austin, belong to the same generation in any substantive sense, because their nationally and regionally specific formative events differ far more than the official label suggests. Meanwhile, an event-defined 9/11 generation — Americans who were children or adolescents at the time of the attacks — feels like more of a real kind than the Millennial category, even though it does not match the official ranges. I also notice that the more people take up a label like Gen Z as a political and cultural identity, the more the category seems to become real, but in a different way: a social kind that feeds on its own use rather than a cohort bound by shared formative events. And when I consider two siblings born in 1980 and 1984, with nearly identical technological and media biographies but assigned by the official line to different generations, I cannot bring myself to treat them as belonging to different cohorts at all.

What I am after is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts, or most of them. The principle should say what makes a generational label pick out a real kind rather than a conventional date range, should say what it implies about categories I have not settled — for example, whether Gen Z taken as a whole is a real kind, and whether the Baby Boom's long official span survives scrutiny — and should be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to revise, if any.
