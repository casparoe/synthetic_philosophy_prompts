I recently spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether a home sign system counts as a language, and we made almost no progress because we had never agreed on what a language is in the first place. My friend kept insisting that the system cannot be a real language because no community shares it; I kept insisting that the child is plainly doing something linguistic even if the system never spreads beyond one household. I want to locate the disagreement precisely rather than just re-run it.

Suppose, for the purposes of this exercise, that a system qualifies as a language only if it exhibits the following features to a substantial degree: (1) semanticity, stable form–meaning pairings; (2) arbitrariness, the connection between form and meaning is not mainly forced by resemblance, though iconicity is permitted; (3) productivity, users can produce and understand utterances they have never encountered or produced before; (4) displacement, communication about things absent in time or place; (5) duality of patterning, a small inventory of meaningless units such as handshapes, locations, and movements recombines into meaningful units, which in turn recombine into larger structures; and (6) transmission, the system is passed on through learning, so that its conventions are shared and persist beyond any single user.

Now consider the following case. A deaf child is born to hearing parents who do not sign, and the child has no early exposure to an established sign language. The child spontaneously develops a gesture system. It contains a stable lexicon of gestures with consistent meanings, some arbitrary and some iconic. The child uses it to refer to absent objects and to past and future events, and combines gestures into longer sequences with a consistent though rudimentary ordering, producing novel combinations rather than merely repeating past ones. The parents learn to understand much of it, but they do not produce it with anything like the child's fluency, and the system is not used by anyone outside the household. Systems developed by other children in similar circumstances differ substantially from one another in vocabulary and in some aspects of structure.

Work through the criteria one by one and say whether the case satisfies each. Then render an overall judgment: does this system qualify as a language under the stated criteria, and if it does not, is that because the system is missing something linguistic, or because the criteria are picking out something too narrow? Where the criteria underdetermine the answer, say so explicitly. Finally, reassess the criteria in light of the case: in particular, consider whether transmission is best treated as a condition on a system's being a language, a condition on its being a shared language of a community, or a condition on something else again, and whether the argument between me and my friend would be better handled by separating those questions.
