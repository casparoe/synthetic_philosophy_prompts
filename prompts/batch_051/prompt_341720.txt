I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about why some philosophers think consequentialism — the view that you should do whatever has the best overall consequences — is in rougher shape than people realize. A philosopher's blog post I read summarizes the "cluelessness objection" like this:

"Consequentialism says an act is right just in case its consequences are at least as good as those of any alternative. The cluelessness objection points out that consequences never stop: every choice ramifies into an unknowably long future, through the lives it touches and the choices those people go on to make. So we can never know what the total consequences of our actions will be. And since, for a consequentialist, rightness depends on those total consequences, it follows that we can never know what's right — the theory is basically useless as a guide to how to live. Consequentialists sometimes reply that we don't need certainty, just rough probabilities and expected value. But that misses the point, because those probabilities are themselves pure guesses: nobody can put real numbers on the long-run effects of, say, taking a job or having a kid. So the objection isn't the naive one that 'we aren't certain'; it's that we lack justified probabilities altogether. That, in turn, shows consequentialism can't be the correct moral theory."

I suspect this is mostly right but overreaches somewhere, and I dont know enough to say where. Could you give me an accuracy audit: which parts a careful consequentialist would accept, which parts are subtly off or misstated, what a defender would push back on, and wether the final conclusion actually follows from the accurate parts? I'd rather not embarrass myself in print.
