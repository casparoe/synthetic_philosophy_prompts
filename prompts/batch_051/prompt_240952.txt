I've been trying to figure out whether the behavioral economics literature on hyperbolic discounting and the philosophical discussion of akrasia are actually about the same thing, and I keep going back and forth. On the one hand, the two seem to overlap a lot: philosophers talk about the person who sincerely judges she should get up or go to the dentist but doesn't, while economists talk about the present-biased, time-inconsistent agent who plans to be patient later but keeps caving when later becomes now. That sounds like the same person. On the other hand, akrasia as philosophers usually set it up is a synchronic failure: you judge, all things considered, right now, that X is better, yet you do Y anyway. There's no delay between now and the moment of weakness, and the problem is the gap between judgment and action, not how steeply you discount the future. Hyperbolic discounting, by contrast, seems to let the agent be wholehearted at each time-slice and just describes how her preferences reverse over time; there may be no all-things-considered judgment being defied at all.

So compare the two accounts directly. Are akrasia and hyperbolic discounting competing explanations of the same phenomenon, or explanations of different phenomena that only happen to overlap? What specific cases can each capture that the other can't, and where do their predictions diverge? I'm especially interested in whether you can have akrasia with no interesting temporal discounting at all, and whether there's a kind of hyperbolic preference reversal (say, preferring $100 today over $110 next week but $110 in a year and a week over $100 in a year) that intuitively isn't weakness of will because there's no internal conflict. Go beyond saying one is richer or thinner than the other, and actually trace the points of contact and divergence—I keep seeing people talk past eachother and I'd like something sharper than that.
