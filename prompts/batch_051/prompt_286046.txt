After a long argument with a friend who works in elder-care ethics, I have the sense that the debate over advance directives is not so much unsettled as stuck. Both sides keep describing the same case: a person, while fully competent, executes a directive refusing life-sustaining treatment in the event of advanced dementia; years later, she has advanced dementia, cannot recall the directive, and seems content with her daily routines, and in the moment she says she wants to keep living. One side says the earlier self had authority over the shape of the whole life, and the refusal she made while competent should bind the later self even when the later self resists it; otherwise, they say, the whole point of directing one's own future collapses. The other side says that the person actually before us is the one whose life is in question, and that her current pleasures, fears, and expressed wishes are what should matter, not instructions issued by a person who, in the relevant sense, no longer exists. Each side seems to find the other's position not merely wrong but almost unintelligible, and the same cases get traded back and forth without either side moving.

My friend thinks the real disagreement is about personal identity: whether the later self is the same person as the earlier one, or a new person in the same body. I am less sure. It seems at least as likely that the two sides are disagreeing about which capacities ground decisional authority — the earlier self's rational agency and power to plan a life, versus the later self's capacity for suffering and enjoyment — or that they are using words like autonomy, interests, and respect in systematically different ways. What I want is not a verdict in the dementia case but a diagnosis of the debate itself. What kind of disagreement is this: a metaphysical one about identity, a normative one about authority, a disagreement about which empirical facts about the later self matter, or an ill-posed question dressed up as a single debate? And given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? If your answer is that no progress should be expected, say why that is the honest conclusion rather than a cop-out.
