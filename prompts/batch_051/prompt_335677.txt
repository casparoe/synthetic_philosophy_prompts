I'm a backend engineer and I keep running into an asymmetry in how we treat causal claims, and I can't tell if it's justified or just professional superstition. If you can flip something—a feature flag, an A/B test, a randomized trial—and the outcome moves, we treat that as the gold standard for "X causes Y." But if all you have is excellent observational data, no matter how carefully controlled, people still say it's "only a correlation." Meanwhile we make causal claims all the time about things nobody can manipulate: smoking caused his cancer, the asteroid caused the extinction, the lead in the water caused the developmental delays. So manipulable causes get a privledged status that non-manipulable ones don't. The fancier version of the gold standard says the relationship has to be invariant under interventions: the effect should stay when you wiggle the cause in different contexts while holding the rest fixed. But here's the thing that bugs me: to know your wiggle was a clean intervention, and to know what "holding the rest fixed" means, you already need a pile of causal knowledge—what causes your flag, whether the metric also causes the flag, whether the flag affects the metric through a backdoor. All of that got borrowed from observation or theory before the experiment started. Is that asymmetry defensible, or is the gold standard just laundering the observational knowledge it's supposed to be better than? If you had to give up one side—the special status of manipulation, or the idea that interventions are a neutral, theory-free way to get at causes—which would you give up and why?
