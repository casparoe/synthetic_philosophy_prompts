In discussions of AI alignment I keep encountering a move that treats biological evolution as the paradigm case of an inner alignment failure, and I would like the analogy audited rather than merely repeated. The story, as I understand it, runs like this. Natural selection is the base optimizer, and its base objective is inclusive genetic fitness. The human brain is the mesa-optimizer that this process produced, and the brain pursues its own mesa-objective: a tangle of drives toward pleasure, status, attachment, curiosity, moral conviction, and the like. Those drives correlated with inclusive fitness in the ancestral environment, but the correlation was contingent. Contraception, adoption, celibacy, devotion to art, and care for non-kin all seem to be cases in which human goals pull against fitness, and the modern world — so the story continues — is a massive distribution shift. Humans are thus presented as a vivid natural case of goal misgeneralization: an optimization process produced an agent whose goals do not reduce to the objective that shaped it. People in my reading group take this analogy to license a strong conclusion about trained machine learning systems: that we should expect mesa-optimizers to arise, that their mesa-objectives will diverge from the base objective, and that this divergence is a central source of AI risk; if evolution could not align humans, they ask, what hope is there for gradient descent? I want to know how much weight the analogy can actually carry. Please spell out the correspondence systematically — what is supposed to play the role of the base optimizer, the base objective, the mesa-optimizer, the mesa-objective, the training distribution, and the deployment distribution — and then identify where the mapping breaks down. For each disanalogy, say whether it is an incidental difference or whether it undercuts the pessimistic conclusion the analogy is being used to support. If the analogy survives only in a weakened form, state the weakened conclusion explicitly, and indicate whether some other analogy would be more apt.
