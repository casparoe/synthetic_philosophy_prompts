I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep hitting a contradiction in how I think about constitutions. On one side, I am convinced that a legal order should behave like a formally specified system: every power must be traceable to an existing rule, every rule must have a defined condition of application, and when the rules run out the only legitimate move is to use whatever procedure the constitution already provides for changing them — not to have an official improvise. I like this because it removes the question of who governs from the discretion of the person currently in the room. On the other side, I cannot shake the intuition that in a genuine gap — say an emergency occurs and the law gives no one authority to do the one thing that would prevent catastrophe, or the authorized process would take days when hours matter — the person who can act should act, and the community afterwards should treat that action as having been legitimate, not as a crime it mercifully pardons.

Locke seems to accept exactly the thing I cannot fit into my picture. In the Second Treatise of Government, chapter XIV, Of Prerogative, he writes:

This power to act according to discretion, for the public good, without the prescription of the law, and sometimes even against it, is that which is called prerogative: for since in some governments the lawmaking power is not always in being, and is usually too numerous, and so too slow, for the dispatch requisite to execution; and because also it is impossible to foresee, and so by laws to provide for, all accidents and necessities that may concern the public, or to make such laws as will do no harm, if they are executed with an inflexible rigour, on all occasions, and upon all persons that may come in their way; therefore there is a latitude left to the executive power, to do many things of choice which the laws do not prescribe.

I do not know whether this is ammunition against my view or just a description of a different kind of system. If a constitution is a formal specification, then a latitude left to the executive looks like a bug, not a feature. But my intuition in the emergency case seems to demand something like it. Walk me through the strongest version of both positions and tell me which should give way: should I amend my theory to say the constitution contains an implicit rule of last resort, or should I treat the emergency intuition as a psychological response to consequences and refuse to build it into the law? Is there a reading of “legitimate” on which both my rule-and-amendment-only view and my intuition survive? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
