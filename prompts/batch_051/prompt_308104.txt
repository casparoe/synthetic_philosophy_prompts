I have been thinking about a particular failure mode in machine translation, and I would like to know whether it has an established name and how it should be carved off from neighbouring concepts. The failures I have in mind are not the familiar complaints. They are not about rare terminology, long-distance agreement, idioms translated word-for-word, or output that reads like translationese. On the contrary, the output is fluent, idiomatic, and confident — the sort of prose a native speaker could skim without pause. What goes wrong is always some element of the physical situation described in the source: which thing contains which, which thing rests on or supports which, which thing is too large or too small for which, what happened before what, which event caused which. A person who had formed even a rough mental picture of the scene would catch the error immediately; the translation seems to have been produced without any such picture.

Two representative instances, given in the spirit of the thing rather than as exact transcripts. In one, the source said, in effect, that a rolled-up tent was too big for a rucksack and so had to be strapped to the outside; the target text said the tent had to be strapped to the outside because the rucksack was too big for the tent. The sentence is grammatically faultless and natural; it is only when you picture the situation that you notice the explanation now runs backwards — a rucksack that is too big for the tent is exactly what would let the tent go inside it, and so it cannot be the reason for strapping it outside. In the other, the source described a glass bottle being packed upright inside a wooden crate with the gaps filled with folded cloth; the target text described the wooden crate being packed upright inside the bottle. Again, nothing in the wording signals a problem; the impossibility is entirely in the scene, and the system plainly never constructed one.

I am not looking for a bare label such as loose translation, low fidelity, or even hallucination. Hallucination, as I usually see it used, is about the confident insertion of content that was never in the source — invented names, facts, or claims. The errors I mean are not gratuitous insertions; they are fluent, coherent, and almost right, assembling the correct objects and actions but arranging them into the wrong physical situation. Is there an established name for this specific class of errors? If there is none, what are the nearest named concepts, and where exactly does each boundary lie? I have in mind the territory around common-sense reasoning, the symbol grounding problem, and certain tests in which changing a single word in a sentence flips what a pronoun must refer to. For each candidate, I would want to know whether my examples are central instances, peripheral instances, or merely superficially similar — and what, exactly, a system would have to lack in order to fail in this way while remaining otherwise fluent.
