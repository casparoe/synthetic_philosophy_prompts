In my violence in history seminar we've spent two weeks on whether the world is getting more or less violent, and I keep ending up in the same uncomfortable place. When a man fatally stabs two strangers on a train, everyone calls it violence and it goes into the databases; when a company dumps effluent that kills two people slowly over a decade, or a ministry withholds funds from a rural clinic and two patients die, almost nobody counts those as violent deaths—it's pollution, or austerity, or a public-health failure. But the body count is the same, and the people doing the dumping and defunding knew or could of known what would happen. I also notice that the apparently factual question—was the twentieth century unusually violent—flips depending on whether we use absolute deaths or deaths per 100,000 people, and nobody seems to have a non-arbitrary reason for picking one. Is there a single principle that both justifies treating the stabbing differently from the dumping and tells us which denominator is the right one for tracking violence? Or are we just stuck with an inconsistent pair of judgments and no fact of the matter either way? Give me the strongest principle you can and an honest verdict on where it breaks.
