I've been reading the Zhuangzi in the old James Legge translation this week, and this passage from the chapter called "The Great and Most Honoured Master" has been rattling around in my head: "The True men of old knew nothing of the love of life or of the hatred of death. Entrance into life occasioned them no joy; the exit from it awakened no resistance." The way I read it, Zhuangzi is arguing that death is not something to resist or grieve because living and dying are just two phases of one ongoing transformation, and the mistake is grabbing onto the living phase and trying to keep it from turning. I took that as, roughly, a consoling argument: the exit should occasion no resistance because nothing is finally being lost.

But then I went down a rabbit hole about emergency preservation and resuscitation — trauma surgeons can now put some cardiac-arrest patients into deep hypothermic arrest, basically a state that would count as dead by ordinary bedside criteria, and then hours later rewarm and revive them — and about the cryopreservation outfits that will freeze a legally dead person's brain or body on the bet that future medicine can bring them back. These developments make death look less like a one-way threshold and more like a status you might be temporarily parked in, or at least a door that could eventually swing both ways for some people.

So I'm trying to figure out what happens to Zhuangzi's argument if you take that development seriously. Does it still go through unchanged, is there a premise that gets dented, and if so is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still important? Or does the question just change on him? I care about the fate of the argument itself, not the details of the medicine.
