In my ethics seminar a guy keeps shutting down arguments with "no one could reasonably reject it," and last week he used it against a triage rule: the hospital would divert resources to prevent moderate long-term complications in forty patients instead of trying to save one patient who will otherwise die. He said the one could reasonably reject the rule, so it's wrong—but each of the forty could also reasonably reject the opposite rule, just with a smaller complaint. I know this is meant to be contractualism of the Scanlon sort, but the phrase is carrying a lot there. What are the different precise principles "no one could reasonably reject" might be expressing? Who exactly is the "one," what's built into "reasonable," and is the view secretly aggregating complaints or refusing to? Lay the readings out as a menu and give a verdict on each: trivial, defensible, false, or just too vague to do the job. In particular, tell me which reading would actually justify dismissing my classmate's triage rule, and whether that reading still looks right once its spelled out.
