My friend and I spent last evening arguing about what gives an oath its binding force. He held that an oath binds only insofar as someone might find out and punish the breach, so that once detection and punishment are secured there is nothing left to the oath itself. I kept resisting, pointing to the older idea that a sworn oath brings its own penalty with it, independent of any human agency. I want to test that older idea against a specific passage. In the Theogony, Hesiod describes the oath by the water of the Styx:

“But when strife and quarrel arise among the deathless gods, and when any one of them who live in the house of Olympus lies, then Zeus sends Iris to bring in a golden jug the great oath of the gods from far away, the famous cold water which trickles down from a high and beetling rock. … For whoever of the deathless gods that hold the peaks of snowy Olympus pours a libation of her water and is forsworn, must lie breathless until a full year is completed, and never come near to taste ambrosia and nectar, but lie spiritless and voiceless on a strewn bed: and a heavy trance overshadows him. But when he has spent a long year in his sickness, another penance more hard follows after the first. For nine years he is cut off from the eternal gods and never joins their councils or their feasts, nine full years.”

Now suppose a society keeps an immutable, universally visible record of every oath, so that any breach is detected at once and the penalty is imposed automatically by an impartial tribunal. Does Hesiod’s account of the oath’s binding force survive that development? If it still has work to do, say which premise does that work and what the Styx supplies that the tribunal cannot. If it does not survive, say whether the binding force of oaths was always an enforcement problem in disguise, and what the residual phenomenon should be called.
