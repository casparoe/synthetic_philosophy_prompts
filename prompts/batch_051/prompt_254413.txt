I'm a PhD student working in philosophy of emotion, and I keep noticing that judgment theories, feeling theories, and perceptual/appraisal theories are each defended by pointing to one favored example — a phobia, a pang of guilt, a surge of anger — and then extrapolating to everything else. Before I take a side, I want to work out the full specification of what an adequate theory of emotion has to get right. Please write out the desiderata: the phenomena a theory must explain, the cases it must get right, and the distinctions it cannot afford to collapse (for example, the difference between an emotion and a mood, or between an emotion's being fitting and its being useful), with the case or consideration that makes each one a requirement. For each requirement, say whether it is negotiable or non-negotiable and why. Then give a brief scorecard of the three main families of theory against the list, and identify the single requirement that does the most work in excluding the popular contenders. The purpose I have in mind is a theory adequate to adult human emotions as they bear on practical reasoning and moral psychology, so the list should be consistent with the current empirical picture in affective neuroscience and psychology, but I want the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring. A structured list or a table is fine.
