Okay, so I'm taking a philosophy of language course and I can't stop thinking that the whole Pluto fight is basically a real-world stress test for the idea that some disputes are merely verbal. The episode, just to have it on the table: in 2006 the International Astronomical Union voted at its general assembly in Prague to define 'planet' partly in terms of whether a body has cleared its orbital neighborhood, and Pluto, which everyone agreed had not cleared its zone, got reclassified as a 'dwarf planet.' A lot of astronomers pushed back, and the public treated it like an injustice, but the underlying facts about Pluto's size, orbit, and the Kuiper belt were never really contested. So the raw material for a deflationary diagnosis is just sitting there: maybe 'planet' had more than one candidate meaning (roughly, anything round that orbits the Sun versus something dynamically dominant in its orbit), and the vote wasn't a discovery about nature but a stipulation about which sense to use going forward.

What I want is an analysis of the episode through the lens of the verbal-dispute / metaontology framework. Is it best classifed as a merely verbal dispute, or is that too quick? To make the case either way, be precise about what each group was actually doing: agreeing on the raw facts, disagreeing about which facts are relevant to the concept, steering how classrooms talk, defending a cultural or historical sense of the word, arguing about what a classification is for. In particular, can a dispute be substantive even when nobody disagrees about the underlying facts, because they disagree about which facts matter for the concept or about the values a taxonomy should serve? If the 'merely verbal' diagnosis is right, what should the actors have done once they noticed the ambiguity—just distinguished the two senses and voted on pragmatic grounds, and would that have actually made the controversy smaller? Then run the analysis in the other direction: what does this episode show about the verbal-dispute framework itself? Does it confirm the thought that once the facts are fixed the remaining fight is just about words, or does it expose places where the framework needs to say a lot more about a term that is doing scientific, educational, and cultural work at the same time? Where does the lens go quiet? I'm less interested in a final verdict than in a disciplined mapping, but do say which reading you end up finding more honest.
