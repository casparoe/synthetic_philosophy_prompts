In Aristotle’s Physics he first distinguishes four kinds of cause: the material out of which something is made, the form or pattern that makes it what it is, the source of motion or change, and the end or purpose for which a thing is done. Later in the same work, however, he turns to natural phenomena such as rainfall and the parts of animals, and appears to say that some regular features of nature occur by material necessity rather than because they serve an end. I am having trouble squaring these two parts of the text. If the final cause is supposed to be one of the four fundamental causes in natural philosophy, how can Aristotle then allow that some natural processes are not for the sake of anything? Are these two claims actually consistent? If so, what distinction between kinds of cause or levels of explanation removes the apparent conflict? If not, which position does he more firmly hold, and how would he explain away the other?
