You are a philosopher of aesthetics with a good grasp of computational and psychological accounts of creativity. Please think carefully, and before you reach a verdict, sketch the strongest case each side could make.

I am a novelist, and I have never been able to settle an argument with myself about what originality is. One voice in my head is roughly Hofstadter's knob-twiddling model: a novel is a variation on existing structures—genre, narrative shape, sentence rhythm, inherited themes—and creation is a matter of turning knobs others have not thought to turn, perhaps many at once. The theory attracts me because it makes creativity continuous with craft, explains why originality arrives in degrees instead of all at once, and turns writing into something I can do deliberately rather than wait for. The other voice is the one I actually write with. On the few occasions I have produced something that seemed genuinely new, it did not feel like a new setting among old settings; it felt like the addition of a new axis of variation, a knob that had not existed until that work made it available. The first voice tells me that is exactly what recombination feels like from the inside. I half-believe it, and half-suspect that is a way of not listening to the evidence of the craft.

I do not want a winner declared. I want to know what would have to be true for each side to yield. Present the strongest case for the knob-twiddling view and for the firm intuition that some works invent new axes rather than merely re-tune old ones. Tell me what it would cost to debunk the intuition as an artefact of how foresight and hindsight feel, and what it would cost to amend the theory enough to produce genuinely new axes—especially whether that amendment leaves any real distinction between variation and invention. Then give your negotiated verdict: must one side be abandoned, or is there a reading on which both survive?
