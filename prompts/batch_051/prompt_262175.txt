I sit on the editorial board of my department's undergraduate philosophy podcast, and next week we are interviewing a visiting political philosopher whose published work defends a restricted version of the inefficacy objection to individual duties in large-scale collective-harm cases. His central claim, as I read him, is that a harm-based reason not to perform an act requires that one's act make things worse than they would otherwise have been, and that in cases such as a single person's emissions-producing drive, a single purchase of factory-farmed meat, or casting one vote in a large and non-swing electorate, the individual act makes no difference to the aggregate outcome. From this he draws both that there is no individual duty to refrain and that the agent is not open to moral criticism for acting. In his published work he resists two standard replies: the expected-value reply, which he rejects on the grounds that the probability of being pivotal is negligible or too ill-defined to ground a duty, and participation-based replies, which he rejects as smuggling in group agency or generating absurd overdemandingness.

I would like eight to ten interview questions that do more than have him restate this view. The questions should force him to say exactly what the underlying efficacy condition amounts to — no change in the outcome, no detectable change, or no change above a morally relevant threshold — and how he distinguishes the ordinary large-scale case from a threshold case in which the harm occurs only if at least k people act and the k-th person's act is plainly a difference-maker. I also want at least one question on each of the following: why the tiny probability of pivotality does or does not license acting when the stakes are very large, and whether his doubts about those probabilities support permissibility or merely agnosticism; whether the step from the absence of a duty to refraining to the absence of any criticism for acting can be resisted by expressive, virtue-based, or imperfect-duty considerations; how the argument avoids treating climate emissions, meat-eating, voting, and consumer boycotts as structurally identical when our intuitive verdicts differ across those domains; and whether his conclusion should shift if our ability to trace marginal contributions improved or degraded, since the underlying philosophical argument should not wax and wane with measurability. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give, where the follow-up presses the point rather than merely moving to the next topic. Finally, for each question and its anticipated reply, add a brief bracketed note separating what is settled or widely conceded in the surrounding debate from what is genuinely contested or specific to this guest's commitments, and be careful not to present a contested position — for instance, that the expected-value reply succeeds or that the threshold case sinks the objection — as the consensus.
