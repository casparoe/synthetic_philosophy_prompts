For S to mean something by uttering x, S must intend to produce some response r in the audience, must intend the audience to recognize that intention, and must intend that recognition to function as the audience's reason for producing r. So the analysis includes an actual-uptake requirement: an utterance means something in Grice's sense only if the audience really does pick up on the intention. For example, if I say to my partner 'the car is on level four' but she is wearing headphones and doesn't hear me, the uptake condition fails, and on Grice's view I haven't meant that the car is on level four, though I have still said it. Turning to the second distinction: what is said is the content fixed by conventional meaning plus the resolution of any ambiguity and reference, while what is implicated is whatever additional content the speaker intends to get across without saying it. In the reference letter, the writer says that the historian attended the colloquium regularly and keeps an orderly filing system. He implicates that the historian is not a promising scholar: a supervisor who could say something about her intellectual work would not fill a letter with administrative trivia if there were better things to say, so the letter writer is counting on the committee to infer that there are no better things to say. That implicature is conventional rather than conversational, because it depends on the well-known conventions of recommendation letters rather than on the meanings of the words.
