I am a novelist, and the part of revision I am least able to do by feel is checking whether a story event is actually supported by the causal logic of my invented world rather than by my authority as the author. I write one novel every eighteen months or so, and over months of revision I repeatedly make local decisions—whether a character’s inference is sound, whether an action explains what follows, whether a later scene can stand without adding an earlier scene—where getting it wrong makes the finished book feel arbitrary. I would like you to give me a reusable procedure, grounded in the causal hierarchy Pearl describes and in what structural causal models actually assume when they license observational, interventional, and counterfactual claims. It should be a procedure I can run in a working day, without equations or causal graphs, by arranging notes on a scene. It should distinguish three kinds of force: something being true because events co-occur in the manuscript; something being true because a character or event intervened in a way that changed the downstream possibilities; and something being true as a counterfactual about what would have happened under a different sequence. It should say which questions I can answer from the draft as it stands, which questions require me to specify an intervention in enough detail that the draft becomes a different draft, and which questions require me to build a model of the unchosen branches. It should also tell me, at each step, which assumptions of structural causal models I am importing into fiction—such as that the story’s variables are well-defined, that there are no hidden common causes unless I specify them, and that an intervention can be cleanly separated from the normal flow of events—and which of those assumptions a novel may violate without breaking its own logic. Then run the procedure on three cases. In the first, a woman recovering from a difficult period often sees a particular friend on the days she does not drink, and the point-of-view character concludes that the friend is the reason she is sober. In the second, a detective chooses not to open a letter and later tells his superior the unopened letter was irrelevant to the death; the draft never shows what the letter says. In the third, the author wants to state in narration that if the messenger had not been delayed, the betrothal would have been broken. Give me for each case: the rung it sits on, what the procedure licenses me to conclude, what it forbids me to conclude without adding structure, and what has to be supplied before I can write the sentence as true. One of the three cases should be the kind of authorial claim I would very much like to keep but which the procedure should tell me is not yet licensed. End by marking the point beyond which a causal model of fictional persons cannot decide whether a character could have done otherwise, because that is a question of the kind of story I am writing and not of the causal structure alone.
