My dad's cancer drug was denied coverage last year, and through a chain of events I'd rather not relive I've ended up on the pharmacy and therapeutics comittee for our health plan. I have to file a statement of how I think the plan should allocate a fixed budget, and people keep telling me my views are just the standard package, but I'm not sure they all fit together. Can you audit the list below and tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what the cheapest fix would be if I wanted to keep as much as possible? I honestly don't know where the problem is, so don't let me steer you.

Here's roughly where I've landed.

(1) With a fixed budget, the default should be cost-effectiveness: fund interventions in order of cost per QALY (quality-adjusted life-year), cheapest first, so the plan buys as much health as it can.
(2) A QALY is a QALY. We shouldn't weight a health gain upward just because the recipient is young, rich, or otherwise sympathetic; so no explicit age weighting and no income weighting.
(3) The standard way of building QALYs is fine: a year in full health counts as 1, years in worse health count as fractions, and the fractions come from time trade-off or standard gamble utilities. We should use those quality weights to work out how many QALYs a treatment buys.
(4) But I also think it's straight discrimination to count a year of life saved for someone with a pre-existing disability as worth less than a year saved for someone without one. For life-extending treatment, a life-year saved is a life-year saved; I shouldn't deduct the person's baseline utility score from it.
(5) I'm suspicious of the rule of rescue. The fact that I can put a name and a face to a patient shouldn't shift priorities, and one reason I joined this committee is to make sure I don't let my dad's case distort the math.
(6) For setting how much the plan should be willing to spend to prevent a death (the value of a statistical life), I trust revealed preference over committee intuition: look at what people actually accept or pay for small reductions in mortality risk, and build up from there.

Please rank the tensions by severity rather than saying everything is equally bad, and for each real tension say what would have to give if I wanted to repair it, and what the repair costs. Don't include pairs that only appear tense.
