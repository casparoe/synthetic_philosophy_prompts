Im a freelance type designer, and this came up because a client is refusing to sign off on an italic a. The brand standards document they gave me at the start of the job includes this clause:

"Every character shall retain one underlying skeleton across all weights, cuts, and styles within the family. Variation in weight, contrast, terminal treatment, and proportion is permitted only insofar as a literate reader, seeing the glyph in isolation, would without hesitation name it as the intended character."

In the roman I drew the lowercase a as a double-storey form: an enclosed upper bowl plus a lower loop. In the italic I used a single-storey a: one round bowl with a tail, partly because that is the traditional italic construction and partly because it suits the slant better. The client says the italic breaches the clause, because the two a's don't share one skeleton — the roman has two enclosed counters and the italic has one, and the strokes attach differently.

I sort of see their point, but I also feel the clause was written to stop the family becoming a patchwork of inconsistent letterforms, not to ban a conventional style difference. But I cant just say "that's how italics work," because some italics do keep the double-storey form, so a reasonable drafter might have meant to forbid exactly what I did.

So how does the clause apply to my italic? On a plain-words reading, is the single-storey italic a still the "same skeleton," or is it a different one that the clause forbids? On a purpose reading, am I safely within what the clause was for? What would the drafter say if asked about the traditional italic form, and what would a reasonable reader take the clause to promise? And if the clause does forbid my italic, is there a version of it that would still catch a genuine patchwork-of-styles problem while allowing the italic a to take the single-storey form? I care as much about that last question as about who wins.
