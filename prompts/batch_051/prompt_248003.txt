I've been reading Mill's A System of Logic, and his treatment of the composition of causes has been bugging me. He draws a sharp line between mechanical cases, where two causes produce a joint effect that is the sum of what each would produce separately, and chemical cases, where the compound has properties that are not the sum of the properties of the elements. Water is his recurring example: from hydrogen and oxygen you get a liquid, not a gas that behaves like a weighted average of the two. Mill seems to treat this as a fact about the world rather than a failure of reduction.

Now vary the picture the way people sometimes vary Newcomb's problem. Suppose we had a continuum of cases between purely mechanical combination and strongly chemical combination, controlled by a parameter that tunes how much the components constrain each other. At one end you get vector addition of forces, at the other you get something like water from hydrogen and oxygen. If the parameter changes smoothly, is Mill's distinction a sharp kind-boundary or just a verbal convenience? Does anything genuinely new appear at some threshold, or do we just have a continuous increase in the complexity of the lower-level laws? And if the lower-level description can in principle specify exactly how the joint effect depends on the parameter, has emergence been explained away or have we just relabeled it?

I find myself torn. The reductionist in me says: once you know the lower-level entities and the interaction laws, the whole is nothing over and above the parts arranged and interacting. But I also find it impossible to look at chemical combination as Mill describes it and not feel that the whole has properties that are not there in the parts, even after you include all the facts about arrangement and interaction. Which should give: the reductionist theory or the intuition of emergence? What would it take for each to be the thing that goes, and is there a reading of over and above on which both can be true?
