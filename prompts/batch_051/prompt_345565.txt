"Epiphenomenalism about consciousness says that phenomenal states are caused by physical processes but have no causal powers of their own. Explain the problem this raises for our phenomenal judgments (e.g., saying 'I'm in pain'), and evaluate one epiphenomenalist reply. Is the reply adequate?"

Here's what the student wrote:

"Epiphenomenalism says mental states are caused by brain states but never cause anything, including my own verbal reports about them. This creates a problem for phenomenal judgments because when I say 'I'm in pain,' that speech act is a physical event, so the epiphenomenalist has to say it was caused by something physical rather than by the pain itself. So the same physical causes could have produced the same report even if the pain were absent or different, which would make my introspective reports about consciousness unreliable. This is basically the paradox of phenomenal judgment. The epiphenomenalist can reply that evolution ensures the brain states that cause pain reports are correlated with the correct pains, so reports will generally be accurate. I think this reply works, because if the neural cause and the pain always co-occur, then my belief that I'm in pain is justified and true. So the epiphenomenalist has no serious problem here."
