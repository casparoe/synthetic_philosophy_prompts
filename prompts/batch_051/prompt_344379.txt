For the past few weeks I have been defending a deliberately deflationary line on Zeno's paradoxes of motion — the Dichotomy, Achilles, the Arrow, and the Stadium. The line is this: once we adopt the at-at theory of motion, according to which being at rest at each instant is compatible with being in motion over an interval, and once we accept the standard limit-based analysis of the relevant infinite series, the puzzles dissolve. The residual sense that something escapes explanation — that a motion cannot get started, or that completing infinitely many tasks in a finite time must be impossible — is, I have been arguing, a symptom of modelling motion as a sequence of discrete acts rather than as something continuous. On this view the paradoxes are, philosophically, a closed case, and someone still moved by them is making a category mistake about the continuum.

A colleague has pressed me on this, fairly: before I claim the paradoxes are closed, I should be able to say what would loosen my grip on the deflationary position, and I realize I have not worked that out in any detail. What I want is a map of the position's actual exposure. Which possible cases, formal results, or unmet explanatory obligations would a deflationist have to treat as genuine evidence against her view rather than as material for further dissolution? To make the task concrete, I have in mind pressure points such as Thomson's lamp, where a supertask is performed and yet no fact appears to settle the final state of the lamp; the concern that the at-at theory describes only where an object is at each instant and leaves out the difference between moving continuously and merely occupying the right points at the right times; the problem that on a densely ordered time there is no first moment after the traversal begins, so it is unclear how the process gets going; and the suspicion that the standard solution quietly assumes the completed infinite in order to explain how an infinite series can be completed. I would like these and any other serious challenges ordered from those that would be decisive against the deflationary view to those that are merely awkward, with a clear statement of which of them a committed deflationist could reasonably decline to count as a test of her position. Finally, I want an honest assessment: does the position as I have stated it risk anything at all, or has it been shaped so that every objection can be turned back as a confusion — and if the latter, is that insularity a defect in a proposed solution to a paradox?
