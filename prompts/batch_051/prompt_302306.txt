I have a standing rule for deciding what to believe about comparative claims. When I am told that one medical treatment works better than another, or that one school or hiring procedure outperforms another, I refuse to be moved by the overall comparison alone; I wait until I have seen the numbers broken down by the obvious ways people differ, such as age, sex, income, prior health, or severity of the problem being treated. My reason is that an overall difference can reverse within every single subgroup, so the headline result may reflect nothing more than which kind of person ended up in which group. A friend has objected that the subgroup breakdown can sometimes be the misleading comparison, not the aggregate, though he could not say precisely why. I want to know whether my rule is defensible as a general policy. Under what conditions is the demand for a subgroup breakdown the right epistemic habit, and under what conditions does it lead me further from the truth than the overall number would have?
