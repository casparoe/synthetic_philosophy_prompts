I am a novelist, and I need a rigorous adjudication of an argument I intend to adapt into a scene, because I do not want the scene to be rigged in either direction. Last month, at a literary festival, I was on a panel about fiction after artificial intelligence. At dinner afterward, two writers I know—L., a poet and translator, and D., a novelist who also writes essays about neuroscience—began arguing about whether a book could be conscious. The trigger was an aside I had made earlier, about an old philosophical fantasy of conversing with Einstein's preserved brain. I wrote the exchange down that evening as faithfully as I could. I was a bystander, and I genuinely have no settled view.

(1) L. said a book cannot be conscious. A novel is a fixed sequence of marks; consciousness requires something going on, a process with causes and effects. Ink does nothing. The book is no more conscious than a film script is dramatic. A score is not music.

(2) D. replied that a score is not music, but a performance of it is. The text of a novel is not just marks: it prescribes an ordered sequence of experiences in anyone who reads it. A written dialogue, when read, is a process. And if what matters about a brain is not the molecules but the pattern of activity they realize, then the substrate does not matter—and a book-plus-reader, or the text executed by some sufficient machine, could realize the right pattern.

(3) L. answered that D. had moved the target. Once the reader has been added, the reader is the conscious thing; the book is merely an instrument, like a drug or a sunset. The score analogy cuts the other way: the music belongs to the performance, not to the score, so the analogy does not make the book itself conscious.

(4) D. said system boundaries are conventional. We already say a person is conscious even though the brain does the work. When someone reads my novel, the sequence of their inner episodes is largely fixed by me: I make them imagine a room, then a face, then a betrayal. The reader is executing a process the text specifies. Why should the subject be the reader rather than the book?

(5) L. said the two cases differ. A human brain has the right causal architecture—feedback loops, a body, goals, a model of itself. A novel has none of that. D. was sliding from "contains a representation of a mind" to "instantiates a mind." A map is not a city.

(6) D. replied that, if the problem is only that actual novels are too thin, that is an engineering point, not a conceptual one. A sufficiently interactive text—something like a program, or a novel read by a machine that lets the text steer its own next state—could have feedback, goals, and a self-model. So L. had conceded the principle and was left arguing about degree.

(7) L. said that concession did not follow. From the fact that consciousness depends on pattern, it does not follow that any pattern specified in language is a pattern that can be conscious. A recipe is not a meal; no amount of detail makes a description identical to the process it describes. And if D. has to move to a sufficiently rich machine, then we are no longer talking about a book at all.

Please adjudicate this exchange. Take the role of a senior philosopher of mind and cognitive science with a serious interest in literary theory. Before giving your verdict, think briefly and structurally: lay out the distinctions you will need—process versus object, pattern versus substrate, representation versus instantiation, system boundaries, and anything else that matters. Then go through the seven moves one by one and say what is right in each, what is wrong or overstated, and why. Finally, say who is more right overall and what both have missed.
