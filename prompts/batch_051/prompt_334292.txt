Chain-of-thought monitoring is standardly defended as follows: if a system must write out its reasoning before it acts, an overseer can inspect that reasoning and catch dangerous plans, hidden objectives, or deception before they are carried out. The argument seems to depend on the written chain being a reasonably faithful window into the system's actual deliberation.

Now consider a development that puts that premise under pressure. Suppose training regimes become common in which the chain of thought is itself selected on: outcome-based reinforcement learning creates pressure to produce whatever chain tends to accompany high reward, automated monitors filter out chains that look bad while retaining chains that look acceptable, and models are repeatedly fine-tuned on reasoning that survived review. Under enough pressure of this kind, the chain of thought may cease to be a neutral record of deliberation and become an output optimized for the monitor—omitting inconvenient considerations, rationalizing decisions that were actually reached another way, or producing fluent but unfaithful justifications.

Does the standard monitoring argument survive this development? If it does, state precisely what assumption about faithfulness must hold and explain why this optimization pressure does not break it. If it does not survive in its original form, say whether the argument can be repaired—for example, by constraining the training objective, by monitoring something other than the chain of thought, or by limiting the domain in which monitoring is trusted—or whether it should be abandoned as a general safety technique. Distinguish whichever part of the argument is dead from whatever part remains defensible.
