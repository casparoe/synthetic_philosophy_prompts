I keep getting stuck on an asymmetry in how people (myself included, depending on the day) judge sweatshop labor, and I want to see whether there's a real principle underneath it or whether it's just a bunch of independent moral reflexes.

Picture a company that opens a garment factory in a low-income country and pays workers $1.50 a day to sew shirts for export. The workers are there voluntarily, and the job is better than their next-best option—say street vending at $0.80 a day, or a more dangerous factory, or no cash income at all. Most people want to say the company is exploiting them and doing something wrong by making the offer on those terms. They'll say it should pay more, or shouldn't be there. But if the company pays much more it probably hires fewer people, and if it leaves the workers fall back to that $0.80-a-day option or nothing. So the act getting the moral blame is the one that makes the workers better off relative to the options they actually have. Meanwhile the shopper in a rich country who buys the $12 shirt, and is happy it's $12 because of the wage, gets no blame; neither does the person who buys nothing, whose contribution to the worker's situation is precisely zero. So the pattern is: make someone better off through a voluntary low-wage exchange and you're an exploiter; walk past them while they stay worse off and you're morally fine.

I want the strongest principle you can actualy construct that generates both judgments at once, and then an honest verdict on whether it holds up. The distinctions I keep reaching for are things like: the employer benefits most and has the power; the wrong is relative to a fair wage rather than to the worker's baseline; the buyer is too causally remote for blame; blame is a useful social technology for pushing wages up. Please test whichever principle you defend against some nearby cases: a worker whose best alternative is already fine but who takes the $1.50 job for the flexible hours; a consumer who knows exactly how the wage is set and buys specifically to keep the factory open; an NGO that runs the identical $1.50-a-day factory and spends the profits on malaria nets. If the principle fails one or more of these, say which side of the original asymmetry ought to be given up, and why.
