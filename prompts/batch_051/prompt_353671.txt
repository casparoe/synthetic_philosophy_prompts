I teach a graduate seminar in research ethics, and every year we get bogged down in the placebo-control controversy around the late-1990s AZT trials, so I want to turn it into a proper case study in clinical equipoise. The background, quickly: a big U.S.–French trial (ACTG 076) reported in 1994 that a long, expensive course of zidovudine cut mother-to-child HIV transmission by roughly two-thirds, and that regimen became standard in rich countries. A few years later, researchers ran trials in sub-Saharan Africa and Thailand testing shorter, cheaper zidovudine regimens against placebo, arguing that the long regimen was unaffordable there and that the real local question was whether a short course beat the local standard, which was no treatment at all. Critics said those placebo arms would never have passed a developed-world ethics board, since it was already known that zidovudine worked; defenders said the critics were importing a rich-world standard of care that didn't exist on the ground. I'd like you to analyze this through clinical equipoise, both the individual version (the treating clinician has to be genuinely uncertain which arm is better for this patient) and the community version Freedman introduced in the late 1980s. What does each version say about whether the placebo arms were defensible? Where does the theory have to stretch to even adress the developing-world case—for instance, is the relevant "community" global or local, and can you reframe the question so that the uncertainty comes back? And in the end, does the episode vindicate clinical equipoise, or does it show that the notion is elastic enough to license whatever trial design the sponsors wanted? I care less about the verdict on the trials themselves than about what the episode reveals about the theory.
