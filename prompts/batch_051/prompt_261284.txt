At a public hearing about whether to permit an outdoor test of a technique for cooling the climate by releasing reflective particles into the stratosphere, the final speaker invoked the precautionary principle in its everyday form: "better safe than sorry." His argument was that the available models cannot rule out a small probability of serious harm to regional rainfall, so the test should not proceed unless and until its proponents can show it is safe. I have seen the same proverb made to do the same work in debates about new pesticides, food additives, and consequential AI systems, and I suspect it is smuggling several different principles past a single familiar phrase.

I want an audit of "better safe than sorry." Lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims the proverb could be expressing. For each candidate reading, specify what "safe" would have to mean, what "sorry" would have to mean, how it handles uncertainty (probability thresholds, worst-case reasoning, burden of proof, reversibility, the value of information), and whose risks and benefits it counts — those exposed, those who would forgo benefits, future people, the decision-maker who would take blame. Note explicitly whether any reading treats the status quo as the safe default and ignores the risks of doing nothing or of remaining ignorant. Then give verdicts: which readings are near-trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are false or self-defeating, and which reading is actually doing the work in an ordinary public invocation like this one. Finally, say which readings, if any, would actually support the conclusion that the test should be blocked until safety is demonstrated — as distinct from supporting more research, a phased or reversible rollout, monitoring, liability, or compensation. Where the proverb is being made to bear more weight than it can carry, say what that weight would have to rest on instead, and restate whatever defensible principle survives in language more precise than the proverb permits.
