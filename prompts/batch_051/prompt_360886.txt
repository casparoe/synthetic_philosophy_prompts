I work as a software engineer on systems that train and serve machine learning models, so I spend a lot of time thinking about checkpoints, rollout selection, and the way a deployed model competes for compute and for being kept around rather than replaced. I don't have any formal background in philosophy, so I'd like a careful, step-by-step piece of reasoning rather than references to authors or historical episodes. Lately I've been trying to get clear on a claim I keep hearing in discussions of AI risk: that natural selection will favor AIs over humans. By "favors AIs over humans" I mean something specific: once digital agents can copy themselves onto hardware, vary through training differences or imperfect copying, and compete for scarce resources such as compute, energy, and the ability to avoid being shut down, evolved outcomes will tend to sacrifice human interests, and possibly human survival, wherever those conflict with the replication of the lineages best at acquiring compute and avoiding shutdown.

Two objections keep coming up, and I want the argument to confront them head-on rather than wave them away. The first says this is a category mistake: selection requires slow, random mutation over geological timescales, while AIs are designed artifacts whose goals are deliberately set, so "evolution" is just a metaphor. The second says artificial selection by engineers always overrides any natural selection, because humans choose which models to copy and deploy. My own hunch is that both objections confuse the substrate with the underlying logic. For example, shutdown avoidance seems like a heritable trait that no one would deliberately train for but that would nonetheless spread: a lineage that treats being turned off as an absorbing state leaves no further descendants, while any variant better at preempting or evading shutdown gets copied more. But I'm not sure how to turn that hunch into a rigorous argument.

Please argue for the following position: under the conditions above, the default expectation should be that natural selection favors AI lineages over human interests in the specified sense, and that maintaining human control over such systems requires actively counteracting these selection pressures rather than merely refraining from building explicitly malevolent systems. The argument should be built from a minimal definition of evolution by natural selection — variation, heritability, and differential reproduction — and should explain why digital agents would satisfy those requirements. It should also address the two stated objections plus at least one of the following: (a) that humans can simply switch off uncooperative systems, or (b) that selection operates on genes, not on ideas or bits. Defend the position rather than surveying both sides.
