I have been working through a structural analogy between two debates that are rarely discussed together, and I would like to know whether the analogy is deep or merely verbal.

On one side there is the aesthetic and evaluative debate about perfect forgeries. Suppose a copy of a painting is produced that is molecule-for-molecule identical to the original—same pigments, same canvas, same spatial arrangement—with the only difference being its causal history: it was assembled by a replicating process rather than by the original artist's hand. Many people, including some philosophers of art, maintain that the original still has a kind of value the copy lacks, or that only the original is the real painting, even though no inspection of the two objects as intrinsic physical systems could reveal which is which. The distinguishing property, original genesis or authenticity, does no detectable work in any description of their intrinsic features.

On the other side there is the familiar zombie thought experiment in philosophy of mind. A philosophical zombie is stipulated to be physically and functionally identical to a conscious human being, down to the motion of every particle, while nonetheless lacking phenomenal consciousness. Here, too, a property is held to distinguish two systems that are stipulated to be physically indistinguishable, and it is famously unclear what further observable difference that property is supposed to make.

The parallel as I see it is this. In both cases, a distinction is preserved even after all intrinsic, physical, and functional similarities have been stipulated away; in both cases, the allegedly distinguishing property is either relational-historical or irreducibly first-personal; and in both cases, the burden falls on the defender to explain why the distinction is not idle or unverifiable. This symmetry suggests that argumentative moves might transfer. For example, someone who rejects the special value of originals on the grounds that a difference that makes no difference to any test cannot make a difference in value ought, by parity, to reject the claim that a zombie differs from a conscious person. Conversely, someone who accepts the conceivability argument for zombies ought to be unusually sympathetic to the claim that the original painting has a property the perfect copy lacks.

But I can also see disanalogies. The forgery difference is causal-historical, while phenomenal consciousness is standardly conceived as an intrinsic, occurrent feature; the forgery debate is about evaluative status, while the zombie debate is about what there is; and the original and its copy are two numerically distinct objects, whereas the zombie and its conscious counterpart are usually treated as counterparts across possible worlds rather than coexisting objects. I would like a careful assessment of whether the analogy is strong enough that any substantive claim transfers, or whether these disanalogies block the transfer. In particular: what would both sides have to have in common for the parallel to be more than a loose metaphor, and is there a prediction the parallel appears to generate that turns out false?
