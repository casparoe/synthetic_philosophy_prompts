Question: In his discussion of the conflict between impartial moral demands and the agent's own commitments, Bernard Williams develops an objection centered on personal integrity. Explain the two main examples he uses for this purpose — the case of Jim and the captives, and the case of the man whose wife is drowning — and state precisely what each is meant to show. Conclude by saying what kind of objection to utilitarianism Williams takes himself to be making.

Student answer: Williams's integrity objection targets utilitarianism's requirement that agents always act so as to maximize aggregate welfare. His first main example is Jim and the Indians: Jim arrives in a village where a captain is about to execute twenty prisoners, and the captain offers to spare the nineteen if Jim will shoot one himself. Utilitarianism says Jim should shoot, since this produces less death. Williams objects that this answer treats Jim's agency as a mere causal channel for bringing about the best outcome and leaves no room for the special relation he stands in to his own actions. The second example is the man whose wife and a stranger are both drowning. Williams says utilitarianism is too demanding here, because it would require the man to save the stranger rather than his wife whenever the stranger's contribution to aggregate welfare would be slightly larger, for example if the stranger were a doctor. The man should save his wife simply because she is his wife; if he added the further thought that saving one's wife is permitted by an impartial rule, that would be one thought too many. This shows that Williams is a deontologist who rejects consequentialism because it would force agents to violate their own deepest commitments in the name of the greater good.
