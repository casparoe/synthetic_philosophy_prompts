I am a philosophy professor, and I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of probability. My students have already worked through the Kolmogorov axioms and can compute elementary probabilities, but they have not seen algorithmic information theory or recursion theory. Next week I want to devote one seventy-five-minute session to a question that sounds simple and is not: whether it makes sense to call a particular finite sequence random. I would like you to plan that session rather than write the lecture itself. My aim is not for the students to settle on an answer but to see why the answer shifts once one distinguishes randomness as a property of a generating process, randomness as incompressibility of a finite string, and randomness as a property of infinite sequences that pass all computable statistical tests. In particular, I want them to leave understanding why the finite-sequence question has no answer until one has said what random is being asked to do.

The plan should specify the order in which the ideas must be introduced and what each later step depends on, so the shifts do not feel like qualifications bolted on at the end. It should build the session around a single binary string short enough to write on the board and inspect by hand; say what that string must have and must lack to move the class from their first intuitions to the formal distinctions. Anticipate the misconceptions students will bring or form—for instance, that a patterned string could not have come from a fair process, or that a string that looks unpatterned is therefore random—and say where in the sequence each should be headed off. I also want to know what you would deliberately leave out at this level and why, and one closing question or small exercise that would show me whether students have grasped the issue rather than merely memorized the three definitions. Do not include a reading list or citations.
