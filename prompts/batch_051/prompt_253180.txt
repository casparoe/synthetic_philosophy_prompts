I was skimming William Morton Wheeler's old paper in the Journal of Morphology—the 1911 one where he argues the ant colony is best treated as a single organism, not as a bunch of separate ants. If Im reading him right, he's not just making a metaphor: he thinks the individual ant is more like a cell in a larger body, with the queen as the reproductive tissue and the workers as the somatic tissue, and that the colony as a whole is the unit natural selection really acts on. The bit that got me stuck was his claim that the colony has an individuality of its own, over and above the ants.

So here's the thing. A classic Ship of Theseus problem: if you replace every plank on the ship, one by one over a season, is it the same ship? Now run that on Wheeler's colony. In a lot of ant colonies, the workers are turning over every few weeks, and even queens get replaced on colony-level timescales, while the nest site, territory, and colony-level traits—aggression, foraging style, disease resistance—seem stable. If Wheeler is right that the colony is the real individual, what persists through the replacement? Is the standard answer for an animal body (cell turnover but continuity of form, function, and repair) just transferable to the colony, or does the fact that ants are themselves independently alive, can reproduce in some species, and can leave and join another colony break the analogy? And would your answer change for a coral holobiont, where the clonal polyps are genetically identical but the symbiotic algae population is a shifting, partly external/acquired community?
