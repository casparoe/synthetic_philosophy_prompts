I'm an undergraduate in a course on the ethics of lying, and we've spent several weeks on Augustine's absolutism: his view that every lie is a sin, including the helpful lie told to save a life or protect an innocent person, and that in hard cases the right response is to keep silent or accept the harm rather than speak falsely. I'm writing a paper on what would actually happen if a society tried to live by that rule, and I want a careful, reasoned prediction rather than a survey of the literature.

Answer as an expert in Augustine's ethics, moral psychology, and institutional design. Before you settle on a prediction, think through the main domains of social life where truth-telling does real work, and identify the three or four where an absolute prohibition on lying would bite hardest.

The scenario: imagine that, beginning in 2026, a modern liberal democracy roughly like contemporary Canada or the Netherlands succeeds in making Augustine's prohibition on lying a genuinely internalized social norm. By this I mean the great majority of citizens come to regard lying as categorically wrong, teach this to children, and enforce it through guilt and social disapproval rather than merely through the legal system. Nothing else about human nature, institutions, or the law changes.

Give me your reasoned prediction of how this society looks after forty years, covering at least four of these domains: medicine, law enforcement and criminal justice, diplomacy and international relations, parenting, advertising and public relations, journalism, and ordinary social courtesies. For each domain, say which practices survive by shifting to silence, evasion, or literally true but misleading speech; which practices collapse or go underground; and what compensatory norms or institutions, if any, arise.

Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.

As you reason, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know Augustine. For example, it is settled that he condemned even the lie told to save a life; it is contested how much room his definition of lying leaves for silence and for speech that is literally true but misleading, and how far his absolutism depends on theological premises rather than on considerations a secular society could share. Do not present a contested scholarly reading as the consensus; if one of your predictions depends on a particular reading of Augustine, flag it.

Finally, tell me which single prediction you are least confident in, and what evidence about real-world communities that have tried to enforce strong truth-telling norms would do most to change your mind.
