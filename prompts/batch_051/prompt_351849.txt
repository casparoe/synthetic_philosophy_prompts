I've been co-organizing a reading group on the philosophy of legal evidence, and after months of argument two of our members have reached the point where they keep talking past each other about naked statistical evidence. One is a formal epistemologist who thinks the law's resistance to naked statistics is mostly a policy overlay on otherwise straightforward probabilistic reasoning. The other is a criminal-law theorist who thinks there is something episodic and epistemically defective, not merely administratively inconvenient, about convicting someone or imposing liability on bare base rates. Both know the standard hypotheticals cold — the blue bus case, the rodeo gatecrasher, the cold-hit DNA case — and neither has moved an inch. We have decided the useful thing is for them to produce an adversarial-collaboration document rather than argue past each other any longer.

Your task is to write the joint statement that these two people could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. It should do the following. First, present a numbered agreed-claims section: what they both accept about the formal structure of the blue bus and gatecrasher hypotheticals, including what a rational agent's posterior credence should be on the stipulated statistical evidence alone and what the actual law would do in those stylized cases. Second, state their residual disagreement precisely in a disagreements section or table, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own position — at minimum, one claim about whether naked statistical evidence can in principle satisfy proof beyond reasonable doubt, and a second claim about whether the distinction between statistical and particularized evidence is epistemic or merely pragmatic. Third, identify the honest cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would genuinely move at least one side. Include at least whether a perfectly calibrated but purely statistical witness would pass the same epistemic bar as an eyewitness, whether the individualization worry survives in the civil gatecrasher case where every attendee is individually named, and whether the standard for legal proof should track warranted belief or knowledge. Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete test, case, or argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Where you judge that part of their dispute is merely verbal, say so explicitly and explain the reformulation that dissolves it.

Please act as a philosopher of evidence law who is also fluent in formal epistemology and Bayesian confirmation theory. Before drafting the final statement, take some private brainstorming passes to find formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted, and discard any wording that one side could reasonably refuse to sign. Then present the document itself, with the numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table and a cruxes-and-predictions table.
