I work as a software engineer, so I tend to think about completion in terms of a specification and a test suite: a system is done when it implements the spec and passes the tests, and everything after that is maintenance. When people talk about physics one day being finished, I don't know what the corresponding specification would be. Give me a working definition of a final theory of physics — a criterion I could hold a candidate theory up against — that gets the following cases right.

1. A compact set of equations with no free parameters that has never made a false prediction, including predictions of measurements made only after the theory was proposed, and that gives a determinate account of what the world is made of.
2. Newtonian mechanics: used every day to land spacecraft, but known to be false as a general account of motion.
3. A lookup table — or a trained neural network — that returns the correct result for every experiment ever performed, but compresses nothing and supports no extrapolation to conditions never measured.
4. A compact theory with no free parameters that in principle fixes the answer to every physical question and has never been wrong so far, but whose equations are computationally intractable beyond simple systems, so most of its consequences cannot actually be checked against experiment.
5. A unified theory that is mathematically elegant but is experimentally indistinguishable, at every energy we can plausibly reach, from the less unified theories we already have.
6. A set of prediction rules that has been right about every measurement for a century, while its users cannot agree on what the rules describe — whether particles, fields, or something else are fundamental.

I would call case 1 final and case 2 not final, and I don't have stable views about the middle ones. Propose the definition, run it through all six cases, and tell me where it surprises you. Then say which of your verdicts is least secure, and whether a definition that settles these cases is also strong enough to tell us that we would ever be in a position to know we had reached the end — because a definition that makes a final theory either impossible by construction or trivially easy to achieve won't be useful to me.
