Lately I have been trying to work out a concept I call comparability aversion, and I would like an honest assessment of it. The core claim is that much of the repugnance we feel toward certain markets — kidney sales, paid surrogacy, vote buying, paying jurors — is not primarily aversion to the transfer itself but aversion to the forced commensuration a price creates. A cash price asserts, publicly, that a kidney, a vote, or a pregnancy sits on a single scale with ordinary purchases, and the felt wrong is that assertion rather than the change in who gets what. If that is right, there should be a distinctive signature. Repugnance should weaken when a materially similar allocation is arranged without an explicit common metric: paired kidney donation rather than a payment, civic recognition for jury service rather than a fee, diffuse reciprocal gifts rather than a posted rate. Repugnance should be strongest for goods people otherwise refuse to compare and weakest for goods already traded like any other commodity. The view would also imply that the debate over legalizing such markets is, at bottom, a debate about whether the act of comparison itself degrades, independently of the standard concerns about exploitation, coercion, inequality, or incentives.

What I cannot tell is whether this is a real contribution, a colorful metaphor, or a rediscovery of something already worked out under another name. I also want to know where it breaks. Paired donation is not a sale and honors are not wages, so perhaps my cases fail to isolate the variable I care about. Perhaps the aversion really concerns exploitation or desperation, and the dislike of the price tag is only a downstream symptom. Perhaps comparability is aversive only when it is public and binding, which would make the concept much narrower than I first thought. Is comparability aversion coherent enough to do explanatory work, and what would count against it?
