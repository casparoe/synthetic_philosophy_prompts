I've been reading around how health systems actually allocate resources — QALYs, NICE-style thresholds, pandemic triage scoring, the value of a statistical life — and I've ended up genuinely stuck in a way I normally aren't. The cost-effectiveness framework feels like the only non-arbitrary way to run a health service: budgets are fixed and need isn't, so every treatment you fund is a treatment you've decided is worth more than whatever else the money would have done, and the only defensible rule is to spend each pound where it buys the most health, measured in quality-adjusted life years, without caring whose QALY it is. I even accept what follows: refusing an expensive drug for a indentifiable patient when the same money could save a dozen anonymous people from something boring and treatable, or fund thousands of cataract operations, is not just permissible but required. In the abstract I'm fine with that. But I also have a conviction I can't shake: when the person is in front of you — the next bed during triage, the kid on the fundraising page — declining because the spreadsheet says no feels like the system has priced him and decided he is worth less, and there's something indecent about a health service doing sums over lives like that. The statistical-life stuff sharpens it: I can't see why a death I don't know about should be discounted just because it hasn't got a face yet. I'm not asking for a referee's decision. I want to know what each concession really costs. If the intuition has to go, what exactly is supposed to debunk it — identifiable-victim effects, our moral myopia for the near and particular, a confusion between decision rules and verdicts about worth — and how would I tell that's a genuine debunking rather than a convenient way to keep the maths tidy? If the theory has to bend, where should it bend: a side-constraint on pricing lives, a two-track system for identified and statistical patients, a fair-innings adjustment, or something else? And is there a version of QALY practice that keeps the decision procedure but doesn't require me to treat my own reluctance as bias?
