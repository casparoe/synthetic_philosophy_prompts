I've been drafting an argument that political compromise is underrated, and it now seems to prove too much; I can't find the faulty step.

(1) Suppose two sides each accept a compromise because each cares more about what it would gain than what it would concede. By the standards of their own value systems, each side is made better off. (2) Any transaction that makes both parties better off by their own lights is a Pareto improvement, and a Pareto improvement is not a loss for either party. (3) So a voluntary moral compromise of this kind cannot be criticized as imposing a cost on either side; it is a pure gain from trade between value systems. (4) This should still hold when one side's values include something the other side regards as unjust. Suppose side A cares intensely about keeping a discriminatory policy, while side B regards the policy as unjust but cares less about abolishing it than about winning a separate reform that only A can deliver. (5) If B agrees not to press for abolishing the policy in exchange for A's support on the reform, each side has given up what it values less and received what it values more. (6) By (3), that bargain is a pure gain from trade and cannot be faulted from inside either value system; so a demand of justice can be traded away for enough progress on other moral fronts, and neither party is entitled to complain. (7) That conclusion seems monstrous: it would turn every moral conviction into a preference with a price and let one side's intense attachment to an injustice count as a legitimate bargaining advantage.

Where does this argument go wrong? I would like to keep (1) and (2) if possible, but I want to know what keeping them costs.
