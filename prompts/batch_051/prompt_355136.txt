I teach a graduate seminar on arguments that run out into infinite regresses, and honestly the whole thing has stalled in a way that's starting to bug me. We've spent three weeks on what makes a regress vicious rather than benign, and we keep covering the same ground. Everyone in the room agrees on the mechanics of the examples: in the epistemic regress a belief gets its justification from another belief, which gets its justification from another, and if you never stop the chain has no source of justification at all; in the truth regress 'p' is true, 'that p is true' is true, and so on, and that chain looks harmless; and the instantiation regress — a thing instantiates a property in virtue of standing in some relation to it, but then that relation has to be instantiated in a further relation, and so on — lands differently for different students. Same examples, same structural point, no movement.

My working hypothesis is that we're not actually disagreeing about anything factual, since there's no observation or theorem we're waiting for; we're disagreeing about what an argument has to do. Half the room thinks a regress is vicious only if it bottoms out in a contradiction or a flat impossibility, so an infinite chain is fine as long as each step is well-defined and nothing breaks. The other half thinks that's letting the regress off the hook: for them a regress is vicious when the original claim keeps deferring its own support, so the argument never discharges what it promised even if no individual step fails. And then there's a third camp that says "vicious" just means "this regress refutes the position," which the first two camps complain just re-uses the word to do the work.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of who says what. Is it verbal, is it a substantive difference about whether an infinite chain can complete an explanation, a justification, or a grounding claim, or is it a bedrock difference about what arguments are for? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — is there a distinction we should be drawing, or a better question we should substitute? Please also seperate what is actually settled among people who work on these arguments from what is genuinely contested; I've seen too many people teach students that "vicious regress" has one standard definition, and I don't believe it does.
