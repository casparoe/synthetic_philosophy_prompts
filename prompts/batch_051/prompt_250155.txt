I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on medieval philosophy, sixteen students, one eighty-minute session. We are about to read the seventeenth discussion of al-Ghazālī’s *The Incoherence of the Philosophers*, where he examines the connection between fire and burning cotton. His argument there is that fire and cotton are two distinct things; the existence of the fire does not logically necessitate the burning of the cotton. What we call a cause and its effect are merely events that God has made habitual, and God could create the contact without the burning just as easily as the burning with the contact. The observed order is a divine habit, not a metaphysical necessity.

I want an in-class activity that makes students feel the force of this view before I give the standard objections. I don’t want the activity to begin with the name “occasionalism” or with the cotton-fire example. Instead, I want them to run into the problem themselves: they should try to locate the necessity in an observed causal sequence and discover how quickly they start appealing either to “that’s just the way things are” or to an unstated guarantee of uniformity. By the end of the activity, they should see why al-Ghazālī did not take himself to be denying ordinary expectations, only denying that those expectations come with a metaphysical ground.

Please design a structured simulation or game, with rules, roles, and small non-monetary stakes (e.g., points that affect a group ranking, or a privilege like choosing the discussion order). It should occupy roughly fifty minutes, leaving thirty minutes for debrief. The activity must be runnable in an ordinary classroom without props beyond cards, slips of paper, or a whiteboard. Include a timeline, a role sheet for each participant type, the exact instructions the facilitator gives at each stage, the debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the argument from al-Ghazālī’s discussion, and an honest note on which feature of occasionalism the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator afterward. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it.
