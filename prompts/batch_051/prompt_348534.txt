I work on a small planning team that produces probability estimates for events we actually have to act on: the chance a supplier fails to deliver, the chance a currency move wrecks a project, the chance of local flooding within our planning horizon. After a costly miss last year that I trace directly to an overconfident point estimate, I adopted a rule: when the evidence is sparse, conflicting, or only partly relevant to the case at hand, I refuse to report a single number. I give an interval instead, or, when I have the time, a set of probability functions all compatible with what we know, and I push for decisions that are acceptable under the whole set rather than under one selected midpoint. My manager says this is a way of never being answerable: the organization still has to commit to one course of action, and a determinate expected value has to come from somewhere. I also notice, with some discomfort, that I find the interval attractive partly because nobody can later say "you said sixty percent." I want to know whether my rule is defensible on the merits, a form of ambiguity aversion dressed up as epistemic rigor, or a policy that is fine for deliberation but must be abandoned at the moment of decision. Is there a principled way to separate cases where refusing a point estimate is the right response to imprecision from cases where it is only commitment-phobia? And when I am put on the spot to settle on one number anyway, what should I do with the interval rather than silently picking its midpoint? One further symptom: occasionally learning a relevant fact makes the interval wider rather than narrower, and colleagues treat that as proof the method is broken; I cannot tell whether the widening is a bug in my approach or a sign that it is tracking something real.
