In my world history class this term, we have been studying how the calendar we use was constructed, and two aspects of it seem to me to pull in opposite directions. Our textbooks and teachers now write BCE and CE, which is presented as a neutral and secular system for labeling years. Yet the hinge between the two eras is still the traditional date assigned to the birth of Jesus, and the sequence contains no year zero: 1 BCE is followed immediately by 1 CE. The calendar therefore presents itself as a neutral numbering scheme while keeping a single religious event as the pivot around which every other year is arranged.

I am trying to work out what this practice actually presupposes. Does using this calendar commit someone to the idea that this event is the central axis of history, or to a linear view of historical time with a single turning point rather than a cyclical or plural one? What, if anything, does the absence of a year zero presuppose about whether years are positions in a sequence rather than intervals measured from a true origin? Is the shift from BC/AD to BCE/CE a genuine change in commitment or only a renaming of the same structure? And if an institution genuinely wanted a neutral epoch, what would it have to alter: the starting event, the introduction of a year zero, or something more fundamental about how it counts time? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined.
