I am trying to work out, not in the abstract but through my own verdicts on cases, what principle should govern the state's power to regulate the raising of children: when it may override parents, and when it may screen who becomes a parent. Please treat this as reflective equilibrium done on request: propose the smallest set of principles that captures my verdicts, state precisely what the principles predict in adjacent cases, and say candidly which of my verdicts they cannot accommodate.

A. After a car accident, a fourteen-year-old needs a blood transfusion and will very probably die without it. Her parents refuse consent on religious grounds; a court orders the transfusion over their objection, and she recovers fully. I regard the court's order as right.

B. Parents keep their ten-year-old out of school and raise him in an isolated religious community, teaching him only the skills required for life inside that community. He is not taught to read or calculate or anything from outside it, and his parents intend to bind him to this life. The state intervenes under an educational-neglect statute and requires an education meeting a general standard. I regard the intervention as right.

C. Parents direct their daughter from age six toward elite gymnastics. She trains about twenty-five hours a week, travels to competitions, and is schooled at home around the training schedule. By the sport's standards she is thriving and says she loves it, but she has almost no time for friendships, free play, or unscheduled days. I do not think the state should intervene, though the situation troubles me.

D. A deaf couple active in signing Deaf culture deliberately selects a sperm donor with congenital deafness so that the child will be born deaf into their language and community. I judge the choice wrong, but I am uncertain whether the state should prevent it through law, and I want my principle to explain that hesitation.

E. The state licenses adoptive parents through home studies and background checks, approving only those who meet a threshold of stability, safety, and caregiving competence. I think this is fine.

F. A legislator proposes that all adults who wish to conceive a child must first pass a basic child-development course and submit to a home visit before attempting to become parents. I think this is not fine, although I cannot yet say why it differs from E.

G. Parents raise their children on a strict whole-food vegan diet that the pediatrician confirms is nutritionally adequate; the children are growing normally and participate fully in school. I think this is fine, whether or not I share their commitments.

The tentative principle I have been using is something like this: the state may override parental decisions only when those decisions would prevent the child from becoming an adult with a reasonable range of possible lives open to her and the capacities needed to choose among them, and otherwise the state must leave families alone. I already see that this principle may not fit E and F, where the state acts before harm and before a child exists, and it may not fit D, where a flourishing community would welcome the child. Test the principle against the cases, identify where it fails, and give me the smallest repair; if no single principle works, give me the small set that does.

Finally, tell me what your repaired account predicts for a case I have not settled. A nonreligious family raises its child off-grid: no formal school and no prescribed curriculum, but extensive practical skills, a large library, and regular contact with the outside world, so that at eighteen she is literate and capable of entering ordinary society if she chooses. Does the state's compulsory-education power reach that family, and which principle decides the question?
