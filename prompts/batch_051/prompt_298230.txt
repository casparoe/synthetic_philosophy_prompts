Hey — I'm a PM at a small fintech startup that makes a savings app, mostly for people who live paycheck to paycheck. Every couple of weeks someone on the team proposes a new "commitment feature" — lock your money til a date, auto-transfer into a locked jar, charge yourself a penalty if you withdraw early, get a friend to hold you accountable, that kind of thing. We have no behavioral economist on staff, and we need a way to triage these ideas in a 30-minute meeting before we greenlight anything; final sign-off is me plus the head of product. The stakes are medium-high: a bad feature can trap someone with no cash when there car breaks down, and a good one can be the difference between a user actually saving and not.

Can you write us a reusable decision procedure that operationalizes the idea behind hyperbolic discounting and present bias — that people discount the near future extra steeply, so they make long-run plans that their short-run self later overturns? I want a procedure a product team (PM, designer, two engineers) can run without theory background: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand off to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Make it actually respect the difference between a sophisticated user who knows they'll cave later and a naive one who doesn't, and live with the fact that opt-in features mostly get adopted by the sophisticated ones. It also has to handle hard cases: hard locks vs. reversible commitments vs. penalties, overcommitment when a genuine emergency shows up, features that are really just friction or shame, and features that make money off the user's failed self-control.

Then run the procedure on three actual proposals on our board: (1) a "lockbox" account where the user picks a date and genuinely cannot access the money before then, no exceptions; (2) a round-ups feature that silently sweeps spare change into a locked jar, and the lock can be broken only after a 48-hour waiting period; and (3) a penalty account where withdrawing before your goal date costs 5% of the amount, with half of that fee going to us. I want at least one of the verdicts to be something I'm not going to like — honestly I think #3 might be our highest-revenue idea — and I want a clear statement of what the procedure can't do (e.g., it probably can't fix the right penalty size or lock length without data, and it can't settle the paternalism question for us).
