I have been trying to decide how seriously to take a specific claim about the aesthetics of games, and I would like a determinate credence rather than the usual hedge that everything depends on one's aesthetic theory. Let me stipulate that I will use "ludic agency" for the player's exercise of choice within a designed space of possibilities: the player, rather than the designer, selects among constrained options; those selections alter the state of the work; and they determine which further contents of the work become available. The thesis on which I want your credence is this: ludic agency is a primary aesthetic property of at least one actual game—that is, a property that can itself make the work beautiful, expressive, or meaningful, and that is not reducible to the aesthetic properties of the game's story, images, sound, or any other element that could be fully preserved in a non-interactive recording. Put another way: a complete, faithful film of the game's best playthrough would not merely be less pleasurable or less immersive; it would be a substantially lesser work as an aesthetic object, because something doing genuine aesthetic work would be missing. To fix intuitions, consider an otherwise austere game—graphically plain and without music—whose branching choices are so composed that the accumulating structure of permitted and foreclosed futures has a contour of the kind one might otherwise attribute to musical form. The thesis says that contour is an aesthetic property of the work itself, not merely a source of felt control.

I am not asking for a favourite example or a survey of positions. I want a number, with the reasoning decomposed. Please assign probabilities to the following sub-claims, each with a single line of justification, and then show how they combine into your final credence: (a) that aesthetic properties can include relational properties of the appreciator's own activity or of what the work affords, rather than only sensory or representational features; (b) that, if (a) holds, the exercise of choice under designed constraint is the right kind of activity to realise such properties, rather than merely causing pleasant feelings of agency, tension, or competence of the sort produced by non-artistic puzzles; (c) that, if (a) and (b) hold, at least one actual game has made agency do enough of the aesthetic work to count as primary rather than merely instrumental to narrative or audiovisual content; and (d) any residual term your estimate depends on—for instance, a claim about how much composed structure an activity must have before its aesthetic qualities attach to the work rather than to the player's private experience. Then tell me which of these terms you are least confident in, what evidence, argument, or critical practice would move that term the most, and what your final credence is after the combination. If you find "primary aesthetic property" too vague to take a credence in, give me the most charitable precisification you are willing to defend, and the credence under that precisification.
