I'm a postdoc in psychology, and over the last couple years I've drifted into a policy I'd like someone to pressure-test. After watching several of my own p = .04 findings quietly fall apart when we tried to replicate them, I now basically treat a study as evidence only if it was preregistered and came out at p < .05; if it lands at .06 or .07, I mentally round it to zero and move on. Me and my collaborator—who's a Bayesian—keep fighting about this. He says the p-value is a continuous measure and throwing away everything below an arbitrary line is exactly the cliff-edge thinking that got the field into trouble. I get his point in the abstract. But in practice, the studies that come in just under .05 are precisely the ones most likely to be p-hacked or optional-stopped, and even preregistration doesn't eliminate all the researcher degrees of freedom in analysis choices. So the cutoff feels less like a report of what the evidence says and more like a screening rule for a contaminated evidence stream—a filter, not a probability report. The thing that's making me ask now is that I caught myself being inconsistent: I refused to update on a p = .06 study, then leaned on a p = .04 result from a huge noisy sample as if it had passed some deeper test. Is my policy defensible, or is it just a falsificationist ritual? Is there a version of it that keeps the protection without treating a continuous quantity as a cliff, and how should someone like me actually use a p = .06 preregistered result when deciding what to believe and what to build my next experiment on?
