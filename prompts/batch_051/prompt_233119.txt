One for the dataset: I'm teaching Dune (the first novel, not the films) to a reading group and I keep trying to read it through Braudel's longue durée — the idea that the deepest historical explanation isn't the event or the great individual but the slow-moving structures of geography, climate, demography, and long-run material life, with events as little more than surface foam on those currents. Arrakis seems almost perversely built for this: the desert and the worm-spice cycle run on geological timescales, the Fremen are working a multi-generation terraforming project, and even Paul's choices are tightly constrained by the ecology and the spice economy. But the book is also a great-man story, and at times it lets a single act — Paul's threat to destroy the spice cycle, say — reach down and alter a deep structure, which Braudel would probably call exactly the wrong way round. So read the novel against Braudel's distinction between the event, the conjoncture, and the longue durée. Where does the novel genuinely dramatise slow-structure explanation, where does it fall back on events and individuals, and where does it half-consciously blur the two? Does it take a side? I'm less after scene-spotting than a verdict on what Herbert's novel, read this way, adds to the debate about whether long-run structures explain history or merely set the stage for individuals.
