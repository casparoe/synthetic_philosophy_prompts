I'm writing a novel where the main character chairs a scare-resources board at a regional hospital, and I've built the board's track record over the past year so the big decision in chapter nine reads as a test of character instead of a philosophy lecture. I could use some help making the chapter's logic honest, so here's the history as I've written it. Ruling one: during the winter surge, when there was one ventilator left, the board gave it to a forty-seven-year-old with an expected quality-adjusted life-year score of seven, over a seventy-one-year-old whose score was about three; the chair's note afterwards says the score tracks expected benefit, not a judgement about which life matters more. Ruling two: at a budget vote, the board put four hundred thousand into a new enzyme-replacement therapy for a named six-year-old, projected at fourteen QALYs, instead of a geriatric falls-prevention program that the analysts said would prevent three statistical deaths among patients averaging eighty-four, maybe four QALYs total, none of them identifiable; a standard value-of-a-statistical-life figure would have priced those three deaths at several times fourteen QALYs, and the minutes lean on both the identifiable child and the softness of the population estimate. Ruling three: the board refused to keep funding a three-hundred-thousand-a-year drug for an eighty-six-year-old that was buying roughly 0.4 QALYs per year, even after the family appealed. Ruling four: last month, for a scarce infusion slot, the board picked a thirty-four-year-old with a 5.5 expected-QALY score over a sixty-two-year-old with a 6.1, on the ground that scores within 0.7 of one another were 'operationally indistinguishable' and that inside that band the younger patient got the slot.

The chapter nine decision is this: there's one slot in a new immunotherapy program, and the finalists are a fifty-eight-year-old whose expected-QALY estimate is 7.0 and a nineteen-year-old whose estimate is 6.2. If the board wants to be consistent with its own precedents, what should it decide? Which of the four earlier rulings is the closest analog, and if the record genuinely underdetermines the call, what principle would the board be adopting with each choice—and what would a defender of the other principle say the board is really smuggling in?
