For decades the debate over material constitution has circled one kind of case: a lump of clay is shaped into a statue, and the lump and the statue coincide throughout the statue's existence while appearing to differ modally and temporally — the lump could survive squashing, the statue could not; the statue came into being with the shaping, the lump predated it. Identity theorists, pluralists about coincident objects, and four-dimensionalists all have well-worn replies, yet the dialectic has long been static: the same moves are made, the same intuitions traded, no side gains ground. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict. My working hypothesis is that the sides are not answering one counting question: the identity theorist asks whether 'the statue' and 'the lump' refer to one thing under two descriptions, while the pluralist asks how many objects the relevant sortals yield in that region, so the conflict is partly an artifact of switching count criteria between arguments. Is that right? If not, where do they share a question and genuinely disagree — over the de re modal facts, over what fixes persistence conditions, over whether sortal-relative identity is coherent, or over which cases count as data — and what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal constraint on counting, a decisive case, or an argument that the question is ill-posed?
