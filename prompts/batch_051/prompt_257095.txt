I've been reading Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief" and one line in the shipowner part keeps bugging me. He writes:

"The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him."

I get the shipowner case: he stifled doubts and sent an unsafe ship. But when I turn that into a general principle it seems to prove too much. Here's the argument as I've reconstructed it:

(1) Beliefs are not purely private; they shape how I later act, so a badly formed belief can harm other people.
(2) If my belief can harm others, I owe it to them to form it only on sufficient evidence that I have honestly examined. That's the quoted point: wrongness attaches to the origin/evidence, not to truth.
(3) So for any belief, I have a moral duty not to hold it unless I have sufficient evidence.
(4) But ordinary life is full of beliefs we hold before checking: trusting a friend who says they didn't lie, trusting a fuzzy memory, thinking your spouse is faithful from years of small signs, or going along with a political claim because people around you repeat it. We often can't give the evidence, and investigating fully would be obsessive or socially destructive.
(5) By (3) and (4), almost everyone is doing wrong almost all the time just by having normal trusting beliefs. If the principle is strict, it seems to demand doubting friends, memory, and even the reasoning I use to check anything.
(6) That conclusion feels too strong.

I want to keep the idea that the shipowner is blameworthy and that at least some beliefs are wrong because of how they were formed. But I don't want to say that trusting a friend or my own memory is a sin against mankind. Where does the argument go wrong? Which premise is weakest, and what do the standard fixes have to give up? Is the problem that (1) overstates how much beliefs leak into action, that (2) ignores the costs of inquiry, that "sufficient evidence" needs a lower or contextual standard, or that belief isn't voluntary enough for "duty" to apply? Once the fix is in, how much of Clifford's principle survives—and does doubt still count as a duty?
