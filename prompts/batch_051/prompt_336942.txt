I retired from thirty years in analytical chemistry three years ago, and I have been reading philosophy in an unsystematic way since then; seventeenth-century rationalist ethics has become something of an obsession. Last month I read the Hatata of the Ethiopian philosopher Zera Yacob, and I have been trying to decide how much of his method it is fair to impose on people in a modern plural society. As I understand him, the center of the method is this: the fact that the inherited moral traditions contradict one another is itself a reason not to accept any of them on authority; a sincere inquirer, beginning from the intelligence that the creator has planted in every person, can re-derive the basic rules of conduct from her own reason; and a claim that cannot survive that test is to be set aside rather than accepted on the standing of its source. I volunteer as a lay member of a community ethics advisory panel for a regional health authority in a city with many immigrant and faith communities. About once a month the panel confronts a dispute of the following form: a planned screening program, vaccination campaign, or care pathway is opposed by representatives of a community, and the panel must decide what weight to give to statements such as 'our tradition forbids it' or 'our religious authorities have not approved it.' The panel currently has no shared procedure for deciding whether such appeals to custom should count as independent reasons in the deliberation, as evidence about the likely acceptability of an intervention, as requests for accommodation, or as something else.

Please write a decision procedure, built as faithfully as you can on the Yacob-like commitment to reason over deference, that the panel can actually run in the twenty minutes before a vote and that panel members with no philosophical training can use. The procedure should state the questions to ask in order, what would count as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the issue is one of values rather than facts. It should also say plainly where this method is likely to be unfair to people who decline to make their case in secular-reason terms, and what the procedure cannot settle. Then run the procedure on three cases: a diaspora association opposing a diabetes screening program on the ground that no community elder has granted approval; an objection to an organ-donation opt-out policy that is expressed in theological vocabulary but is plausibly translatable into a harms-and-benefits argument; and an objection raised by one of our own panel members, who argues that the board should simply defer to the clinical guidelines issued by the health authority itself. The last case matters to me because I suspect the method will cut against scientific authority as much as against religious authority, and I want to see that cost before I recommend adopting it.
