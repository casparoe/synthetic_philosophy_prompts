In the philosophy of photography there is a well-known argument that photographs are transparent in a way hand-made pictures are not: that when we look at a photograph, we are seeing through it to the photographed object itself, not merely seeing a depiction of it. The argument rests on the thought that a photograph is produced by a belief-independent causal channel running from scene to image, roughly the way a mirror or a telescope carries information from the world to the eye, whereas a drawing is mediated at every point by what the artist believes, intends, and selects. On this view, the decisive difference is not how the picture looks but what kind of causal history it has.

I am trying to determine whether that argument survives how images are actually produced now. A photograph taken on a modern phone is not a single optical trace: it is assembled from several exposures, tone-mapped, denoised, its depth reconstructed by algorithms, and partly edited before the user sees it. There is still a scene in front of a lens and a causal chain from scene to file, but the chain now runs through processes that contribute information beyond what the lens recorded. Further along the spectrum, text-to-image systems produce pictures for which there is no single scene in front of a lens at all, although the output is causally downstream of a vast archive of images. So: does the transparency thesis still go through for the computational photograph, or is it quietly tied to an obsolete picture of photography as a mechanically recorded trace? Which premise is actually under pressure—the belief-independence of the process, the continuity of the causal chain, or the claim that the relevant seeing is of the original scene—and is there a repaired version that preserves transparency for some digital images but not others? I am most interested in what happens to the argument itself, not in a taxonomy of imaging technologies.
