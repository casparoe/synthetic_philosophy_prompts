I've been reporting on the spread of algorithmic exam-proctoring tools, and while sitting in on a university working group I heard an exchange I have not been able to adjudicate. The participants kept returning to a single hypothetical. A proctoring system suffers a logging failure and cannot tie suspicious activity to individual students. For one exam it reports only that, of twenty-five test-takers in a room, twenty used unauthorized assistance at some point; it does not identify which twenty. The honor code uses a preponderance standard, and the committee is considering whether to sanction every student in the room on the strength of that aggregate figure alone.

The first participant, a legal scholar, argued that the case should be straightforward: for any given student the probability of cheating is 0.8, which clears preponderance; whatever one might demand at the criminal standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, honor proceedings are not criminal trials, and declining to sanction all twenty-five means letting twenty cheaters escape because the system lost the identifying data. The second participant, a philosopher, replied that no evidence had been offered about any particular student: the 0.8 figure is a property of the group and would be exactly the same even if a given student were innocent. That, she said, is why fact-finders have been uneasy with naked statistical evidence, as in the familiar blue-bus and gatecrasher cases, where a plaintiff's only proof against a bus company is that it operates most of the buses in town, or where most spectators at a rodeo are known to have crashed the gate. The legal scholar answered that those cases reflect a psychological aversion rather than a principled distinction: DNA evidence is also probabilistic, yet courts admit it routinely, and if 0.8 is good enough for civil liability it is good enough here. The philosopher countered that DNA evidence reaches the particular event through a physical chain, from a sample at this scene to this body, whereas the proctoring report contains no such chain to any individual. The legal scholar said that insisting on a causal chain is question-begging, because a vivid causal story can mislead, and all that should matter is the posterior probability of wrongdoing. The philosopher conceded that the posterior matters but denied that it is the only thing that matters: a finding of misconduct must be a finding about what this student did, not about what students in this room tend to have done. The legal scholar then asked whether she would let twenty cheaters go unpunished, and if not, what she would do instead; the session ended before she answered. I would like a point-by-point adjudication of the exchange: who is right about which specific claim, where each side overreaches, what both parties are missing, and how a committee could proceed if it refuses both to sanction students on bare group statistics and to ignore strong aggregate evidence.
