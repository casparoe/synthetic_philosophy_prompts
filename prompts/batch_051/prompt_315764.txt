I want to test a specific passage from your own Essay, not your better-known later sections about persons and consciousness unless they are forced on you. You write in An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter XXVII, “Of Identity and Diversity”:

“From whence it follows, that one thing cannot have two beginnings of existence, nor two things one beginning ; it being impossible for two things of the same kind to be or exist in the same instant, in the very same place ; or one and the same thing in different places. That, therefore, that had one beginning, is the same thing ; and that which had a different beginning in time and place from that, is not the same, but diverse.”

Here is my case: suppose a person undergoes a slow replacement of her brain. One at a time, each neuron is replaced by a manufactured part that performs exactly the same operations, while the person remains awake, keeps talking, keeps her memories and habits, and no one observing her can detect any interruption. At the end none of the original material parts of the brain remain. Now by the words you just wrote, the thing that had one beginning is the same, and the thing with a different beginning is diverse. But does this gradually replaced person have a new beginning at some point, or does she keep the original beginning by continuity? If you say she keeps the original beginning and is therefore the same thing, how do you avoid the implication that the very same being can survive the total loss of its original material parts when you also wrote that if one atom be taken away or one new one added, a mass is no longer the same body? If instead you say she is diverse from the original, at exactly which replacement did the new beginning occur, given that no one could say at the time that a new thing had begun?
