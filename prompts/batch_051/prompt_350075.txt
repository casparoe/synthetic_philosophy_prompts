Suppose that in 1995, the Republic of Meridia — a consolidated parliamentary democracy of roughly thirty million people with proportional representation, strong civic registers, an aging native-born population, and, at the time, no pressing constitutional crisis — adopted in a single reform package three interlocking changes to its electoral system. The voting age was lowered from eighteen to sixteen. The franchise was extended to every lawful resident aged sixteen or older who had lived in the country for at least twelve months, regardless of citizenship, including permanent residents and holders of renewable work visas but excluding short-term visitors and tourists. Voting was made compulsory for every eligible person, with non-voting penalized by a small fine collected administratively through the tax system rather than through criminal courts. Registration was made automatic and mobile, derived from the residence records already maintained for identity documents. At the time, roughly one resident in seven was a non-citizen, concentrated in the larger cities and in agricultural regions; the native-born citizen population was aging; no other major constitutional change was adopted simultaneously; and no political party had anticipated how the reforms would interact.

Set aside the question of whether these reforms were wise or just. Write a reasoned prediction of how Meridia's political system looks thirty years later, in 2025. Trace the mechanisms in sequence: who actually enters the electorate, with what turnout, and how their participation diverges from the expectations of the reformers; how parties, candidates, interest groups, and media reposition themselves; which policy questions gain or lose salience (secondary and vocational education, housing, immigration enforcement, pensions, language requirements, local services, and the like); and how campaign strategy, political communication, and coalition politics adapt when almost the whole adult resident population is a dependable electoral audience. Also address second-order effects on party finance, citizenship policy, administrative costs, civic culture, and the perceived legitimacy of elections. For each major link in the argument, indicate whether the predicted effect is robust or speculative, and state what would have to be different about Meridia for that prediction to be wrong.
