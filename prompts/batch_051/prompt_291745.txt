I've been re-reading some of the older debates about whether culture can be a cause of large historical outcomes, in particular the arguments around the rise of modern capitalism, and I've noticed a pattern in how people—including, I suspect, me, and most of the rationalist- and economics-adjacent forums I read—allocate explanatory scrutiny. When someone proposes a material or institutional cause, the claim is granted causal standing almost by default. A historian can say that an early industrializing region got there because wages were high, because secure property rights lowered the cost of long-term investment, or because cheap coal made mechanization profitable, and the discussion moves straight to questions of magnitude and timing. Hardly anyone demands that the author prove the mechanism isn't just a redescription, or that the proposed cause was measured independently of the outcome, or that it wasn't itself downstream of something deeper. But when someone proposes a cultural cause—say, the Weberian line that a particular religious ethic of thrift, literacy, and methodical work created the dispositions on which early capitalism ran—the room suddenly tightens. People want independent measurement of the values, explicit microfoundations, evidence that the beliefs are not rationalizations of prior material interests, and a falsifiable specification of the mechanism. The same audience that was content with a plausible material story now behaves as though the cultural story is guilty until proven innocent.

That is a double standard, and I want to know whether anything actually justifies it. Give me the strongest principle you can construct that separates cultural-value explanations from material-incentive and institutional explanations in a way that would license this asymmetry—something in the neighborhood of measurement independence, causal priority, or the idea that incentives screen off beliefs—but do not just gesture at it; push it against nearby cases. For instance, consider an incentive explanation that is just as post hoc as the cultural one it competes with, or a cultural explanation in which the values are independently documented, precede the outcome by a generation or two, and can be shown to propagate through specific institutions such as schools or churches. Does the principle survive those cases, or does it begin to look like prejudice in favor of the causal vocabulary we happen to prefer? End with an honest verdict: should the asymmetry be kept, reversed, or given up, and what would a symmetric standard for admitting cultural and material causes look like?
