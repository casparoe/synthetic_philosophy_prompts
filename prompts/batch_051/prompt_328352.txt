So my studdy group has been chewing on this argument, and I can't find the exit. A friend built it and says it forces a choice: either you can bootstrap your way to trusting any source whatever, or you can't even trust your own eyes. I'd rather keep trusting my eyes, but the argument keeps making me say things I don't believe.

Here it is step by step.

(1) I can know that the wall in front of me is red just by looking at it, on the strength of its looking red, without already knowing in advance that my color vision is reliable. (If you deny this, it's hard to see how I ever know anything by looking.)

(2) In that situation I also know that the wall looked red to me. And if the wall is red and it looked red to me, then this particular color appearance was accurate — that instance of my color vision got it right.

(3) I can do the same thing case by case: the stop sign, the tomato, the fire hydrant, whatever. In each case I know the thing is the color it is by using my color vision, I know my color vision said it was that color, so I know my color vision got that case right. After enough cases, I can infer by plain induction that my color vision is reliable.

(4) So I can come to know, using nothing but my color vision itself, that my color vision is reliable. No independent check — no other people, no touching, no optometrist, no instrument — is needed.

(5) And there's nothing special about vision in the recipe. The same reasoning works for any source you form beliefs on: a horoscope, a dowsing rod, a tarot deck. Trust the source in enough particular cases, note that it "got them right" (because you're using it as your way of knowing), and infer that the source is reliable.

(6) But that can't be right. A source can't certify itself just by being used. If my only reason to think the dowsing rod works is that it said there's water and I believed there was water because it said so, then the conclusion "the rod is reliable" is worthless — I've chased my own tail.

(7) So (1) must be false: you need independent reasons to trust a source before its outputs can give you knowledge. But then ordinary perceptual knowledge collapses, since any independent evidence I could give for the reliability of my senses would itself come from my senses.

Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather reject something in (3) or (5) than give up (1), but I can't see why the induction fails when every premise in it is something I know. Is the move from "each particular appearance was accurate" to "my color vision is reliable" okay when the sample was collected by the very faculty being tested? And whatever I say about the dowsing rod — does it apply equally to my eyes, or is there a differenece of principle?
