In my former life as a research scientist, I would have dismissed the following as speculation without a measurement model. I have been reading in the philosophy of history and the study of collective memory, and I keep meeting a claim that strikes me as either importantly true or conveniently vague: that a community's shared memory and the documentary record answer different questions, so that when they conflict the record does not automatically defeat the memory. On this view the record delivers one kind of accuracy — roughly, what happened, under standards of independent verification — while collective memory delivers another, namely what the event continues to mean for the community, registered in the surviving patterns of attention, obligation, and avoidance. The apparent factual errors in memory would then be local failures of description riding on a largely faithful transmission of relevance.

I would like this claim subjected to the discipline it has so far escaped. Take it seriously as something that could turn out to be false. Design the strongest feasible test you can of the proposition that collective memory transmits the significance of a contested event independently of its descriptive accuracy. You may assume field conditions, access to communities with living memory of a documented event, and ordinary interview and behavioural measures; you may not assume that significance can be read off brain states or measured directly. Say exactly what observation or measurement would count, what pattern of results would favour the different-questions view, and what pattern would favour the simpler view that divergent memory is merely corrupted history. Then be honest about what even a strong result would leave unsettled — in particular, whether the residual disagreement is a limit of our instruments or a defect in the idea that memory and record could conflict without one being wrong. Aim for the precision of a grant panel's methods section.
