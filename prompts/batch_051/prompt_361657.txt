Had a long argument with a friend last night about the meta-problem of consciousness — not the hard problem itself, but why the hard problem seems so hard. Why do we have this stubborn intuition that a complete physical/functional account of the brain would still leave something out? Why do the Mary and zombie thought experiments feel so forceful even to people who think, on reflection, that they're bad arguments? Why does the explanatory gap feel like a gap in the world rather than a gap in our current explanations? The friend thought the only thing an account has to explain is why we say the words; I think that's obviously too thin, and we never got too an actual list of what would settle it.

So before I read another proposal, write me the desiderata. What would an adequate solution to the meta-problem of consciousness actually have to get right? I want the full spec: the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must handle, the distinctions it can't collapse, with why each one is a requirement. And for each, tell me if it's non-negotiable or just a preference of one camp. E.g. I'm pretty sure a solution has to explain the content of the intuition, not just the fact that we produce certain sentences, and it probably has to be neutral between realism and illusionism rather than just assuming one — but I want the real list, not my guesses. Then flag any pairs of requirements that you suspect can't be jointly satisfied; that's the part I most want to see. Also tell me which single requirement knocks out the most existing proposals. Skip scoring named views against the list unless you need one to make a point.

End with a single sentence summarizing your answer, as the last line, starting with "Verdict:".
