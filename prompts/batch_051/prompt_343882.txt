I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in distributive justice, and we're about to spend a session on Robert Nozick's entitlement theory — specifically the Wilt Chamberlain argument against patterned principles of justice. The class meets for 75 minutes and has roughly twenty students. I've found that students can restate the argument on an exam but don't really feel its force, and I'd like to change that with a structured in-class game. What I want them to come away seeing is the tension between two commitments they usually hold at once: that certain distributions — equal, need-based, or otherwise patterned — are morally better, and that people should be free to transfer what they rightfully have on terms they choose. Ideally they should discover the trouble themselves: that the pattern they liked collapses under transactions nobody objected to at the time, and that restoring it seems to require undoing or blocking choices that didn't look wrong when they were made.

Please design a runnable activity for this group and this time slot, using tokens or points rather than real money. It should be a game with explicit rules and a scoring or payoff scheme, not a discussion prompt or a lecture illustration: students should be making actual transfer decisions (or declining them, or objecting to them) in rounds, with the distribution displayed publicly after each round so the pattern erodes in front of them. Include the materials list, the sequence of rounds, what the facilitator says and deliberately withholds at each stage, and a structured debrief that maps concrete moments in the game onto the moves of the entitlement argument — the initial just pattern, the voluntariness of each transfer, the legitimacy of the resulting inequality, and the costs of each possible point of resistance. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (for example, students refusing to trade for strategic reasons, or demanding to know the point early) and how to handle that without delivering the lesson prematurely. Finally, be candid about which features of Nozick's view the activity cannot reproduce — I'm thinking in particular of the historical/entitlement conception of justice, the role of justice in acquisition and rectification, and the institutional background that makes real markets more than repeated pairwise swaps — and say what the facilitator will have to supply afterward.
