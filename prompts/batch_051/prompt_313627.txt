I lead a practical philosophy workshop for conservatory students—mostly pianists, string players, and singers—who have years of deliberate practice behind them and who already use the word flow for their best performances. Next month I have a single ninety-minute session to teach them the Daoist concept of wu-wei, and the point is not that they leave feeling vaguely warmed by the idea of spontaneity but that they can say what wu-wei is not: how it differs from the flow they already talk about, and why the two are easy to run together. Plan the teaching rather than the explanation itself. Give me the order in which the components have to be introduced and what each stage presupposes, so the paradox of trying not to try arises as something earned rather than mystical. Give me the one example or scenario that should carry the whole session, and state what it must have and lack to do that work. Map the misconceptions this particular audience will bring or form—I expect at least that wu-wei is just passivity or going with the flow, that it is identical to flow or being in the zone, and that trying not to try is an irrational paradox to be admired rather than a real structure of skilled action—and say at exactly which point in the sequence each should be headed off. Tell me what I should explicitly set aside as too fine-grained for this level, and end with one check that would distinguish a student who has actually understood wu-wei from one who has merely learned to repeat the term.
