I lead a small philosophy of religion seminar for adults, and for the past several weeks we have been stuck on one question: whether the doctrine of an eternal hell can be reconciled with the principle that punishment must be proportionate to the offence. The group has settled into three fixed camps. The first defends the view that the damned suffer consciously and without end, on the grounds that a sin's gravity is measured by the dignity of the one against whom it is committed, and offences against a being of infinite dignity carry an infinite debt. The second defends annihilationism, or conditional immortality: the wicked pass out of existence, and this extinction is itself the punishment. Its advocates insist that a finite creature with a finite lifespan and finite understanding cannot contract an infinite debt, and that endless conscious torment would therefore be disproportionate in a way that extinction is not. The third defends universal reconciliation, treating hell, if it exists, as finite and remedial, because a perfectly loving creator could not institute a punishment that serves no restorative purpose and never ends. What puzzles me is that the three camps seem to agree on most of the relevant moral principles, especially that punishment must fit the crime, and yet none has moved an inch.

I have also come to care about this personally rather than merely as a seminar leader: after a funeral earlier this year, a family member asked me, in good faith, how literally she should take the traditional picture she grew up with, and whether it could really be just for a person to suffer without end for sins committed within a finite life. I found I could reproduce each side's moves but could not say why the disagreement was so durable. My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement is not about hell at all, but about a prior question: what fixes the unit in which an offence is measured, whether the dignity of the wronged party, the capacities and lifespan of the wrongdoer, or the purpose a punishment is meant to serve. If that is right, the arguments about duration, extinction and restoration are mostly downstream of this one choice.

Please respond as a professional philosopher working at the intersection of philosophy of religion and normative ethics. Take your time and reason carefully before you commit to a diagnosis. Start by brainstorming several candidate diagnoses: is this a verbal dispute, a difference about which moral principles are treated as non-negotiable, a hidden formal question about how desert scales from finite acts to infinite penalties, a difference about the authority of tradition versus independent moral theory, or something else? Then give your considered diagnosis, grounded in what each side actually has to say, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given that diagnosis — a distinction that could be drawn, an argument that could be tested, or a cleaner reformulation of the question. If you think no progress should be expected, say so and say why. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a survey of the three positions and not a verdict on which one is true.
