I've been rereading Paradise Lost for the first time since college, and I keep getting stuck on a passage in Book 5, right after Eve tells Adam her disturbing dream about eating the fruit. Adam reassures her that a pure creature can't harbor evil, but then gives this explanation of what happened in her mind:

But know that in the Soule
Are many lesser Faculties that serve
Reason as chief; among these Fansie next
Her office holds; of all external things,
Which the five watchful Senses represent,
She forms Imaginations, Aerie shapes,
Which Reason joyning or disjoyning, frames
All what we affirm or what we deny, and call
Our knowledge or opinion; then retires
Into her private Cell when Nature rests.

What I'm trying to think through is how this passage relates to the poem's treatment of Satan and the rebel angels. Milton seems to give two different accounts of how evil enters a good creation. In Book 3 God says, roughly, that angels were made free enough to stand, and that those who fell did so by their own choice. But here Adam's explanation suggests something less like free choice and more like a built-in vulnerability: the soul has lower faculties like fancy that can produce evil-seeming images on their own, and reason has to catch and correct them. The source of moral danger is internal to the creature's psychological structure, not simply a will that chooses badly. I'd like a reading that takes both passages seriously. Does Milton apply Adam's faculty psychology to Satan's fall, or does he treat the angelic case as different in kind? If he does apply it, does that make Satan's fall less culpable, or more tragic, or both? If he doesn't, is that a real tension in the poem, or is there a principled reason only human minds work this way? Basically, read Paradise Lost through this passage and tell me what it does to the problem of how evil can arise in a good creation with free angels.
