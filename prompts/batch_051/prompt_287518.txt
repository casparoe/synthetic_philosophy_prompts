I'm a software engineer at a video platform, and I've somehow ended up as the token technical member on our trust and safety advisory board. I've never studied philosophy — my actual job is telling people what our deepfake detection tooling can and can't do. We're stuck on a case and I've been asked to draft the board's decision memo this week.

The case: a clip is circulating that appears to show a city council member at what is supposed to be a private dinner, using a racial slur and bragging about a plan to push tenants out of a rent-stabilized building. It's been viewed millions of times. The council member insists it's a deepfake, and his supporters are mass-reporting it. Our internal forensic tools say the clip is probably not synthetic, but the error bars are wide and getting wider as generation models improve. The old company default was: if it's a recording of a public figure and no rights holder objects, treat it as authentic unless there's an obvious splice. That default is now exactly what's in question.

The board is split. One side says recordings have always been the thing you fall back on when witnesses disagree — the epistemic backstop, they call it. If we start taking down or flagging every video a politician calls fake, we've dismantled one of the only pieces of evidence that doesn't require taking someone's word for it. The other side says the backstop is already gone: a video that could be synthetic can't settle what actually happened, and leaving the clip up launders a potential forgery as fact while it does real-world damage. They want it down, or at least stuck behind a synthetic or not-yet-verified label. Please draft the decision memo, with a clear bottom line — take the clip down, leave it up with no label, or leave it up with a caveat or verification label — plus the reasoning for it, including an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the side we reject. I need something the rest of the board can actually act on. And please write it in American English.
