I did jury duty for the first time last month and it's left me thinking about blame in a way I wasn't prepared for. We convicted the guy — I'm pretty sure we got the facts right — but ever since I've been turning over Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, and it's made me weirdly convinced that nobody, including that defendant, is ultimately morally responsible for anything they do.

The argument as I understand it: everything you do comes from the way you are — your brain, your temperament, your history. But you didn't choose the way you are, and any attempt to change yourself would itself come from the way you already were. So you can never be the original source of yourself, and so you can never be responsible in the deep sense that would make blame or punishment genuinely deserved. It's not just that we can't tell whether someone is responsible; the idea of being ultimately responsible is supposed to be incoherent.

I'm basically sold on it. What I want is a map of what would count against it. Not the standard compatibilist move that we're responsible in a shallower sense — I know that move and half think it changes the subject. I mean: which premise is actually the weakest, and what would the world have to look like for it to be false? Are there real cases — radical self-change, addiction and recovery, the way someone slowly takes ownership of their character over time — that pose a genuine problem rather than an apparent one? What evidence or arguments should actually move me, and which of them would a committed defender of the Basic Argument concede as real costs rather than dismiss? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me honestly wheter the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that's a problem for a view of this kind.
