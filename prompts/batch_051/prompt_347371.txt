A textbook I am using for a course on nineteenth-century moral philosophy gives the following summary of the conclusion of Sidgwick's *The Methods of Ethics*.

According to the textbook, Sidgwick begins by showing that the rules of common-sense morality can be systematized, and in the process they come to approximate the demands of utilitarianism. But Sidgwick also holds that each person has a self-evident reason to pursue his or her own happiness. Since both the utilitarian principle and the principle of rational egoism rest on intuitions, practical reason is left with two equally authoritative methods that can conflict. When they do conflict, the textbook says, Sidgwick concludes that there is no rational way to settle the matter: what is right from the impartial point of view may be irrational from the personal point of view, and vice versa. The textbook says Sidgwick therefore leaves ethics in a kind of contradiction. It adds that the passage on which this reading rests is the one in the concluding chapter where Sidgwick describes how the apparent cosmos of duty becomes a chaos once the claims of both methods are admitted. The textbook then draws the lesson that, for Sidgwick, any preference for morality over self-interest, or for self-interest over morality, is ultimately arbitrary.

I cannot check the exact wording of that concluding passage, but its gist is given as: rational egoism and utilitarianism each rest on self-evident principles, and when the two methods conflict, the practical reason is divided, so that the moral order which had seemed a cosmos is left a chaos.

How fair is this summary as an account of Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason? I am inclined to think the last step about arbitrariness overreaches, because Sidgwick seems to treat the conflict as a serious problem for rational ethics rather than as a proof that morality is arbitrary, but I am not sure. Please work through the summary and separate what is accurate, what is a simplification, and what a careful reader of the *Methods of Ethics* would reject. Does the conclusion the textbook draws follow from the accurate parts of the summary?
