i've been trying to systematize my moral verdicts, and the only prinicple i keep coming back to is: an action is wrong iff i couldn't will that everyone in my situation acted on the same rule. but it keeps overshooting and undershooting. here are the cases.

if i make a lying promise to borrow money i have no intention of repaying: wrong. the principle nails that one—if everyone did that, promising would just break down.

if my standing rule is never to help anyone in distress unless it benefits me: wrong, but it doesn't feel like the same kind of wrong. a world where no one ever helps is perfectly thinkable, no contradiction, yet i can't will it. so the principle just calls it out differently, and i guess that's fine.

if i let every skill i have rot and just coast through life: i don't think that's forbidden, just a lousy way to live. the principle seems to say i can't will everyone to do that, which sounds too strong, unless "can't will" is meant to be weaker than "forbidden."

if i double-park outside a pharmacy for ninety seconds to grab a prescription: fine, in my book. but if everyone double-parked for quick errands, traffic would lock up and nobody could do it. so the principle says wrong, and i don't buy that.

if i tell a friend we're going to dinner when really everyone is waiting at their apartment for a surprise party: fine. and the principle maybe says fine—but only if i describe the rule as "deceive someone to keep a surprise from being spoiled." if i describe it as "lie whenever it makes the evening go more smoothly," the principle turns against it. so the verdict seems to ride on how i phrase the rule. that's suspicious.

so, can this universalizability idea be made precise enough to get my verdicts, or most of them? which of my verdicts does it force me to drop—the double-parking, the "talents aren't forbidden" one, or the principle itself? and what does your repaired version say about a rule like "when the stakes are high enough, i'll break a promise"?
