Epistemologists sometimes run two ideas together that need not stand or fall together. The KK principle says that if a subject knows a proposition p, then the subject is in a position to know that they know p. A condition C is luminous just in case, whenever C obtains, the subject is in a position to know that C obtains. On the standard "in a position to know" reading, the condition of knowing p is luminous exactly if the KK principle holds.

Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument purports to show that no non-trivial condition is luminous. It takes a condition that clearly obtains in one case and clearly fails in another, connects the two by a long series of cases each too similar to its predecessor for the subject to discriminate reliably, and then applies a margin-for-error premise: if the subject is in a position to know that C obtains in one case, then C also obtains in the next, sufficiently similar case. Iterating from the first case to the last yields a contradiction. The argument is standardly illustrated with a phenomenal condition such as feeling cold, but it is a further question whether it transfers to the condition of knowing p. If it does transfer, it is an argument against the KK principle. Yet there may be disanalogies: knowledge is factive, and it may not vary along the sort of continuous series the argument requires; a subject's epistemic position with respect to p might be too coarse-grained or too stable for a sorites running from "clearly knows p" to "clearly does not know p" to get a grip. One might reply, however, that the margin-for-error premise is exactly as plausible when the target condition is "knows p" as when it is "feels cold," and that one's epistemic position with respect to p does vary continuously as evidence or reliability degrades.
