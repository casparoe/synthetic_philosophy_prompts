I have been developing a distinction between what I call defensive complexity and concessive strength in competitive argumentation, and I would like an honest assessment of whether the distinction holds up, whether it already exists under another name, and where it breaks.

By defensive complexity I mean argumentative moves whose main function is to make a position harder to refute rather than more likely to be true: multiplying distinctions, hedging every premise in advance, burying the load-bearing inference inside a long list of qualifications, or shifting the burden of proof. By concessive strength I mean the capacity to grant an opponent's strongest point, state it plainly, and then show that one's conclusion still survives. An example: when faced with a serious objection, a defensively complex speaker responds by adding distinctions and qualifications so that the objection no longer cleanly applies, while a concessively strong speaker accepts the objection, says what it forces them to give up, and then defends the narrowed position on its merits.

My central claim is that ordinary competitive formats — formal debate, scored seminars, public debate-for-spectacle events — tend to select for defensive complexity. Concessions are scored as losses, while complexity is read as depth, so the incentive gradient pushes participants toward arguments that protect the speaker rather than arguments that improve the audience's or the opponent's understanding. This, I think, explains a familiar pattern: the strongest competitive debater is often not the person from whom one learns the most.

I am not claiming that argumentation could never be a sport. I am claiming that it could be an epistemically improving sport only if the scoring rules were redesigned to reward concessive strength and penalize defensive complexity explicitly, since the default dynamics of competition otherwise favor the wrong kind of rigor.

I am familiar with the idea that argumentation is commonly conceptualized as combat, and with the practice of steelmanning. My distinction is meant to be finer: steelmanning concerns how one represents the opponent's argument, whereas my terms concern how one constructs and presents one's own argument under competitive pressure.

Is defensive complexity a coherent and useful concept, or is it just a pejorative relabeling of ordinary rigor? Does something like it already exist in argumentation theory, debate pedagogy, or epistemic game theory? And what is the strongest objection to the claim that competition, under default scoring, worsens the epistemic quality of argumentation?
