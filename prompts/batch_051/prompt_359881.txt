i've been half-working-out a view and want it checked before i lean on it anywhere. working title is the 'cortex weight rule', which is a bad name so rename it if it survives. the claim: when we're asking how much a creature's welfare should count morally, scale it by the number of neurons in the parts of the nervous system that plausibly carry experience — cerebral cortex in mammals, the pallium in birds (their cortex analogue), whatever the functional equivalent is elsewhere. definitely not whole-body mass, and not even total brain mass. a blue whale with a cerebellum the size of a fridge shouldn't get moral credit for neurons that are just running balance and autopilot; those aren't adding to how much the animal can feel. and scale by something like the number of neurons actually recruited into the relevant state, not just cells sitting there unused. damage to a brain that can run more simultaneous strands of experience destroys more possible experience, so it's worse. that's the whole rule: moral weight tracks experience-bearing neural machinery, and within that machinery more nodes means more at stake.

i like it because it gives a non-mystical answer to 'are some animals more morally significant than others' without me having to claim i know what a mouse's experience is like. it's also meant to seperate the brain-size question: raw size isn't the point, experience-bearing cell count is.

two cases i keep hitting. first, my rule says a parrot probably counts more than many mammals because bird palliums pack neurons much tighter, and i honestly can't tell whether that's a feature or a bug. second, an octopus breaks it in an interesting way because so much of its nervous system is out in the arms; a naive count makes the arms outrank the head, which feels deeply wrong but maybe isn't. also unclear what it says about a deeply asleep human whose brain is fully wired but idle right now, or an embryo that has the cells but no running activity yet.
