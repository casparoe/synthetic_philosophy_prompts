A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether "the unit of selection" is even a well-posed question — he thinks the whole debate dissolves once you have the Price equation, I think only part of it does. The argument forced me to write down where I've landed, and I want a consistency audit before I defend this in a reading group. I've tried not to signal which of these commitments I am already worried about.

Here is where I've landed:

1. The Price equation gives the correct general decomposition of evolutionary change in a structured population: for any trait, the total response to selection can be partitioned into a within-group and a between-group component, and a nonzero between-group component just is group selection.

2. I therefore think group selection is common rather than exceptional, and I want to say that even an ephemeral trait-group — a herd, a school, a temporary association — can be a unit of selection when its average trait value predicts its fitness.

3. But I also distinguish selection in a group from selection of a group. Selection of a group requires the group itself to have heritable group-level variation and differential reproduction of groups as groups. Colonies qualify, species perhaps; a transient trait-group does not.

4. I am not a deflationary pluralist. I think the within/between-group partition picks out real causal structure, so whether a selection process is described as within-group or between-group is not a matter of taste.

5. At the same time, I accept that inclusive-fitness and multilevel-selection calculations are formally equivalent in the standard structured-population models I was taught, and I think much of the old gene-versus-group fight was a disagreement about bookkeeping rather than about the facts.

6. I also think species selection is real and irreducible in macroevolution: differential speciation and extinction tied to lineage-level traits such as geographic range size or dispersal ability pick out a process that cannot be rewritten away as gene-level or organism-level selection.

7. Selfish genetic elements — segregation distorters, homing endonucleases, transposable elements — seem to me to be cases where the gene is the unit of selection in a strong sense, since their spread is explained by a replication advantage at the gene level even when it lowers organismal and group fitness.

8. Nonetheless, I hold that the organism is the unit that does most of the adaptive work we care about — physiology, behavior, life history — and I will not reduce organisms to mere vehicles for replicators.
