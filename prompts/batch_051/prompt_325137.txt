On my team we basically live by the idea that you can recover what people want by watching what they do. We fit reward functions to interaction logs — clicks, dwell time, purchase sequences — under the assumption that the user is approximately maximizing some latent utility, and the reward we learn gets shipped around as user preferences. I've been doing this long enough that I treat stated preferences as noise and behavior as ground truth; whenever someone objects, I tell them it's just a non-identifiability problem we can regularize away. Lately though I've started worrying that I've set the view up so nothing could count agianst it, which feels like the kind of thing that happens when a framework stops touching reality.

So play it straight: what would have to be true for the core claim to be wrong — the claim that, given enough varied choice data, a person's choices reveal their preferences and an approximately-rational inference procedure can recover them? I already know the standard in-field objections: the reward ambiguity problem (the zero reward trivially makes every policy optimal, so "the behavior is optimal for some reward" is vacuous without a prior or a tie-break), sensitivity to the discount factor and to the feature space you represent rewards in, distribution shift, and users clicking on things for reasons that have nothing to do with wanting them. Treat those as awkward but not neccesarily fatal. I want the deeper defeaters: preferences that don't pre-exist the choice and get constructed on the fly, choices that are attempts to change one's own future preferences, meta-preferences like not wanting to want something, social signaling, adaptive preferences that track what the menu makes feasible. Sort these by how badly each one damages the inference: fixable modeling bug, boundary where the method stops applying, or evidence that the whole latent-reward picture is the wrong kind of model for humans. For each, be honest about whether a committed revealed-preference/IRL person would have to admit it counts against the view or would just classify it as out of scope. And give me a verdict on whether the framework has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so whether that's actually a problem for a methodology as opposed to a hypothesis.
