I'm a backend engineer at a meal-kit company, and I got voluntold onto our "sourcing values" committee. Ops decides roughly weekly which proteins go into next month's boxes — mostly beef, chicken, farmed salmon, or a plant-based swap — and they keep coming to me because I'm "the person who can turn it into a rules engine." I can write the code once I know the rules, but the rules keep flipping sign on me.

Here's the mess. If I just minimize animals killed per kilo, chicken is obviously terrible compared to beef — you need a couple hundred broilers to get the meat from one steer. But those broilers live about six weeks while a steer lives a couple years, so if I care about "bad days per plate" instead of "deaths per plate," beef starts looking worse. Then someone on the committee went down a rabbit hole about how crop farming kills field animals — mice, voles, maybe insects depending on how far you go — so the plant-based option isn't zero, and chicken fed on soy is kind of double-dipping. But grass-fed beef uses way more land, and if land stops being farmed it grows wild animals, and wild animals starve and get predated and diseased, so "less farming" doesn't obviously mean "less suffering." In the middle of all this our ops lead asked: "if we stop buying chicken, aren't other buyers just going to take it, so the total number of chickens barely moves?" And nobody could say weather that's a problem for the procedure or just a reason to give up.

What I need from you is a reusable decision procedure — a flowchart in words, not an essay. Ops will run it in maybe 25 minutes per protein-source option, with partial data, and it has to end in one of ACCEPT / REJECT / ESCALATE, where ESCALATE means "a human has to make a values call, don't pretend the checklist decided it." The people running it have no philosophy background, and the head of ops has to be able to sign off on the logic without reading anything further. Tell me:

1. The questions in the order they should be asked, including what counts as a good-enough answer to each when we don't have exact numbers.
2. Which steps are hard gates — stop and REJECT whatever we're testing — versus tilts that just make an option easier or harder to justify without deciding it alone.
3. Which steps must force ESCALATE because the underlying question is a values call rather than a factual one.
4. Then run the procedure on two cases: (a) replacing our default chicken breast with the same weight of beef mince, and (b) replacing that beef mince with a pea-protein patty. Make sure at least one of the outputs is something our committee is going to hate, and tell me which one.
5. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot settle even in principle, so I know where to hard-code a human in the workflow.

I'd rather have a procedure that's honest about its holes than a clean number that gives me false confidence.
