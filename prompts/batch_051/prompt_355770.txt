I don't have any formal background in philosophy; I've just been thinking about the trolley problem on my own after hearing it discussed, and I've become interested in a question about why the case persuades people the way it does. The version I started from is the standard switch case: a runaway trolley is headed toward five workers, and you can pull a lever to send it onto a side track where it will kill one worker instead.

One feature of the standard telling keeps catching my attention: everything happens within a few seconds and directly in front of the person at the lever. I wanted to know how much of the case's force comes from that feature rather than from the underlying structure of the choice, so I worked out a variation that I think isolates the timing. Suppose everything is as in the standard case, except that the main track is much longer than the side track. If you pull the lever, the trolley will hit the one worker within seconds, just as before. If you do nothing, the trolley will reach the five workers and kill them one hour from now. You know this with certainty, and you also know with certainty that the five will remain on the track and that no one will warn or rescue them. To keep the five as vivid to you as the one, imagine that a live video screen shows them on the track while you stand at the switch; once you decide, there is nothing further you can do.

What should the standard ways of analyzing the original case say about this version? In particular, do the utilitarian calculation, the doing/allowing distinction (between killing and letting die), and the idea that there is a moral difference between harm you intend and harm you merely foresee all deliver the same answers here as in the original, or does the one-hour delay change any of them? And if those standard analyses stay intact while the intuitive force of the case moves — if allowing five deaths an hour from now feels less wrong than allowing five deaths now, even though the numbers, the certainty, and the mechanism are the same — does that show that the original was acting partly as an intuition pump, getting its persuasive power from the immediate, vivid collision rather than from the structure of the decision? I am less interested in a final yes-or-no than in seeing the layers separated.
