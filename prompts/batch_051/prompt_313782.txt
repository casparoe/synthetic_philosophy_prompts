I referee for a mathematics journal whose editorial board is deadlocked over whether to accept a paper whose main theorem is reduced to roughly ten trillion cases, all checked by a custom program. The strange thing is that no one on the board disputes the underlying facts: the code runs, the enumeration terminates, and no one has yet found a bug in it. The disagreement is entirely about what those facts establish. One side holds that a proof is a surveyable sequence of inferences that a competent mathematician can, in principle, follow and understand as a whole; a computation no single human can verify step by step is an instrument reading rather than a proof, however reliable the instrument is. The other side holds that proofs have never required surveyability: long classifications, massive collaborative verifications, and results whose pieces no single referee ever reunites are already accepted, and a proof's job is to reduce a claim to accepted rules and checks that the community can trust, with a computer being just a very careful checker.

The same two positions have been replayed almost verbatim since the Appel–Haken proof of the four-colour theorem, and again around the machine-checked proof of the Kepler conjecture. My working hypothesis is that the impasse persists because the two sides want proof to do two different jobs—one wants proof to guarantee correctness, the other wants it to produce understanding—and that the factual-sounding question "how reliable is the software?" is a distraction from that divide. But I am not confident. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this a verbal dispute, a substantive epistemological dispute, a clash of values about what mathematics is for, a hidden empirical question about machine reliability, or some mixture? Whatever your diagnosis, support it with what each side actually says, and be specific about what would count as progress given that diagnosis—a conceptual distinction, a formal result, a new mode of verification, a reformulated question—or explain why we should expect no progress and why.
