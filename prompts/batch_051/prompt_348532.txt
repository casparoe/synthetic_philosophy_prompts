I keep trying to sort my intuitions about brain data and none of the usual privacy lines fit. Here are my verdicts on a bunch of cases; I'd like a principle that captures them, or a diagnosis of which ones I need to drop.

1. A locked-in patient uses a device that reads out yes/no brain signals so he can tell his doctor he's in pain. Fine.
2. Same device, same patient, same broad consent form he signed. Now the insurance company reviews the log and says the pain levels don't justify the treatment. I think not fine, but I can't identify what changed.
3. A warehouse gives workers headsets that beep when attention dips, to prevent forklift accidents. Fine. But if the boss uses the same data to discipline the daydreamers, then not fine, even though the readouts are identical.
4. Police get a warrant to search a suspect's apartment, and they also want to read a few episodic memories off a BCI he wears. I think the warrant covers the apartment and not the memories. Something about memory feels different from the rest of the body.
5. A marketing firm pays people to wear EEG bands and see which ads produce a flash of excitement before conscious liking. People opt in, data is anonymized and aggregate. I hate it, but I'm stuck thinking it's fine.
6. A depressed patient has an implanted stimulator that predicts a mood crash and adjusts stimulation before she notices. She says the mood changes aren't chosen, but they're still her. I'd allow it, and the "that wasn't really me" worry feels overblown here.
7. A school issues BCI headsets that flag students who are zoning out. Not fine, even though the warehouse case looked similar.
8. A border agent uses an EEG deception detector on a traveler. Not fine, even if I stipulate it's accurate and the traveler agrees because it'll get him through faster.
9. A researcher reconstructs faces people are looking at from brain scans, with consenting subjects. Fine. Police doing the same thing with a warrant: not fine. I realize that sounds backwards, since normally I'd favor the police-with-a-warrant version.
10. I set up my own BCI to nudge my mood down when I'm about to send an angry email. Fine. The platform doing the same nudge without asking, because it knows my pattern: not fine.

My best guess at a principle is something like: what's protected is the content of thought — beliefs, memories, decisions, maybe perception — not attention, mood, or raw signal. That handles 1, 4, and 9, but it seems to say 3 and 7 should be fine and they don't feel fine, and it has nothing to say about the cases where the device changes your mood, like 6 and 10. Is there a better principle, or a small set, that gets most of these? Which verdicts would it force me to give up, and what would it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a witness who is asked to wear a BCI at trial so the jury can tell whether her memory report is sincere and not just confident — fine or not?
