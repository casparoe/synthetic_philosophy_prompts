I am taking an upper-level seminar on trust, deception, and the ethics of childhood, and I want to step back from one-off case judgments to get the specification right before evaluating any of the views. In the readings, benevolent lies to children get lumped together even though they seem to me quite different in structure: Santa Claus and the tooth fairy are collectively maintained, embedded in ritual, and almost always discovered on a predictable schedule; telling a child that a dead pet has been sent to a farm, or that a family's financial strain is nothing to worry about, is a private protective falsehood with no intended endpoint; and there are the small daily fictions of childcare that are not even meant to be believed for very long. In my notes I have been calling these fictions with a handoff and fictions with no exit, but I am unsure whether that boundary carries normative weight or is a red herring.

What I would like is a list of desiderata for an adequate theory of caregiving deception of children: the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, and the distinctions it cannot collapse. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it. In particular, I would want the list to settle whether an adequate theory must treat the difference between a falsehood the child is eventually invited to reinterpret as shared pretense and a falsehood intended to stand indefinitely as a difference in kind; where the wrong of a Santa-style falsehood, if there is one, lies in the false content itself versus the way the fiction is used to manage behavior; and what account the theory owes of the apparent asymmetry between how the two kinds of discovery are ordinarily experienced later, with one recalled as initiation and the other as betrayal. Please also say which requirements you judge non-negotiable and which are merely one camp's commitment, and flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Keep the emphasis on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring the standard theories.
