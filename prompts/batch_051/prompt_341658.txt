I have been rereading Stephen Wolfram’s *A New Kind of Science*, and I keep noticing an asymmetry in the way scientifically literate people talk about different kinds of generative models. When a pattern in nature is captured by a compact set of differential equations, those equations are normally treated as explanatory: they are said to specify the mechanism, even if they are only a phenomenological fit. When a closely analogous pattern is generated by an elementary cellular automaton with a simple local rule, however, the result is often dismissed as “just a simulation,” a computational curiosity, or a picture that happens to look like the phenomenon. Wolfram’s principle of computational equivalence, in the form he introduces near the end of the book, suggests that above a rather low threshold this distinction should not carry much weight: the automaton is not necessarily a less exact description of the system’s causal structure, and its behaviour may be computationally irreducible in the same way the phenomenon itself is.

I am not asking you to adjudicate Wolfram’s broader empirical claims about the ubiquity of simple programmes in nature. What interests me is the asymmetry itself. If an equation and a cellular automaton rule are both compact symbolic objects that generate the same observable data, what, if anything, justifies treating the equation as science and the rule as merely a model or an analogy? I want the strongest principle you can find that would ground this asymmetry—perhaps something about continuity, derivability, measurement, or the role of idealisation—and an honest verdict on whether it survives cases where the automaton recovers a known physical law and the equation is only a curve-fit. Should the asymmetry be kept, reversed, or given up?
