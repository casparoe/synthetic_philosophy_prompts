Below are my considered judgments about seven arrangements among states. I want to know what principle of membership and sovereignty they add up to; treat the judgments as data, but do not be afraid to tell me if one of them should be abandoned for the sake of a simpler principle.

A (free-trade area). Two states abolish tariffs on trade between them, each keeps its own external tariff and its own courts, and each can terminate the arrangement on short notice. My verdict: they have not formed a customs union and have pooled no sovereignty; they have merely assumed reciprocal obligations.

B (customs union with a unanimous council and exit). Three states remove internal tariffs, adopt a common external tariff, and create a council whose decisions on the tariff require unanimity; any state may withdraw with a year's notice. My verdict: each state remains sovereign, and the union is an instrument of their joint will rather than an authority standing over them—though I admit that, while a member, no state may set the tariff unilaterally.

C (an associate inside the customs area). A fourth state is admitted by treaty to the customs area: it applies the union's external tariff and rules of origin to its imports, and its goods circulate freely inside the union, but it has no seat or vote in the council that sets the tariff, and the union may conclude trade agreements that it is expected to follow. My verdict: it has joined the union commercially but not politically; I cannot call it a full member of the customs union, but I also cannot call it an outsider. I am stuck with "partial membership."

D (monetary union without fiscal union). Several states create a common central bank whose monetary-policy decisions bind them by qualified majority, adopt a common currency, and agree not to withdraw for a fixed period, while budgets, taxes, defense, and foreign policy remain national. My verdict: a bundle of monetary powers has been transferred to a shared body, but the states remain sovereign states; sovereignty has been pooled in one domain, not extinguished.

E (federation). The units give foreign policy, defense, money, and border control to a central government; they lose the capacity to make treaties in their own name; and no unit may secede unilaterally. My verdict: the units are no longer sovereign states, whatever they are called; they have not joined an association but have merged into a new state.

F (confederation with a court). As in B, but with a permanent court whose rulings bind the member states, which may impose fines and suspend voting rights, and which can interpret the founding treaty without unanimous consent; the treaty can still be amended only unanimously, and members retain the right to withdraw. My verdict: this is still a confederation of sovereign states, not a federation, even though binding adjudication is present. The difference, I want to say, is that the court applies a compact among states rather than a constitution over them, but I cannot yet make that distinction precise.

G (unilateral alignment). A neighboring state, without any treaty, adopts the union's entire tariff schedule and product standards as its own law, and enacts a statute automatically incorporating future amendments; it has no seat, and it can repeal the statute by ordinary legislation at any time. My verdict: it has not joined anything; it is an outsider that has chosen to make itself look exactly like a member for customs purposes.

The two pairs that most trouble me are C versus G—operationally alike, since in both cases a state applies the tariff and has no vote, yet I judge one as partially inside and the other as wholly outside—and B and F versus E—apparently separated only by degree, yet I judge them as differing in kind, since sovereignty survives in the former and is extinguished in the latter.

What I would like from you is this. Propose the best principle, or a small set of competing principles, that captures these verdicts. State it precisely enough to apply to new cases, and for each clause say which of my cases motivates it. Then apply the principle to two cases I have not listed:

(H) a customs union whose council sets the external tariff by simple majority, with no exit clause and a court that can fine members for failing to implement its rulings, but with no other shared competences;

(I) an association whose members keep separate international legal personality but entrust defense and foreign policy to a common council that acts only by unanimity, while any member may withdraw on six months' notice; third states have begun to treat the association, rather than the members, as the relevant bloc in negotiating certain treaties.

Finally, state which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and say whether you would rather overturn that verdict or complicate the principle to save it.
