My two housemates have spent much of this term going back and forth about what one of them calls the freegan exception: whether a vegan may eat animal products that would otherwise go to waste, and which such products, if any, are different. I have followed the whole argument and still cannot decide who has the better of it at any given step.

Elinor is the stricter of the two: she eats no animal products at all and will refuse a bowl of soup if she suspects it contains chicken stock. Daniel eats plant-based in the ordinary course, but he will salvage sandwiches from the bins behind supermarkets, take leftover chicken from a catered event rather than see it binned, eat roadkill, and eat the eggs laid by the rescued hens at the sanctuary where he volunteers, on the grounds that they would otherwise be thrown away. Elinor says this is a muddle. Her first claim is that if animals are not ours to use, then it should not make a moral difference that a particular animal product happens to be bound for the bin; eating an animal's body or her eggs still treats her as a thing, and refusing to do that is what veganism is. Daniel replies that the wrong, if there is one, lies in the harm caused and the demand created, and his salvaging causes neither: no shop orders more chicken because he took a sandwich from its bins, no hen was bred or kept so that he could eat her eggs, and the deer was already dead at the roadside. Elinor concedes that he is probably right about the causal chain, but says the causal chain is not the whole of the wrong; veganism is not merely a consumer boycott, it is also a practice of refusing to see animals as resources. She then narrows the charge: roadkill and rescued-hen eggs may be tolerable, but supermarket waste is not, because it is the downstream residue of the very industry he claims to reject, and living off that residue is still a way of being in relation to the industry. Daniel answers that this is a strange line to draw, since the industry neither knows nor cares what happens to its waste, and the alternative is edible food rotting while he goes without.

The argument then moved to other people. Elinor pressed the point that when Daniel eats a chicken sandwich from a bin in a shared kitchen, nobody watching can tell it from a bought one; whatever his private intentions, he is publicly normalising the eating of animal products, and that is a harm to the social norms veganism depends on. Daniel said that would be a reason to be discreet, or to explain, not a reason the act itself is wrong, and that if the worry is being misread she should object equally to a vegan burger that looks like a beef one. Elinor replied that the vegan burger is not made from an animal, so the objection to it lapses, whereas with a discarded chicken sandwich the observer would be right about what he is doing and wrong only about where it came from. Daniel then returned to the sanctuary eggs: the hens would not exist but for human use, so eating their eggs cannot increase their number, and if Elinor would simply throw the eggs away, what principle forbids him from eating them? She said that once you begin treating a rescued hen's eggs as yours, you slide towards keeping the animal for what she produces and deciding what counts as waste in a way that always seems to benefit you, which is exactly the attitude she is refusing to adopt.

Could you go through their exchange point by point and say who is right about what, and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
