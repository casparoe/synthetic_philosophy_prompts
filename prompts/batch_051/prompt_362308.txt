I retired a few years ago after a career in chemical research, and I have lately been reading moral and political philosophy, mostly on desert, reciprocity, and the welfare state. The old distinction between the deserving and the undeserving poor strikes me as intellectually disreputable, and yet it keeps turning up in my own reactions to concrete cases. I find that I have firm verdicts about who should receive support and under what conditions, but I cannot state the principle behind them, and I would like your help extracting one, or a small set of candidates, and then testing it.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts stated as plainly as I can. (1) A healthy man in his mid-twenties, with no dependents and no work history, has been offered several ordinary jobs through the employment service and has refused them all, preferring to live on an unemployment payment. I think his payment should be reduced, and if he persists in refusing suitable work, cut until the refusal genuinely costs him; I feel no unease about this. (2) A woman of fifty with advanced and thoroughly documented Parkinson's disease receives a disability benefit with no work-related conditions. I think this is exactly right, and that no question of reciprocity should arise for her. (3) A young mother of two children works sixteen hours a week and receives a wage top-up, and the relevant office proposes to require her to move to full-time work once the younger child turns three. I think the requirement is wrong: paid work is not the most valuable thing she is doing at that stage, and she should not be forced into that trade-off before the child is settled in school. (4) A man of fifty-eight lost his factory job when the plant closed and lives in a region with few vacancies; he is required to attend fortnightly work-search interviews and to apply for a set number of jobs he is unlikely to get. I accept this requirement, though it troubles me that its main function may be to prove willingness, and I notice that I cannot say why this is fair while the demand on the mother in the previous case is not. (5) A woman receiving an unemployment payment has for a year taken cash-in-hand cleaning work without declaring it, averaging roughly fifty pounds a week, and used the money for school uniforms and to clear a loan. I think she should repay the overpayment, but prosecuting her or parading her as a benefit cheat seems to me disproportionate, almost cruel. (6) A physically fit man has for several years run a steady cash business repairing furniture while claiming to be unemployed and declaring nothing. I think this is straightforward fraud and he deserves prosecution; he seems to me a clear case of an undeserving recipient. (7) A man who has not worked for two years says depression makes work impossible, but the medical evidence is thin and the assessments conflict, and he has been placed under a work-search condition. I go back and forth: if he is genuinely ill the condition is an outrage, and if he is not, it is more than lenient.

The principle I keep reaching for is roughly this: society owes everyone a floor sufficient for a minimally decent life, but above that floor support is owed as a matter of right only to those who cannot reasonably be expected to support themselves through paid work; for those who can, assistance is conditional on a demonstrated willingness to work, enforced through sanctions; and honesty in claiming is a separate matter, handled by repayment and, where systematic, by prosecution. But I can already see the strain. On that principle, the mother in (3) seems liable to the very requirement I reject, and the small fraud in (5) seems to matter more than my verdict says it does, while the open refusal in (1) seems to matter less. There is also an internal contradiction between my professed floor and my wish to cut the man in (1) until the refusal hurts. My instinct that (1) calls for sharper sanction than (5) is strange on its face, since only (5) involves deception; if the principle cannot explain that asymmetry, then either the principle or one of the verdicts must go. What I would like is for you to state the principle or small set of principles that best captures my verdicts, identify the clause that does the work in each case, tell me honestly which of my verdicts no single principle can accommodate, and then apply the result to two cases I have not settled: a recent graduate who wants to spend a year writing a novel on an unemployment payment rather than seeking work, and a woman who volunteers thirty-five hours a week at a food bank and asks to be exempt from job-search requirements on those grounds. I do not want a survey of positions; I want the principles drawn out of my own cases and then made to earn their keep.
