So me and my friend got into it about the problem of evil last night, and by the end we were arguing less about God and more about what a theodicy even has to do to count as one. We ended up with four criteria that we both sort of accepted for a theodicy to actually answer the problem rather than just deflect it:

1. It can't appeal to mystery or our cognitive limits as the whole story; it has to positively describe a reason God might permit the evil.
2. It has to preserve both God's omnipotence and God's perfect goodness without quietly redefining either one.
3. It can't just say the evil is outweighed by some good; it has to explain why God couldn't get the good without that exact evil, or without something worse.
4. It has to make sense of the distribution of suffering, not just it's existence—especially the suffering of nonhuman animals, where the usual free-will moves get no purchase.

Now here's the borderline case my friend proposed. Maybe animal suffering is the residue of God's commitment to developing minds through competitive, error-correcting processes—evolution, in other words. Suffering isn't punishment, or pedagogy, or the price of free will. It's the selection pressure that drives organisms toward self-models, foresight, choice, and eventually the capacity for moral awareness, which is the only kind of good worth creating. On this view, localized, apparently pointless suffering is the cost of an impartial selection function that couldn't achieve that end if it were tuned to spare every individual. So the distribution of suffering is supposed to look indifferent. My friend says that's not a bug; that's the feature.

Now I'm stuck. Go through my four criteria honestly and tell me whether this theodicy qualifies. Where it fails, say whether the criteria are too strict or the proposal is just too thin. And then tell me: is saying the distribution is *supposed* to look indifferent ever a theodicy, or is that just my first criterion—mystery—dressed up in evolutionary language?
