I'm a retired analytical chemist — spent forty years reading chromatograms and melting points — and I've only recently started in on the Critique of Pure Reason, so be patient if I've mangled the jargon. The bit I keep turning over is Kant's claim that cognition has two stems that can't be reduced to one another: intuitions, which are singular and immediate and give us objects through sensibility, and concepts, which are general and discursive and let the understanding think those objects. His line, as best I remember it — and I'm paraphrasing rather than quoting — is that intuitions without concepts are blind and concepts without intuitions are empty.

Could you write a sonnet for me that makes the distinction felt rather than just asserted? Use the octave for the manifold of intuition without concepts: particulars spread across space and succeeding in time, colours, noises, a this-here-now, but no categories keeping them together, so the lines should feel deliberately bitty and unresolved. Then the volta at line 9 is the moment the understanding turns up and starts applying concepts. In the sestet, show concepts trying to do the work on their own — abstraction, generality, judgement — but with nothing intuited to land on, so they're spinning empty. The closing couplet should say precisely why neither is enough seperately and what is true only of the two together. I'd rather it be accurate enough that a Kant specialist notices you've actually read him than pretty in a way that fudges it. And could you write it in American English?
