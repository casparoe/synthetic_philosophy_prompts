I'm in a Berkeley seminar and I keep going back and forth on the master argument in the *Principles*. The setup is frustrating because I can see both sides. The theory side: Berkeley first diagnoses the problem, in the preface he says, "Prejudices and errors of sense do from all parts discover themselves to our view; and, endeavouring to correct these by reason, we are insensibly drawn into uncouth paradoxes, difficulties, and inconsistencies, which multiply and grow upon us as we advance in speculation." Then the master argument says: try to conceive a tree existing unperceived. You can't, because in the very act of conceiving it you're conceiving it, so the object is still present to a mind — yours. Therefore the supposition of an absolutely unperceived object is inconceivable, and talk of mind-independent matter is strictly meaningless. When it's laid out like that, I find it genuinely hard to resist: every move I make to think the unperceived tree just reinstalls a perceiver. But here's the intuition side: I also cannot shake the conviction that the tree outside my window is there at 3 a.m. when no one is looking, that the room I just left continues to exist unperceived, and that its not merely a way of speaking.

I don't want someone to tell me "Berkeley's right, bite the bullet" or "Berkeley's obviously wrong, here's the refutation." I want to know what it would actually take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the argument to be amended or abandoned, and whether there's a reading of "exists" or "unperceived" on which both survive. Also, is Berkeley's preface line a clue — maybe he thinks the problem is exactly that we're trying to "correct" common sense by reason, and the master argument is meant to vindicate common sense rather than replace it? If so, does my 3 a.m. tree intuition count as common sense or as one of the "prejudices and errors of sense" he wants corrected?
