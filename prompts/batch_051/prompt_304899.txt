I run a monthly film-and-philosophy night, and next month we're doing Kurosawa's Rashomon. The group is pretty mixed — some film people, some philosophy people, a few just there for the conversation — and everyone comes in already knowing the shorthand: the four contradictory accounts of what happened in the grove, the so-called Rashomon effect, the idea that the movie is about how truth is subjective or unreachable. I'd like to push the discussion past that, because I think the lazy reading flattens both the film and the philosophy. I want to frame it through the Jain idea of anekāntavāda: roughly, that reality is irreducibly many-sided, that any single standpoint or naya yields only partial knowledge, and that a properly careful claim is qualified with syāt, a marker meaning something like in some respect, not unconditionally. But I also know anekāntavāda is not just everyone has their own truth or we can never know anything; Jain philosophers usually want to say that opposing claims can each hold under different respects, while some standpoints are still partial, misleading, or simply wrong. So my question is whether Rashomon actually dramatizes that — or whether it is really dramatizing the collapse of a knowable shared world and the way testimony gets corrupted by self-interest, which is a different and darker thing.

I'd love a reading that treats the film and the doctrine with equal care. Where does the film earn a many-sided-truth reading, and where does it resist it? How should I think about the woodcutter's fourth account and the possibility that he took the dagger: is that one more legitimate naya, or does it show that some accounts are lies rather than partial truths, a distinction anekāntavāda has to be able to draw? What does the final scene with the abandoned baby and the clearing weather do to the epistemological point — does it smuggle in a privileged perspective after all, or change the subject from truth to trust? And if I bring up the blind men and the elephant as the standard illustration, am I importing a story whose provenance and point are themselves contested? I'd also like a note at the end on what the film adds to a dry account of the doctrine that the doctrine itself cannot.

One more thing: please clearly separate what is settled about anekāntavāda from what is genuinely disputed among people who study it, and dont present a contested interpretive claim about the film — say, that the ending is straightforwardly redemptive, or that the woodcutter is simply a liar — as if it were the standard reading.
