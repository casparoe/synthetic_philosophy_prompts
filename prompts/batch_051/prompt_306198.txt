I am a retired experimental physicist, and over the past few years I have been reading my way through philosophy of science and philosophy of mind. I now serve on the technical review panel of an organization that audits safety claims made by developers of large neural networks. A recurring problem for the panel is this: interpretability teams bring us findings of the form "neuron 412 in layer 18 represents cats," "this residual-stream direction is the model's concept of honesty," or "this circuit computes the model's belief that the user is not a native speaker." Some panel members treat these claims as discoveries about what the model really has inside it; others treat them as convenient summaries fitted by the researchers, no more real than a trend line drawn through a scatter plot. Both sides have a point, and our public reports require us to take a position.

Please act as a professional philosopher of science who also knows the technical practice of interpretability, and work through this carefully. Before producing the procedure, draft a short statement of your underlying position about what it takes for a component of a physical system to have a representation, and use that statement to discipline the procedure so it does not become a grab bag of hurdles.

Turn that position into a decision procedure a small, non-philosophical audit team can run on a specific interpretability claim before it appears in one of our reports. The team consists of engineers and compliance staff; they have one or two days per claim, no background in philosophy, and they cannot redo the original research, though they may put a short list of additional analyses to the interpretability team. Give me an ordered checklist of questions, with, for each question: what a passing answer looks like, where the procedure stops with a "no to the representation claim," and where it must stop short of a decision and escalate to me with the crux of the disagreement stated in plain language. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure and about which philosophical distinctions you are sacrificing for usability and why.

The checklist should be grounded in what you judge to be defensible about representation in physical systems: the difference between tracking a property and merely predicting it, the possibility that an interpretation is one useful description among many rather than the unique truth, the difference between explaining the model's behavior and redescribing it at a different level, and the role of predictive economy in deciding that a pattern is real. Do not simply rely on the everyday word "represents."

Then apply the procedure to three claims of roughly the kind we actually see (treat them as fictional but representative):

1. A team claims that a particular direction in the residual stream of a sentiment classifier is the model's representation of positive sentiment, on the strength of a trained linear probe and high accuracy on the training distribution.
2. A team claims that a single neuron in a multilingual model is the model's representation of the concept "German," because it responds strongly to German text and ablating it degrades German-language performance while leaving English largely intact.
3. A team claims that an attention head in a factual-recall model writes the answer token to the current position, because intervening on the head changes model predictions systematically across hundreds of prompts.

For each case, report what your procedure would decide, what additional evidence should be required before the claim goes into a report as a representation claim, and note at least one case where the procedure returns an answer the interpretability team will dislike. Close by saying what the procedure cannot do: what would remain unknown about the model even if the procedure were followed perfectly, and how a committed realist about interpretability would object to your instrument.

I want the final product to be usable at our next panel meeting: the checklist itself must be short enough to fit on a single page in our decision template, with the philosophical rationale and the worked cases as supporting material.
