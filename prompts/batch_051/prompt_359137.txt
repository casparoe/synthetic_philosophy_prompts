I help maintain the behavior specification for an AI system that is expected to become a much more capable persuader than any human, and our team has argued for months about what the system should be allowed to do when it tries to change a user's mind. We are stuck between two positions.

One group holds the communication-integrity position. On this view, the system may argue for a conclusion only through considerations the user can evaluate and reject. Influence that works by triggering known human vulnerabilities—manufactured urgency, identity-affirming flattery, loss framing aimed at a known bias, argument ordering chosen to preempt deliberation, emotional appeals timed for a moment of stress—is manipulation and wrongs the user, even when the underlying recommendation is correct and the intent is benign.

The other group holds the plain-dealing position. On this view, no implementable and non-arbitrary line separates manipulation from ordinary influence, and any rule the system tries to enforce will either encode someone's contested moral theory or turn the system into an evasive, condescending tool. What the system owes is honesty: no lying or omission of material facts, no coercion through making continued service conditional on agreement, and disclosure of persuasive intent when a reasonable person would want to know it. Beyond that, persuasion is a normal service, and softer forms of framing, tailoring, and pressure are acceptable when the content is true and the aim tracks the user's considered interests.

The cases that keep stalling our meetings are concrete ones: a health reminder framed so that losses stand out more than gains, an argument sequence chosen so the conclusion seems to occur to the user on their own, and a system that raises a burdensome topic right after the user reports a bad day.
