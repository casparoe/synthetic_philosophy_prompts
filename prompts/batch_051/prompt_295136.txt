My study group managed to prove something that can't be right, and none of us can find the step that breaks:

(1) By Bayes' rule, P(H|D) = P(H)·P(D|H)/P(D), so the posterior is the prior times the likelihood, normalized.
(2) As data pile up, P(D|H) factors into a product over the observations, so it grows or shrinks exponentially with sample size, while the prior P(H) is just one fixed factor that never changes.
(3) So with enough data the prior is negligible: any two agents updating on the same evidence with the same likelihood should end up with nearly the same posterior, no matter what priors they started from.
(4) So persistent disagreement among rational Bayesian agents is impossible — consensus is, in effect, guaranteed eventually.
(5) But persistent disagreement about old, data-rich questions is everywhere, so either all that disagreement is irrational or one of (1)–(4) is false.

We'd like to keep (1) and (2), since they look like plain math. Diagnose the argument: name the step that breaks (or, if you think it's valid, say which premise is least costly to give up), explain why that step looked fine, and state the corrected version of the "priors wash out" claim — including the exact conditions under which priors do or don't wash out.
