As someone trained in the natural sciences and now reading Mill with fresh eyes, I am puzzled by what looks like an internal tension in On Liberty. Early in the work Mill states the principle that coercion is justified only to prevent harm to others, and he applies it to expression in a strong form: an opinion is not to be silenced merely because it is false, offensive, or damaging to people's feelings. Yet later in the same work he seems to allow that even expression may be punished when it becomes a positive incitement to harmful conduct. His example is a speaker who addresses an angry crowd gathered outside a corn-dealer's house by saying that corn-dealers are starvers of the poor; in that setting, he says, the words are no longer protected as mere opinion. Are these two claims consistent, or does the second surrender the first? If they are consistent, what exactly distinguishes the protected expression from the punishable incitement, and does that distinction already follow from the harm principle as first stated, or does it require a further principle about how speech causes action? Give what you take to be the strongest reconciliation, then assess whether that reconciliation is faithful to the text or amounts to special pleading.
