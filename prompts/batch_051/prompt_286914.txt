Here is an argument I keep constructing and cannot dismantle. I find the conclusion absurd, but each premise looks right by itself, and I cannot locate where it slides.

Define two experiences as equivalent for a subject when the subject could not distinguish them under any circumstances. Then:

(1) The human mind is finite in its discriminative capacities. Along any sensory dimension, only finitely many levels can be told apart; the same is true for each modality and for combinations across modalities. So the space of pairwise discriminable total experiences is finite.

(2) If two experiences are indiscriminable for a subject, there is no experiential difference for that subject. A difference that could not, under any circumstances, show up in the subject's discriminations is not a real difference in the experience itself.

(3) Therefore, there are only finitely many possible experiences available to a human being.

(4) A sufficiently long-lived human being, run through a suitably varied sequence of inputs, could in principle have all of them. After that, nothing would be genuinely new; every experience would be a token of a type already passed through.

(5) But this conclusion seems plainly false. For any catalogue of experiences, it seems possible to produce a new one: a hue between two hues on the list, a chord with one more voice, a street never before seen under weather never before felt. Experience appears open-ended, not a finite stock that a long-lived mind could use up.

(6) So one of the steps, or the inference from (1) and (2) to (3), must be rejected — unless the open-endedness of experience is an illusion. Which step do you reject, why did it look compelling, and what does rejecting it cost for the rest of the picture?
