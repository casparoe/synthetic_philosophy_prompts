For years the standard explanation of the trust gap between the Nordic countries and the United States has been that it is earned: Swedes, Norwegians, and Danes trust their governments because those governments have delivered competent, low-corruption, broadly fair performance over decades, while Americans are more suspicious because their institutions have repeatedly disappointed them. On this view, trust and suspicion are rational inferences from lived experience, and the question of which attitude is more reasonable collapses into the question of which country's institutions have actually performed better. Suppose the best cross-national and second-generation-immigrant evidence pointed elsewhere: that trust and suspicion are in substantial part inherited, culturally self-perpetuating stances that people carry into new institutional settings and revise only weakly. I want to know what becomes of the performance-based argument under that supposition. Which premise is actually under pressure—that these attitudes track institutional quality, that being reasonable requires updating on current evidence, or something else—and is there a repaired version that still vindicates Nordic trust or American suspicion in a weaker form, or should the claim be replaced by a different sort of claim, say about the cultures or about the institutions themselves? Where you are uncertain, indicate how confident you are and single out the point you are least sure of.
