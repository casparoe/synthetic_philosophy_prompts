Every time I read David Lewis on convention I end up chewing on the same edge case, and I want you to help me work out the right verdict. In *Convention* he basically uses driving-on-the-left versus driving-on-the-right as the cleanest example of a convention: neither side is intrinsically better, but once everyone drives on the left, any individual would be crazy to drive on the right, so the regularity holds itself in place through mutual expectations and common knowledge. Fine. But then there's Dagen H, 3 September 1967, when Sweden actually switched from left-hand to right-hand traffic, more or less overnight. A friend from Stockholm told me her grandfather still talks about it. The setup: Sweden had been a left-hand-traffic country while Denmark, Norway and Finland drove on the right, and most Swedish cars were left-hand-drive, which made overtaking especially dangerous because the driver sat on the curb side and couldn't see past the car ahead. In 1955 there was even a referendum and about 83% voted to keep driving on the left; the goverment ignored it and went ahead with the change anyway. On the day itself traffic was stopped in the early morning and then restarted on the right, with all the signs and road markings already redone.

What I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions. First, use Lewis's model of convention to say what the Swedish drivers and the state actually faced: what were the coordination games, the options, the payoffs, and what should Lewis's theory predict about a coordinated side-switch like this? Then say which features of Dagen H the theory explains well (the self-enforcing old regularity, the arbitrariness of the side, the importance of a publicized common-knowledge moment) and which features it strains to explain or goes silent about (a top-down legal decision replacing a stable convention, majority opposition being overridden, the difference between acting on a law and acting on a convention, and whether post-1967 traffic-side is even a convention rather than just a law everyone obeys). Finally, turn it around: what does Dagen H show about the convention framework itself — does the case confirm it, refute it, or just mark the limits of applying a small-scale coordination model to something as huge and law-backed as national traffic? I know the general outline; I want you to be disciplined about the mapping, and to actually commit to a conclusion rather than ending on a fence-sit.
