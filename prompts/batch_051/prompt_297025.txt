I'm a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of animal minds course, and I'm preparing a short position paper for the seminar. Before I defend it publicly, I want an outside consistency check on the set of claims I keep finding myself endorsing; I'd rather locate the cracks now than have them exposed during discussion. My notes currently read more or less like this:

1. I accept Morgan's canon, at least in the corrected form it takes in contemporary debate: where two accounts of a piece of animal behavior both fit the evidence, one involving a more sophisticated psychological capacity and one involving a less sophisticated capacity, the burden of proof rests with the more sophisticated account, and the less sophisticated account may be treated as the default until it fails.
2. I think the lower/higher distinction is scientifically workable as a rough ordering: reflexes and fixed action patterns at the bottom, then associative learning and simple forms of representation, then concept-like or belief-like cognition, then the capacity to attribute mental states, and so on.
3. I also think our caution should be symmetrical. Anthropomorphism is an error, but so is refusing to attribute a capacity simply because the animal's evolutionary history is unlike ours; the two mistakes should not be treated as if only one of them has serious costs.
4. I accept some version of evolutionary continuity. Capacities that look distinctive in humans — affect, working memory, perhaps conscious awareness — are likely present in graded forms across at least some other lineages rather than arriving all at once.
5. As a case verdict, I think the octopus behavior we discussed — unscrewing jar lids to reach food, discriminating among individual human handlers, adjusting body patterns to new backgrounds over short timescales — is best explained by genuine learning plus some form of structured representation, not by a fixed action pattern or simple response chaining.
6. I think parsimony is a real scientific virtue, but I deny that it means preferring the fewest psychological capacities at any cost; it has to be weighed against explanatory adequacy and evolutionary plausibility.
7. I think the historical use of Morgan's canon did genuine damage in comparative psychology, in that it licensed a flat dismissal of animal intelligence for decades, and yet I still believe the corrected version of the canon is the right methodological rule today.

I suspect some of these pull against each other, and I suspect at least one apparent tension dissolves on a closer reading, but I cannot reliably sort the genuine conflicts from the merely apparent ones. I would like a committed audit rather than a survey of options: go through the set, identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only seem to be, rank the genuine tensions by severity, say what each tension would force me to give up or reformulate and whether that fix is cheap, and finally tell me whether this profile hangs together and what the single least costly revision would be if it does not.
