I keep coming back to a weird counterfactual and I want a careful, mechanism-based prediction rather than a guess. Suppose that starting tonight, one thing about human beings changes: whenever a person acquires a cultural item from someone else—a song, a recipe, a joke, a ritual, a political slogan, a technique for doing something—they reproduce it exactly as they received it, with no unintentional copying errors. No forgetting, no simplification, no accidental drift or distortion. But deliberate modification is still possible: people can consciously decide to change a recipe, rewrite a joke, improve a technique, or combine two items into something new. Tastes, social networks, memory, and incentives all stay the same; only the default fidelity of transmission changes.

Predict what the next hundred years or so of cultural evolution looks like. Specifically: which kinds of cultural items become more uniform and stable, and which keep diversifying? Does cumulative cultural change speed up, slow down, or stay roughly the same? I care about the chain of reasoning, not the verdict—how much of cultural novelty is actually driven by unintentional copying error versus deliberate tinkering and recombination, how selection pressures change once variants stop mutating during transmission, and whether things like oral tradition, folk music, religious practice, and office gossip should behave differently from science, technology, and internet slang. Please be explicit about which links in the argument are robust and which are fragile, and say which empirical fact—about where cultural novelty comes from or how people actually copy—would most change you're prediction.
