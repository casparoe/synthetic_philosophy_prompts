I am a PhD student in moral philosophy trying to figure out whether my judgments about reasons are best explained by a general principle or by moral particularism. Below are my verdicts about whether a given consideration is a reason in a concrete case, and I would like you to tell me what account of reasons, if any, captures them.

1. That an act would give someone pleasure counts to me as a reason across very different cases — a child's birthday outing, a colleague's enjoyment of a well-earned break, my own satisfaction on a long run. But I do not think it is a reason at all when the act is allowing a sadist to enjoy another person's humiliation.
2. That an act would cause pain counts against it when the pain is the bruise left by shoving someone, but not when it is the brief sting of a vaccination that prevents serious illness.
3. That I promised counts as a reason to do what I promised even when doing it is inconvenient; I also think the reason can be outweighed. But I cannot convince myself it would be a reason at all if the promise were to help someone destroy evidence of a crime.
4. That an act would reveal something true is a reason to do it when the audience has a right to the information; it does not seem to me to be a reason when the disclosure would ruin a reputation and is offered as dinner-party gossip.
5. That an act would prevent embarrassment is sometimes a reason, as when I refrain from mentioning a friend's medical condition to a stranger, and sometimes not, as when I omit a colleague's plagiarized passage from the materials a hiring committee will review in order to spare his feelings.

I have tried out the thought that some background features are not themselves reasons but enablers or disablers — conditions that determine whether a feature such as pain or having made a promise counts as a reason at all. But I cannot tell whether this distinction lets me keep my verdicts without making the account gerrymandered. Is there a single principle that yields all or nearly all of these verdicts, and what does it say about a case I have not settled: I am asked to break a trivial promise in order to help a neighbor carry groceries when no one else is available? Or do these verdicts push me toward particularism? If you give me a principle, state it precisely and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop. If you opt for particularism, tell me what the view says beyond the bare denial that such a principle exists.
