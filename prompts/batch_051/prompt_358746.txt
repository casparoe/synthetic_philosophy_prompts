So I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the Scopes trial, and I'm still annoyed about it. He kept insisting it was religion versus science and nothing else, and I kept saying that flattens the whole thing into uselessness — that a lot of the actual fight in Dayton was over what it means to read Scripture literally, and what a parent or a legislature is entitled to demand a public school teach. We never got passed repeating ourselves, so now I want it done properly.

Take the 1925 Scopes trial (John Scopes prosecuted under Tennessee's Butler Act, William Jennings Bryan for the prosecution, Clarence Darrow for the defense, conviction later tossed on a technicality) and analyze it through the lens of the long-running debate over literal versus allegorical or spiritual reading of Scripture, the thread that runs from Origen and the Antiochene reaction against him, through the medieval fourfold sense and the Reformers' push back toward the plain sense, all the way down to 1920s fundamentalism on one side and its modernist Christian critics on the other. I know the basic facts of the trial, so don't spend much time recounting them. What I want is the disciplined mapping: where Bryan and the prosecution actually stood as readers — I've read he wasn't a strict six-day man — what the word "literal" was doing rhetorically and theologically for each side, what alternative reading of Genesis the moderate religious position had available, and which features of the trial this lens genuinely explains. Then say where the lens stops doing work: the law, local control of schools, Northern condescension, Bryan's political history, the ACLU's test-case strategy, whatever it misses. Finally, tell me what the episode shows about the literal-versus-allegorical scheme itself: does it confirm the standard story that fundamentalism is just literalism's endpoint, does it complicate that story, or does it suggest that "literal meaning" was doing too many different jobs at once for the framing to carry the case?
