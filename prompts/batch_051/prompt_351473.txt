I am an undergraduate enrolled in a philosophy of science course on evidence, prediction, and the evaluation of long-run forecasts; we have been working through falsificationism, Duhem's problem, and Lakatos's distinction between core commitments and adjustable auxiliary hypotheses, and the instructor keeps circling a puzzle I would like to work through with your help.

The puzzle is whether a predictive theory can be 'right but early.' The episode I want to analyze through that lens is the Simon–Ehrlich wager. As I understand the episode: in 1980 the economist Julian Simon publicly challenged resource-catastrophe claims, arguing that rising populations and scarcity prices would provoke substitution, efficiency gains, and declining real resource costs over time. The biologist Paul Ehrlich and colleagues accepted the challenge. They selected five industrial metals—copper, chromium, nickel, tin, and tungsten—and wagered that the metals' real prices would rise over the decade, as a straightforward expression of the neo-Malthusian claim that growing demand for a finite resource base outruns supply. By 1990 every one of the five was lower in inflation-adjusted terms, and Ehrlich's side paid Simon the wager. Some people treat this as a clean refutation of neo-Malthusian scarcity predictions; others treat it as a poorly chosen horizon that missed the underlying mechanism: not wrong, just early.

Please analyze the Simon–Ehrlich wager as a case study in falsification and rescue. Do not write a brief for either side. I want a disciplined mapping of the theory onto the episode and the episode back onto the theory. Concretely:

1. Specify what was actually at stake. Separate the narrow market bet (five metals, ten years, real prices) from the broader neo-Malthusian claim. The bet tests a conjunction that includes assumptions about substitution, extraction cost, demand, and whether commodity prices track scarcity at all; lay out what a falsificationist should say is refuted when that conjunction fails.
2. Say what the theory requires of the actors. On a strict falsificationist picture, what should each side have specified in advance—which observations would count as dispositive, over what horizon, under what auxiliary assumptions—and did either side behave accordingly once the result came in?
3. Apply the Duhem/Lakatos distinction between core commitments and adjustable auxiliary hypotheses. For each plausible neo-Malthusian response to the wager (wrong horizon, wrong commodities, wrong index, technology as a one-off reprieve, prices as the wrong proxy for scarcity), say whether it looks like a legitimate protective-belt adjustment with independent testable content or a degenerate, face-saving rescue.
4. Assess the episode's force in both directions. What does the existence of a settled, time-limited price prediction show about whether neo-Malthusian claims are testable? And what does the episode miss about long-run predictions—claims whose timescale may exceed any bettable interval—and what does that silence reveal about a falsificationism built around short, decisive tests?
5. Take a verdict. What is the most defensible description of the wager's epistemic upshot: refutation, postponement, rescue, uninformative noise, or something else? Tie the verdict to an explicit criterion for distinguishing genuine rescue from abandonment.
6. Close by saying what would change your mind: which single consideration or finding would be most likely to shift your verdict, and where in the chain of reasoning above you judge yourself most likely to be wrong.
