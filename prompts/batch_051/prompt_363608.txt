Please articulate the most important implications of Good's theorem on the value of information for the design of autonomous systems that can choose which data to request or ignore before acting. Take Good's theorem as the claim that, for an ideal expected-utility maximizer, the expected value of cost-free information is never negative: the option to condition a subsequent choice on an informative signal cannot be worse in expectation than committing to a choice without the signal, because the agent can always ignore the signal. Do not stop at restating the theorem. Your discussion should bring out at least the following. First, the gap between ex ante expectation and ex post regret: why an ideal agent may rationally expect that some information, once received, will lead to choices it later wishes it had not made, even though acquiring the information was worthwhile in advance. Second, the precise assumptions whose failure can make information bad in expectation: information that changes the agent's preferences or affects its welfare directly, strategic settings in which third parties can observe that information was acquired, failures of bounded reasoning or limited commitment when information is imperfectly processed, and acquisition processes that are not causally inert. Third, the practical design consequences: when a planner should permit an agent to query a free, oracle-like sensor without restriction, and when it should instead impose information budgets, commitment mechanisms, or shielding from data. Conclude by stating the strongest general rule you can defend, and the conditions under which a naive application of the theorem would most overstate or most understate the value of information.
