I've been chewing on infinite regress arguments and I think I've found a distinction that does the work everyone wants, but I want it pressure-tested. I'm calling it the difference between a discharging regress and a handoff regress.

Here's the idea. In a discharging regress, each step in the chain actually does some of the work—pays down part of whatever demand kicked the regress off—so even though the chain never ends, the demand gets fully met in the limit. Zeno's runner is the clean case: the intervals 1/2, 1/4, 1/8... each cover part of the remaining distance, and the whole distance gets covered even though there's no last interval. In a handoff regress, by contrast, each step discharges nothing; it just passes the whole demand along intact. It's like paying off a loan by taking out an identical loan, forever. Nothing ever gets payed down; the original problem just gets re-issued at every stage.

So my claim is: a regress is vicious exactly when it's a handoff regress, and benign when it's a discharging regress. The classic epistemic regress would be vicious because "S is justified in believing p only if S is justified in believing q" doesn't give p any partial justification—it just hands the whole justificatory burden to q, and q to r, and so on. Whereas a chain where each supporting belief confers a little justification and the total converges could be perfectly fine even if infinite.

But I can already see a worry: aren't I just restating the problem in fancier language? How do you tell whether a regress 'discharges part of the demand' or 'hands it off' without already knowing whether it's vicious? And doesn't infinitism about epistemic justification say a handoff-style regress is fine as long as the whole infinite chain exists, since nobody has to actually run through it? I'd rather you not just lay out the options.
