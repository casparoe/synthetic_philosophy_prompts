Two friends of mine got into a long argument about our other friend Jonah, who might be the most disciplined person I know. He fasts two days a week, runs every morning before work, doesn't look at his phone after nine, and he keeps all of it even when he's exhausted, traveling, or genuinely miserable. Hes also completely open, almost performatively, about how much he wants the cake or the extra hour of sleep on those days — and then he does the thing anyway.

Marta said Jonah is basically a model of temperance. The fact that he wants the cake and doesn't eat it is the whole point for her: he's governing his appetites instead of being governed by them. If temperance meant "never having a bad desire," then hardly anyone could have it, and the ones we admire would be disqualified for the same reason we admire them.

Dev said that's a weird valentine to willpower. In his view Jonah hasn't changed his desires at all, he's just become very good at suppressing them. At best that's prudence — useful discipline in service of health and work — and at worst it's repression with a halo: the desire stays, gets fed by all the attention paid to it, and comes out as irritability, smugness, or moralizing. He also pointed out that the true temperate person in the old picture doesn't fight himself; his desires are already organized around what matters.

Marta came back that Dev was holding Jonah to an ideal no one could meet without first being the thing he's dismissing. You don't just choose to have well-ordered desires; you get there by repeated refusal. That's habituation. She said the traditional picture is threefold — the temperate person, the continent person who still wants the wrong thing but doesn't act on it, and the incontinent person who acts on it — and Dev was collapsing the second into the third by calling all inner conflict repression.

Dev granted that, but said it makes self-control a repair kit rather than a virtue in itself: continent self-command is only as good as the process of desire-reform it actually cashes out in, and after years of Jonah still fixating on what he's denying himself, maybe the repair isn't working. He said he's not pathologizing all discipline; he's asking what the good is that Jonah's particular rules serve. If the answer is "being the kind of person who keeps rules," then the discipline has become its own object, and there's a real difference between healthy self-control and a self-referential fortress that could equally house an eating disorder or scrupulosity.

Marta answered that this just relocates his suspicion rather than earning it. Fasting and exercise do serve goods outside themselves, and a person's consistency over years is decent evidence they aren't arbitrary. She said Dev's standard makes the boundary between virtue and pathology depend entirely on whether he happens to approve of the goal, which means personality is doing all the philosophical work. Dev paused and said maybe he'd accept that the real disagreement is over whether self-control is a virtue in itself or only the operationalized form of some other virtue, pointing outward. They asked me to referee, and I genuinely couldn't.
