I keep running into the slogan "no self, no problem" — most recently from a visiting teacher at a meditation retreat, where she deployed it to brush off a question about whether Buddhist practice says anything about the philosophical problem of personal identity: whether there is a persisting subject across time, whether it survives fission-style cases, whether we are responsible in the same way if there is no enduring owner of our actions. I know enough to recognize that this is touching on anatta, that anatta is often rendered as "no self" while many scholars prefer "not-self," and that the two translations pull in rather different directions, but I can't tell which of the slogan's possible meanings is doing the work when someone uses it to close down that kind of debate.

I'd like you to treat the sentence itself as the object of analysis and disambiguate it. What are the distinct precise claims "no self, no problem" could be making — from a flatly metaphysical claim about there being no persisting owner of experience, to a narrowly therapeutic claim about self-referential rumination, to a normative stance about not taking things personally, to something more radical still? For each reading, spell out what "self" and "problem" would have to denote for it to come out true, and mark which readings are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false or in tension with the not-self strategy the Buddha actually uses in the discourses on the aggregates. Then say which reading or readings would genuinely support the retreat teacher's dismissal of the personal-identity and moral-responsibility question, and which readings would not. I want a menu of precisifications I could bring back and test against what she probably meant, not a single favored interpretation.
