I have been thinking about the way governments apologize for historical wrongs. A current government will issue a formal apology for something done generations ago — a wartime internment, a colonial dispossession, a system of enslavement — and will address the apology to the descendants of the people originally harmed, even though neither the officials apologizing nor most of the citizens on whose behalf they speak were alive at the time. Often the same government also insists that no legal liability or reparations follow, because the perpetrators are dead or the acts were not illegal when committed. What view of collective responsibility, and of what a state is, does this practice presuppose? Does it require thinking that a state persists as one and the same moral agent across centuries, that present citizens inherit its obligations, and that an apology can be owed to people who did not experience the original wrong? Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the practice, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits with, and explain what would have to change about the practice if one rejected the idea that states and peoples remain the same moral agent over time.
