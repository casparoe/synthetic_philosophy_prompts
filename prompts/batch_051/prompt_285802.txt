I keep coming back to a parallel between reinforcement learning and welfare economics, and I can't decide whether it is deep or superficial. In reinforcement learning, future rewards are multiplied by a discount factor γ per timestep; the standard justifications are that γ<1 keeps the total return finite, expresses the agent's horizon, and models a per-step chance of termination, not that later reward is intrinsically worth less. In long-run welfare economics, the Ramsey equation multiplies future utility by e^(−ρt), where ρ is the pure rate of time preference; there the same exponential shape is routinely read as a normative claim that later welfare matters less, and people argue about whether ρ>0 is defensible. The formal structure is identical: value at a future time is weighted exponentially in the time lag. But the two fields seem to treat the discount parameter in opposite ways—RL as a technical device, economics as a moral parameter. Could the RL justifications for γ<1 be imported to defend ρ>0 in intergenerational judgments, or does the analogy break because RL discounts one agent's own future reward while ρ discounts many distinct people who merely live later?
