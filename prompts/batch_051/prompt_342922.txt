I'm a science journalist working on a piece about what it means to say English actually "has a rule" for things like past tenses and plurals, as opposed to just having a big list of memorized forms that happen to look tidy. I've been running through a bunch of cases and my own verdicts feel like they don't all sit together, so I want to see if there's a principle behind them or if I have to give some up.

Here's where I've landed so far. The regular past tense in -ed is a real rule: new verbs get it automatically (googled, texted), and it doesn't feel like you store each verb separately. But go/went doesn't feel like a rule—it's suppletion, a form grabbed from a different word, and you just memorize it. Foot/feet, tooth/teeth, goose/geese feels more rule-like than go/went because there's a small class sharing a vowel-fronting pattern, but it's also dead: if I coined a noun "blorp," I'd pluralize it blorps, not whatever the vowel-fronted version would be. So I want to call that a rule for a closed class. Same with the f-to-v plurals, leaf/leaves, knife/knives—a sound-change fossil that now looks systematic inside its set—but I say safes and not saves for a new noun "safe," which suggests its not a real productive rule either. Then there are the errors and hypercorrections. Pedants say octopi is a mistake for octopuses (whatever the actual history), but a speaker who learned "octopus, octopi" sure looks like they have a rule, just not the prestige one. And when my niece says goed or bringed, everyone calls it overregularization—but that's exactly what having a rule looks like; she can't have memorized those forms. Yet I don't want to say bringed is a rule of English and brought isn't, especially since brought sits in a little pattern: bring/brought, think/thought, buy/bought, catch/caught.

So the question: is there a coherent account of what a grammatical rule is that captures all these verdicts at once—the productive -ed, the closed vowel-change and f/v classes, suppletion, overregularization, hypercorrection—or does some principled account force me to drop one or two? And where does "still lifes" fall? I insist it's still lifes for the painting genre, not still lives, because the whole compound is a noun; but I can't say why the f/v alternation in life is blocked inside the compound when it's alive in leaves and lives. Give me the principle, test it on cases I haven't considered, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't keep.
