I'm a backend engineer at a company that builds electronic health record software, and for the last few weeks I've been implementing the part where patients store end-of-life wishes, code status, advance directives, things like that. So I ended up reading more than I ever wanted about what a patient is legally allowed to request. Heres the part I can't get straight. A competent adult can basically refuse any treatment on offer — a ventilator, dialysis, a feeding tube, antibiotics — even if refusing means they will definitely die within hours or days. No terminal diagnosis, no waiting period, no second opinion required beyond maybe a capacity check. But the same person, if they want a doctor to actually help them end their life, faces a completely different wall. In the US states that allow medical aid in dying, it's limited to people with a terminal illness and a prognosis of six months or less; two doctors have to certify it; the person has to ask multiple times with waiting periods between; and they generally have to take the lethal medication themselves. If you have a slow degenerative condition, or awful psychiatric suffering, or even a terminal cancer that was found too late to fit the timeline, you're just out of range.

I keep poking at this and it feels like the practice is quietly assuming a bunch of things: that stopping an intervention and causing a death with a prescription are two totally different acts, that terminal illness is somehow more legitimate than other suffering when it comes to choosing death, and that a doctor's job includes letting people die but not (except in this narrow window) helping them die. But I can't tell which of those assumptions are actually doing the work. If you only cared about respecting a patient's own choice, the restrictions would look incoherent; if you thought it's never okay to deliberately shorten a life, then letting someone refuse a ventilator would also look incoherent. What are the possible positions here, which ones does the current practice rule out, and is the whole thing actually consistent, or is it just a political compromise that papers over a contradiction?
