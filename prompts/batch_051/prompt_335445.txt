i've been trying to work out what a future would need to be like to actually be good to live in, not just good on paper, and i keep coming back to something i call the trouble constant. the idea is that a human life carries a roughly fixed amount of felt trouble, and if you remove the old kinds (hunger, back-breaking work, early death) the trouble doesn't dissapear, it just migrates: anxiety about trivia, empty boredom, the sense that nothing you do matters, rows picked for their own sake. so the question for a good future isn't how to eliminate trouble, it's how to grow the right kind of trouble deliberately. by 'right kind' i mean difficulty you've chosen because it matters to you and you can genuinely fail at it: making something, caring for someone, getting good at a craft, trying to answer a real question. the wrong kind is difficulty you didn't choose and can't walk away from. my theory is then that a future is good to live in just in case it keeps the trouble roughly constant while swapping bad trouble for chosen trouble, and that any future that tried to drive trouble to zero would end up producing lives that feel worse, not better. is this just a dressed-up version of something people already say (hedonic treadmill, flow, maybe Aristotle), or is there something of its own in it? and where does it break? is it maybe only true of people like me, not people in general? give me the strongest objections and tell me wich ones, if any, actually kill it rather than just bruise it.
