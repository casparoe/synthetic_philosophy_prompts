Heres an argument I keep running into, and I hate the conclusion, so I'm pretty sure I'm messing something up.

(1) If generational categories like Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z are real kinds (of any sort, not necessarily natural kinds), then there has to be a non-arbitrary fact of the matter about who belongs to one rather than another.
(2) But there isn't: the cutoff years are all over the place, different sources draw different lines, and shifting a boundary by one year doesn't track any underlying change in the people on either side of it.
(3) So there is no non-arbitrary fact about membership in a generation.
(4) So generational categories are not real kinds; they're just conventional ways of slicing up a continuous date range.
(5) If a category is just a conventional slice, then the most it can do is give you an average over an arbitrary window. Any real differences we find between slices are better explained by age effects (people changing as they get older) or period effects (an event hitting everyone alive at once), not by membership in the slice itself.
(6) So generation is not an explanatory variable; it's a crude proxy, and social scientists should stop treating "Gen Z" and "Millennial" as if they named causes.

I'd like to reject (5) or the inference from (5) to (6), because I really dont buy that "shared formative years in a particular era" reduces to age or period effects. But I'm not sure I can defend that without sliding back into the idea that the cutoffs pick out something real. Where exactly does this argument go wrong, if it does?
