I'm running a small book club, and this month we're reading Kurt Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five. Independently, I've been going down a rabbit hole about whether all moments of time are equally real, and the two converged in a way I can't stop thinking about. In the novel, Billy Pilgrim lives the episodes of his life out of sequence, and eventually learns that the aliens who abduct him, the Tralfamadorians, see every moment of time at once, the way one sees a whole mountain range at once. For them, a death is just one bad moment within an arrangement of moments that all exist permanently; the book's flat response to death seems to flow from that. Nothing, in their view, can be altered.

The rabbit hole is the block universe. From popular treatments I've gathered that special relativity implies that which events count as simultaneous with your present depends on how you're moving, so two observers can disagree about which events are already real; and if every such present is equally legitimate, then past and future must be just as real as the present, and the impression that now is uniquely lit up is an illusion. I've seen an argument of this shape associated with the names Rietdijk and Putnam. The Tralfamadorian picture sounds nearly identical, but it also slides toward fatalism: because each moment is fixed, one might as well not try to change anything. I'm not convinced the block universe actually licenses that passivity.

I'd like a careful reading of the novel through this lens, for a group that has read the book once but has no philosophy background, so it should stay close to the story. Where does the Tralfamadorian view genuinely track the block universe, and where does it trade on a confusion between all moments being real and nothing mattering or nothing being changeable? Is Billy's skipping around his own life best read as the world really being that way, or as something the novel leaves deliberately ambiguous—perhaps a mind coping with the firebombing of Dresden? Where does the novel itself, as opposed to the aliens, seem to know the difference between accepting the permanence of the past and giving up on the future? And what does the novel add to the metaphysical picture that the argument alone doesn't capture?
