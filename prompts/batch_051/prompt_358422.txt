I am a doctoral candidate in the philosophy of law, and I sit on the Legislation and Statutes Committee of my university's governing senate. The Committee meets next week to decide how to draft one section of an ordinance integrating the Hartwell Archive into the university, and I would like you to draft the short opinion memo I will circulate beforehand. The memo should recommend a concrete drafting choice, give the reasons for it in terms my colleagues could act on, and state fairly the strongest consideration against the recommendation. Committee members come from several faculties and are mostly not lawyers, so technical terms should earn their place; no citations or reading list, please, since the Committee will not read them.

The background is this. For forty years the Archive was run by an unincorporated association under an informal constitution. It never had legal personality: contracts were signed by successive convenors in their own names, staff were nominally employed by individual convenors, and the original deed of gift names the Hartwell Archive Association as beneficiary. The association dissolved last year, and the archive and endowment are now in limbo. The university has agreed to take the Archive over, and the Committee must choose between two drafting strategies, though it is free to recommend a variant or hybrid.

Option A, the deeming clause: the ordinance would declare that the Association was, and is deemed always to have been, a legal person distinct from its officers from the date of the original gift; that title to the archive and endowment vested in it at that date; and that the university now succeeds to that legal person by operation of the ordinance.

Option B, the transfer schedule: no deeming language. The same end-state would be reached by ordinary conveyancing — current title-holders and creditors execute assignments, agreements are novated, and the university establishes a new archival foundation to receive the assets. The Association would never be declared a person; it would simply be wound up.

The Committee is split in a way that tracks a familiar philosophical disagreement. Some members regard the deeming clause as a paradigm legal fiction and think we should reject it precisely because Option B is available: the clause asserts of the past what was not the case, and the law should not state a falsehood when a truthful restatement is within reach. Others reply that legal personality is already an artifact of legal rules rather than a natural fact, so the deeming clause does not assert a falsehood any more than any other act of legal recognition; on their view, Option A preserves the identity of the Archive across time, while Option B replaces it with a successor and lets the original beneficiary of the donor's gift quietly disappear. A third group thinks the options come apart not in ontology but in consequences — transaction costs, certainty for third parties, fidelity to donor intent — and that the Committee should therefore stop talking about fiction altogether and decide on the drafting merits.

Please draft the memo, roughly 700–900 words, recommending one of the options, a variant, or a hybrid. The memo should end with an actionable bottom line — a form of words for the ordinance, or a sequence of steps for the Committee to adopt — and it should engage the strongest consideration on the losing side without caricature.
