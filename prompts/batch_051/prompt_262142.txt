Most states run an elaborate system of military decorations designed to single out individual acts of valor. A soldier can receive a nation's highest honor for a deed that occupied a few minutes on a single day — storming a position, dragging a wounded comrade through fire, holding a post alone — and the citation fastens on that datable act, performed at serious risk of death, rather than on the soldier's prior character or later life. The occasional civilian bravery awards that some states and private charities maintain mostly track the same model: a single, dramatic, physical rescue under threat to one's own life. What gets no comparable recognition is sustained moral courage — the safety official who spends a decade refusing to sign falsified inspection reports while it costs him every promotion, or the witness who keeps testifying against a powerful organization through years of threats.

I have been trying to work out what theory of courage this institutional pattern presupposes. Does it presuppose that courage is episodic rather than a settled trait, that it concerns physical rather than moral danger, and that its paradigm is the battlefield rather than civic life? Or is the pattern too thin to presuppose any of that — reflecting only the fact that institutions can verify and attribute isolated, datable acts but not slow-burning dispositions, so that a quite different theory of courage might license the identical sets of awards?
