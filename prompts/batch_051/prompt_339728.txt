I edit the science-and-ideas section of a general-interest magazine, and a steady stream of pitches leans on the gender-equality paradox: the reported finding that more egalitarian countries show larger male–female differences in field of study or occupation than less egalitarian ones, offered as evidence about nature, culture, or what justice requires. We need a standing procedure, usable by editors without statistical training in roughly an hour per paper and executable by our fact-checkers, for deciding whether any new version of this result is strong enough to cover and how far our coverage may go in what it asserts. Write that procedure. It should give our editors, in order, the checks that separate a genuine empirical pattern from an artifact of which countries are selected or of how "gender equality" and "gendered career choice" are operationalized, including whether the result is best described as a paradox at all. It should mark where the data stop being able to settle questions of nature versus culture, and it should state which headlines or policy inferences we must refuse whatever the study claims. Apply the procedure to two or three representative cases, including one in which it would require us to kill a pitch we would otherwise have run, and flag the points where editorial judgment cannot be eliminated. Throughout, separate what is broadly settled among researchers from what is genuinely contested, so that we never present a disputed interpretation as the field's consensus.
