People in the wild-animal-suffering debate often talk as if the claim that the average wild mammal's life is net-negative in welfare is a straightforward empirical statement—that there's a fact of the matter about whether predation, injury, parasites, hunger and fear outweigh whatever positive states the animal has. Others say it's mostly a values choice about how to weigh different kinds of experience. I want to know how much evidence could actually settle here. Pick a concrete case, say a well-studied population of deer or voles, and design the best test you can of the claim that their lives are net negative. What would you measure—stress hormones, injury rates, time budgets, play, that sort of thing—and what pattern of results would count as evidence each way? Be specific about where the testable part ends and the value judgment begins. Then: if the test came out net-negative for that population, what would that let us say about wild animals generally, and what would it still leave open? Please separate what's settled among people who actually work on animal welfare and ecology from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
