You are a moral and legal philosopher who works at the intersection of coercion, speech, and the foundations of criminal law, and I want you to think carefully through a variation on the paradox of blackmail for a handout I am preparing for an advanced seminar on coercion. As you know, the canonical puzzle runs roughly as follows. B has a true, reputationally damaging fact about A, obtained lawfully. B threatens to publish it unless A pays him. Publishing the information would itself be a legal act, and asking A for money would itself be a legal act, yet the conditional proposal—pay me or I publish—is treated as blackmail or extortion. The puzzle is to explain how two independently permissible acts can compose an impermissible one, or alternatively to argue that the standard legal treatment rests on a mistake. For purposes of this exercise, set aside the fact that some statutes specifically require a demand of money or property; the philosophical question is whether the underlying wrong, if there is one, depends on the currency of the demand.

Now consider a variation that changes only what is demanded, leaving everything else fixed. B still holds a true, reputationally damaging fact about A, a prominent academic, and B is still fully at liberty to publish it. But instead of demanding money, B threatens to publish the fact unless A publicly endorses B's research program in A's professional circles—an endorsement that A does not want to give, does not fully believe is deserved, and would not give but for the threat, though it is not outright false. B's threatened act (publishing) and A's demanded act (endorsing) are each, taken alone, legal acts; indeed, both are speech acts. Yet the conditional proposal—endorse me or your secret becomes public—strikes most observers as wrongful in roughly the way that blackmail is wrongful, even though no property changes hands and nothing is sold.

Please work through this in stages. Start with a short brainstorming pass: list the structural features of the canonical one-sided, money-for-silence case that any solution must explain, before committing yourself to any account. Then reconstruct, in your own words, two or three leading families of solutions to the original puzzle—at minimum one that locates the wrong in the coercive structure of the conditional threat, one that locates it in the nature of what is demanded (payment, property, or the sale of silence), and any other family you regard as doing important work. For each family, work out in detail what it predicts about the speech-for-silence variant: does it still condemn the conduct, does it collapse, or does it over-generate and condemn plainly permissible agreements, such as two scholars freely agreeing to praise each other's work? Finally, step back and assess what the variation reveals. Does it show that the original paradox is fundamentally about the structure of the conditional threat rather than about what is demanded, or does it expose the extent to which the standard solutions were silently relying on economic features that disappear once the demand becomes non-economic? Identify which family of solutions the variation damages most, and be explicit about the points at which your own reasoning is uncertain or hinges on contested premises.
