Write a dialogue between two interlocutors, one committed to Schopenhauer's account of compassion as the foundation of morality and the other committed to Nietzsche's critique of pity, in which the entire dispute turns on a single passage from Schopenhauer's On the Basis of Morality. That passage, in Part III, section 5, is where Schopenhauer distinguishes actions of genuine moral worth from actions of self-interest: on his account, a morally worthy action must have as its motive the immediate participation in another's well-being or suffering, not the agent's own well-being, whereas the egoist treats another's well-being or suffering only as a means. The Nietzschean should attack this specific criterion, drawing on the critique of pity developed in Daybreak: that pity harms the one who receives it, that it gives the pitier a hidden feeling of superiority or power, and that it spreads rather than relieves suffering. The Schopenhauerian should argue that these objections target corrupted or sentimental forms of pity rather than the immediate non-egoistic motive Schopenhauer identifies, and should press the Nietzschean to say whether any non-egoistic motive remains on which moral worth could rest. The dialogue must not reduce either side to a caricature: the Nietzschean should be capable of granting that ordinary pity sometimes produces good outcomes, and the Schopenhauerian should be capable of granting that pity can be intrusive, self-deceiving, and harmful. The exchange should be a real back-and-forth in which each character responds to the specific argument the other has just advanced, not a pair of set speeches. End without resolving the disagreement, but in a way that exposes the exact premise on which the two positions diverge.
