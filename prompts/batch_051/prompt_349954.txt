I am a philosophy PhD student working on aggregation in population ethics, and a cosmologist in my department has been pressing an argument on me that I cannot refute to my own satisfaction. It appears to prove far too much, and I would be grateful for a precise diagnosis: identify the step that should be rejected, explain why it looked right, and say what rejecting it costs for consequentialism in an infinite universe.

Here is the argument as he gives it.

(1) Classical act-consequentialism ranks an action by the total well-being realized in the world under that action, and says an act is right only if it realizes at least as much total well-being as any alternative.

(2) Our best cosmology gives serious probability to space being infinite and, under standard assumptions, to there being infinitely many welfare-bearing lives. Treat this as given for the sake of argument.

(3) Any ordinary action available to an agent — saving a drowning child, breaking a promise, doing nothing — has a finite causal footprint: it changes the well-being of at most finitely many lives.

(4) So for any two such actions A and B, the total well-being under A and under B can be written as S + a and S + b, where S is the contribution of the infinite background of lives untouched by the choice, and a and b are finite quantities capturing the local differences.

(5) But infinity is insensitive to finite additions: adding finitely many elements to an infinite collection leaves its cardinality unchanged, in the way Hilbert's hotel can accommodate finitely many new guests without gaining a new guest. So S + a = S = S + b.

(6) Therefore A and B produce the same total well-being, and by (1) neither is better than the other. Every action with a finite causal footprint is on a par with every other, including doing nothing.

When I object that a consequentialist can compare actions by taking limits over expanding finite regions of the universe, my colleague has a ready reply. The partial totals over expanding regions need not converge, and when both positive and negative well-being are present they resemble a conditionally convergent series: the order in which the regions are summed can change the limit, while the choice of a center and a sequence of expanding regions is morally arbitrary. So the limit repair either yields no value at all or yields too many values, none privileged. That leaves the original conclusion standing: no finite action is better than any other.

This is where I am stuck. If the argument is sound, it does not just refute consequentialism — it takes every aggregative axiology down with it, and it makes ordinary moral distinctions look like artifacts of a conveniently finite universe. I would most like to keep (1) and (2); I am prepared to revise the arithmetic or the aggregation rule, but I need to know exactly which step is doing the illicit work and what the revision costs. Which step do I reject?
