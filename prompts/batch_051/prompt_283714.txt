I am a novelist rather than a philosopher, and I have backed myself into an argument I need a professional to take apart. I am writing a near-future novel set inside a frontier AI company, and my antagonist holds a view I want to get right: that so long as advanced AI is developed by firms answering to investors, a catastrophe is unavoidable. The version I have drafted for her runs as follows. (1) A firm that voluntarily spends heavily on safety, by slowing down, running expensive audits, or declining to deploy, will be slower or costlier than a competitor that spends less. (2) In a competitive market, the less cautious firm will therefore tend to win customers, capital, and talent, and the cautious firm will be outcompeted or acquired. (3) So no individual firm can afford to treat safety as anything more than a public-relations constraint. (4) Safety can therefore be secured only through binding coordination such as regulation or treaty, which removes the choice from individual firms. (5) But firms and investors, driven by profit, will predictably lobby to weaken, delay, or capture such rules, or move to jurisdictions that refuse to impose them. (6) Therefore, in a global economy organized around the profit motive, no stable regime of binding coordination will emerge in time. (7) Therefore, so long as the profit motive remains the organizing principle of the economy, a catastrophic accident or misuse is essentially guaranteed. (8) Therefore, the profit motive is the root cause of the risk.

The difficulty is that, written out this way, the reasoning seems to apply to every dangerous industry: chemical plants, air travel, nuclear power, vaccine manufacturing. If the same argument shows that each of those is doomed to kill us and that no regulation can ever suffice, then either the argument proves too much, or it really is that general and I should accept a conclusion far larger than my antagonist needs. I cannot tell whether the argument is valid and the only question is which premise to pay for, or whether there is a subtle slide somewhere that I keep missing. I am not attached to any single premise enough to insist on keeping it.

I would like you to act as a professional philosopher working on institutional design and AI risk. Start by reconstructing the argument in its strongest form and flagging the assumptions a critic would push on; then work through it step by step and tell me where it goes wrong if it does, which premise is the least costly to reject, and what rejecting it would commit my character to. If it does not go wrong, say so plainly and tell me exactly which conclusion I must accept. I need the architecture of the reasoning more than a label for it.
