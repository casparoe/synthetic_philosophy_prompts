I'm a PhD candidate in philosophy of art, and I've been asked to run a one-off session for an intro aesthetics course: 22 undergrads, mostly non-majors, in an 80-minute slot. The topic is imaginative resistance. You know the puzzle: people happily imagine all sorts of false or absurd things in fiction (talking spiders, a world with no gravity, a sky that's green) but when a story asks them to imagine something morally deviant as correct—like a narrator treating a mother's decision to kill her child because the child is a girl as the right thing to do, and expecting the reader to share that evaluation rather than just note it as horrible-in-the-story—they stall. I want the students to run into that stalling themselves, not hear about it from me.

What I'm after is a runnable activity for that 80-minute block: rules (or roles, or a structured small-group task), materials, a rough timeline, and a structured debrief. The activity should get them to discover for themselves the difference between refusing to imagine something and being unable to, and ideally the point that imaginative resistance isn't only about moral content—it also tracks who's asking, and whether the story is trying to redefine what counts as "right" or just "true in the story." By the end, students should see why this phenomenon is puzzling for accounts of fiction, not just a case of people being squeamish. They shouldn't hear the phrase "imaginative resistance" until the debrief. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which feature of the phenomenon the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself. Start your response with the activity itself—no preface, no commentary—then give the debrief and troubleshooting afterwards.
