My friends Dana and Marcus got into an arguement last weekend about A New Kind of Science, and they're both still sore, so I'm not going to say which side I was on. I just want someone to go through it point by point. This is roughly how it went:

Dana: "The whole thing is basically a stamp collection. You enumerate a bunch of cellular automata, generate the pictures, and then say, look, rule 30 makes a messy pattern, so maybe nature is a cellular automaton. Pattern tourism."

Marcus: "But the actual claim is that very simple rules generically give complex, and even universal, behavior. That's supposed to explain why the world looks complicated even though physics is simple. That is a real claim about what to expect from nature."

Dana: "Real how? What observation would falsify it? If a system looks complicated, that counts as support for the principle of computational equivalence. If it doesn't, it's simple enough to be excepted. You can't lose."

Marcus: "Sure you can. If a physical system turned out to compute an uncomputable function, or if the only way to get the kind of complexity we see were an astronomically large underlying rule, the principle would be dead. The claim is specifically that simple rules are enough."

Dana: "But 'simple' is doing all the work. Any pattern you like, you can find some simple CA rule that reproduces it after you've already seen the answer. That fits the data, it doesn't predict it."

Marcus: "Well, when a tiny rule reproduces something that everyone thought needed a complicated mechanism, that is a checkable claim. It's not unfalsifiable to say rule 30 gives you randomness without any seed, or that rule 110 is universal. People verified those."

Dana: "Rule 110 being universal is a theorem about cellular automata, and rule 30 looking random is a computer observation. Neither is a prediction about nature, which is the whole pitch. The book mostly moves from 'these patterns are neat' to 'therefore the universe might be this way,' and the second step has no test attached."

Marcus: "But science is allowed to have a cataloguing phase. Taxonomy was science before Darwin. Maybe NKS is the taxonomy stage, and the testable stuff comes later. You're demanding it be a mature theory on day one."

Dana: "Taxonomy doesn't announce itself as 'a new kind of science' and claim that enumerating simple programs subsumes centuries of physics. If you want credit for a method, you don't get to wait indefinitely for the predictions to show up."

I couldn't get either of them to budge. Could you go through this and say who's right about which specific point, and where each of them is overstating or strawmanning? I don't just want a winner; I want the points called one by one, including anything neither of them brought up.
