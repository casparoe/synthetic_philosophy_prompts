I recently joined the research oversight committee at a teaching hospital, and next month I have to lead a one-hour session for incoming residents on why randomized controlled trials are ethically permissible at all. Most of them arrive with a strong clinical intuition that a physician must always give each patient whatever the physician believes is best for that patient, and they have been taught to see the duty of personal care as absolute. I want to teach clinical equipoise in a way that does not make it feel like a bureaucratic loophole or a philosopher’s dodge.

Do not explain clinical equipoise to me. Plan how I should explain it to them. The session has twenty-five residents, one whiteboard, no slides, and no reading beforehand. I need the explanatory sequence: what must come first so that the notion of equipoise is intelligible rather than convenient; which example should carry the whole thing and what the example must have and lack (I suspect flipping a coin at a patient’s bedside is the wrong opening); the misconceptions they will predictably form or bring—for example, that equipoise means the doctor has no opinion, or that it is the same as uncertainty in the individual physician—and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off; and a check for understanding at the end that would show whether they have grasped equipoise as a condition on the community of expert clinicians rather than as a state of one doctor’s mind. Also tell me what to leave out at this level and explicitly say so, especially the difference between theoretical and clinical equipoise if you think it is premature. The session should end with them understanding why a trial can be ethical even though each enrolling physician may personally believe one arm is probably better.
