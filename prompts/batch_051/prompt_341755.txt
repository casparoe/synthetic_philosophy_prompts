I'm co-teaching a unit on the modern Stoicism revival, and I keep going back and forth with my co-teacher about whether the dichotomy of control is an empirical claim about how people get miserable or just a trick of phrasing. The usual line, from Epictetus onward and from a thousand self-help summaries, is that some things are up to us (our judgments, impulses, desires) and others arent (our body, property, reputation, whether our careers pan out), and that suffering comes from caring about the second category as though it were the first. Fine. But the 'trichotomy' objection is that plenty of outcomes are only partly up to you: whether a paper gets accepted depends partly on how good it is and partly on the reviewer's mood. And there's the 'reserve clause' move, where Stoics tell you to add a rider like 'assuming nothing blocks it' to every plan, which seems to convert an external outcome into an internal intention just by rewording what you intended. That makes me suspect the boundary between the two categories isn't discovered at all — it's drawn by how you choose to describe your aim.

So here's the claim I want pressure-tested: the dichotomy of control makes a causal-psychological claim — that people suffer specifically because they treat things outside their control as though they were inside it — and not merely because any old piece of cognitive reframing (distraction, acceptance, lowering expectations) happens to help. Youre an experimental psychologist who also knows the Stoic literature; start by brainstorming the research design, then commit to one. Give me a concrete study that would put real pressure on that claim. Be sure to (a) operationalize what counts as 'treating X as up to me,' (b) include a condition that tests the trichotomy or the reserve-clause reading so we can tell apart 'the taxonomy is a true map' from 'the taxonomy is just good advice about how to rephrase your goals,' and (c) say exactly what a positive result would and would not establish, and how you'd read a null result. I want a committed verdict at the end: does the claim survive, and what do we have to give up to keep it?
