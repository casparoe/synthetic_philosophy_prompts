I am a novelist, and I want to understand the philosophical commitments of an everyday literary practice: the way writers, critics, and readers speak and argue about fictional characters. We say that Sherlock Holmes is a detective and lives at 221B Baker Street, and in the next breath say that Sherlock Holmes does not exist, without feeling that we have contradicted ourselves. We correct the reader who claims that Holmes was married; we call an adaptation unfaithful because it depicts something that never occurred in the stories; we argue about what a character would or would not do in situations the author never wrote. We even make mixed claims that set the fictional beside the real: that Holmes is the most famous detective in the world, or that no real person ever solved the case of the Baskervilles. All of this assigns something very like truth and falsity to sentences whose subjects are, strictly speaking, nothing at all.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of names, reference, and truth under which these moves make sense. Must the practice treat *Sherlock Holmes* as referring, whether to a nonexistent object, an abstract artifact, or a set of properties? Or can it get by just as well on the view that the name refers to nothing and that fictional sentences are true only in a special, non-literal mode? Is being true in the story a species of truth, a relation to a corpus, or something else entirely? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories would license the same corrections and disputes. Then tell me what would have to change in how critics, readers, and I talk about our characters if one of those rivals replaced the practice's implicit theory. End with a one-sentence summary, on its own line, beginning "Summary:".
