I've been trying to reconstruct the anti-luminosity argument for myself, and I've ended up with the following version. I can't tell whether it is valid, and if it is, whether it proves as much as it is usually taken to prove.

Set-up definitions: a condition C is luminous just in case, whenever C obtains, the subject is in a position to know that C obtains. A subject is in a position to know p just in case she can come to know p by reflection alone, without further empirical investigation and without changing the fact in question. The KK principle says that if a subject knows p, then she knows that she knows p.

The argument:

(1) A subject feels cold at dawn and, through a gradual warming, no longer feels cold at noon.

(2) Divide the interval into adjacent moments t0...tn, each so close to the next that the change in the subject's thermal feeling from t_i to t_{i+1} is imperceptible.

(3) If the subject knows at t_i that she feels cold, then she feels cold at t_{i+1}. Knowledge requires a margin for error: since t_i and t_{i+1} are near-duplicates, a belief formed at t_i could easily have been formed at t_{i+1}; if that belief were false at t_{i+1}, the actual belief would be unsafe.

(4) Assume for reductio that feeling cold is luminous: whenever she feels cold, she is in a position to know that she feels cold.

(5) At t0 she feels cold, so by (4) she is in a position to know that she feels cold at t0.

(6) At each moment she exercises the reflective capacity she is by definition in, forming the relevant belief without changing the fact in question; so whenever she is in a position to know, she comes to know.

(7) So she knows at t0 that she feels cold (from 5 and 6).

(8) By (3) and (7), she feels cold at t1. Reapplying (4)-(7) at t1, she knows at t1, so by (3) she feels cold at t2, and so on through every moment up to tn.

(9) But by construction she does not feel cold at tn. Contradiction.

(10) Therefore feeling cold is not luminous. And since the argument used nothing about feeling cold except that it fades through imperceptible stages, no condition that comes in such gradual transitions is luminous.

I half-suspect the move from being in a position to know to actually knowing is too quick, but I can't see where that matters. Is this argument valid? If not, identify the exact step or transition that fails and say on what reading it would go through. If it is valid, say which premise is doing the real work and which one a defender of luminosity should deny. Then say how much of a blow this deals to the KK principle, if any.

When you respond, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
