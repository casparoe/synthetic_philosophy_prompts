I have spent the past few months trying to think systematically about invertebrate welfare, partly because I keep a vegetable garden and last summer I found myself genuinely unable to decide whether it was wrong to clear an aphid infestation, and partly because a friend who works in food policy keeps insisting that farmed insects will be one of the next great moral questions. The argument I have found hardest to shake is what I have seen called the scale argument. It runs roughly as follows. Insects are extraordinarily numerous—on the order of 10^18 to 10^19 alive at any one time, before even considering farmed black soldier fly larvae—so if there is even a modest probability that an individual insect is sentient, the expected quantity of welfare or suffering at stake across all insects swamps the corresponding figures for livestock, pets, or humans. Expected-value reasoning then appears to say that under uncertainty about insect sentience we should act as though they matter, perhaps a great deal: avoid gratuitous harm, change pest-control practices, take farmed-insect welfare seriously, and treat the question of their sentience as morally urgent. When I step back, the reasoning looks almost compulsory—it is just decision theory applied to moral uncertainty, with the numbers doing the rest.

But I also have an intuition I cannot dislodge. When I actually look at an aphid or a wasp, I do not believe it has the kind of inner life that would make its suffering interchangeable, even heavily discounted, with the suffering of a dog or a human child. The practical upshot bothers me too: if the argument works, then an uncertain welfare in astronomically many tiny animals should outrank much of what we confidently owe to a few clearly suffering people or other mammals, and that feels less like moral insight than like a failure mode of arithmetic. I genuinely do not know which side should yield. Present each side at its strongest and give me a negotiated verdict. In particular, is the intuition best debunked as scope insensitivity, status-quo bias, or a failure to imagine alien minds? Or is the theory best amended—by restricting expected-value reasoning under deep uncertainty about other minds, by adopting ambiguity aversion, or by refusing to treat uncertain insect welfare as commensurable with everyone else's? Or are the two compatible on some reading I have not seen, where we can take insect suffering seriously without granting insects full moral standing and without letting the arithmetic set our priorities? And say explicitly what would justify each move rather than merely dressing it up.
