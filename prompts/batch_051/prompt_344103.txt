I'm helping to organize a small philosophy-of-probability reading group that keeps getting stuck in the same debate. The topic is anthropic reasoning: how we should update on facts like "I exist," or "I am one of this many observers," when evaluating cosmological theories or prospects for humanity. Our problem is that people announce allegiance to positions first and then rationalize their answers to thought experiments. I'd like a sorting instrument that goes the other way: readers answer concrete cases, then a scoring key tells them which position they actually hold.

Please act as an expert in anthropic reasoning and Bayesian probability, and start by brainstorming the diagnostic axes that actually split the positions. Then build a 9- or 10-question "which anthropic position do you actually hold?" quiz plus a detailed scoring key. Each question should present a concrete scenario and ask for a probability judgment or a decision, without using labels like "self-sampling assumption," "self-indication assumption," "presumptuous philosopher," or "doomsday argument" in the questions themselves. Please use at least these cases, though you may adapt or add others: the god's coin toss (a fair coin creates either one observer or a million, and you wake up not knowing the coin); the presumptuous philosopher scenario (two well-supported cosmological theories that predict hugely different numbers of observers); an incubator-type case where a coin determines how many observers exist in separate rooms; and a doomsday-argument case involving your birth rank among all humans who will ever live. For each question, give three answer options phrased in terms of credences or choices, and indicate how each answer option maps onto the underlying commitments.

Then present the scoring key as a small decision tree or set of combination rules. The leaves should include at least a self-sampling-assumption view, a self-indication-assumption view, a no-anthropic-updating view, and any coherent but usually unnamed combinations you find in the answer space. For each leaf, write one sentence on the most awkward commitment the person has to accept, and flag any pairs of leaves that your quiz cannot separate. Where an answer combination is logically possible but no one defends it, say briefly whether you think it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Be committed: choose the exact questions and scoring rules you think best, and defend those choices rather than offering a menu of alternatives.
