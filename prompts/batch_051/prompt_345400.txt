Spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a mate about whether a constitution is a Ulysses contract. His line was that entrenched clauses, supermajority amendment rules and constitutional courts are just a polity binding its own future self, because it knows it will be tempted to panic after a terrorist attack or crash the currency during a recession. I kept saying the analogy breaks down at the point where the people doing the binding and the people being bound are not the same people at all — later generations never tied themselves to the mast, so its less self-binding than dead-hand control. We went round in circles for hours.

I dont want the usual conceptual analysis of that dispute. I want to know whether any of it is testable. Suppose someone made the precise claim that constitutional entrenchment really is collective self-binding — the Ulysses story taken literally enough to do explanatory work, not just as a metaphor. What observation or natural experiment could bear on that claim? Design the strongest test you can, with the result that would favour the self-binding reading, the result that would favour the dead-hand reading, and an honest account of why the two can't be fully seperated. Be specific about what a positive result would and would not show — where does the test still leave conceptual residue? And since I'm trying to work out my own view, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to the opposite verdict, or where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
