I write novels, and I am used to the fact that the book exists for me all at once: I draft the final chapter before the middle, and the sequence the reader eventually experiences is one I actively assemble out of material that never presented itself to me as a sequence. Recently I read Borges's essay A New Refutation of Time, in which he argues, through Berkeley and Hume, that temporal succession is not found but projected. Once matter is reduced to perceptions and the persistent self to a bundle of them, there is no enduring subject left to underwrite a before and an after; what remains is a series of states that we retrospectively stitch into time. This looks to me like the eternalist, block-universe view in philosophy of physics, in which past, present, and future are equally real and the flowing now is an artifact of an observer's standpoint. I therefore have three cases before me: Borges's idealist dissolution of time, the atemporal manuscript, and the four-dimensional block. Are these one move—de-privileging the present—run with different materials, or am I only pattern-matching on the image of simultaneity, and does the move succeed in only one of the cases? In particular, is the novelist's relation to the finished book a working model of Borges's argument, of eternalism, or of neither? And if the parallel were exact, what would it predict that turns out false?
