When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other.

—David Hume, An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section VII, Part I

I am teaching a graduate seminar on Hume and the New Hume debate, and I would like to close the session by reading aloud a poem that takes this passage as its epigraph. The poem should have two movements. The first should give the regularity reading: causation as nothing more than constant conjunction plus the mind's habit of expecting the usual. The second should give the New Hume reading: the thought that there might be real causal powers behind the observed regularities, powers of which we can have no impression and which the passage's "never able, in a single instance, to discover" does not rule out. The form should carry the argument. Make the first movement tightly rhymed iambic quatrains, so that the regularities of the verse enact the regularities of nature. Then let the second movement break that meter, loosening into unrhymed lines of irregular length, as though the poem itself has begun to suspect a hidden power beneath the surface. End with a final couplet that does not resolve the debate but shows why this passage, read carefully, can be claimed by both sides. Do not name "regularity" or "New Hume" in the poem; let the imagery and the shift in form do that work.
