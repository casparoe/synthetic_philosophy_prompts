I teach an upper-level seminar that sits between philosophy of action and organizational studies, and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question on whether planning is overrated, but it needs to defeat the standard A-papers: students can recite the planning-fallacy story and the embrace-emergence story from the syllabus without ever engaging the weirder claim we spent two weeks on. The claim is that a plan's main job isn't to predict the future but to let your future self and other people know what you'll count as deviating, and that improvisation isn't the opposite of planning but the same commitment-and-revision loop run at higher speed. I want a question that makes them work with that idea instead of just repeating it. Could you write the question, a model answer around 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 that separates an A from a B by listing the specific moves each must make and the specific confusions that should cost marks? In particular, the marking scheme should flag the mistake of treating poor predictive accuracy as automatically an argument for planning less, and the matching mistake of treating commitment's usefulness as automatically an argument for planning more.
