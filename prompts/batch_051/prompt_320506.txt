During a recent interdisciplinary faculty discussion — prompted by a colleague who argues that legislatures should be bound by referendum results — the phrase "the will of the people" did an enormous amount of argumentative work. When another colleague observed that the referendum proposal was ambiguously worded, that turnout was uneven, and that voters who supported it cited incompatible reasons, the first colleague replied that such objections amount to refusing to respect "the will of the people." I have heard this phrase used to preempt deliberation many times, and I would like to get clear about what it could actually assert. Please lay out the distinct precise claims that someone could be making when they say that a policy or political outcome reflects, or that an institution should defer to, "the will of the people." For each reading, say whether it is a near-platitude that any democrat can accept, a substantive and defensible thesis, or a false or incoherent claim. Then tell me which of those readings would actually justify the move of treating the phrase as a reason to stop examining the wording of the ballot question, the composition of the electorate, or the alternatives available, and which reading was most plausibly doing the work in my colleague's argument. Present the readings as a menu rather than defending one, and be explicit about where the phrase shifts from an empirical claim about what voters wanted into a moral or rhetorical claim about what institutions may do.
