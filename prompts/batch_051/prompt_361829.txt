I've basically come round to the Hacking-style view that psychiatric classifications are interactive kinds. The idea is that classifying people isn't like classifying electrons: people respond to the labels they're given, reinterpret their life histories through them, clinicians tweak the criteria in response, and so the category loops back and changes the very people it was supposed to just describe. Borderline personality disorder, ADHD, the old multiple-personality stuff - these look like cases where the loop is pretty audible.

I want to know what would count against this, genuinely - not the strawmen versions. What would have to be true for the view to be wrong or deflated to nothing? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which of them a committed looping-effects person would themselves accept as a real cost, rather than just what a critic says. For instance: would a psychiatric category that stayed diagnostically stable for decades despite massive public uptake of the label be fatal, or just show looping isn't universal? What would seperate looping from ordinary diagnostic drift or measurment error, and what observations would show we're only seeing the latter? Are there empirical findings about labelling, expectation effects, or diagnostic reliability that would have to come out differently? Does the view even risk anything, or has it been qualified into unfalsifiability - looping happens a bit everywhere, to varying degrees? And if it has been insulated, is that actually a problem for a thesis of this kind, or just a sign that it's a frame rather than an empirical claim?

Personally, I'd be most moved by a category whose criteria shifted after public uptake but where you could show the shift tracked an independent change in the population rather than the classification itself - but tell me if that's even a coherent test.
