I'm the governance lead of a worker cooperative with about 180 members. Every couple of months someone proposes a new way of making a group decision — a ranked-choice ballot for budget priorities, a points system for allocating conference travel, a consensus-with-veto process for policy changes, a two-stage up-or-down vote for electing officers — and I'm the one who has to tell the governance committee whether we should adopt, reject, or amend it. The decisions at stake are real: pay bands, layoff criteria, which projects get funded. I have about 45 minutes before each committee meeting to run an evaluation, the committee has no training in social choice, and the committee has to sign off on whatever I recommend. I want a reusable screen, not a one-off opinion.

I know the rough outline of Arrow's impossibility theorem: the conditions of unrestricted domain (the rule must cope with any coherent spread of preferences), Pareto efficiency (if every member prefers X to Y, the group should too), independence of irrelevant alternatives (the group ranking of X and Y shouldn't flip merely because some third option enters or leaves the picture), and non-dictatorship (no single member's preferences always decide); and I know the result says no rule for aggregating ranked preferences over three or more options satisfies all of them. But I don't know how to turn that into a working test for the proposals that land on my desk, especially when a proposal isn't framed in the theorem's language — a scoring rule, a multi-stage vote, a consensus process. Please write me a decision procedure, a checklist or flowchart in words, for evaluating any proposed group-decision rule in this setting. It should translate the Arrow conditions into plain-language tests a non-specialist committee member can follow, say what to do when we can't elicit a full preference profile or get a clean number, and be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Make sure it handles the hard cases: rules that look democratic but quietly fail a condition (Borda-style scores and independence of irrelevant alternatives is the obvious one), rules that pass the formal conditions but still feel wrong in practice, and blocking powers such as an individual veto that aren't exactly dictatorship but should set off alarms.

Act as a social choice theorist who regularly advises cooperatives and civic-tech groups. Before drafting, think through the failure modes of each worked example below, and don't soften a result just because it's awkward for me. Structure the response as follows:

1. Bottom line (1–2 sentences): what adopting this procedure commits the committee to.
2. The screen (about 350 words): the checklist itself, in order, each step phrased so a non-specialist can follow it.
3. Running it in 45 minutes (about 200 words): the abbreviated version, including what to do when the relevant preferences can't be fully elicited and when to say the decision can't be made under the constraints.
4. Judgment calls (about 250 words): the steps where reasonable people can disagree, explicitly flagged, with the reason they can't be mechanized.
5. Worked applications (about 450 words): apply the screen to (a) a simple two-option majority vote, (b) a Borda-count rule for allocating a budget across five projects, and (c) a consensus process in which any single member can veto a proposal. For each, give the verdict the screen returns and what the committee should do next. Include at least one case where the screen returns an answer I won't like.
6. Limits (about 200 words): what this procedure cannot do — the questions it is not designed to answer and the ways it can be gamed or misread.
