After a long argument with a friend last night about transhumanism, I want to test whether my preferred vocabulary is actually capable of carrying the debate. I am a software engineer, and I have spent years maintaining a long-lived production system; I have no formal philosophy training. My friend defended the view that radically transforming one's mind and body—even into something that would not share most of one's current memories and projects—is not necessarily death, and may be the best available option. I found myself wanting to say that the whole dispute was really about backwards compatibility.

Please restate the main positions in the debate over whether radical self-transformation is desirable and what, if anything, is lost, one position at a time, in the vocabulary I actually use: versions and releases, breaking changes, backwards compatibility, migrations, technical debt, deprecated interfaces, forks, hot-swapped components, stable identifiers versus mutable contents, and the difference between a system continuing to run and a system merely being replaced by a descendant. Include at least the following positions: the view that what matters in survival is psychological continuity and connectedness rather than strict identity; the view that personal identity is brute and all-or-nothing, so radical transformation is a form of death even if a flourishing successor exists; and the view that identity matters less than the quality of the continuation.

Then give me a residue report. Which of those claims translate cleanly, which can be carried only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in the systems vocabulary at all? I would particularly like to know whether my intuition that the death objection is just a backwards-compatibility requirement is right, or whether the translation is smuggling in an assumption the debate does not share. Please write your answer in British English.
