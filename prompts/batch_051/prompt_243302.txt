Ok so in my philosophy club we've been reading about Indian epistemology, specifically the Nyaya school's four pramanas (their accepted sources of knowledge): pratyaksha (perception), anumana (inference), upamana (comparison or analogy), and shabda (testimony). The one I keep getting stuck on is upamana. The classic example is a person who has never seen a gavaya (a kind of wild ox) and is told by a forester that a gavaya looks a lot like a cow. Later the person sees a gavaya in the forest, recognizes it, and in doing so learns what the word gavaya actually refers to. The Nyaya philosophers thought that bit of knowledge—the connection between the word and the thing—is produced by comparison itself, and that it can't be reduced to just perception plus memory plus testimony.

So here's the policy I've ended up with, and my friend says I'm just being stubborn about it. Whenever someone tells me they know something by analogy or comparison, I refuse to count comparison as a source of knowledge on its own. I make them cash it out in one of the other three: did you actually perceive the thing, did you infer it, or did a reliable person tell you? If it can't be reduced like that, I file it under guess. It feels like a good filter, because in my experience analogy is how people smuggle in a conclusion without doing the actual work. But Nyaya kept upamana as a seperate pramana for a reason, and maybe I'm throwing out a whole category of reasoning just because I've seen it abused alot.

Is my rule defensible? When would it be a reliable policy and when would it just be a bias dressed up as a rule? And is there a restated version I could keep that gets the filtering benefit without losing whatever upamana is actually good for?
