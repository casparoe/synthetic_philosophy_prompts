I'm a PhD student in music cognition, and I keep trying to think through the Blurred Lines verdict with Hofstadter's knob-twiddling model of creativity. The model says creative acts are variations on a theme: you take a conceptual skeleton from some existing work and turn various knobs — timbre, register, harmonic rhythm, lyrical content, whatever the relevant dimensions are — until you get a new thing. The interesting question on that view is not whether something is original in any ex nihilo sense, but which knobs were turned and how far.

Take the 2015 Blurred Lines case as a stress test. Robin Thicke and Pharrell Williams made a song that clearly was meant to evoke Marvin Gaye's Got to Give It Up; they didn't sample it, and nobody seriously claimed they copied the melody or lyrics note-for-note. The Gaye estate won anyway, with the case coming down to a claim that Blurred Lines had taken the underlying feel, the groove, the party vibe. The verdict scared a lot of musicians because it seemed to make a style or a feel protectable. So here's what I want: map the two songs onto the knob-twiddling model. What was the original theme, what knobs did Thicke and Williams actually turn, and which knobs did they leave at Gaye's settings? Is Blurred Lines, on Hofstadter's picture, a variation on Got to Give It Up or a new theme that merely references it? Then do the second direction: what does the case show about the model? My worry is that the model has no principled way to mark the boundary between variation on an existing theme and new theme, and copyright law had to draw exactly that line. Did the jury just pick the wrong boundary, or does the episode show that the knob-twiddling model is descriptively right but normatively useless for distinguishing permissible creative borrowing from infringement?
