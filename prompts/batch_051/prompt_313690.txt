In a seminar on the problem of evil, I tried to reconstruct the free-will response as it applies to the fall of Satan and the rebel angels, and I ended up with an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept even though each step seems forced. I want a diagnosis: either tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs, or tell me the argument is valid and which conclusion I should learn to live with.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) God is omnipotent, omniscient, and perfectly good, and created the angels good, with a free will.

(2) On the account I am working with, the rebel angels were not deceived by another creature, coerced, or subject to ignorance or unruly appetite; their sin arose solely from their own free choice, so nothing about their creation explains the fall.

(3) To be free in the sense that makes a creature morally responsible just is to have the power to choose between good and evil; a creature that could not choose evil would not be free in the required sense.

(4) Now consider the blessed in the final state. Either they retain the power to choose evil, or they do not.

(5) If they retain it, then a second fall remains possible even in heaven, and no created being's final state is ever settled; the tradition, however, treats the final state as one that cannot be lost. If they do not retain it, then the highest good for rational creatures is compatible with lacking that power — but if so, God could have created the angels in that sinless condition from the beginning, and permitting the fall, with all the evil that follows from it, served no purpose that the free-will response actually supplies.

(6) Therefore the free-will response to the fall of the angels either contradicts the finality of heaven or makes the fall a risk a perfectly good God had no reason to run; in either case the response fails.

I would most like to keep (1) and (2), since they are the commitments I actually hold, and abandoning them seems to give up more than the argument is worth. But I want each candidate rejection priced: what does it cost the free-will defense for human sin, and what does it cost the claim that the blessed in heaven genuinely merit their final state? If you think the argument is valid, tell me which horn of (5) you take and why it is less bad than it appears. And if the argument as I have set it out rests on a confusion or a false presupposition, I would rather be told that plainly than have it repaired politely.
