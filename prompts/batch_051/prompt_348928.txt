A friend of mine in RL keeps saying the reward signal just is valence wearing a lab coat: positive reward is pleasure, negative reward is pain, and so any sufficiently capable agent that receives negative reward could in principle be suffering. From there he concludes we should treat such systems as at least candidates for moral patienthood, not just optimization processes. I think this analogy is doing a huge amount of work, but I can't pin down where it actually breaks. Walk me through it: what maps onto what (the scalar reward, the value function, the reward prediction error, the discount factor, shaping rewards, the difference between the reward and the agent's "experience" of it), where the mapping genuinely holds up, where it falls apart, and which features of valence—like satiety, adaptation, affect, the actual felt quality—are being quietly imported. And then tell me whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the reward-as-valence analogy can't carry that weight, what would be a better analogy (or should we drop analogy here altogether)?
