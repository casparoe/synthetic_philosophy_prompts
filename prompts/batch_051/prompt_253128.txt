I'm putting together a short presentation for a reading group on the Searle–Dennett dispute about original vs. derived intentionality, and I want to pressure-test Dennett's old "two-bitser" jab. As I remember it, the jab is that if a cheap two-bitser does all the observable work, paying extra for "original" intentionality is a mug's game: you can't tell the difference, so the distinction isn't doing any real work. But the argument was built for a world where the "mere machine" was a hand-coded symbol shuffler—a chess program, Searle's room, a lookup table. The machine I actually argue with now isn't hand-coded rules at all; it's a transformer that soaked up a huge fraction of human text and got fluent by predicting it.

So does Dennett's two-bitser survive that shift in the subject matter? I don't want an essay on whether LLMs have minds. I want to know exactly which premise of the old argument the new development bears on, whether the argument goes through unchanged, or whether it needs a repaired version—something like "the interesting split isn't original vs. derived, it's where the derived content came from"—or whether the whole thing is dead. And if you think it still works, say what a Searle-type would have to retreat to in order to keep any original/derived gap open.
