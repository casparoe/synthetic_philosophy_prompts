A friend made this argument at dinner and I cant figure out if it's airtight or if there's a sleight of hand somewhere. Take some ordinary thing you know, like that your bike is locked up outside the cafe. She said: (1) If you know your bike is outside, then your bike is outside. (2) If your bike is outside, then any evidence that it's not outside is misleading evidence. (3) So if you know your bike is outside, then any evidence against it is misleading. (4) If a piece of evidence is misleading, then when you encounter it you can rationally set it aside, because being moved by misleading evidence is just being fooled. (5) Therefore, as long as you know your bike is outside, you can rationally ignore any evidence that it isn't. But that can't be right. You could walk outside, see an empty bike rack, and watch a van drive off carrying a bike that looks exactly like yours, and on this argument you'd be rational to say 'well, all of that is misleading evidence' and do nothing. Surely at some point you should update, even give up the belief. So which step fails? If step 4 is the culprit, what's the correct version of it? Or is the conclusion maby actually true and just less scary than it sounds?
