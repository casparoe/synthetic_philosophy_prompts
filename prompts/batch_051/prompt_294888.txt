I'm a novelist, and I've been chewing on something for the past couple years while splitting time between Berlin and the Midwest, and I want someone to tell me whether it's an actual idea or just my narrator-brain mistaking style for substance.

Ive been calling it the difference between "floor conscience" and "ceiling conscience." In the American folk morality I grew up with, conscience is the floor: it's what you stand on when the rules feel wrong. You break the rule and you're not falling into anything — you're standing on something more solid than the rule was, and you can justify yourself from there. In the German folk morality I've slowly absorbed from my partner's family, conscience is the ceiling: it's the upper story, the thing the rules hang from, and you only look up at it when two rules collide or a rule has obviously run out. Day to day, the rule is the load-bearing thing, and asking what your conscience says about an ordinary rule is almost a category mistake, because the rule is already conscience doing its job in manageable pieces.

Example. My American brother will drive an empty highway at 3 a.m., see a speed limit that strikes him as absurd, and slow down only if he feels he's actually endangering somebody, which at that hour he doesn't. My German mother-in-law sits at a red light on a street with no cars and no pedestrians at 2 a.m. and waits, not resentfully, as if the light is what's holding the night together. Neither one thinks the other is a bad person. They think the other is morally mis-wired.

The central claim, I guess, is that in a floor-conscience morality, rules are heuristics — shortcuts conscience has laid down for ordinary cases, always open to renegotiation — whereas in a ceiling-conscience morality, rules are the primary moral fact, and conscience is a rare emergency organ, not a standing source of permissions. I keep wanting to use this to explain why American characters in my novels feel licensed to improvise, and why German characters keep asking permission, and also why each culture so reliably produces its own peculiar form of moral self-congratulation.

So: is this a real distinction or a novelist's projection? Does it already exist under a better name somewhere in moral psychology or cultural sociology, and I've just been reinventing water? Where does it break — what kinds of cases does it get wrong or smuggle in? And if some version of it survives, which arrangement actually has the better of it once you strip out the national romanticism?

Please be candid rather than polite, and when you answer, say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure about.
