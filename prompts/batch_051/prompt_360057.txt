In my political philosophy class we discussed Spinoza's account of democracy, and I wrote down three criteria that seem to be doing the work. (1) Each person transfers their natural right not to another person or to a smaller group, but to the whole society of which they remain an equal part, so that everyone stands equally under the common decision and no one becomes the subject of someone else's private will. (2) The right and power of the commonwealth is just the united power of the multitude, not the power of a ruling few. (3) The laws express, at least roughly, a common mind or shared judgment of the multitude; if laws were simply imposed on a diffuse population that never formed such a mind, the label would no longer fit.

Now consider a large modern state with universal suffrage, legal equality, and contested elections, but in which almost no citizens ever deliberate together; policy agendas are shaped by microtargeted advertisements and engagement-maximizing recommendation algorithms; legislators vote on thousands of pages of negotiated packages; and the majority that elects a government is itself produced by the same machinery the government later claims to represent.

Is that state a democracy in Spinoza's sense? Go through the three criteria, and for each say whether the state clearly meets it, clearly fails it, or whether the criterion does not yet decide the case. Then say whether the hardest failure shows that one criterion is doing all the real work, or that the criteria themselves need to be revised for mass, mediated societies.
