I'm a retired chemist who spent the last year going through Epictetus and Seneca more or less from scratch, and I've gotten kind of obssessed with how the online "modern Stoicism" scene mangles the dichotomy of control. I'd like you to write a satirical piece targeting that practice — specifically, the way Stoic ethics gets turned into a productivity hack by people who use "control" to mean influence over outcomes.

Write it as a mock Terms of Service / FAQ for a self-optimization app called Prohairesis (the Stoic term for the faculty of choice — I assume the founders googled it), in deadpan corporate legalese and cheery FAQ copy, around 600 words. Keep the irony completely straight-faced, and make the jokes come from real Stoic content, so an ancient philosophy specialist laughs instead of wincing. I don't need it to be funny to a general audience; I'd rather it be accurate about the philosophy.

Here's what I want it to get right. Epictetus's "up to us" is much narrower than the modern self-help sense of "what you can influence": it covers only the activity of the ruling faculty — opinion, impulse, desire, aversion — while body, property, reputation, and career are not up to us. So the app should constantly sell externals as if they were up to us (say, promising that users can simply choose not to be stressed by a server outage) while simultaneously disclaiming all responsibility for that outage. Also, work in preferred indifferents and the reserve clause: the app should have a "Preferred Indifferent" premium tier and recommend attaching a reserve clause (if nothing prevents it) to calendar invites, while treating both as emotional hedging rather than as ideas about where the good lies and how a rational agent pursues preferred externals without making them ends. And get the Stoic theory of emotion in there too: the app should advertise that it will help you "extirpate all emotions," which is not Stoicism, since the goal is transformation of the passions (pathe) into rational good feelings (eupatheiai), not affectlessness. If you can, add the Lazy Argument confusion: the terms say that since server outages are fated, maintaining the servers is pointless, but that users are nonetheless liable for their data — when the actual Stoic reply is that our actions are co-fated with outcomes, not idle.

End with a straight-faced paragraph from the app's founder complaining that the academics criticizing the app clearly haven't read Epictetus — and have her own sentence immediately misread one of these distinctions, so the reader sees exactly what she has read (probably one blog post). And please don't wink at the reader or break the corporate voice.
