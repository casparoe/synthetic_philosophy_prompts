You are a decision theorist and philosopher of economics who works on risk, stochastic processes, and the foundations of cost–benefit reasoning. I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-audience magazine. In my reporting I keep running into two lines of argument that feel as though they share a single underlying structure, and I want a considered opinion on whether the resemblance is deep or whether I am merely pattern-matching on the vague idea that averages can mislead.

The first line of argument comes from ergodicity economics. It claims that maximizing expected value can be the wrong guide for a single decision-maker facing a gamble that repeats over time, because the average across parallel realizations, the ensemble average, and the average along one realized history, the time average, need not coincide. A gamble that multiplies one's wealth by 1.5 with probability one-half and by 0.6 with probability one-half has an expected return of +5 percent per round, yet the typical long-run trajectory loses about 5 percent per round and wealth collapses toward zero almost surely. The expectation is being pulled upward by the rare histories that get lucky many times in a row, and a single player cannot count on living one of those histories.

The second line of argument is the precautionary principle as it tends to be invoked in environmental and public-health regulation. On one structural reading, the principle objects to something very similar in cost–benefit reasoning: an expected outcome can look acceptable because the disasters are low-probability, while the one history the world actually follows can be catastrophic and irreversible; once the harm occurs, it cannot be averaged away by the scenarios in which it did not happen.

The parallel I think I am seeing is this. Both arguments hold that an expectation computed over a distribution of possibilities can be a poor guide to the single path that is actually realized, and that the divergence matters when bad outcomes are absorbing in a technical sense: ruin or extinction ends the story, so no later run of good luck can repair the damage. Is that a genuine identity of structure, and if so, what transfers from one setting to the other? For instance, does the ergodicity literature's prescription to maximize a time-average growth rate give the precautionary principle something it has always lacked, namely a precise criterion for when to be precautionary and when not to be? Or is the analogy superficial, because the precautionary principle is about unknown probabilities, irreversibility, and the distribution of costs across people, whereas ergodicity economics assumes a known stochastic process and a single decision-maker?

Please think carefully and work through the analogy before committing to a verdict. Begin by brainstorming the strongest version of each argument and the senses in which harm can be absorbing. Then state the mapping you would defend between the two arguments, say what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer, and identify at least one thing the parallel seems to predict that turns out false. Finally, give a clear verdict on whether the parallel is deep, superficial, or a mix, and where exactly the line between the genuine structure and the false analogy falls.
