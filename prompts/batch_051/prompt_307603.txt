In their 2012 article "Free-energy minimization and the dark-room problem" (Frontiers in Psychology), Friston, Thornton, and Clark stage the dispute over the free-energy principle as a dialogue between an information theorist, a physicist, and a philosopher. At one point their information theorist raises a circularity worry that I think has never been fully answered:

"Surprise must be defined relative to the right interpretation (i.e., model of probability) in order to get the adaptive effect. Invoking the surprise-reduction hypothesis as an account of how this interpretation comes into existence then seems to produce a circular explanation."

I keep returning to this passage because it seems to me to be the point at which the long-running impasse over the free-energy principle stops being a scientific dispute and becomes something else. On one side, Friston and his allies treat the principle as a deep unification: any self-organizing system that persists must minimize free energy in some sense, and the substantive work consists in deriving process theories such as predictive coding and active inference from that constraint. On the other side, critics reply that the principle cannot be tested against behavior, because any behavior can be redescribed as free-energy-minimizing under some choice of generative model or prior preferences, so the empirical content is smuggled in by that choice. So far as I can tell, neither side disputes the mathematics; they dispute what the mathematics entitles anyone to say.

My working hypothesis is that this is less a disagreement about brains than a disagreement about what an explanation of the brain has to do: one side accepts a constraining principle as explanatory even when it does not by itself issue in behavioral predictions, while the other side denies that formal derivability is itself empirical content. But I can think of rival diagnoses too. It might be a hidden disagreement about teleology and whether goal-directed attributions are legitimate in biology; a difference about which examples count as data (the dark-room objection versus simulations and neuroimaging); or a largely verbal ambiguity in "minimizes free energy" between a state claim and a process claim.

Using the passage above and what the two sides actually say, diagnose this impasse. Is it verbal, substantive, or two groups answering different questions under a single heading, and how would one tell? Most importantly, be concrete about what would count as progress if your diagnosis is right: a distinction, a theorem, a decisive experiment, a reframing of the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
