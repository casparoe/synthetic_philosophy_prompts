I cover science and ideas for a living, and the Malthus argument keeps happening on a loop: somebody makes a big long-run prediction, the date comes and goes, and the theorist says it wasn't wrong, just early. Malthus claimed population tends to outrun the food supply; Ehrlich in the late sixties predicted mass famines by the 1980s; the limits-to-growth folks said resource collapse within decades. A demographer told me over coffee last month that Ehrlich has basically been vindicated on a longer timer, and I found myself agreeing with half of it and wanting to throw the other half out. I've been trying to figure out when that 'just early' move is legitamate. I've arrived at a two-part test I'm calling the early/absent distinction. First, ask whether the mechanism the theory actually named was operating in the predicted direction, just slower or swamped by other forces. If it was, the theory was early. If the mechanism never operated at all and the bad outcome later arrived (if it arrived) by a different route, the theory wasn't early, it was wrong. Second, a theory only gets to say 'not yet' if it said in advance what would make it give up; otherwise right-but-early is just unfalsifiable forever.

I can't tell whether this is obvious, original, or quietly circular. My gut says Malthus passes the first test only in a weaselly way: the arithmetic-of-food mechanism looks plain false, while 'closed systems have carrying capacities' is true but says almost nothing. Ehrlich mostly fails the second test. But those are exactly the cases where my own judgment is probably polluted, so I want an outside check. Is the early/absent distinction a real distinction, or am I reinventing something philosophy of science already has a name for? If there is a name, what is it? What are the strongest three objections to the distinction, and is any of them fatal rather than just uncomfortable? And give me the best counterexample you can: a theory that passes both tests and is still bad science, or one that fails both and is still good science.
