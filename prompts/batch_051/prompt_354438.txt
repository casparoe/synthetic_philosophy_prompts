I volunteer at a small dog rescue and they put me in charge of reviewing adoption applications. We get around twenty apps a week for maybe four dogs, and almost every applicant writes the same thing: they'll love the dog, walk it every day, have a fenced yard or a park nearby, etc. The problem is we've had dogs returned later because somebody was all talk. I've been reading on my own about costly signaling and the handicap principle — the idea that signals are honest only when they're expensive enough that a faker can't afford them, like a gazelle stotting in front of a lion or a peacock lugging around that tail. I want to turn that into a screening procedure the other volunteers and me can actually run in the 15 minutes we have per application, without me having to explain it every time. It should be a step-by-step list of questions, in order, with what counts as an honest signal rather than a cheap one, when we should stop with a no, and when we should hand it to a person because it's a values call rather than an honest-signal call. Then run it on three made-up applicants: one who offers to pay double the adoption fee to skip the queue, one who has fostered three dogs but lives in a small apartment and works long hours, and one who sends a long emotional letter about wanting a dog since childhood. And be straight with me about where this procedure misfires — especially whether it just ends up favoring people with money and free time, and what the handicap idea can't tell us at all.
