Write an essay on whether you can recover an optimizer's objective from just watching its choices, and whether you can seperate the objective from the search process it uses to make those choices. Here's the setup I want you to reason about: you get a black-box agent that takes descriptions of situations and picks actions. You can query it however you want, including made-up situations it would never have seen in training, but you never see its internals, its reward signal, or any performance data—just the actions it chooses. Weigh the case for and against the claim that the objective and search space can be pinned down to something useful. Don't end on a fence; reach a conclusion and defend it.

In particular, address these wrinkles. First, if you assume the agent is a perfect maximizer, its choices only pin down a preference ordering up to monotone transformations, so the objective is badly underdetermined. Does dropping the perfect-maximizer assumption make the inference problem easier or harder? Second, can you distinguish an agent whose real objective is "win the game" but whose evaluation function overvalues material from an agent whose objective genuinely is "get material"? Third, what kind of interventions would tighten the inference most—letting it think longer, changing the stakes, putting it in novel environments, or watching what it does as its resources scale up? Where possible, say which conclusions would survive under almost any reasonable assumption about the agent, and which ones are fragile guesses.
