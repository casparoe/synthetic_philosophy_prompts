Write a decision-tree handout for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the Presocratics that sorts a reader into one of seven positions on the Eleatic denial of plurality and change: strict Parmenidean monism, Melissian monism, Zenonian aporetic denial, atomism, plenum pluralism (Empedocles and Anaxagoras taken together), Platonic two-worlds, and Aristotelian actuality–potentiality. Build the handout from the logical space of three questions before attaching any names:

1. Can something genuinely come to be from what is not, and perish into what is not, or is every apparent coming-to-be and perishing only the rearrangement or mixing of what already exists?
2. Is what-is in the strict sense exactly one, or more than one?
3. Does change among many things require independently existing empty space, the void, or can it occur in a plenum?

Open with a small table of the answer combinations these questions produce, marking cells where a later question is moot or where a combination is unstable given the earlier answers. Then locate the seven named positions in the occupied cells, and give every unoccupied cell a one-sentence diagnosis: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unattested among the ancient positions.

Then give the branching key itself. Within the cell for those who deny genuine coming-to-be, hold that what-is is one, and deny the void, subdivide: first ask whether the reader actually affirms a positive account of the one or only the negative conclusions that plurality and motion are impossible; that separates a Zenonian aporetic leaf from the positive monists. Then, for the positive monists, ask whether the one is a complete, bounded, timeless whole or an everlasting and unlimited one; that gives separate Parmenidean and Melissian leaves. Within the cell that allows genuine coming-to-be, plurality, and no void, split Plato from Aristotle with a question about whether changing things are a deficient image of an unchanging order or are fully real and explained by their own potentialities.

Phrase every node as a question a student could answer without already knowing which philosopher it leads to, so the handout classifies readers by what they believe rather than by which label they prefer. At the end, say explicitly which pairs of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate, and note that Empedocles and Anaxagoras are deliberately grouped: state which further question would separate them (a finite number of basic elements moved by opposing forces, versus an indefinite mixture of all things set in order by mind) and either add that as an optional sub-branch or explain why the handout stops short of it. Begin with the handout itself, with no preface, commentary, or restating of the assignment.
