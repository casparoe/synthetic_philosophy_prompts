Give me a working definition of a costly signal that gets the following cases right: a peacock's train; a gazelle stotting (the stiff-legged bounding display it performs in front of a predator); a poison dart frog's bright warning coloration; a rattlesnake's rattle; a red deer stag's antlers; a luxury watch worn conspicuously during a negotiation; an expensive credential that demonstrably teaches nothing job-relevant; and a spontaneous blush when someone makes an accusation. I am confident the peacock's tail, the stotting, the watch, and the credential are costly signals, and fairly confident the blush is not a signal at all. I am genuinely unsure about the warning coloration and the antlers. Take the handicap principle as your starting hypothesis—that a signal is honest because it is expensive to fake—but do not let it do all the work: your definition should also say where honesty is secured instead by the physical impossibility of faking (what some call an index), and where a costly trait is no signal at all. Propose the definition, run it through all eight cases, revise it where it fails, defend any surprising verdicts, and finish by saying which of your verdicts is least secure. Please write in American English.
