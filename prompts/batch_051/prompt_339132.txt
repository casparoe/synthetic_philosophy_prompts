I have spent the last few months reporting on the researchers and pet owners now debating whether dogs trained on "communication boards" are doing something more than producing reinforced button-presses. The argument circles back, again and again, to a nineteenth-century sentence I first had to translate for my readers: Morgan's canon, in its developed form: "In no case is an animal activity to be interpreted in terms of higher psychological processes if it can be fairly interpreted in terms of processes which stand lower in the scale of psychological evolution and development."

The case I cannot settle is this. A border collie in a long-running household study has a board of about sixty buttons — people, places, outside, play, later, sad, water, and so forth. Over two years it produces strings that no one explicitly shaped or rewarded: "stranger" then "outside" then "sad" after a frequently visiting guest leaves for good, and "water" together with "toy" on the day its ball is sitting in the water bowl. Assume, for the sake of the question, that the reporting is accurate and the trainer did not train these combinations; the dog put them together. The canon was written for a world of garden gates and terriers, not for an animal operating a human-built symbolic interface: is trained button-pressing an "animal activity" in its intended sense, or is much of the load being carried by the human design of the board's symbols? And does it matter whether one reads the canon as Morgan's methodological restraint — prefer the lower explanation for the purposes of science — or as a general epistemic principle that the lower explanation is more likely to be true? I would like the response to tell me how each of those readings applies to this case; what "fairly" could mean here, when the lower story is not a simple habit but a sprawling associative network as hard to specify as the competence it would displace; and which reading should govern if the two diverge. Then, since I expect to keep returning to this argument as the story develops, say explicitly where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what piece of evidence — a controlled experiment, a longitudinal record, a particular button sequence — would most plausibly change your verdict.
