Suppose a constitutional democracy creates a Standing Office of Emergency License, a three-member body empowered to issue dirty-hands licenses. When all three members certify that a specified action is the only way to avert a catastrophe and that no lawful alternative remains, the named officials may carry out the action while shielded from criminal and civil liability. Licenses expire after ninety days. Ten years later, every license and its supporting certification is automatically unsealed, and a randomly drawn tribunal of retired judges decides by majority vote whether the certification was reasonable at the time. If the tribunal finds it unreasonable, the members who signed it lose their immunity retroactively and face ordinary prosecution. Former members receive indexed pensions but are permanently barred from any other public office.

Analyze this as a mechanism rather than as a moral proposal. What does it incentivize among the members, among presidents and agencies that request licenses, among the review tribunal, and among voters? How would it likely be gamed or captured, for example through certification drafting, the choice of which cases to bring, the timing of unsealing, or the selection of tribunal members? What second-order effects would it have on the distinction between lawful and unlawful state action? Where would the scheme fail first, and can any of its failure modes be fixed without creating worse ones?
