I've been trying to sort out what the simulation argument does to expected-value reasoning about the far future, and I keep returning to what may be a real structural analogy or merely a seductive one. The simulation argument, in broad outline, is a trilemma: civilizations at roughly our stage almost never survive to the point where they could run ancestor simulations; or civilizations that reach that point almost never choose to run them; or we are almost certainly living inside one now. When I plug this trilemma into longtermist cause prioritization, it behaves the way a Pascal's Mugging behaves in ordinary decision theory. In a mugging, an unfalsifiable, low-probability claim comes attached to an arbitrarily large payoff, and a straightforward expected-value calculation says the mugger should dominate my choices, even though the calculation was supposed to serve my goals rather than allow every imaginable story to reorder them. The simulation argument appears to have the same shape at civilizational scale: a low-credence, unfalsifiable possibility, that there may be no recognizable far future or that my actions matter mostly through a simulator I cannot inspect, threatens to collapse or redirect the astronomical expected value that longtermism assigns to later centuries. Yet there is an obvious disanalogy: the mugger extracts a concrete cost in exchange for a promised payoff, while the simulation argument demands nothing and instead changes which future I should count as ours, not which bet to accept. I cannot tell whether that difference breaks the analogy or merely relocates it. I would like to know whether simulation-dominated futures are genuinely a species of Pascal's Mugging, so that the standard responses, such as penalizing complexity, refusing to countenance unbounded payoff spaces, and requiring independent evidence that the posited mechanism exists, carry over cleanly to fanaticism about the far future, or whether the trilemma's disjunctive structure makes the parallel merely verbal. If it is deep, the parallel should make a prediction; what does it predict that turns out false, and what would have to be true of both sides for the transfer to go through?
