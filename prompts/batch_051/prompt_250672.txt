I am presenting a paper to a mixed criminal-law theory workshop. My thesis is that limitation periods for offences are best justified not by the familiar evidentiary rationale — that memories fade and witnesses die — but by a moral principle about the passage of time: punishment becomes unfitting as time passes, even when the evidence remains perfect, because the person who would now be punished is, in a morally significant though partial sense, no longer the person who committed the offence. I argue that responsibility tracks narrative continuity between a person's present and past selves, and that decades of otherwise law-abiding life can thin that continuity enough to make punishment unfitting rather than merely impractical. This commits me to saying that even a perfect-evidence prosecution decades after the offence would be pro tanto objectionable. The audience includes two retributivists convinced that culpability never diminishes, a legal epistemologist who considers the fading-evidence rationale the only respectable justification, a reductionist about personal identity who doubts that narrative continuity can bear this moral weight, and several criminal-law generalists concerned about victims and symbolic condemnation. Prepare me for the question period: give the objections in the order I should expect them, phrased as those audience members would actually put them, what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.

Give the prep sheet as a table with these columns: Order (integer, expected sequence); Source (the audience member or commitment behind the objection); Objection (two or three sentences, in their voice); Likelihood (high, medium, or low); Severity (high, medium, or low); Reply (one or two sentences, what I say back); Concession (what I should grant); Weakest point (where my reply is most exposed). After the table, add a single sentence headed Feared objection stating the question I am most hoping nobody asks.
