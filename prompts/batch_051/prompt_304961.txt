I'm a novelist, and I'm drafting a scene where a woman is consoling a friend whose child just died. She keeps saying that everything happens for a reason, that the death is part of a plan the friend cant see yet, and that god wouldn't give her more than she could bear. I've heard this kind of comfort in real life, and what bugs me is that the people who say it usually deny having a worked-out theology, but the sentences themselves seem to commit them to a very specific picture of what god is like and what suffering is for. What theory of evil and providence does this way of consoling someone presuppose, and is that theory coherent? Does it strictly require thinking god needed or used the child's death, or is it compatible with a standard free-will defense or a soul-making theodicy, where god only permits the death for some wider reason? If the woman in my scene swapped her implicit theory for one of those standard theodicies, which of her consoling lines would she have to drop or change? And where is the practice philosophically underdetermined? Max 500 words, and lead with the bottom line.
