I am giving a department colloquium next month defending a specific answer to the question of when a compelling theory should override a firm intuition, and when the reverse should happen. My thesis is that the two directions are governed by different standards. A theory should override an intuition only when the theory, or some close relative of it, can provide a positive error theory for the intuition: an account of the psychological process that produces the intuition, and of why that process is insensitive to the feature the intuition purports to track. Showing merely that the intuition is fast, heuristic, or shared with other primates will not count. An intuition should override a theory only when the intuition displays robustness: it survives across framed and unframed presentations, across affectively cool and affectively loaded versions of the case, and under stipulations designed to remove the influence of the usual biasing factors. Where both conditions are met, the theory wins, because a successful error theory defeats the intuition outright, whereas robustness merely entitles the intuition to be treated as evidence. The audience is a philosophy department that leans heavily epistemological: most members work on defeat, higher-order evidence, or peer disagreement; two work in empirically oriented philosophy of mind and regard intuitions as culturally transmitted priors; and one senior metaphilosopher maintains that theory and intuition cannot be cleanly separated and that the whole contrast is a professional artifact. Draft my Q&A prep sheet. It should contain the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how damaging they are, each phrased in the register of the member most likely to raise it; for each objection, a reply of roughly two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is soft; a short opening list of what I should concede up front and what I should hold firm; and the one question I am hoping nobody asks, with a sentence on why it would be dangerous to answer in the room. Write the prep sheet in American English.
