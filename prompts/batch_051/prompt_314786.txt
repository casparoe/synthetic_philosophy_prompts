I'm a decision theory PhD student and I keep seeing Dutch book arguments waved around as if they settle that rational degrees of belief have to satisfy the probability axioms. Before I read any more of this stuff, I want the spec: what does a Dutch book argument have to actually establish before it earns that conclusion, rather than just showing that a sufficiently scheming bookie can make an incoherent person lose money in an artificial setup? Write it as a list of requirements — the cases it has to handle (risk aversion, decreasing marginal utility of money, agents who refuse to bet, agents with bounded rationality), the assumptions it can't smuggle in (that money is linear in utility, that accepting each bet one at a time commits you to the whole package, that the agent is logically omniscient), and the distinctions it has to respect (synchronic coherence vs diachronic updating, vulnerability to a bookie vs irrationality, pragmatic cost vs epistemic defect). For each requirement say what case or example motivates it and whether its non-negotiable or just one camp's taste. Then flag which requirement does the most to expose the gap between the theorem and the conclusion, and say whether you think any pair of requirements are jointly unsatisfiable.

Structure it like this: (1) Preliminaries — short, one paragraph on what exactly the conclusion is and what a Dutch book argument would have to show to earn it; (2) The requirements — the bulk of the answer, each requirement named, with the case behind it and whether it's non-negotiable or camp-dependent; (3) Joint satisfiability — short, on which pairs can't both be honored or what has to be given up; (4) The load-bearing requirement — short, on the single requirement that does the most work. I dont care about a scorecard of authors, and close with a one-line take on whether an adequate Dutch book argument is even possible.
