I'm taking a course on existentialism this term, and we've been working through the Sartrean position that for human beings existence precedes essence: there is no fixed human nature given in advance that determines what we are, and we constitute ourselves through our choices. A recurring objection in class is that this position becomes incoherent the moment Sartre starts distinguishing authentic existence from bad faith and claiming that when I choose for myself I choose for all of humanity. The objection runs roughly like this. If there is no pregiven essence, there is no fact of the matter about what a human being ought to be, so the distinction between authenticity and bad faith cannot be a normative one about how one should live; at best it is a description of two ways of exercising a freedom that both sides of the distinction equally possess. But then the existentialist's demand to choose, to own one's choices, and to avoid bad faith is either smuggling in a norm — and with it something very like an essence — or it is empty advice, since on the existentialist's own account one cannot fail to choose. I can gesture at a reply (bad faith might be a failure of self-transparency in one's choosing rather than the violation of an antecedent human nature), but my attempts to make the defender's position strong feel tendentious.

Please compose a conversation between two characters: a defender of this Sartrean position and a critic pressing the charge of incoherence I just outlined. Both must be genuinely competent, and neither should be a strawman. The critic should not merely repeat the objection but should develop it, for example by pushing on whether the appeal to self-transparency quietly does normative work, and whether the move from choosing for myself to choosing for all of humanity can be derived from a metaphysics that denies a shared human nature. The defender should have real resources and should use them, but should also concede when a point lands rather than deflecting. The exchange should respond point-by-point: each character should engage the specific claims the other actually made, not talk past them.

After the dialogue, add a short note telling me which character you think has the stronger overall position and why, and how confident you are in that assessment; also flag any place where the dialogue leans on a contested reading of Sartre. If you think the charge as I have framed it rests on a confusion — for instance, if it assumes existentialism is trying to be a normative ethical theory when it might not be — then say so plainly instead of writing the dialogue to fit a bad framing.
