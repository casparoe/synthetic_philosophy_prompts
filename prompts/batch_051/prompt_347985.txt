I am drafting a two-page research proposal for a one-year postdoctoral fellowship in political philosophy, and I would like your help turning a half-formed idea into something a selection committee would fund. The area is the ethics of voting, with a focus on "lesser-evil" voting: the situation in which a person supports a seriously flawed candidate because the likely alternative is worse.

The idea I want to develop is this. Justifications of how one should vote tend to fall into two broad families. One family treats a vote as a small contribution to a collectively produced outcome, so the vote is justified by the expected difference it makes. The other treats the vote as an expressive act, so the vote is justified by whether what it says is something the voter can truthfully and wholeheartedly say. In the lesser-evil case, these two families are usually depicted as pulling against each other: the causal contribution seems to support holding one's nose and voting for the lesser evil, while the expressive content seems to support refusing to use one's voice to say something one does not mean. But I suspect the dilemma depends on thinking that a vote has a single expressive content. A vote is addressed to many audiences at once — the candidate one votes for, that candidate's campaign and donors, one's political allies, the opposing side, the public record, and in some sense oneself — and there is no obvious reason those audiences must all receive the same message. A vote for a lesser evil might function as strong endorsement to the candidate's campaign, as a reluctant and heavily qualified "lesser harm" signal to one's allies, and as a private record of moral compromise to the voter themself.

I would like the proposal built around a question such as this: does an adequate account of lesser-evil voting require an audience-relative notion of what a vote expresses, and does adopting such a notion resolve the alleged conflict between outcome-based and expression-based justifications, or does it merely relocate it? That is, I want to ask whether the standard dilemma is real, or whether it is an artifact of treating the vote as possessing a univocal expressive content.

Please turn this into a formal proposal. State the research question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why existing positions — including expected-utility or causal views, expressive views, and complicity-based views — do not already settle it; and explain what a philosophical project contributes that no empirical study of voting behavior could. Set out the method you would actually follow over the year, month by month, and say why that approach is better than the obvious alternatives. I also want an honest account of what the possible conclusions are, which considerations would decide between them, what would count as the project failing, and how the scope should be trimmed so it can be completed in a year. Do not include a bibliography or citations; I will add those myself.
