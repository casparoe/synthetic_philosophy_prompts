I coordinate reasonable adjustments for taught postgraduates at a large UK university, and most of my caseload is students with ADHD, where the thing we go round in circles on is deadlines. A lot of our students clearly have what the behavioural economics people call present bias — they fully intend to start the chapter on Monday, but each Monday the immediate options win — and I've been trying to get my head around the distinction between sophisticated and naive present-biased agents, where the sophisticated ones know next week's self will cave and actually ask us to tie their hands, while the naive ones genuinely believe a flexible extension plus a firm intention will sort it. I'd like a decision procedure my team could run in a twenty-minute review meeting, before we sign off that term's adjustments, to decide when a hard deadline set by us is acting as a commitment device and when it's just going to wreck someone's mental health, when a self-chosen deadline with an accountability check is likely to do the work, and when a flexible extension is genuinely the right call rather than the kind-sounding one. I can't measure anyone's beta or delta, I'm not a diagnostician, and some accommodations are legally mandated, so the procedure has to work from a short conversation and the student's track record over the previous term.

Please write it as something a non-economist can actually run: the questions in the order to ask them, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values or disability law rather than the model. Then run it on two or three invented but realistic students, and make sure one of them is a case where the procedure tells me to do something I'd really rather not do — for example refusing a flexible extension to a student I feel protective of, or granting one to someone I privately suspect is gaming the system. Finish with a paragraph on what the procedure can't do, including the fact that the model treats deadlines as commitment devices without telling us whether the student's own past self would have consented to them. And please, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables: just continuous prose, as if you were writing me a memo.
