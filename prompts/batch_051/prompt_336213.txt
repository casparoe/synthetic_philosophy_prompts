I'm a science journalist and I've been reporting a piece on why economists treat expected utility as the definition of rational choice, and a source of mine—a decision theorist who thinks the Allais pattern is just a mistake people make—walked me through an argument I now can't get out of my head. It runs like this.

(1) Rationality requires, at a minimum, that your preferences obey the standard axioms: completeness, transitivity, independence, and continuity. (2) For any violation of these axioms, there is a Dutch book or money-pump argument: a series of trades, each one acceptable given your stated preferences at the time, that together leave you worse off no matter what happens. (3) A person whose own preferences can be used to pump money out of them in that way is irrational in a basic, untechnical sense—their evaluations are at war with each other. (4) So rational preferences must satisfy the axioms. (5) The representation theorem then says that any preferences satisfying the axioms can be represented as maximizing expected utility with respect to some probabilities and utilities. (6) So "rational" and "expected utility maximizer" are the same thing: if your preferences don't maximize expected utility, they violate at least one axiom, and by (2) and (3) you're open to a sure loss. (7) Now take the Allais choices: most people prefer A, a sure $1 million, to B, an 89% chance of $1 million and a 10% chance of $5 million and a 1% chance of nothing; and they also prefer D, a 10% chance of $5 million otherwise nothing, to C, an 11% chance of $1 million otherwise nothing. That pair violates independence. (8) So the argument says those people are irrational in exactly the same sense as someone whose preferences go A over B, B over C, C over A—they can be money-pumped. (9) But that seems to prove too much. The Allais pattern isn't obviously a consistency failure in the ordinary sense; it's a coherent, describable attitude about certainty and regret, and the argument seems to promote a formal bookkeeping requirement into a substantive theory of what it is rational to want.

I can't find the slipped step. Which premise do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you think no step fails and the argument is sound, then tell me which of my intuitions I have to abandon instead. I'd most like to keep (3)—the idea that a genuine money pump is a mark of irrationality—but I'm prepared to be told its the step that has to go.
