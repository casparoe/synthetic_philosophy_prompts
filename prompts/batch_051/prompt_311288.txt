Here's something thats been bugging me. A blackletter 'a' from a fifteenth-century manuscript and a geometric 'a' on a motorway sign look almost nothing alike as shapes — one is a spiky tangle, the other a near-circle with a tail — yet nobody hesitates to call them the same letter. Same goes for a child's first wobbly 'a' and a formal italic 'a' on a wedding invitation. But open a single typeface and nudge one little tail (the terminal) a few degrees, or swap a serif for a clean cut, and a designer will tell you it's now a different style, maybe a diferent font. The same underlying features — stroke weight, serifs, the shape of the terminals, the size of the opening, the x-height — get treated as pure noise when we identify the letter and as critical signal when we identify the style. I want to know whether a single principle can do both jobs, or whether we've just bolted two systems together and called it perception.

If you think there is such a principle, defend it against some awkward cases: a child's first 'a', which has no stable style yet is still unambiguously an 'a'; a logo where the 'a' has been pulled so far toward an 'o' that people genuinely disagree about what letter it is; and letters done up so ornamentally that they start being read as pictures rather than letters. If your principle can't handle all three, say which one breaks it and why. And at the end I want an honest verdict: is the asymmetry justified, or should we give it up and admit that "same letter" and "same style" are just two unrelated judgements we keep confusing with one another?
