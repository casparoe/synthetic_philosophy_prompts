Act as an expert in the philosophy of mathematics, attentive both to the geometry of the proofs and to the epistemological questions they raise. Work through the problem carefully: begin by reconstructing the proof's hidden assumptions, check exactly what the counterexample does to them, and only then adjudicate the disagreement claim by claim rather than choosing a side wholesale.

In the philosophy-of-mathematics reading group I attend, two regulars got into an argument over Euler's polyhedron formula and what a counterexample can do to a proof. I could not settle it and would like a point-by-point adjudication.

Jules opened by presenting the classic proof of Euler's polyhedron formula, V − E + F = 2. The proof runs like this. Remove one face of the polyhedron and stretch the remaining surface flat onto a plane, without tearing or overlapping; the boundary of the removed face becomes the outer boundary of a planar network. The network has the same V, the same E, and the same number F of regions, counting the exterior as a region this time. Next triangulate every region by drawing diagonals. Each diagonal adds one edge and one region, so V − E + F is unchanged. Then remove triangles from the outside inward. A boundary triangle with one outside edge can be deleted by removing that edge and that region; a boundary triangle with two outside edges can be deleted by removing the vertex, the two edges, and the region. In both cases V − E + F is unchanged. The process ends in a single triangle, for which V − E + F equals 2; since every step preserved the quantity, any polyhedron must satisfy V − E + F = 2.

Nadia said the proof must be invalid, because the theorem is false. Take a picture frame: a solid cube with a square tunnel bored through it from one side to the opposite side. Its surface can be built from flat polygonal facets, so it is as much a polyhedron as the cube, yet it gives V − E + F = 0, not 2. And a valid proof cannot yield a false conclusion, so the proof must conceal a false step. The culprit, she said, is the flattening step: with the frame, after you remove a face you cannot stretch the remaining surface flat onto the plane without overlap.

Jules replied that this only shows that the picture frame is not a polyhedron in the relevant sense. A polyhedron, he said, is a closed surface built from flat faces that can be flattened after one face is removed; the frame has a tunnel, and the flattening operation does not apply to it. The proof never claimed to cover such a shape. The formula is proved for the objects it is about.

Nadia answered that this is definitional gerrymandering. The original claim was not some technical statement about flattenable surfaces; it was a claim about polyhedra, and a picture frame plainly belongs to the ordinary class of surfaces people call polyhedra. If Jules now defines polyhedron to mean whatever makes the flattening step succeed, he has not defended the theorem: he has changed the definition so that any counterexample is disqualified by fiat. Any theorem can be saved that way.

Jules then tried a different tack. Perhaps the first formulation was too broad, but the proof tells us exactly how to repair it. The hidden assumption in the proof is simple connectedness. For polyhedra without tunnels, the proof gives V − E + F = 2; for a surface with h tunnels the formula generalizes to V − E + F = 2 − 2h, which yields 0 for the single tunnel in the frame and 2 for the cube. So the counterexample did not refute the enterprise; it identified the missing lemma and led to a deeper theorem. The proof was doing real work precisely because it exposed the feature the counterexample violated.

Nadia replied that this is a generous reinterpretation, but it is not what the proof proved. The proof purported to prove a universal claim about every polyhedron; that claim is false, and the proof as presented contains a step that is false for the frame. A method that responds to every counterexample by generalizing the formula or redefining the domain has no conditions under which it admits error. If V − E + F = 2 can be swapped for a different formula each time it fails, then the original statement was not a claim but a moving target. You can keep the original theorem and the original proof, or you can adopt the revised theorem and a new proof; you cannot credit the original proof with having proved the revised theorem.

Jules answered that this misunderstands how mathematical concepts get fixed. In practice, a proof often decides what a concept must be. The flattening step was not a covert assumption smuggled in; it is how the proof teaches us that polyhedron, as loosely used, is ambiguous, and that the mathematically interesting concept is the simply connected one. The frame's recalcitrance is not a failure of the proof but evidence that the concept was not yet well defined. Mathematics grows by exactly this dialectic of proof and counterexample: the proof proposes a definition, the counterexample refutes the over-broad claim, and the concept is tightened, with the proof preserved as the guide to the tightening.

Nadia had the last word. In that case, she said, Jules has abandoned proof in the classical sense for a thesis about the development of mathematics. If a proof is allowed to revise its own concepts whenever a counterexample appears, then proof no longer demonstrates truth; it expresses a temporary equilibrium of a research practice. That may be how mathematics develops, but it does not make the original proof valid, and it does not make the original theorem true. Jules began by claiming a proof; he is now claiming something closer to an experiment plus a theory of concept formation.

Adjudicate the exchange move by move and say who is right about which specific point and why. Address at least these: the status of the picture-frame counterexample against the original claim; the principle that a valid proof cannot establish a false conclusion; the definitional defense in the first reply; the generalized-formula response; and the final charge that the proof has been allowed to dodge falsification. Then say what each party is missing, both about the geometry of the proof and about the philosophical question, and give an overall verdict on which position is stronger and on what remains of the original proof after this kind of exchange.
