I was explaining Goodman's grue problem to a friend who works in machine learning the other week, and heres the version I gave: define 'grue' so it applies to anything examined before some time t just in case it is green, and to anything not examined before t just in case it is blue. Every emerald we have looked at so far is green, so every emerald we have looked at is also grue; the same evidence supports the green generalization and the grue generalization, but they say different things about an emerald first examined after t. My friend shrugged and said grue is just a classifier that overfits: it memorizes the training data and then flips the labels on the test set. I laughed, but now I cant stop thinking about it.

The superficial version of the parallel is obvious: both are hypotheses that fit everything observed so far and fail later. But it feels like there is more going on. Goodman's point, as I understand it, is that there is no language-neutral basis on which grue is the gerrymandered predicate, because if grue and bleen were our basic color words then 'green' would look gerrymandered from that standpoint, and his only candidate answer was that green is the one entrenched in our actual practice. Machine learning instead reaches for inductive bias, regularization, capacity control, a held-out test set. But the choice of inductive bias is the original problem one level up, and the no-free-lunch results say no bias wins on average across all possible worlds. So is grue a genuine early statement of the generalization problem, with overfitting as one symptom, or is the ML analogy missing the specifically philosophical part about predicates, entrenchment, and projectibility?
