I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so if I'm butchering a standard distinction, tell me. Lately I've been trying to formalize the line between science and pseudoscience, and a historical case keeps bugging me.

The rough checklist I was taught for something being a science goes like this: it makes testable claims, you can run controlled experiments, other people can check the results, you publish failures as well as successes, and you actually drop the idea when the evidence keeps going against it.

Now here's the case. In the 1880s, a well-respected British philosopher named Henry Sidgwick, mostly remembered for careful work in ethics, helped found the Society for Psychical Research. Their goal was to figure out, as rigorously as possible, whether things like telepathy, mediums, and apparitions were real. And they didn't just go to seances and write down impressions. They ran controlled guessing experiments with claimed psychics, did a large census of reported hallucinations, kept records of mediums caught cheating, published negative results alongside positive ones, and generally treated the whole thing as a data problem. Sidgwick himself was apparently pretty skeptical about the evidence and never claimed any of it was proven, but he thought the question of whether anything survives death was too important to leave to anecdote.

So my question is: does this count as science by the checklist above? Go criterion by criterion, point out where the checklist alone underdetermines the verdict, and then say whether this case shows the checklist is missing something. Also, write your answer in American English.
