I'm a philosophy PhD student working on social epistemology and argumentation theory, and I've been going back and forth with my officemates about whether you could turn argumentation into an actual sport—not debate club, but something with matches, rankings, and a scoring rubric—and whether doing that would make people reason better or just make them excellent at gaming a scorecard. The pessimistic line in my department is that adversarial formats can only ever select for persuasion, because competitors optimize for winningness above everything. I'm not convinced: I think the scoring system does all the work, and the competition itself is bascially neutral.

Here's the case I want you to build. Design a competitive argumentation format (match structure, scoring rules a trained lay panel could actually apply, maybe a season or ranking system) such that the strategy that maximizes a player's chance of winning is the same strategy that maximizes a substantial epistemic good. You can pick the epistemic good, but make it nontrivial—something like expected posterior accuracy or genuine belief revision on strong counterevidence, not "speaks clearly" or "stays on topic." Constraints: there's no omniscient judge or private answer key, so judges never get to check the actual truth; the topics are contested normative questions (lowering the voting age, whether parents should get a say in a teenager's medical decisions) where no fact-check settles the match; players are rational and will Goodhart whatever you score as hard as they can; players may have to defend positions they don't believe, so sincerity can't be doing any work; and anything that gets points has to be visible in the match transcript or behavior, not in somebody's head. Keep the scenario itself to a paragraph or two.

After you give me the format, do the check. State the epistemic target and the equilibrium strategy under the rules, then show point by point that winning tracks the epistemic target rather than ordinary persuasion-only debate. Tell me which feature of the rules is doing the real work, and what happens if you remove it—does the sport collapse back into rhetoric-optimization? How would someone who thinks adversarial argumentation is constitutively truth-insensitive attack the design? And give me an honest final verdict on whether formalizing argumentation as a sport would improve reasoning or just produce a fancier way to game the scorecard. If you think the strongest version is impossible under these constraints, say so and give the nearest design that satisfies all but one constraint, naming the constraint you weakened.
