My gut on blackmail cases, and I'd like to know whether one principle can get all of them or nearly all.

(1) I find out a coworker is cheating on his wife. I ask him for $5,000 or I'll tell her. Wrong — clearly, even though the info is true and I'd be within my rights to tell her for free.

(2) Same situation, but instead of money I say: "Stop leaning on the junior staff, or I'll tell your wife." Fine, maybe even admirable.

(3) Same secret, but I approach him as a seller: "I know something about you that your wife would want to know; I'll sell you the information for $5,000." Less wrong than (1), maybe okay, but I can't say why.

(4) He finds out I know and offers me $5,000 to keep quiet. I take it. Feels wrong to accept, though not as wrong as (1).

(5) He has a side business; I tell him "Pay me or I'll post a truthful one-star review." Wrong, and I'd call it extortion.

(6) He said something false about me at work; I tell him I'll sue for defamation unless he settles for $5,000, even though my case is weak. Fine — that's just how litigation works.

(7) Before I've said anything about the secret, he asks me to sign a non-disclosure agreement and pays me for my silence. Fine.

(8) I find out he's been dumping waste in the river. I ask for $5,000 or I report him. Wrong, even though I think reporting him would be praiseworthy and charging someone for a service is normally fine.

My working hunch is that revealing true information is allowed and asking for money is allowed, but combining them becomes wrong — except (2) and (6) seem to break that hunch in opposite directions. Is there a principle that captures all eight, and if not, which of my verdicts should I drop? What would the best principle say about a pair I didn't list: a reporter who says "Pay me and I'll kill the story" versus one who says "I won't take money, but I'll sit on it a week for a heads-up on future scoops"?

Please mark which parts of your answer are close to a standard view in the blackmail literature and which are your own repair, and don't present contested points as consensus.
