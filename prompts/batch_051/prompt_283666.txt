I am currently taking a seminar on self-knowledge and moral psychology, and one question from the course has begun to organize much of my daily life. I am trying to determine when examining my own thoughts and feelings helps me and when it does me harm, and I have gathered a set of gut verdicts about particular cases. I would like you to tell me whether a single principle captures them, or whether some of my verdicts should be given up.

When I went through a painful breakup recently, I spent weeks asking myself why it failed, what the failure showed about me, and replaying old conversations in detail. This made me clearly worse. By contrast, when I had to decide whether to accept a job I was ambivalent about, I wrote out my values, listed costs and benefits, and deliberately challenged my first impulse; the exercise helped, and I am confident in the decision I reached.

When I feel angry at a friend, I often sit with the anger, attending to the sensations in my body and trying not to judge the feeling; it usually fades, and I avoid saying something I will regret. But when I cannot sleep, I lie there checking whether I am falling asleep yet and asking myself why I am still awake, and this reliably keeps me awake.

After I gave a mediocre presentation, I went back through it point by point to see what went wrong and what I should do differently next time. It was unpleasant but useful. After a party, I replayed everything I had said and imagined what each friend must have thought of me, and that was pure rumination that made me withdraw for days. These two cases trouble me because they are structurally so similar.

When a close friend told me I had been defensive lately, I spent a week trying to catch myself in the act of being defensive, keeping a mental record of examples and testing whether the accusation fit. It was uncomfortable, but I came to understand myself better and changed my behavior.

The half-formed principle I keep reaching for is this: self-examination is beneficial when it serves a decision or a future change in behavior, and harmful when it is really an attempt to appraise a state of mind or settle what it means. But I can already feel the principle straining against several of the cases, especially the anger case, the sleep case, and the defensiveness case.

Please tell me whether there is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts. State it precisely and say which case motivates each part of it. Then apply it to a case I have not settled: I have been using a mood-tracking app that pings me several times a day to name and rate my mood, and I cannot tell whether it is making me more resilient or merely more preoccupied with myself. Finally, say honestly which of my verdicts, if any, your preferred principle cannot accommodate. Please write in continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
