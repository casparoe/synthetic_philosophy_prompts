I write about science and ideas, and I have been looking into a family-law proposal inspired by Bertrand Russell's claim that marriage actually yokes together two different institutions: an adult sexual and domestic partnership, and a child-rearing arrangement whose stability matters independently of the adults' feelings. The proposal makes that separation concrete as follows. Any two adults may register a renewable companionship contract lasting five years. At the end of each term the contract lapses unless both parties affirmatively renew it; either party may decline to renew without stating a reason, and no fault-based compensation attaches to non-renewal. Separately, the birth of a child automatically creates a parental union between the two legal parents. The parental union cannot be dissolved by either party until the youngest child reaches adulthood, regardless of what happens to the companionship contract. It carries joint financial responsibility, a presumption of shared custody, and a duty not to disrupt the child's residence or schooling because the adults' romantic relationship has ended. Adults who have a child without any companionship contract incur the parental union automatically, and the law forbids them to contract out of it in advance.

Treat this as a piece of institutional design rather than as a moral position. What would it incentivize among adults deciding whether to register, whether to have children, and whether to renew; among parents before and after a companionship contract lapses; and among lawyers, employers, and state agencies now forced to deal with two statuses instead of one? How would people game it — by timing pregnancies, by disputing parentage or residence, by moving to another jurisdiction, by using the renewal deadline as leverage over a partner, or by avoiding legal parentage altogether — and which of these games would be serious enough to undermine the scheme? Finally, when the proposal collides with these incentives, does it show that the companionate and parental functions cannot actually be separated as cleanly as the Russellian picture suggests, or can the failure modes be repaired by rule changes without collapsing the distinction?
