For several years I have been recording the case-verdicts of a colleague who reasons almost exclusively by asking what would happen if everyone did what an agent proposes, but who refuses ever to state the principle behind her rulings. Her recorded verdicts include the following. (1) Jumping the turnstile on a nearly empty late-night bus, with no fare inspector in sight, is wrong. (2) Padding a conference expense report by a small amount is wrong, even when the institution is wealthy and would barely notice. (3) A hiker who pockets a small rock or a handful of wildflower seeds from a protected national park acts wrongly, even though the park suffers no measurable loss. (4) A person who chooses never to have children does nothing wrong, even though if everyone made that choice the human species would die out and every project that depends on future generations would fail with it.

That fourth verdict rules out the crudest form of the generalization test, so she is not simply assuming that any conduct is wrong if universalizing it would have very bad results. But I cannot tell whether her actual rule is a narrow fair-play principle, on which the wrong cases are wrong because each agent free-rides on a cooperative practice that others maintain; a Kantian contradiction-in-willing test restricted to practices and institutions the agent relies on; or a more sophisticated rule-consequentialist generalization test with an overload condition that permits childlessness only because requiring procreation would institutionalize something worse. The new case I need to predict is this. A senior academic in a rich country, with an ordinary income, takes two or three long-haul leisure flights each year, knowing full well that if every human being flew for pleasure at that rate, aviation emissions would make the planet dangerous to inhabit. The flights violate no institutional rule; the flyer relies on no cooperative scheme in the way a turnstile-jumper relies on the bus service; the harm lies entirely in what happens when the conduct is generalized.

Please do four things. First, lay out the candidate principles that fit her recorded verdicts, including at least the fair-play, Kantian, and rule-consequentialist reconstructions, and say precisely how each handles all four verdicts. Second, say what each candidate principle predicts about the leisure-flight case. Third, give your best guess about how she will rule on that case, defending the choice of principle that yields the guess. Fourth, propose one further case, described concretely, that I could put to her tomorrow and that would do the most to discriminate among the candidate principles, and explain why that case is decisive rather than merely additional evidence. Work slowly through the cases before committing yourself.
