I got into this with a public-law friend and I cant get myself out. His conclusion is that, strictly speaking, the state can never do legal wrong — which can't be right, because that would make the whole practice of holding states responsible (tort judgments against the government, courts striking down statutes, reparations for state crimes) one big category mistake. His argument, as he laid it out:

(1) The state is an artificial person; it only ever acts through officials.
(2) For an official's act to be the state's act, it has to fall within the powers the law confers on that official.
(3) If it does fall within those powers, then the act is authorized by law — and an act the law authorizes can't be a wrong that the state commits.
(4) If it falls outside those powers, it's ultra vires: it isn't the state acting at all, just an individual abusing an office, and that individual answers personally.
(5) So every official act either isn't really the state's or isn't really a wrong. Therefore the state can never do wrong.
(6) But we constantly treat states as doing wrong — and not just in loose talk. Courts award damages against the government, constitutions get violated, and states apologize and pay.

So unless all of that practice is confused, one of (1)–(4) has to give. My friend is happy to give up (6): he says the state doesn't act wrongly, it only incurs liabilities. I can't stomach that — it feels like a get-out-of-guilt-free card for anything a state does, and like it guts the point of calling something a state crime. But then I owe him a diagnosis.

I keep tripping over the way (3) moves between "the law gives you the power to do this" and "the law hereby approves of the particular thing you did with that power." Maybe that's the exit, but I cant make the distinction precise without it swallowing everything: if authorized power can be exercised wrongfully, then "authorized" stops doing the work it's supposed to do. Can you tell me which step you reject, why it looked solid, and what rejecting it costs — especially whether it drags (2) down with it?
