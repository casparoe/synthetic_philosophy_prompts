I am a member of a commission established in a country emerging from a long internal armed conflict. Under the peace agreement, our commission is authorized to grant individual amnesties to perpetrators who give full and honest testimony about their own acts, and this guarantee was a condition of the former armed groups laying down their weapons. The country is deeply divided over it. Some relatives of the disappeared support the arrangement because they have spent decades not knowing where their family members are buried and believe only the perpetrators can tell them. Others, especially survivors of sexual violence, argue that allowing perpetrators to go free after testifying is an insult to those who suffered, since a confession made under a guarantee of amnesty costs the perpetrator almost nothing; they say the courts, however weak, should at least try to do their job. My question is about principle rather than strategy. Can a confession given in exchange for amnesty ever be a genuine form of accountability or repair, or is it only information purchased at the price of justice? Is there a moral difference between an individualized, conditional amnesty of this kind and a blanket amnesty? And what would make it legitimate to trade punishment for truth — the consent of victims, the completeness of the testimony, the risk of renewed war, or something else? I am not asking for a menu of possible policies but for help thinking through whether this bargain can be defended. Please say how confident you are in what you claim and identify the point you are least sure of.
