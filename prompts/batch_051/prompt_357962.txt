I've been rereading Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day for a course, and I keep thinking it's really a novel about the same thing Nietzsche was after in that early essay of his on the uses and disadvantages of history for life. You know the three modes he sketches, roughly: monumental history, where the past is mined for great deeds and examples that inspire; antiquarian history, where the past is preserved and revered for its own sake, the way you'd tend an inherited house; and critical history, where the past is put on trial and broken up so that life can move forward. Stevens, the aging butler narrating his 1956 motoring trip to see the former housekeeper Miss Kenton, seems to me to be stuck in the first two modes and almost incapable of the third — he curates Darlington Hall like an antiquarian, and he keeps Lord Darlington as a monumental figure even after the man's public disgrace over his Nazi dealings. I can point to the scenes with his father's death, the dismissal of the two Jewish maids, and the final conversation with Miss Kenton on the pier, but I want more than scene-spotting.

What I'd like is a real reading: trace where each of the three modes show up, what the novel does with them, and whether Ishiguro ends up endorsing any of them or instead showing how each can be a way of refusing to live. Where does the novel resist being squeezed into Nietzsche's categories, and what does it add to the essay's point that argument alone doesn't? Be the honest critic in both directions. And because I'm still learning the Nietzsche side, please mark for me what's fairly settled about the essay and what's genuinely contested among people who work on it, and don't present one contested interpretation as the consensus.
