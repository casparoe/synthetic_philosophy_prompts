I teach an undergraduate philosophy of cognitive science seminar, 14 students, one 80-minute session. The unit is on concepts: whether concepts are fixed rules, prototypes, or context-sensitive capacities. I want a structured activity that makes students feel conceptual slippage in a letter-string microdomain before I name the phenomenon.

The microdomain is analogy puzzles of the form X changes to Y; what does Z change to? over lowercase letters. I want the activity built around this sequence, which tends to elicit rival answers:

1. abc → abd; ijk → ?
2. abc → abd; xyz → ?
3. abc → abd; aabbcc → ?
4. abc → abd; zyx → ?
5. abc → abd; cba → ?

Puzzle 1 invites a single rule: replace the rightmost letter by its alphabetic successor. Each later puzzle puts pressure on one component of that rule. Puzzle 2 puts pressure on the edge of the alphabet, where the successor relation either wraps around (xya) or has no output. Puzzle 3 puts pressure on the object the rule applies to: the rightmost letter (aabbcd) competes with the rightmost group of identical letters (aabbdd). Puzzle 4 puts pressure on the relation itself: taking the successor (zyy) competes with continuing the direction of the string, which at the low end means taking the predecessor (zyw). Puzzle 5 puts pressure on the meaning of rightmost: position in the string (cbb) competes with position in the alphabet (dba).

Design an activity in which participants commit to answers, notice that their own rule shifts as the cases change, and then defend or revise their rule against rival rules. The phenomenon should show up in what participants do and say, not in what I tell them afterwards. Include exact rules and roles; a timeline for the 80 minutes; the materials to put on the board or hand out; a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto components of conceptual slippage and connects them to the broader question about concepts; a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it; and an honest note on which feature of fluid concepts the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the wrap-up. No lecture up front, and do not introduce the term conceptual slippage until the debrief.
