Something I keep noticing when I interview scientists: the same sentence shape gets a completely different reaction depending on what its about.

Say a physicist tells me a rainbow is just sunlight refracting through water droplets, or that a diamond is just carbon atoms arranged in a lattice. Nobody flinches. These count as good, reductive explanations. But when a neuroscientist says romantic love is just oxytocin and dopamine, or an evolutionary psychologist says moral outrage is just a reputation-management adaptation, the same people tend to wince. The grammar is identical: X is just Y. Yet we treat the first batch as illuminating and the second as, well, rude.

Dennett's vocabulary seems built for this. He was all for cranes—bottom-up, mechanistic explanations—and had no use for skyhooks, the spooky top-down fixers. But he also warned against greedy reductionism: skipping levels and pretending the lower-level story does more work than it actually does. So here's my question. When is a crane a good reduction and when is it greedy? Is there a principled line, or do we just draw it around the things we care about protecting?

There's also a second asymmetry hiding here. People who happily accept "a gene is just a sequence of base pairs" often insist consciousness can't be just information processing. But if skyhooks are off the table, consciousness still has to be explained bottom-up—just by a much longer chain of cranes across levels. So the "can't be just" reaction starts to look like special pleading by a thing that doesn't want to be explained.

Give me the strongest justification you can for treating the two batches differently. Steelman it, test it against a nearby case (a joke is just a pattern of air pressure and neural firing, or a contract is just ink on paper), and tell me whether the asymmetry survives. Should we get more comfortable with the reductions we flinch at, more suspicious of the ones we accept, or is there a real feature—not subject matter, but something structural—that draws the line? And yes, try to do all this without reaching for a skyhook.
