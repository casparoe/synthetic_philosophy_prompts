Start from the standard Laplacean setup: a closed, deterministic, Newtonian universe; a predictor who knows all the laws and can integrate them without error; and — the crucial idealization — a single snapshot of the exact microstate at some instant. Change one parameter: the predictor does not receive the exact microstate. Instead it receives each normalized coordinate to K decimal places, so its initial uncertainty is δ = 10^{-K}, and it issues its forecasts after that one observation with no further data. To make this parameter do real work, stipulate that the universe is chaotic: nearby microstates diverge exponentially, with leading Lyapunov exponent λ > 0.

Work the calculation in detail. Under the standard linearized rule, an initial error of size δ grows approximately as δ e^{λt} until it saturates at the size of the accessible region. Show that the time at which the forecast ceases to carry point-wise information about the true microstate is therefore of order (K ln 10)/λ. Then assess how the original analysis changes. Does the Laplacean conclusion — that the present state together with the laws fixes the entire future — survive this variation, and if so in what precise sense? What does the dependence of the predictability horizon on K and λ establish about the relation between determinism and predictability; in particular, should the loss of long-range predictive skill in a chaotic system count as evidence against determinism, or is it fully explained as an epistemic limitation of finite-precision access to initial conditions? Finally, vary the setup once more by allowing the predictor to keep observing the system and revising its state estimate as time unfolds rather than being cut off after one snapshot. State precisely what this continuous observing would and would not restore, and whether it recovers anything like the Laplacean conclusion.
