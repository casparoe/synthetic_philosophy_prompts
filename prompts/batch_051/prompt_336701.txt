I've been reading about scapegoat rituals — the Leviticus thing where the sins of the community are loaded onto a goat and it's sent off into the wilderness, and analogues elsewhere — and a friend of mine who's an anthropologist keeps insisting that these rituals don't merely symbolise a transfer of guilt, they actually transfer it. For her the community's guilt is a real social-psychological quantity, like a debt, and the ritual moves it onto the scapegoat the way a bank moves a loan from one account to another. Afterward, she says, the group isn't just telling itself a story about being clean; it is measurably different: lower mutual suspicion, more willingness to cooperate, less intrusive rumination about whatever was done. I find the claim slippery, because 'transfer' sounds like shorthand for something else, but she won't accept that it's just a metaphor.

I want to know whether that claim can be brought down to earth and tested. Design the strongest concrete study you can — real or hypothetical — to distinguish between (a) a scapegoat ritual genuinely transferring guilt from the group to a target, and (b) the thinner view that the ritual gives people a shared narrative that makes them feel better without any actual transfer. Spell out who the participants would be, what you'd measure, what comparison conditions you'd need, and crucially what pattern of results would count as evidence for genuine transfer rather then mere relief or performance. Then be honest about the residue: if the results came out favoring transfer, what exactly would still be unsettled — for instance, whether guilt was ever 'in' the group in the first place, or whether 'transfer' is really the right word instead of something like discharge, dilution, or distraction. Commit to one design and a definite verdict criterion rather than offering a menu of possibilities, and keep the whole response under 700 words.
