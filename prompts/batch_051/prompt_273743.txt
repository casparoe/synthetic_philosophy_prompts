I'm a PhD student working on social epistemology, and I've been using "common knowledge" as if it's a piece of equipment, but I've realized I cant actually say what it takes for something to be common knowledge rather than just widely known. I mean the notion that's supposed to do real theoretical work in coordination problems and epistemic game theory—the thing that makes a public announcement different from a pile of private messages.

Act as an epistemologist who works on the formal side of common knowledge (epistemic logic, game theory) but who also cares about making the formal notion fit ordinary talk. Think carefully and maybe start by brainstorming two or three candidate definitions before you commit. Don't worry about citations; I want your sharpest reconstruction.

Here are the cases I need the definition to handle. The first two feel like clear positives, the next two clear negatives, and the rest are where I start to flounder:

1. A fire alarm goes off in a lecture hall. Everyone is awake, everyone hears it, and everyone can see everyone else hearing it.
2. Two rational players face a stag hunt. The payoff matrix is pinned where both can read it, each can see the other looking at it, and each takes the other to be rational in the ordinary way. (The game and their rationality are what I'm claiming are common knowledge—not their choices.)

3. I learn about a proposed merger from a text while I'm home alone, and I've no reason to think anyone else has been told.
4. Ten directors each get the same merger news by separate bcc email. Each reads it, but none knows who else received it.

5. The chair's resignation is an open secret in my department: everyone has heard it through private one-on-one conversations, everyone assumes the others mostly know, but no one has ever said it to the whole group, and each person is a bit unsure which colleagues know that they themselves know.
6. A coordinator sends a group-chat message moving a meeting to 5:00. Everyone in the chat received it and opened it, but read receipts are off, so no one knows whether the others have actually opened it. Then, over the next hour, everyone replies "ok" in the chat. I want to know whether the new time is common knowledge before the replies and after them.
7. A professor announces in class that the exam has been moved to Tuesday, but one student is asleep at that exact moment and nobody notices.

I keep running into the textbook idea that p is common knowledge when everyone knows p, everyone knows that everyone knows p, and so on up an infinite ladder. That infinity seems suspicious to me as an account of two finite people's mental lives. Is the ladder doing real work, or is it just a clunky way of saying there's no higher-order uncertainty anywhere? If it is doing real work, I'd like to see which of my cases expose that.

Once you've got a definition, run it against all seven cases and say where it stumbles. If it gives a verdict I said I didn't expect, either show me why the definition is right and my case judgment is off, or revise the definition. Bonus if you can say whether "common knowledge" is one thing or a loose family of ideas that ordinary talk blurs together.
