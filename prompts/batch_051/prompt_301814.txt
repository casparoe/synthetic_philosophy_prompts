I am a novelist, and the book I am drafting turns on three characters who are each, in her own way, systematically lying to herself about what she is and what she has chosen. I have been rereading Sartre on bad faith: the waiter who plays at being a waiter, the woman on the date who lets her hand rest in the man’s as though it had stopped being hers, the friend who insists that someone be sincere and declare that he is simply what he is, and the gambler who discovers that an earlier resolution settles nothing because it must be chosen all over again. Beneath these I can see one structure: the self is at once facticity (what it is, has been, is situated as) and transcendence (what it is not yet, what it goes beyond, what it can choose), and bad faith exploits that gap, now collapsing one pole into the other, now playing the two off against each other. I would like you to write me a sorting quiz of eight to ten questions that I can use on each character to diagnose the particular shape of the lie she tells herself. Every item should be a concrete scene (at work, on a date, after a confession, facing a broken resolution) with two or three answer options about what the character does, says, or notices. No item should ask what the character believes about freedom, whether she is in bad faith, or which label she would give herself; the questions should sort her by how she handles the scene, not by what she calls it.

Please build the scoring key from the facticity–transcendence matrix rather than from a pre-existing list of names: let the answer patterns locate the positions, name them afterward, and flag any cell that is logically available but psychologically unstable or rarely inhabited, with a sentence on why. The instrument should at least capture the forms my reading keeps surfacing: identifying with one’s role or one’s facts as though they exhausted what one is; disowning the meaning of one’s own embodied conduct while continuing to pursue it; treating one’s past acts, confessions, or labels as one’s fixed essence, whether in one’s own voice or through another’s demand for sincerity; and treating a past resolution as a standing guarantee about the future that no longer needs to be remade. For each leaf in the key, give one sentence on the particular freedom the character is hiding from, and note explicitly where the quiz cannot cleanly separate two positions. The instrument is for my private use while drafting, so the characters should not need to have read a word of existentialism for the items to work, and I would ask that bad faith not collapse into ordinary hypocrisy or lying to others.
