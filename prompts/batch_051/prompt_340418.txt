I've been going down a rabbit hole on animal welfare policy, and there's an inconsistency across a few different practices that I can't seem to get to cohere, and I'd like someone to reconstruct what's actually being presupposed underneath the whole thing. In the U.S., the main federal humane slaughter law requires cattle and pigs (and horses, mules, sheep) to be rendered insensible before slaughter, but it leaves birds out entirely — chickens and turkeys, which are the huge majority of land animals killed for food, get no equivalent federal protection, and not because anyone seriously denies that birds feel pain. At the same time, in the way normal people and even food writers talk, beef gets treated as morally heavy and chicken gets treated as the lighter, more conscientious choice — even though, if the unit that matters is the individual animal, this is backwards, because a single cow produces an enormous amount of meat and it takes many more individual birds to produce the same amount of meat from chickens. And then there's the accounting that realy confuses me: animals killed as a side effect of crop farming (mice in combines, insects killed by pesticides) and wild animals starving or being eaten alive out in nature barely show up in public policy or in most people's moral ledgers, while the death of a farmed cow or pig counts as a real moral event.

I suspect these aren't just three separate oversights; I think there's an implicit theory of moral status, of causation, and of responsibility holding the pattern together. Reconstruct that theory as charitably as you can, clearly distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely happen to fit with, and flag where they're underdetermined. Then tell me what food policy, wildlife management, and ordinary consumption choices would look like if we replaced the implicit view with a thoroughly suffering-focused one.
