Explain the demandingness objection to act-consequentialism. Does the objection show that act-consequentialism is false, or only that it is revisionary? In your answer, discuss at least one consequentialist reply.

And here's his answer, typed up from the blue book:

Act-consequentialism says the right action is the one with the best overall consequences, where everyone's welfare counts equally and nobody gets special weight. Peter Singer made this concrete with the drowning-child example: on your way to work you pass a shallow pond where a child is drowning, and you obviously should wade in and ruin your shoes, because a child's life outweighs some shoes even if the shoes are yours. His principle is that when we can prevent something bad without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to do it. Applied globally, that means almost everyone in a rich country acts wrongly when they upgrade their apartment, eat out, or take a trip, because that money could have prevented deaths through effective charities. The demandingness objection says this is asking too much: if we always had to do the act with the best overall consequences, there would be no room left for our own projects, close friendships, or even a lazy evening, since almost any act has some alternative with better consequences. Bernard Williams made a version of this objection when he said that a husband who pulls his drowning wife from the water but first has to deliberate about whether he's allowed to treat her as special, and why, has already gone wrong — the very need for that extra thought shows the theory asks something inhuman of us. A standard reply is to switch to rule consequentialism, which tells us to follow rules like give ten percent of your income instead of maximizing act by act. Since the rules are picked for their consequences, the view stays consequentialist, but it becomes livable. So I think the objection shows act-consequentialism is revisionary rather than false, but with rule consequentialism it doesn't even have to be very revisionary.
