Some of our field workers just count one-letter-one-sound mismatches and declare the orthography deep, which confuses a predictable context rule with a genuinely lexical irregularity, and getting that wrong means we build the wrong amount of phonics practice. Could you write me a reusable decision procedure, grounded in whatever linguistics actually knows about the phonemic principle — roughly, that an alphabet should encode a language's distinctive sound contrasts rather than its predictable variation — that a non-linguist can run on a new orthography to rate how much decoding instruction the language will need? I want the questions in order, what counts as enough evidence to move on, where the procedure stops and hands off to an actual phonologist, and where it gives a score rather than a yes/no. Then run it on three cases: a newly alphabetized language whose spelling is aggressively one-symbol-one-sound; a language that deliberately writes the same root the same way even when pronunciation shifts; and a community orthography our staff are attached to that the procedure will probably force us to call deep — seperately from the score, tell me what we're supposed to do when the procedure outputs something politically impossible. And write the whole thing as continuous prose, like an email: no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
