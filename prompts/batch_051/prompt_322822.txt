Last week in my moral philosophy reading group we were going back and forth about whether big moral changes—ending dueling, abolishing slavery, that kind of thing—happen because the better arguments finally win, or for messier reasons like shifts in honor culture, material interests, or just sheer exhaustion. I'd been defending the Williams/Appiah line that its mostly the messy stuff, not some rational convergence on the right answer. One of the older grad students cut me off with the old line about the moral universe having a long arc that bends toward justice, and everyone nodded like that settled it. I didn't have the words to push back, but I'm pretty sure that sentence is doing a lot more work than they think. Can you lay out the different precise claims the slogan could be making about how moral change actually works? For each reading: is it trivial, substantive and defensible, or false? And which of those readings, if any, would actually defeat the Williams/Appiah point about norms like dueling and slavery not ending through arguments alone? I want to go back to the group with a menu of readings, not just one gloss.
