I've spent the last few weeks in a metaethics reading group that has completely stalled on evolutionary debunking arguments, and I'm trying to understand the structure of the stalemate rather than just pick a side. The setup we keep coming back to is Street's Darwinian dilemma. The realist about moral facts has to say either that the evolutionary forces shaping our moral judgments are unrelated to the moral facts, which seems to make our hitting on those facts a wild coincidence and undermines our judgments, or that there is some relation between the selection pressures and the moral facts, in which case the realist owes us an account of that relation and how we could be justified in relying on it. Everyone in the group accepts the evolutionary background; nobody is denying that selection massively shaped moral psychology. But the two camps won't move.

The debunkers press a simple point: selection cares about fitness, not truth, and for a judgment like the belief that helping kin is obligatory, the obligation itself plays no causal role in producing the belief. What does the work is a disposition that helped ancestors propagate, whether or not the moral fact obtained. So the realist's claim to be tracking moral truth looks like a just-so story. The realists answer with a parity point: evolution shaped our perceptual and logical capacities too, and nobody concludes from that that perception or arithmetic is systematically unreliable. If the debunking argument cannot be stopped from spreading to those cases, it proves too much, and we should reject it, or at least reject the master argument behind it. The debunkers then say the realist's carve-outs, where some moral beliefs are truth-tracking and others are not, are ad hoc, since the realist is just asserting from inside realism which judgments are the good ones. And from there the conversation loops.

My working theory is that this is not an empirical disagreement: I doubt more data about which specific judgments were selected for, or how much of morality is a side effect, would move anyone. It also does not feel merely verbal. It looks like a disagreement about what a non-question-begging explanation of our moral judgments would have to look like: what counts as an acceptable stopping point, and who bears the burden of showing the other side could be right. But I'm a participant, so I don't trust my own read. Can you give a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, whether factual, methodological, about burden of proof, about which cases count as data, or something less tidy, and say what, if anything, would count as progress given the diagnosis? I'm not after a survey of everyone's positions; I want the disagreement itself taken apart.
