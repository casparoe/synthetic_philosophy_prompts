Below is the view I have arrived at about the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. I would like an exact audit of whether the commitments hang together, before I build anything further on top of them.

1. Knowledge is closed under known single-premise entailment. If I know P, know that P entails Q, and competently infer Q from P, I come to know Q.

2. Knowledge is not closed under multi-premise entailment, and the preface paradox is my central counterexample. A careful author can know each of C1 through C400 individually, on the strength of her work on that claim, and can also know, on the strength of a long track record of small errors in long manuscripts, that at least one of C1 through C400 is false. She cannot know the conjunction C1 ∧ ... ∧ C400. So the step from each premise to the conjunction is not, in general, knowledge-preserving.

3. I explain this with a safety requirement: a true belief is knowledge only if it could not easily have been false. Each individual claim in the book is safe; the four-hundred-way conjunction is not, because it is easy for at least one of four hundred separately supported claims to be wrong. A belief can therefore be safe even when it belongs to a set whose conjunction is unsafe.

4. The same safety requirement explains the lottery case. From the odds alone, I cannot know that my ticket will lose, however many tickets there are. A world in which the machine selects my ticket is close enough, and a purely statistical basis leaves that world no less easy than any of the others.

5. I deny that the lottery and preface cases are ultimately parallel. In the lottery, the proposition in question is arbitrary from my point of view and rests only on a bare first-order base rate. In the preface, each individual claim rests on its own first-order evidence, and the claim that at least one is false rests on a higher-order track record. That difference is meant to do the work: it is why safety denies knowledge in the lottery but not in the preface.

6. Finally, I do not regard the preface author as irrational. Her binary beliefs would be inconsistent only if belief were closed under conjunction, and the case teaches that it is not; there is no single proposition she both believes and disbelieves.

Please go through these commitments and tell me where the genuine tensions are, ranked by severity, and which apparent tensions dissolve once the relevant distinctions are drawn. Then take a definite position: does the package hang together, and if not, which single commitment should I revise first, and what is the cheapest repair overall? I am asking for a committed audit rather than a survey of available positions. Please write in American English.
