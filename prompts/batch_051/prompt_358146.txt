In a morphology study group the other day, someone was arguing that English irregular verbs don't undercut the idea that there's a real past-tense rule in the language. Their line was that go/went and sing/sang only stand out as exceptions because there's a regular -ed pattern they're exceptions to, and that kids saying "goed" and "singed" shows the regular rule is doing real work. Then they said "the exception proves the rule" like that settled it. I've always thought that phrase was pure nonsense: doesn't an exception, if anything, disprove the rule? But maybe I'm not being careful about the different things it could mean. Can you seperate out the distinct precise claims someone could be making with "the exception proves the rule"—the trivially true readings, the substantive ones, and the ones that are just false—and then say which reading, if any, actually supports what they were claiming about irregular verbs? Also, would your answer change for suppletion like go/went and good/better, or for sound changes with exceptions?
