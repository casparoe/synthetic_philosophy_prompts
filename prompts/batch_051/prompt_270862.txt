Consider the following reconstruction of the familiar "rational escalation" in a dollar auction. A one-dollar bill is auctioned off. Bids rise in five-cent increments. The highest bidder pays her bid and receives the dollar; the second-highest bidder pays her bid and receives nothing. There is no predetermined upper limit on bids, and we abstract away from bankruptcy. Suppose you are the second-highest bidder at $0.95, the current highest bid is $1.00, and the next legal bid is $1.05.

(1) If you stop now, you lose $0.95 for certain.
(2) If you raise to $1.05 and the other bidder then stops, you pay $1.05 for a one-dollar bill — a net loss of $0.05 — which is ninety cents better than losing $0.95.
(3) If you raise to $1.05 and the other bidder raises again, you are back in the same strategic situation, with both of you having advanced another five cents; the very same reasoning will apply at every later point.
(4) Therefore, at any level the bidding reaches, a rational bidder in second place should raise rather than stop.
(5) Since the other bidder is rational and symmetrically situated, she reasons exactly the same way whenever she is in second place.
(6) Hence two fully rational bidders who enter such an auction will escalate without limit; the only way to avoid disaster is never to enter.

Is this argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and give the corrected version of that step. In particular, explain whether (1)–(4) show that raising at $0.95 is rational all things considered, or only that it looks rational on a local comparison that treats the other bidder’s eventual stopping as given.
