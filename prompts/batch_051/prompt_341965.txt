I am trying to settle the principle that should govern which agent the state sends when a situation is reported: ordinary armed police, officers present but held back, unarmed civilian specialists, municipal inspectors, medical personnel, or no state agent at all. I take the state's claim to a monopoly on legitimate force to be the deep issue here, and the question is what that monopoly entitles or requires the state to do in each kind of case. I have fairly settled gut reactions to a list of scenarios, but I doubt they all follow from one clean rule. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures them, and whether one of my verdicts must go.

Here are my cases and verdicts:

1. Midday, a person is asleep in a bus shelter, apparently intoxicated but unarmed, with nobody in danger. My verdict: sending two armed patrol officers is not the right response; an unarmed outreach or medical team should go. If officers happen to be the only available responders and simply check on the person without force, I would not call it a serious wrong, but it is still the wrong default.

2. A person is pacing outside a convenience store, shouting and evidently in acute psychological distress, perhaps delusional, but touching no one. My verdict: ordinary patrol should not be the first response; a trained mobile crisis team should respond, perhaps with an officer waiting at a distance. If patrol arrives first and handles it gently, that is a mistake of system design rather than an outrage.

3. A caller whispers from a locked bathroom that someone has broken into the house. My verdict: armed police are clearly the correct response, and it would be wrong not to send them at once.

4. An officer stops a driver solely for an expired registration tag on a quiet street. My verdict: the state is entitled to enforce this rule, but not by the default of an armed stop. It should be done by mailed notice, automated license-plate enforcement, or civilian code officers. I am comfortable with the enforcement and uneasy about the gun.

5. There is a loud party and repeated noise complaint, with drunk adults but no violence. My verdict: sending police is not the right response. This belongs to civilian municipal enforcement at most, and possibly to toleration.

6. An older person has not been seen or answered calls in two weeks; a neighbor asks for a welfare check, and the door may have to be forced. My verdict: a joint police-and-paramedic response is acceptable, because someone may have to justify and carry out entry into a home under conditions of real uncertainty. I would be uneasy about police attending alone, but I accept police involvement here.

7. A large protest blocks a major road at rush hour. My verdict: officers diverting traffic and keeping the scene safe is fine. Officers appearing in riot gear and ordering dispersal while the crowd is nonviolent is not fine, unless there is a specific and imminent threat of harm to persons.

8. Officers force entry into a home at 5 a.m. to arrest someone for a past, nonviolent fraud offense. My verdict: not fine. Some coercive presence may be needed to execute any arrest, but dynamic entry at that hour for this offense is unjustified; a summons or a daytime, announced approach would have been adequate.

9. Two teenagers fight in a school corridor, one with a bloody nose, no weapons. My verdict: school staff should separate them and handle discipline; calling police to make arrests is not fine. But if one student keeps striking another in the head and staff cannot stop it, police involvement or forceful restraint becomes acceptable.

10. A person in a park is holding a knife to their own arm and threatening self-harm, with others nearby but no one being attacked. My verdict: a specialist negotiator should take the lead, and I would prefer minimal visible weapons, but I accept that armed officers will probably be on scene because the person has a weapon and may turn it on others. This is acceptable as a last-resort containment, not as the first response. This is the case I most often reverse myself on.

Two tensions stand out to me. In the bus-shelter case and the convenience-store case I am confident that armed police are the wrong first response even though the situation is unpredictable. But in the welfare-check case I accept police involvement precisely because the locked door makes the situation unpredictable and entry may need force. And in the traffic-stop case I keep separate a judgment I am sure about, that the state may enforce the registration rule, from a judgment I am also sure about, that it should not do so by routine armed stop; yet I know many people would not grant that distinction.

The closest I can come to a principle is something like: the state should deploy the least coercive agent competent to meet the likely demands of the situation, and should reserve armed police for crises where a credible, imminent, serious risk of harm makes coercive force genuinely likely to be needed. But that principle fits cases 3, 5, and 7 well, fits case 6 badly, and leaves case 4 ambiguous about whether the enforcement interest matters at all. I do not want to gerrymander a rule just to keep all ten verdicts, but I also do not want to give one up merely because a slogan cannot cover it.

Please work out the principle or principles behind my verdicts. In doing so: state the principle precisely and identify which clause is motivated by which case; say what each candidate principle implies about the knife case and about a case I have not settled, namely a person calls police saying her ex-partner, against whom she has a restraining order, is outside her apartment, but she reports no current threat or violence; and be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, cannot be captured by a non-gerrymandered principle and which one I should abandon if I must abandon one.
