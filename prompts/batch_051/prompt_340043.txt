I am presenting a paper next month to a seminar whose regular attendees are operational weather and climate modelers, two quantitative risk analysts, and one applied statistician. The audience is broadly empiricist and practical: they care about forecast skill, verification, and what they can defensibly tell a client, and they tend to hear phrases like "intrinsic unpredictability" as either unfalsifiable or as a disguised excuse for a deficient model. My thesis is that unpredictability in chaotic systems is not a defect of our models and not merely a synonym for "we do not know enough yet," but a positive structural feature of the dynamics: sensitive dependence on initial conditions and the geometry of a strange attractor impose a genuine, state- and tolerance-dependent predictability horizon that no amount of better data or model improvement can remove. My practical conclusion is that forecast products in strongly nonlinear regimes should report uncertainty as conditional on the attractor geometry rather than presenting ensemble spread as if it were ordinary statistical confidence.

My argument has three moves. First, "unpredictable" conflates three distinct claims — that no deterministic rule exists, that finite precision prevents arbitrarily long useful prediction, and that model error exceeds our tolerance for error — and the interesting one for chaos is the second, which holds even under a perfect model. Second, the relevant uncertainty in such regimes is state-dependent, because local finite-time error growth varies across the attractor; ensemble spread is not a uniform confidence interval. Third, we can distinguish structural limits from model error by appealing to perfect-model experiments and error-growth saturation, but the resulting horizon is always relative to a chosen tolerance.
