I have been defending a quasi-empirical account of working mathematics. On this account, an informal proof is not merely a justification of a theorem but a generator of the theorem's conditions of validity: a counterexample to a step in the proof acts as a potential falsifier, and the mathematical community first resists it through monster-barring, then absorbs it through lemma-incorporation and concept-stretching, so that the theorem survives only as a refined conjecture rather than as a deduction from fixed definitions. The rival picture is that counterexamples to informal proofs are just error-detection: they show a step was invalid for that case, and repairing it is no different in kind from fixing a faulty calculation. I want to put pressure on the first claim against the second by a test rather than by further historical illustration. Design the strongest single test you can: specify what you would observe or measure, what comparison or control would rule out the rival error-detection interpretation, and what pattern of results would support the quasi-empirical picture and what pattern would support the rival. Then state precisely what the test could not settle even in principle about whether mathematics is quasi-empirical as an epistemology rather than as a description of one community's habits.
