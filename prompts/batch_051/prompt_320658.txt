Since retiring from a career in the natural sciences, I have been reading philosophy, and I find myself unusually sensitive to the distinction between a statement that can be checked against observation and one that merely expresses a point of view. Lately I keep returning to a puzzle about the law of defamation. The governing principle, as I understand it, is that a false assertion of fact can be actionable, while a statement of opinion is protected. But forecasts, ratings, and other predictive judgments seem to occupy exactly the boundary between these categories, and their legal treatment strikes me as philosophically unstable.

Consider two cases. A credit-rating agency assigns a sovereign borrower a grade that is marketed to investors as a rigorous, evidence-based assessment of the probability of default. An equity analyst publishes a twelve-month price target for a company's shares. Both products are produced by professionals, sold or widely distributed, and relied upon by third parties, and both can be badly wrong. Yet when litigation follows, the producing institution often argues that a rating or price target is an expression of opinion about the future rather than a factual assertion about how things are. Outside the courtroom the same communications are treated as if they asserted something about the world that could turn out to be correct or incorrect: clients pay for them, regulators scrutinize them, and a grossly inaccurate rating is treated as a failure of the agency, not as a difference of taste.

I want to know what philosophical theory of the fact–opinion distinction, and of the status of future-directed statements, this practice presupposes. Does it presuppose that claims about the future do not yet have truth values, so that a prediction cannot be false at the time it is made in the way a description of the present can? Does it presuppose that a statement qualifies as factual only if it is presently verifiable? Or does it commit the law to a third category: a statement that is asserted with confidence and relied upon by others, yet is not treated as factual when questions of liability arise? Is such a category coherent, or is it a way of refusing to take seriously the thought that a forecast can be better or worse supported by evidence?

More generally, I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory as you see it, say whether the practice actually requires that theory or is compatible with several rival theories, and explain what would have to change in our treatment of ratings and forecasts if we adopted the view that a prediction is simply a factual claim about a probability distribution, one that is true or false precisely insofar as the probabilities it assigns diverge from the best available evidence at the time it is made. I am not looking for a survey of doctrine or citations; I want the underlying philosophical commitments made explicit.
