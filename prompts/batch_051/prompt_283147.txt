You are an expert in formal epistemology, the philosophy of probability, and the psychology of reasoning. Below is an argument I want you to diagnose. Please think carefully: begin by restating the argument in your own terms and by canvassing at least two or three candidate diagnoses before committing to the one you think best.

A colleague has been defending the following line of thought, which he calls the "not that improbable" argument.

(1) Any actual event, if described in sufficiently fine detail, has a minuscule prior probability under a chance hypothesis.
(2) But for any reported coincidence, there is a broader event type under which it falls — some pair in a room shares a birthday; some person in a large population has a dream loosely resembling a later tragedy; some coin somewhere lands heads many times in a row. Given enough opportunities, the probability that some event of that broader type occurs is high, often close to 1. This is the lesson of the birthday paradox.
(3) Therefore, for any reported coincidence, the event is just an instance of a high-probability type, and its occurrence under chance is not improbable.
(4) If an observation is highly probable under the chance hypothesis, then it cannot raise the probability of a non-chance hypothesis such as design, precognition, hidden influence, bias, or rigging.
(5) Hence no reported coincidence ever supports a non-chance explanation. The feeling expressed by "what are the odds?" is always just a failure to count opportunities.

I find this conclusion too strong. It would imply that a particular coin's landing heads twenty times in a row is no evidence at all that it is double-headed or biased — since, across enough coins in enough hands, some coin was bound to produce such a run. It would likewise imply that a casino could never treat a specific player's long winning streak as even mildly suspicious, because such streaks are guaranteed to occur somewhere across all casinos and players. But these are exactly the cases where an apparently improbable token event seems to warrant revising our view of the chance hypothesis. Which step or premise should be rejected, and why does that step look right? What would rejecting it cost? In your diagnosis, be explicit about the distinction the argument is blurring, and say whether a sound warning about anecdotal evidence survives the argument's failure.
