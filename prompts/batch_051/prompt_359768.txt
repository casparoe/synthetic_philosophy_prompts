I teach a second-year undergraduate philosophy of biology seminar (about 18 students, one 90-minute session) and am preparing a unit on the units of selection debate. I would like an activity that makes students feel, rather than merely hear, the central tension: that a trait or behaviour can be favoured at one level of organisation while being disfavoured at another. Ideally the session should be a simple game with tokens, points, or cards in which students are sorted into groups and choose between strategies that pay off differently within a group and between groups, so that they run into the divergence between individual success and group success themselves. They should first play a version in which individual-level advantage wins, then modify a single structural feature—such as migration between groups, group extinction and recolonisation, or limited dispersal (population viscosity)—and observe the outcome shift. The phrase “units of selection” should not be introduced until the debrief. The debrief should map what happened onto the within-group and between-group components of selection, indicate why this matters for both the gene’s-eye view and multi-level selection, and note where the game departs from real selection dynamics. Please give the full rules, a rough timeline, a non-monetary scoring scheme, likely failure modes, and debrief questions. Write the answer in British English.
