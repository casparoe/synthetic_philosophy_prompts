So I got into it with my friend last night about virtue ethics and the situationist critique — he thinks the classic social psych studies show people just don't have the stable character traits virtue ethics needs, and I think he's cherrypicking the studies and anyway the whole thing is more subtle then he thinks. We argued for hours and got nowhere, but it made me realize I've never actually sorted out what I think on the cases. Before I talk to him again I want to figure out whether my own verdicts even hang together. Here are my gut verdicts, and I'd like you to tell me what principle, if anything, captures them.

1. Priya is running late to pick up her kid when she sees a man drop a $50 bill in the parking lot. She grabs it, runs after him, and gives it back, which makes her extra late. Gut: Priya is honest.

2. The same day, Priya realizes the daycare undercharged her by $80 and she doesn't mention it. Gut: I don't want to call her dishonest, and I definitely don't want to take back saying she's honest. This is the one that bothers me — 1 and 2 feel like they should clash but I don't actually feel the clash.

3. Sam spends a free afternoon helping a coworker move boxes. The same week, he walks past that same coworker struggling with a huge heavy box because Sam is late to meet a friend for lunch. Gut: Sam is helpful, and the second episode doesn't cancel it. But I also get why someone would say he's only helpful when it costs him nothing.

4. Maria is extremely reliable about her weekly food-bank shift — never missed one in two years — but she's bad at listening to her friends and often kind of checks out when they are upset. Gut: Maria is charitable or generous, but not kind. So I do want to draw lines between virtues here.

5. David is scrupulously honest at work, even when it makes him look bad, but he constantly tells small lies to his mother to keep her from worrying (yes I've been eating fine, no I'm not feeling sick again). Gut: David is honest; the mother stuff is a separate little box and doesn't count against it.

My friend would say my verdicts are exactly the problem: I'm treating "honest with strangers" and "honest with his mother" as two different traits, when that's just the situation doing the work. I'm groping toward a principle like: a person has a virtue if they are reliably disposed to act in the relevant way across a range of situations, where the range is fixed by what the virtue is about — but that feels circular and maybe question-begging. Can you state a precise principle (or a small set of candidates) that gets my verdicts, or most of them, and say which of my verdicts it would have to overturn?

And here's a further case I keep going back and forth on: Elena found a wallet with $200 cash and ID on the sidewalk and went well out of her way to return it untouched, but two weeks later she cheated on a take-home exam. Do I have to say she's honest in a very narrow wallet-and-cash kind of way but not honest overall, or is one of those two verdicts just wrong? And would whatever principle you land on end up conceding my friend's situationist point — that the stable cross-situational traits virtue ethics talks about don't really exist — or can virtue ethics absorb it?
