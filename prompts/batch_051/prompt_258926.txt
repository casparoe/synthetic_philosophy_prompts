I am a PhD candidate in philosophy of mind and language, and in a week's time I am presenting a working paper to our department's joint philosophy–NLP reading group. The paper defends the following claim: when people say that a large language model "bullshits" in roughly the sense Harry Frankfurt made famous, they are making a category error if the subject of the attribution is the model itself, because Frankfurtian bullshit requires a truth-directed motivational posture, however defective, which a language model lacks; the appropriate target of the charge lies one level up, in the human and institutional decisions to present statistically generated text as if it were warranted assertion while remaining indifferent to its truth. I support this with three moves. First, I distinguish asserting from merely issuing truth-evaluable content, and I propose that an LLM's output be treated as simulated assertion rather than assertion proper. Second, I argue that the familiar contrast between lying, bullshit, and error does not have a slot for this case, and that forcing the case into one of those slots obscures the fact that the normative failure is distributed across a socio-technical system rather than located in a single speaker. Third, I separate the charge against the developer, which is usually better described as negligence or miscalibration, from the charge against the deployer, which can genuinely be Frankfurtian bullshit when the deployer neither knows nor cares whether the outputs are true but presents them as if they carried a speaker's warranty.

The audience matters here. It is a fortnightly workshop of about fifteen postgraduates and staff, jointly run by the philosophy of mind and language group and the NLP group. Two senior speech-act theorists in the room are sceptical of extending speech-act vocabulary to machines at all and will resist any slide from "the model said" to "the model asserted". A formal epistemologist who works on scoring rules and calibration will want the whole dispute stated without folk-psychological idioms, in terms of probabilities, expected accuracy, and whether the system's reported confidence tracks its reliability. One member of the NLP group thinks "hallucination" is already a perfectly good technical term and will suspect that my paper is a merely verbal intervention. And a philosopher of social ontology with Frankfurtian sympathies will push back on my one-level-up move by asking what it means for an institution, a company, or a product team to be indifferent to truth, and whether I have simply relocated the category mistake from an individual agent to a collective one.

What I want before the session is a speaker's prep sheet, not a general survey of the debate. Give me the objections I will actually receive from this audience, in roughly the order they will come, each phrased as those specific people would phrase it, with the likely damage it can do, the two-minute reply I should give, and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks, and say what I should concede up front so the discussion does not stall on the most obvious points. Please write the answer in British English.
