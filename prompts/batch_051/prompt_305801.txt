On the state and law, our course frames sovereign immunity as a procedural doctrine: there is no court with jurisdiction to hear a claim against the state unless the state has first consented to be sued. I cannot make that sit with the rest of the picture. When an official acting in the name of the state does something that would otherwise count as a wrong, the system's response is that no remedy is available — and I do not see how that is meaningfully different from declaring that the state has done no wrong at all. Does sovereign immunity merely deny a forum, or does it commit the law to a substantive claim that the state cannot wrong those subject to it? And if a statute later waives immunity for some claims but leaves others barred, is the state not still implying that the remaining acts are not wrongs? Sort out what the doctrine strictly presupposes, and tell me how confident you are in your answer and which point you are least sure of.
