You sit on the membership committee of a small self-managed housing cooperative. Members pay dues that cover the mortgage and utilities, but almost all other work — cleaning, maintenance, gardening, committee service — is done by volunteers. Roughly a tenth of the members enjoy the shared spaces and services while contributing little or nothing: some say their dues already purchase these things, others appeal to illness, caregiving, or principled objections to the volunteer system, and a few rely on the fact that the work gets done whether or not they help. Every quarter the committee must decide how to respond to each such member, from a reminder to reduced access to shared amenities, on up to expulsion. It must make all of these decisions in a single two-hour meeting; its members have no philosophical training; the facts about people's capacities and circumstances are often incomplete or contested; and any decision can be appealed to the full membership.

Please turn the philosophical discussion of free riding and social parasitism — the question of what, if anything, is wrong with taking the benefits of cooperation without contributing, and when non-contribution is justified or excused — into a reusable decision procedure for the committee: an ordered set of tests to run for each member, with the points where judgment cannot be eliminated marked as such, an explicit account of which distinctions from the underlying idea you are preserving and which you are deliberately dropping in order to keep the procedure usable, and an honest statement of what the procedure cannot settle. Then apply the procedure to two or three hard cases, including one in which the procedure directs the committee to do something it would rather not do.
