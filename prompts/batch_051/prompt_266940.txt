I'm a software engineer, and most of my work is on multilingual models and machine translation. Lately I've been trying to pin down Quine's indeterminacy of translation argument through the gavagai example. As I understand it, Quine's claim is that translation is indeterminate: two rival translation manuals could accord with all possible behavioral evidence from a speaker community and yet still conflict, with no fact of the matter to decide between them. The force of the argument looks to me like it rests on a particular setup, namely a radical translator who arrives with no bilingual resources and must infer the language from scratch by prompting natives and recording their dispositions to assent or dissent. But modern neural machine translation seems to change exactly that setup. A transformer trained on web-scale parallel corpora never tests speakers one at a time; it learns a probabilistic mapping between the two languages, often through shared subword vocabularies and a shared representation space, and then emits whichever target-language sequence the model treats as most probable. Bilinguals accept those outputs as natural translations. Does Quine's argument still go through in that setting, does it need repair, or is it undermined? I want to know which specific premise the development bears on, whether that premise can be restated in a form that survives, and what the argument still shows if it does. I'm asking about the fate of the philosophical argument, not about whether machine translation works practically.
