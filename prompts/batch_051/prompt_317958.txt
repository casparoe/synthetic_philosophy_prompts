In much of ordinary life, memory is treated as central to who someone is: people say of a relative lost to dementia that the person they knew is already gone, and a person who cannot remember a period of their own life may feel, and be told, that whoever lived through it was not really them. But when practical responsibilities arise, the same judgments tend to run the other way. A criminal who has entirely forgotten committing a crime is still tried and punished as the person who did it; a person who wakes with complete amnesia is still bound by their prior contracts, debts, and marriage. Complete loss of the relevant memories is not generally accepted as ending the person.

This is an asymmetry worth taking seriously. Locke proposed a criterion that seems to explain the memory side of it: a person is the same person just insofar as their consciousness can reach back and appropriate a past action or thought. Reid objected with the brave-officer case: an old general can remember being a brave young officer, and the officer can remember being a boy flogged for stealing at school, but the general cannot remember being that boy. If identity must be transitive, the general is the boy; if memory is the whole criterion of identity, the general is not.

Taking the ordinary asymmetry together with Locke's criterion and Reid's objection: is there a defensible principle that explains when memory should matter for identity and responsibility and when it should not, or is the ordinary practice simply inconsistent?
