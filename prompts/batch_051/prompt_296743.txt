I teach a seminar on the philosophy of interpretation, and I have been using the following four-part test for when an author's intention settles the meaning of a literary work: (i) the author actually formed a determinate prior intention that the work communicate a specific content p; (ii) that intention was causally responsible for the relevant features of the finished text, rather than being merely a post hoc description of what the author hoped readers would find; (iii) the language of the text can bear p without semantic or syntactic distortion; and (iv) p is at least as natural a reading of the finished text as any rival. I want to stress-test this account against a case I have not seen discussed cleanly.

A poet wrote the first half of a long poem in her twenties and published it as a standalone work; she intended it, and it was received as, sincere devotional verse. Forty years later, after a documented change in her religious commitments, she returned to the poem, wrote a second half that is unmistakably ironic about that same devotional posture, lightly revised some connective passages, and issued the two halves together as one continuous poem. In interviews she now says the unified poem is ironic through and through: the early sections are to be read as quoted material, enclosed by the later sections' perspective, and the work's meaning is the exposure of the devotional voice rather than the voice itself. Her notebooks from the period of the second composition say the same. But the first half, as incorporated, remains almost word-for-word the sincere text of forty years ago; the combined work contains several passages that read naturally as sincere devotion, several that read naturally as parody, and no uncontroversial editorial signal, such as a preface or typographic marking, that tells the reader which register governs which stretch.

Is the meaning of the completed poem authorially determined in my sense? Go through the four conditions, and be honest about which ones this case satisfies, which it violates, and which fail to apply without further specification. I especially want to know whether the later intention to enclose the early text counts as causal responsibility for the early text's contribution to the whole, and whether the absence of any editorial signal is a problem for condition (iii), for condition (iv), or for the account overall. If the conditions do not return a verdict, tell me whether the fault lies in the case or in the test: do I need a fifth condition about whether an intention must be in place at the time of each stretch's first composition rather than at the time of the work's final assembly, a distinction between intended meaning and intended use, or something else?
