At a recent faculty colloquium on the ethics of belief, two of my colleagues—one a moral epistemologist, the other a philosopher of science—debated how far a famous argument by William Kingdon Clifford extends. The text under discussion was this passage from Clifford’s essay “The Ethics of Belief,” which I reproduce verbatim:

The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him.

They applied the passage not to Clifford’s shipowner, but to this case: a director of research at a public health institute became convinced that a new vaccine was safe. He formed this conviction partly because he trusted a charismatic laboratory chief, partly because he dismissed a statistician’s worry about the trial’s stopping rule as bureaucratic fussiness, and partly because he avoided reading a critical safety report because doing so made him anxious. A later independent review found that the vaccine was safe. The two colleagues’ exchange ran as follows:

(1) “Clifford’s principle applies straightforwardly. The question of right or wrong concerns how the director got his belief, not what it turned out to be. He acquired it by trusting authority, dismissing a methodological objection, and dodging unpleasant evidence. On Clifford’s account he had no right to believe on the evidence before him, so his belief is morally wrong even though true.”

(2) “That cannot be right. Clifford’s shipowner is condemned because he sent an emigrant ship to sea on an unearned belief, not because he had a private hunch. The director did not expose anyone to risk on the basis of his belief. If epistemic irresponsibility by itself were a moral wrong, then every optimistic self-assessment and every hurried generalization would be a sin.”

(3) “But Clifford anticipated this exact reply. He says the shipowner would still be guilty even if the ship never sank, and that the owner would not have been innocent, only not found out. So harm cannot be what makes the belief wrong.”

(4) “That shows Clifford overreached. He conflated an epistemic failing with a moral failing. The director’s belief may have been epistemically unjustified, but epistemic norms govern the relation between a belief and its evidence, while moral norms govern action and character. The director’s only moral question was whether to rely on the belief in decisions, and he did not.”

(5) “That separation is false for a public official. A research director’s beliefs are not private; they shape which memos get written, which colleagues get heard, and which later studies get funded. There is no sharp line between believing and being disposed to act. Clifford’s point is precisely that unearned belief corrupts one’s future conduct, and that is a moral matter.”

(6) “But the director’s belief turned out true, and he formed it on the basis of a generally reliable environment. That is not a case of insufficient evidence in the relevant sense; it is a case of arriving at a correct conclusion through imperfect but serviceable heuristics. If Clifford’s principle condemns every true belief formed with less than ideal scrupulousness, it collapses into an unattainable demand for epistemic sainthood.”

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating, identify which of the two colleagues is closer to correct on the central question, and state what, if anything, both parties are missing about Clifford’s actual position.
