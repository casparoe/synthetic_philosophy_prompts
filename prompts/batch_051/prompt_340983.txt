I am preparing a long-form interview for a philosophy podcast whose audience is familiar with the rudiments of Chinese philosophy but not with the details of Wang Yangming’s thought. The guest argues that Wang Yangming’s doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action (zhī xíng hé yī) is a defensible and illuminating account of moral failure and moral education. I do not want the interview to be an exposition of the doctrine; the listener will have been given a short introductory segment beforehand. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press the doctrine where it is most strained, and after each principal question include the one follow-up that would be strongest if the guest gives the reply he is most likely to give.

To anchor the questions, the guest’s central commitments are as follows: a person who sincerely judges an action to be right but persistently does not do it does not genuinely know that it is right; that person’s knowledge is merely verbal and has not ripened into the knowledge that is inseparable from acting; from the first-person standpoint one cannot simply identify one’s own conviction as knowledge independently of what one does; and self-cultivation consists in allowing an innate moral responsiveness to operate through action rather than in accumulating theoretical understanding.

The questions should press at least the following pressure points, though you may replace any with a more incisive one:

- whether the doctrine dissolves akrasia only by redefining ‘knowledge’, and whether the resulting thesis is still informative;
- whether the view collapses into behaviourism, or can instead distinguish outward conformity from genuine knowledge;
- whether the view destroys first-person authority: how I can be wrong about what I know, and how I could ever detect that I lack knowledge I take myself to have;
- whether treating an incipient intention as already a form of action trivialises the distinction between deliberation and deed;
- how the view can account for gradual moral habituation if knowledge and action are not temporally separable;
- whether the unity claim is plausible only for moral knowledge, and how the guest would handle its extension to non-moral skill or factual knowledge.

For each pressure point, anticipate the guest’s most natural Wang Yangming-style reply—for example, that selfish desire has obstructed the natural movement of knowing into doing, or that knowledge is the inception and action the completion of a single event—and make the follow-up expose what remains unresolved in that reply. Ask no generic introductory or biographical questions, and do not ask for citations or textual history. Write in British English.
