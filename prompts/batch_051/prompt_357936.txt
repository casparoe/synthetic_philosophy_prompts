Construct a thought experiment in which the familiar public alignment on biotechnological suffering relief inverts. This is for an essay arguing that incremental technologies for reducing suffering can quietly displace the goal they appear to serve.

Set up two positions. Suffering abolitionism, the project sometimes called the hedonistic imperative, holds that suffering is bad wherever it occurs and whomever it befalls; that the long-run imperative is to use biotechnology to phase suffering out of sentient life altogether; and that any intermediate intervention is to be judged chiefly by whether it moves that project closer to completion or further from it. Suffering constitutivism holds that some forms of suffering are internally tied to goods without which a life would lose its seriousness — grief to love, struggle to achievement, guilt to repair — and that the proper object of intervention is therefore not suffering as such but only gratuitous suffering: suffering that is objectless, destructive, or disconnected from anything the sufferer could count as good.

The scenario: a concrete, near-future biotechnology is up for adoption in a recognizable health system. The technology must genuinely reduce some felt suffering for those who receive it, and it must look, on its face, like a humane reduction of suffering that a casual observer would expect the abolitionist to champion and the constitutivist to resist. The case must deliver the opposite verdicts. The suffering abolitionist opposes adoption; the suffering constitutivist supports it. The abolitionist's opposition must not rest on the technology being a sham, a placebo, or net harmful in the short run, nor on its advocates being obviously bad actors. It must fall out of a plausible long-run calculation, specific to this technology and this rollout, about what adoption would do to the trajectory toward full abolition. The constitutivist's support must fall out of their own distinction between gratuitous and constitutive suffering, not from abandoning that distinction.

Constraints: one intervention; nothing beyond what is already on a near-future biomedical horizon; no perfect prediction of how individuals or the public will respond; no stereotyping of any patient group; and the scenario must be told in a single paragraph.

After the scenario, do the check. State exactly what the technology does, which suffering it removes and which it leaves in place, and then work through how each position reaches its verdict, showing where the two lines of reasoning cross. Finish by naming the single feature of the case that is doing the work — the feature such that removing it would collapse the verdicts back into the familiar alignment — and by giving the strongest reply a suffering abolitionist could make to the criticism that their own principles straightforwardly require supporting the intervention.
