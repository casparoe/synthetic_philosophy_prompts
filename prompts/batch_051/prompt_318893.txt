I'm a philosophy professor and I sit on the disciplinary committee of a very old student debating society, and we've hit a case that has us genuinely split. Our rules date to 1928 and Article IV still reads:

"Any member who has given his word to the Secretary that he will attend a meeting, and is not in his place when the clock strikes the hour, without having sent word to the Secretary before the day, shall be deemed to have broken his word; and any member who twice in one session so breaks his word shall be required to withdraw."

Here's the case. The Society now takes attendance pledges through a website: a member clicks a button marked "I'll be there," and the system messages the Secretary and also writes the event into the member's calendar automatically. A member of forty years' standing clicked it for the annual dinner. The Society's calendar system had a time-zone bug and put the dinner in his calendar one hour later than it really was; everyone accepts this was the platform's fault, not his. He sat down at his desk ten minutes before the hour he had been given, shaved and in a dinner jacket, clicked the video link, and found the dinner already halfway through. He hadn't sent the Secretary any separate note, because he believed he was early.

The younger officers say the text is plain: he was not in his place when the clock struck the hour, and he did not send word. He says he did everything a punctual person could, and if the Society's own agent gave him the wrong hour, he shouldn't be charged with breaking his word.

I want less a ruling than an interpretive anatomy. Please take it through four approaches and then a verdict: "The words", "The purpose", "What the 1928 drafters would have said", "What a reasonable member today would understand", and "Which should win". Roughly 100 words each.
