I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am working on a feature about the way Avicenna's flying man thought experiment is being rediscovered by contemporary researchers of the sense of self. I want the piece to go beyond the familiar observation that Avicenna anticipated Descartes, and for that I need a rigorous counterfactual reconstruction of how Avicenna himself would have judged later developments. The setup, as I understand it, is this. Avicenna asks us to imagine a human being created all at once, fully developed and intelligent, suspended in empty air with the limbs spread so that no part of the body touches any other, the eyes covered, and with no incoming sensation from any modality. Avicenna holds that this person would nonetheless affirm the existence of his own self, and he concludes that the self is known directly to itself, without the senses and without the body.

What I would like from you is a reasoned account of the positions Avicenna would have taken on the following posthumous developments, derived specifically from the commitments behind the flying man rather than from a generic mind-body dualism. For each, give his likely verdict, the reasoning behind it, and whether he would treat the case as confirming his argument, as irrelevant to it, or as a genuine challenge to it.

1. Locked-in syndrome: a patient who is almost totally paralyzed while wakefulness and cognition remain intact, often communicating only through eye movement, and who reports a full sense of being present inside a body he cannot command.
2. The rubber hand illusion and related body-ownership experiments, in which synchronized visual and tactile input makes a healthy subject feel that an artificial hand belongs to him.
3. Experimentally induced out-of-body experiences, in which a subject reports that the felt location of the self seems to lie outside the physical body.
4. Prolonged sensory deprivation in an isolation tank, in which a healthy adult's structured external input is sharply reduced for hours.
5. The modern dissociation between the sense of bodily ownership, understood as the feeling that this body is mine, and the sense of agency, understood as the feeling that I am the author of my actions.

Finally, synthesize. Which of these cases would Avicenna regard as the closest real approximation of his flying man, and which would he say miss the point of the argument entirely? Where would his reading most sharply conflict with the reading a contemporary consciousness scientist would give? And would he accept the now-common gloss that the flying man establishes only a thin or minimal self, or would he insist that the self it reveals is already a substantial one rather than the residue of integrated bodily signals? I am not looking for citations or source-hunting; I am looking for a reconstruction that tracks his actual commitments closely and flags the points where those commitments would force an unfamiliar reading of the modern evidence.
