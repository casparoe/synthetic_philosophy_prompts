I've been keeping a list of times I actually changed a moral view on somebody else's say-so, and times I didn't, and I'm trying to figure out whether there's one principle underneath it all. My working rule has been something like: I should defer to moral testimony only when I already trust the source's values or character, not when I merely trust their expertise. But a couple of these cases make me think that rule needs repairing, so maybe you can fix it.

Here's the data.

1. L, a close friend who reads far more animal ethics than I do and shares most of my broader values, tells me that buying farmed fish is wrong. I basically stop buying it. I couldnt state her argument if you asked me.

2. A well-known moral philosopher says on a podcast that we are morally required to boycott a major international sporting event over labor abuses. I find myself completely unmoved; I'd want the actual argument before acting on it, even though his relevant expertise is obviously greater than L's.

3. A colleague I've known for years, who is not an intellectual but is probably the most scrupulous person I have ever met, tells me I acted wrongly in how I handled a hiring decision. This bothers me for weeks and I eventually conclude he was right.

4. A stranger at dinner announces, with no argument, that eating octopus is morally monstrous. I treat this as roughly zero evidence.

5. I read a statement signed by about forty academic ethicists saying that a specific border policy amounts to a grave injustice. My confidence that the policy is wrong jumps a lot, more than I can justify from the brief argument printed alongside the statement.

6. A religious leader from a tradition I don't share tells me that a surgery I'm considering is morally impermissible. I don't defer in the slightest.

7. A thoughtful friend who shares my politics but reads less philosophy than I do tells me I'm wrong that a certain kind of mocking joke is harmless, and I find myself revising my view halfway, even before she gives reasons.

So: is there one principle that handles all seven? If not, which of my verdicts should I overturn? Then state the repaired principle precisely and tell me what it says about a new case I havent listed.
