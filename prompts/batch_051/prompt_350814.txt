I keep having a version of this argument with a friend who works on LLM evals, and I'd like to actually think it through instead of just winning on vibes. He claims his model "knows" a fact even when it can't answer the question, because the fact is a logical consequence of other things the model demonstrably does know. I counter that this is a motte-and-bailey: the defensible version is trivial (the information is recoverable under some prompting), and the interesting version (the model already represents that consequence) is unfalsifiable. But maybe Im the one being sloppy. The old logical omniscience problem says an ideally rational agent already believes all the logical consequences of its beliefs; real minds and real models don't do that. The interesting question, to me, is whether a real system can be "partly logically omniscient" in a restricted domain — not that it's good at derivation, but that it genuinely represents consequences it would otherwise seem not to represent, and that we can tell the difference.

So here's what I want. Design the best concrete test, in a restricted domain of your choosing, of the claim that a system — an LLM, a human subject, your pick — already represents Q whenever Q is a logical consequence of premises it has been shown to know, as opposed to merely possessing the premises from which Q could be derived. Be specific: choose the domain, the premises, and the exact probes; say what pattern of results would support "it already had Q in there" versus "it only has the premises and derives Q on demand (or not)." Then be honest about what even the cleanest result would not settle. In particular, I suspect a positive result just shows the model can recombine information in a way your probes happen to catch, while a negative result just shows your probes are bad, and no data can settle whether "belief" is the right word. Tell me if that's wrong.

As the very last line, write "Verdict:" followed by a one-sentence summary of what the strongest test settles and what it leaves open.
