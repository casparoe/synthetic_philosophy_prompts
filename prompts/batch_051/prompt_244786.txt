You are a professional philosopher of language acting as a neutral facilitator between two camps in the dispute over Frege's puzzle about identity statements. The Fregean camp holds that the difference in cognitive significance between *Hesperus is Hesperus* and *Hesperus is Phosphorus* must be traced to something beyond the object referred to: the two names express different senses or modes of presentation, and these senses explain both why some identity statements are informative and why substitution fails inside belief reports. The Millian camp holds that a proper name's contribution to what is said is exhausted by its referent, so the two sentences express one and the same singular proposition; on this view the apparent difference is to be handled pragmatically, psychologically, or metalinguistically, without treating senses as part of semantic content. The two camps have spent years arguing past one another, and your job is to write the document they should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Begin by brainstorming, for your own use, the linguistic data both sides ought to agree to save—including the relevant identity sentences, belief ascriptions, reports embedded under negation, sentences containing empty names, and the behaviour of competent speakers who assent to one of a pair of co-referential sentences but not the other—then discard formulations that are slanted, vacuous, or merely verbal. Then draft the joint statement itself in two parts. The first part should be a numbered list of agreed claims that a clear-headed Fregean and a clear-headed Millian could both sign, stating what a theory of proper names and identity statements must explain and which parts of their long-running dispute are merely terminological. The second part should be a disagreements table. Each row should set out a remaining crux: a neutral formulation of the sub-question, wording of the Fregean position that a Fregean would accept, wording of the Millian position that a Millian would accept, and a concrete case, experiment, or argument that both sides agree bears on that crux, with each side's prediction about it recorded in advance. Do not adjudicate the debate; the aim is a constructive text that both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact and any merely verbal residue recognised as such.
