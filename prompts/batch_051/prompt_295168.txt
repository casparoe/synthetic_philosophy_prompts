Here is a parallel I have been turning over, and I cannot decide whether it is deep or superficial. In cost-sensitive decision theory, the judgment that one kind of error is worse than another is represented by an asymmetric loss function: a missed harm carries a higher cost than a false alarm. The resulting optimal rule shifts its threshold toward safety, but it is still doing ordinary expected-loss minimization, and for any finite cost ratio a large enough prospective benefit will eventually justify taking the risk. Negative utilitarianism sounds formally similar: it attaches greater moral weight to suffering than to an equal amount of happiness, so the moral decision rule tilts toward preventing harms rather than producing benefits. In its weaker, non-lexical forms this seems to be exactly a utilitarian calculation with a skewed value function — different from classical utilitarianism in its parameters, not in the kind of theory it is. In lexical or threshold forms, however, negative utilitarianism refuses certain trades outright: no finite amount of happiness is allowed to outweigh certain kinds of suffering, suffering above a threshold, or risks of astronomical future suffering (s-risks). That looks inexpressible by any real-valued loss function, since a real-valued function cannot make one unit of harm outweigh every finite quantity of benefit.

So my question is whether the parallel is deep or whether it misdescribes negative utilitarianism. If it is deep, weak negative utilitarianism is just expected-utility reasoning with extreme weights, and criticism of it becomes criticism of a parameter setting. If it is superficial, I want to know exactly where the mapping breaks — whether at lexicality, at the asymmetry between creating new happy lives and avoiding new suffering lives, or at the special role of s-risks — and whether that breaking point shows that strong negative utilitarianism cannot be captured within a consequentialist, expected-loss framework at all.
