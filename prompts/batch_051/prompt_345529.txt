Me and my friend went back and forth all evening about moral foundations theory — specifically whether the left/right divide is about genuinely different moral modules or just the same modules with different weights — and we got completely derailed by a more basic question: what even counts as a foundation? The criteria I was taught are something like this. A foundation should pick out its own adaptive challenge from our evolutionary past, not just a modern value that happens to be fashionable. It should come with a characteristic emotion or family of emotions, the way care/harm comes with compassion and anger, authority/subversion with respect and fear, sanctity/degradation with disgust. It should have a recognizable virtue vocabulary of its own. It should show up across cultures rather than being the private property of one political camp. And, this last one is more negotiable, it should pay for itself statistically: adding it to the list should let you predict moral judgments that the other foundations alone miss.

Now the awkward case. Alot of people working on the theory now include liberty/oppression as a kind of sixth foundation. The story is that our ancestors lived in dominance hierarchies and got a fitness advantage from detecting and resenting when the alpha tried to control them; the characteristic response is reactance, that bristling feeling of being pushed around, and the virtue talk is all freedom, independence, self-determination. My friend's counterargument, which ate an hour, is that liberty isn't a sixth foundation at all, it's fairness in a costume. Oppression is just someone rigging the game or taking more than their share, and freedom talk is just the fairness foundation applied in an individualist way instead of a groupish way. What bugs me is that the evidence honestly looks split. People who score high on a liberty/oppression dimension — libertarians especially — don't score high on the standard five, which smells like a distinct module, but it's just as consistent with them having an unusual fairness-first weighting where loyalty, authority and sanctity barely register.

So does liberty/oppression qualify as a foundation or not? Step through the list criterion by criterion, say which of them are actually doing the work and which underdetermine the answer, and say whether the case shows the criteria themselves need changing. And since this is where the argument started, also say whether settling the sixth-foundation question matters for the question of whether the psychology of left and right tells us anything about who is right, or whether that part is just a taxonomy fight.
