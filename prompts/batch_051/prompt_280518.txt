Spent the whole walk home after a long argument with a friend about the simulation argument and whether it makes caring obsessively about the far future look ridiculous. His line was: if you take the trilemma seriously—roughly, either civilizations like ours almost always go extinct before they can run ancestor simulations, or posthuman civilizations almost never bother to run them, or we are almost certainly in a simulation right now—then there is a decent chance we are in an ancestor sim. And if we are, then the far future is just whatever the simulators let run, or it gets switched off, and the beings in it are just code—so why act like trillions of future simulated lives should dominate everything now? I pushed back but never got the counterargument into one piece.

So please make the strongest argument you can for the claim that accepting the simulation argument should not dampen fanaticism about the far future—in fact it should sharpen it. By fanaticism I mean the stance that astronomically large expected values from the deep future should swamp ordinary near-term considerations. Go through the trilemma branch by branch and say what each branch actually does to far-future reasoning. Take seriously the shutdown worry, the sims-don't-count worry, and the small-probability-of-us-being-in-base-reality point. And as you go, seperate what is actually settled from what is genuinely disputed by people who work on this—dont dress up a contested read of the argument as the consensus.
