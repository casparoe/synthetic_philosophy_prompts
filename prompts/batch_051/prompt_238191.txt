I've been poking around the old imagery debate — Kosslyn's scanning studies, the Shepard–Metzler rotation results, Pylyshyn's tacit-knowledge reply, then the later neuroimaging back-and-forth — and I'm struck by how it never really got resolved; it just ran out of steam sometime in the 90s and 2000s. The depictive side says mental images act like quasi-spatial, analog, maplike representations: people take longer to scan across farther distances and to rotate through bigger angles, and later work claimed early visual cortex lights up in a way that fits a pictorial format. The propositional side says all of that is compatible with a language-like description underneath, because subjects could be simulating what they tacitly know they'd see or do, and the brain data doesn't pin down the format. Both sides mostly accepted the same experimental results and still couldn't agree about what the results show. My working hypothosis is that they were answering two different questions under one heading — Kosslyn asking what format the visual system actually deploys, Pylyshyn asking what you're entitled to conclude from chronometric and imaging data before you've ruled out a cheaper propositional story, so the argument turned into a fight about standards of evidence rather than about imagery itself. But maybe I'm caricaturing Pylyshyn, and it's actually a substantive empirical disagreement that better methods could eventually settle. What kind of disagreement is this, really — substantive, verbal, methodological, or a difference in starting intuitions about what representation has to amount to? I want a diagnosis, not another summary of the back-and-forth. Given whatever diagnosis you settle on, what would actually count as progress: a new kind of experiment, a formal argument, a sharper distinction, or just admitting the question was ill-posed? And one last thing: regardless of where you land, tell me what would change your mind about the diagnosis and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
