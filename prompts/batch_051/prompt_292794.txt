A Benthamite legal reformer proposes to replace a jurisdiction's entire adult sexual-offences code with a single harm-based statute. A separate, unchanged code continues to govern minors and persons adjudicated incapable of consent. For adults, the proposed statute provides:

1. Any sexual act is lawful if every party is an adult, is capable of consent, and has not been brought to the act by force, threats, or material deception.
2. An act is punishable only if it causes, or creates a substantial and unjustified risk of, a harm of a kind the law already recognizes in non-sexual contexts: bodily injury, coercion, fraud, serious communicable disease, or serious public nuisance.
3. No act may be punished merely for being non-procreative, commercial, unusual, or widely disapproved of; the disgust of third parties counts as no harm at all.
4. Where an act is punishable, sentence is set by the same tariff that applies to the analogous non-sexual harm, never by the character of the act.
5. A standing commission issues guidance under two stated rules of thumb: sexual gratification is a positive element of utility, and punishment is justified only by the prevention of greater harm, never by antipathy, taste, or ascetic scruple.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a moral position. Walk through how it would actually operate if adopted. Specifically: what does it do to enforcement discretion and to the behavior of prosecutors, complainants, and people whose practices sit near the edges of the harm categories? How is it gamed first, and by whom? What happens to consent norms and to the information people disclose to partners once fraud, coercion, and disease are among the few gateways to criminal liability? What second-order effects appear in non-legal institutions—employers, platforms, insurers, landlords—once moral disapproval ceases to be a legal ground? Where does the system fail first, and are those failures fixable without reintroducing the very moralism the statute was designed to expel?
