I'm trying to pin down what I actually think about competitive argumentation by going on my gut reactions to cases, becuase my principles keep coming out inconsistent. Defending a position I don't hold in an evening argument with a friend, where we both know we're just stress-testing the ideas: fine, even good. A school debate club assigns a shy kid to defend a motion she finds repellent, and she's marked on persuasiveness: fine, though it makes me uneasy. A PR firm pays well-known academics to argue in public that a food additive is safe when they privately think the studies are too shaky to sign: not fine. In an adversarial trial, a barrister doesn't mention a document that would help the other side: not fine, yet I still accept the system as a whole. A televised debate with two-minute answers, a clapometer and a declared winner: not fine, but I can't say why it's really worse than the school club. An adversarial collaboration between two scientists with a referee they both trust: excellent. The only principle I keep reaching for is that it's fine when everyone involved knows the arguments are a kind of game, but that falls apart on the trial and the PR firm. Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? If a clean one costs me one or two of my verdicts, which should I drop and why? And what does the surviving principle predict about the original question—turning serious argumentation into an actual spectator sport with leagues, rankings and prize money?
