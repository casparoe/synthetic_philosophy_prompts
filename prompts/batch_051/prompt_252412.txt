I'm rereading the Harry Potter books for the first time since I got into the life-extension / anti-deathism corner of the rationalist internet, and its kind of jarring how hard the series stacks the deck against immortality. Voldemort is basically the only character who seriously tries to overcome death — horcruxes, the Philosopher's Stone, the works — and he's also the arch-villain whose terror of death is presented as the root of everything wrong with him. Meanwhile Dumbledore keeps framing death as an adventure to be welcomed, Nicolas Flamel actually has the Stone and chooses to let himself die, and the one brother in the Tale of the Three Brothers who accepts death on its own terms is the one who gets the happy ending. It reads like a deathist morality play: mortality gets rationalized as what gives life meaning, and the only people who reject that are evil or pitiable.

I want a reading of the series through the lens of deathism — the idea that a lot of pro-mortality sentiment is sour grapes, status quo bias, or terror management rather than an honest weighing of whether longer or indefinite life would actually be good. But I don't want a one-sided take; I'm writing an essay and want to steelman the books too. So: which scenes and character choices do the most work for the deathist reading, and where does the series actually complicate or push back on it? Does any pro-longevity position ever get stated fairly, or is it always poisoned by being Voldemort's? And is there anything in the books that isn't just status-quo bias — say, what the horcruxes do to a person, or what the story implies about identity over time — that someone who wants to live a very long time should genuinely have to answer? I'd rather you end up saying the books are a mixed case than force them into either being deathist propaganda or just a kids' story I shouldn't overread.
