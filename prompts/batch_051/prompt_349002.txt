I'm a philosopher of language, and my NLP collleague won't stop saying that word embeddings are a map of meaning in exactly the way a geographic map is a map of terrain: nearness in the space is nearness in meaning, distance is real distance, and you can navigate the one exactly as you navigate the other. He uses the analogy to license the conclusion that semantic similarity just is closeness in a high-dimensional space, so when a model puts two words near each other it has literally learned that their meanings are close. Can you audit how far the analogy actually runs? I'd like the full correspondence table—what plays the role of the terrain, the distance, the roads, the landmarks, the scale, the projection—and then the places it breaks down; I care especially about polysemy (a word with a dozen senses sits at a single point), context-sensitivity, anisotropy (the space's weird global shape that crowds everything into a cone), and the fact that embeddings trained on the same corpus with different algorithms or different seeds put different words near each other. Then tell me which disanalogies actually matter for his conclusion and which are just nitpicking, and whether a better cartographic concept—a projection, or a subway map where only the order of stops matters—resuces the conclusion or quietly gives it up.
