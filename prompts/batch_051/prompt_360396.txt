I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I do a monthly eight-minute explainer spot on a public radio evening news show. Next month's piece is the dollar auction and escalation of commitment — how people and organizations keep pouring resources into losing courses of action. The audience is general listeners, smart but with no economics or behavioral-science background, and I'm one voice with no callers, no slides, just a script.

Don't explain the material to me; plan how I should explain it. I want the order of pieces so the dollar auction feels like a trap closing on the listener rather than a parlor trick. What has to land before what? Where should each turn happen, at roughly which minute? Pick one carrying example — it doesn't have to be the literal auction, but it has to have the right structure, and you should say what that structure must have and must lack. If you use a news case, it has to be tellable in thirty seconds with no background.

The thing I'm trying not to do is deliver the tired 'sunk cost fallacy' moral. My sense is the auction is mostly not about sunk cost at all: each new bid is a forward-looking comparison between the next small increment and the loss you'd lock in by stopping, and the trap is that locally sensible moves can compose into a jointly ruinous outcome. Sunk cost is one factor among several, alongside self-justification, loss-frame risk seeking, and the way the last steps near a goal feel disproportionately worth it. Tell me which of those actually earn their place in eight minutes and which should be collapsed or cut. I also want the segment to say something about organizations, not just individuals: in a newsroom or a state agency, the person who kills a doomed project risks blame if it would have turned around, while the people who keep it going just blend in, so escalation can be individually sensible even when its collectively ruinous. I don't know if that's teachable in eight minutes, or whether it cuts against the simplicity of the auction — tell me.

Map the misconceptions in the order listeners will form them, and say exactly where in the sequence to head each one off. My guesses: 'sunk cost fallacy, done'; 'just set a limit before you start'; 'I'd never bid past a dollar'; 'only irrational people do this.' Fix the list if its wrong. If it's right, show me how to make each one land as something the listener recognizes rather than something they feel smug about dodging.

Finally, a check for understanding at the end. I can't take calls or do Q&A, so it has to be one question I ask the listener to answer silently, phrased so a listener who got the idea can answer it in one sentence to themself and a listener who didn't can't fake it. What should I ask, and what wrong-but-plausible answers would indicate the explanation failed in which specific ways?

Keep it to eight minutes and one episode. I don't need citations or reading suggestions; I need a sequence and a script spine. Start with the plan itself — no preamble, no restating the assignment.
