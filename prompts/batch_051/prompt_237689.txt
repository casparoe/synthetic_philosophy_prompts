Whenever I hear about an official or politician outside my own country distributing cash, jobs, or public contracts to people who supported them, I form the working judgement that what I am looking at is corruption, and that the surrounding political arrangement is compromised. I revise that judgement only when the exchange is either clearly ceremonial and trivial in value, or transparently regulated and open to everyone on equal terms.

Recently I applied this to a friend's account of a village headman who steered small road contracts to his campaigners but who, by my friend's account, also delivered the roads and was the only figure who got anything built. I called the practice corrupt without having observed it myself. My friend says I am imposing my own Western standards on a community where favours to supporters function as a form of reciprocity and mutual insurance.

Is my standing rule defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as a principle? Under what conditions is an outsider entitled to default to suspicion when judging gift-and-favour politics, and when does that default collapse into prejudice? If the rule is partly right and partly wrong, how should it be restated?

Please answer in British English.
