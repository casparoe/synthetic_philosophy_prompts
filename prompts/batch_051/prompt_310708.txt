Ancient atomism combines two commitments that seem not to fit together. Atoms are wholly solid, with no void inside them, so nothing in an atom can give way when one strikes another; atoms also move through the void and collide with one another. But if two atoms cannot interpenetrate, then at the moment of impact they must be in contact with no void between their surfaces. Yet if there is no void between them whatsoever, their surfaces coincide; and once two surfaces coincide, it is hard to see how one atom is not already entering the region occupied by the other, which is precisely the interpenetration the theory rules out. How, then, can impenetrable atoms ever collide, or so much as touch? Is the atomists' picture coherent only if they allow void even at the point of contact? As you answer, please separate what is settled about this issue from what is genuinely contested among people who know ancient atomism well, and do not present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
