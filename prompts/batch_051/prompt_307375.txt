I have just come from a long argument with a friend about the Knobe effect, and I want a way to make the point felt rather than litigated. I had walked him through the usual chairman case: a corporate officer approves a project while saying he cares only about profit and not at all about the effect the project will have on the environment, and people who hear the version in which the environment is harmed judge that he harmed the environment intentionally, while people who hear the version in which the environment is helped judge that he did not help the environment intentionally, even though the officer's foresight is identical in both. My friend's reply, pressed through several variations, was that all this shows is that ordinary speakers use the word "intentional" as a disguised moral verdict. In the harm case they are condemning the officer, and in the help case they are withholding praise, so what looks like a discovery about the concept of intentional action is really just blame and credit leaking into a word. His fallback was that even if the asymmetry is real, it would only appear because the two cases differ in blatant moral valence — one side effect is obviously bad and the other obviously good — and that a subtler case would wash the effect out.

I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind: fourteen students, one eighty-minute session. I would like a runnable classroom activity that makes them feel the force of the Knobe effect instead of having it explained to them. Specifically, I want them to produce the asymmetry in their own judgments before the phenomenon is named, and ideally to do so in a version of the case in which the side effect is not simply harmful-versus-helpful — for instance, a side effect that violates a norm without causing obvious harm, or one that is evaluatively mixed — so that the question of whether the effect depends on blatant moral valence is put to the students rather than assumed. What matters is that they catch themselves treating two formally similar cases differently, or feel the pull to do so, before I supply any theory.

Please give me: a protocol with roles or materials, such as vignettes, response sheets, and a voting or ranking procedure; a timeline for the eighty minutes with no lecture up front; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the distinction between what an agent intends and what an agent merely foresees, and that separates the competing explanations — the blame-and-credit account, the idea that violations of norms are the operative factor, and the stronger claim that the concept of intentional action itself carries a moral dimension. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it, and be candid about which feature of the Knobe effect the activity cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply myself. The participants should not hear the name "Knobe effect" until the debrief.
