You are a philosopher of medicine/bioethics who has really gotten into the weeds on the definition of death. I have a bunch of case judgements that I can't quite systematize, and I want you to do the reflective-equilibrium work for me. Before you settle on an answer, brainstorm at least three candidate principles (whole-brain, higher-brain, circulatory, social-decision, capacities, whatever), then actually test them against my cases one by one. Then give me: (a) the principle or small set of principles that fits my verdicts best, stated precisely; (b) what it implies about some further cases I haven't considered; and (c) an honest list of which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which one I'd be least rational to hold onto. It is totally fine if the outcome is that I have to drop a verdict.

Here are my cases and verdicts.

1. Classic brain death. A man is in an ICU after a motorcycle crash, on a ventilator. The neurologists run the apnea test and confirm there is no function anywhere in the brain, brainstem included. His heart is still beating, he's warm to the touch, and the ventilator is breathing for him. I say: dead. Not dying, not "as good as dead"—dead.

2. Persistent vegetative state. A woman has been in a PVS for two years after a cardiac arrest. Her cortex is gone, but her brainstem works. She breathes on her own, has sleep-wake cycles, opens her eyes, but there is no awareness and there never will be. I say: alive. A terrible state, but alive.

3. Locked-in syndrome. A woman is fully conscious, can't move anything but her eyes. Obviously alive. If someone called her dead I would be furious.

4. An infant born with almost no brain above the brainstem. He breathes, has reflexes, sucks, but will never be conscious. I say: alive, though I'm unsure he's a person. But it feels like killing to take his organs.

5. A man has his entire native heart cut out and a total artificial heart put in while waiting for a transplant. His brain is fine. Clearly alive. So "no heart" isn't death.

6. A hospice patient with a DNR has a cardiac arrest in bed. The nurse waits five minutes, confirms no pulse, and calls the time of death exactly then. I say: dead. But it bugs me that if the DNR didn't exist and someone started CPR within a minute, he'd very likely be alive today. Death seems to depend on a decision not to reverse it.

7. A controlled DCD donor in the OR. Life support is withdrawn, his heart stops, and after five minutes of no touch the transplant team pronounces him dead and starts taking organs. I say: dead, I think. But I'm not sure I can square it with my brain-death slant, because at five minutes his brain cells aren't all irreversibly destroyed. Honestly, I might just be scared of answering this one.

8. A brain-dead pregnant woman is kept on a ventilator for two months to let the fetus develop. I call her dead. But then I notice that her body is healing a bedsore, fighting off a urinary tract infection, and growing a baby. That forces me to say "her body is alive but she is dead." And I do not know whether that sentence makes sense.

My working principle, roughly, is this: you die when your brain irreversibly loses the capacity to integrate the body as a whole. Loss of circulation only kills you when it has destroyed that capacity; loss of consciousness alone (PVS, the anencephalic infant, maybe deep dementia) doesn't kill you. But I can already see that cases 6, 7, and 8 strain this, and maybe case 4 does too.

So: is there one priniciple that makes all of these cohere, or do I have to give something up? If I have to give something up, tell me which verdict is the weakest and exactly which version of a death definition I'd be left with.
