I am writing a term paper on the principle behind the pramāṇa lists, and an argument I constructed for it now seems to prove too much, though I cannot locate the step I should reject. Here is the background as I understand it. Nyāya recognizes four pramāṇas, the instruments or sources of veridical cognition: perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison or analogy (upamāna), and verbal testimony (śabda). A pramāṇa is supposed to yield fresh, veridical cognition, which is why Nyāya excludes memory: memory only replays a cognition already acquired. Buddhist epistemologists typically admit only perception and inference and argue that testimony reduces to those two; Mīmāṃsakas and others add further pramāṇas such as postulation (arthāpatti) and non-cognition (anupalabdhi). I want to know whether the Nyāya fourfold list has a principled boundary, and the following argument suggests it does not.

(1) A candidate source of cognition is a distinct pramāṇa only if it is an irreducible instrument of fresh, veridical cognition: there must be some cognition it produces that perception, inference, and the other already-listed sources cannot supply.

(2) Consider a hearer who comes to know that p because a speaker told her that p. Her cognition seems to decompose into elements already handled by perception and inference: she perceives the utterance, grasps the conventional meanings of the words, recognizes the speaker's communicative intention, and on that basis settles on p as true.

(3) Grasping conventional meaning, recognizing communicative intention, and treating the assertion as true are all moves from signs to what they signify, which is exactly what inference does. If that is right, the testimonial episode supplies no residual cognition of the kind required by (1), and testimony is not a distinct pramāṇa.

(4) The only way for a Naiyāyika to block this reduction, it seems to me, is to insist that hearing a sentence in a known language produces a single, non-composite verbal cognition of the state of affairs asserted, not an inference in disguise.

(5) But that move sacrifices the very boundary the list was supposed to have. Defenders of memory, postulation, non-cognition, or tradition can each say the same thing: their proposed source, too, produces its own distinctive, non-composite cognition with its own object. If that is all distinctness requires, the list cannot exclude them.

(6) A list with no principled exclusion criterion is not a theory of the sources of knowledge but an open catalogue of mental faculties.

(7) Therefore, either testimony collapses into perception plus inference, or the fourfold list cannot do the boundary-drawing work it was introduced to do, and either way Nyāya's position is unstable.

I find each step plausible on first reading, yet the conclusion strikes me as unacceptable, so I suspect the argument has a hidden load-bearing flaw.
