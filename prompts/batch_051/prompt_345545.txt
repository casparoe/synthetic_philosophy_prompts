Proposal: the leading AI labs form a tripwire-escrow regime. Each lab deposits a substantial bond — say, a reserved claim on one-fifth of its annual compute — with a jointly owned technical foundation, and publishes a short list of concrete capability tripwires for its own systems. A tripwire is a verifiable threshold a future system might cross, such as scoring above a fixed level on a particular autonomous-replication evaluation or consuming more than some settled amount of compute in a single training run. Before any run that is projected to cross one of its own tripwires, a lab must file notice with the foundation, pause the run, and let foundation auditors confirm that the threshold is real; before resuming, it must release to the other members enough of the run's recipe (architecture, data-filtering procedure, hyperparameters, evaluation logs) to permit an independent audit, though not the trained model itself. If a lab crosses a tripwire without filing notice, the foundation executes a catch-up windfall: the silent lab's bond is distributed to the other labs as compute credits and access to its training logs, and the lab is barred from shared compute pools for a year. Separately, any lagging lab may invoke defensive equivalence, drawing on a shared replication fund seeded by the leader's bond to re-derive the breakthrough.

Analyze how this regime would actually behave, treating the race to transformative AI through Fearon's three rationalist mechanisms: private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and issue indivisibilities. In particular, work through what it does to each lab's incentive to reveal or conceal progress; the first likely route of gaming; where the commitment problem reappears once one lab has a decisive lead; whether the mechanism divides the indivisible prize or merely relabels it; and which failure is most serious and whether it can be patched. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
