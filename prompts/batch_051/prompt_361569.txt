Juries are the one place where a modern state puts coercive power in the hands of citizens chosen by lot. A randomly selected panel can, after deliberation behind closed doors, send a person to prison for decades; in common-law systems its verdict on the facts is effectively final and no reasons are published; and no one campaigns to become a juror. The same state insists that the much broader power to legislate must pass through elections, party platforms, and re-election. Meanwhile, citizens' assemblies of the past two decades — randomly selected panels on constitutional design and climate policy — produce recommendations that are treated as optional input. Reconstruct the theory of political legitimacy this two-track practice presupposes: the implicit views of representation, political equality, competence, and accountability that make random selection legitimate for individual verdicts but not for general statutes. Distinguish carefully what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to be compatible with, and say where it is internally inconsistent and where it is simply underdetermined by any theory. Then tell me what would have to change institutionally if we inverted the presupposition and treated sortition, not election, as the canonical way to legitimate law-making.
