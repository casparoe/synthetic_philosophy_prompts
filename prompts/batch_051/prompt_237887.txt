I'm a retired physical chemist, and I've fairly recently started reading philosophy of mind. The debate that keeps snagging me is whether the brain is a computer. It occured to me that both sides seem to use 'computation' as if the word had sharp edges, but I've realised I don't actually know what physical system is supposed to count as computing and what's ruled out.

So here's my request. Give me a working definition of 'computation' (or 'computer', if you think that's the term carrying the weight) that sorts the following cases into yes, no and borderline without simply defining the brain in or out by fiat. My own rough intuitions: the calculator and the abacus clearly compute, the bucket of water clearly doesn't (or so I'd like), and the slide rule, thermostat and brain are the awkward ones. You don't have to respect my intuitions, but I want to see where the definition forces you to move.

The cases:
1. A pocket calculator adding 7 and 5.
2. An abacus being worked by a skilled operator to multiply two numbers.
3. A slide rule.
4. A thermostat's bimetallic strip switching the heating on as the room cools.
5. A human brain doing a bit of mental arithmetic.
6. A bucket of water left on a bench, where someone points out that the water molecules can, with enough ingenuity, be mapped onto the steps of some program or other.

I'm especially worried about case 6, because if any old physical system counts as computing, the claim that the brain computes stops being interesting.

Please reply as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Point 1 should state the definition. Points 2 to 4 should run the definition against the cases and say where it gives a surprising or unclear answer. Point 5 should tell me which of your classifications you're least confident about and why.
