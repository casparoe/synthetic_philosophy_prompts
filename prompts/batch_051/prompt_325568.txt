I am working on a feature about how neuroscience has unsettled the assumption that each human body houses exactly one conscious subject. Split-brain patients, whose corpus callosum has been surgically severed to treat otherwise intractable epilepsy, provide the standard case. When a picture is flashed to the left of a fixation point, a patient may verbally report seeing nothing while his left hand, which is controlled by the hemisphere that received the image, reaches out and selects the matching object; in experimental settings, the left hand can sometimes act on information that the patient's speech disavows. The demonstrations are no longer controversial, but their interpretation is.

One camp holds that when the hemispheres process conflicting material, the patient harbors two relatively independent streams of consciousness. On this view, the mute right hemisphere is not merely an unconscious processor; there is something it is like to be it, and something it is like to be the speaking left hemisphere, with no direct channel between them. The other camp holds that the patient remains a single subject whose experience has become fragmented or partly inaccessible to itself, and that talk of two minds mistakes a failure of integration for a multiplication of subjects.

I would like you to write a dialogue between two equally sophisticated proponents of these views, each treating the other as a serious interpreter of the evidence rather than a strawman. Please frame the discussion around a thought experiment I plan to use in the piece: a reversible implant that can gradually reduce signaling through the corpus callosum, from normal bandwidth to total functional disconnection, so that the transition from an ordinary integrated mind to whatever split-brain patients exhibit can be traversed in small steps. The interlocutors should respond to each other's actual arguments and press each other on the hard question: in such a graded transition, is there any fact of the matter about when a second stream of consciousness appears, and what sorts of evidence, behavioral, introspective, or neurophysiological, could show whether the unity of consciousness is all-or-nothing or a matter of degree?
