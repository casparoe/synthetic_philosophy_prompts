I've been reading rationalist blogs since the Sequences era, so the Jaynes/Cox line feels like part of my native firmware: probability theory isn't just a tool for dealing with random noise, it's the unique logic of plausible inference, and Cox's theorem is supposed to be the proof that any consistent reasoning engine has to be a Bayesian one. Every so often someone on the internet says something like "Bayes or bust," and I half-agree and half-wince. My day job is machine learning — I spend my time picking loss functions, setting up constraints, checking calibration, and staring at representation theorems — so I cant shake the feeling that Cox's theorem looks a lot like the representation theorems I already live with (Savage, von Neumann–Morgenstern): tell me your axioms first, and the theorem will dutifully tell you the only representations left are exactly the things the axioms were designed to license.

What I want is a translation plus a residue report. Restate the Cox/Jaynes position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use. Plausibility becomes a learned real-valued scoring function over propositions; the desiderata become constraints on an inference API (consistency no matter what order you condition in, monotonicity, the Boolean algebra of propositions being respected); the sum and product rules become the only update rules that don't blow up the objective. Then tell me exactly what does not survive the translation. Where did my ML vocabulary have to fudge the philosophy, and where does Cox's theorem itself lean on assumptions that are genuinely contested — real-valued plausibility, the differentiability or continuity assumptions, what happens with infinite sets of propositions, and the gap between merely being consistent and being rational?

I want the epistemic-hygiene version, not the slogan version: separate what Cox actually proved (under explicit, stated assumptions) from what the LessWrong/Jaynes memeplex usually appends, and don't present a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus among people who have actually studied this. If the honest verdict is that the theorem is a real result with real loopholes and the slogan oversells it, say that. If my ML translation is quietly importing assumptions rather than revealing the structure, say that too — I care more about the residue than about the translation being neat. Two-ish pages is fine.
