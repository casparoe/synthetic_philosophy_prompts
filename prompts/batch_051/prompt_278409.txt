I am a novelist, and I keep encountering the same way of treating generic claims in my workshop and in essays about social observation: that a generic sentence is a compressed statistical report. The idea is that "ducks lay eggs" is true because enough ducks lay eggs for the frequency to serve as the rounded-off meaning of the sentence, and that "mosquitoes carry malaria" is the same device applied more aggressively — true because the proportion of mosquitoes carrying the malaria parasite, while small, is high enough to be worth compressing into a sentence of that form. The analogy is made to do real work: it licenses a method for assessing generic claims — decide whether they are true or false by finding the underlying proportion and checking whether it clears a threshold of typicality or salience — and it is used to conclude that generic claims about human kinds or social groups in fiction and elsewhere are nothing but frequency claims, so that correcting them is a matter of correcting the arithmetic.

The trouble is that the numbers do not cooperate. "Ducks lay eggs" is not true in virtue of a majority of ducks laying eggs: drakes and ducklings never do, and the sentence remains true. "Mosquitoes carry malaria" is true of mosquitoes in general even though only a tiny minority of mosquitoes carry the parasite at any time; the sentence is the standard way to state a genuine danger, but the frequency is minuscule. Once both examples are in front of us, the statistical picture seems forced to say that "true" means a different threshold in each case, chosen after the fact.

I would like an audit of how far this analogy actually holds. Give me the correspondence table — what in the statistical picture is supposed to play the role of the generic's subject, its predicate, and its truth conditions — and then the places where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that matter for the intended use from the incidental ones. In particular, address which features of a statistical generalization the analogy quietly imports that generics lack, whether the licensed method — settle a generic by counting cases and comparing them to a threshold — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what a better picture of what generics do would be if the statistical one fails.
