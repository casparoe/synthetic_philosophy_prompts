I read a bioethics article that summarized clinical equipoise like this. A randomized trial is only ethical while the doctor running it is genuinely unsure which treatment is better. The moment she has even a weak hunch, randomizing patients means knowingly giving some of them what she believes is inferior care, so the trial is living on borrowed time. The article then said this is why most long comparitive-effectiveness trials are ethically shaky: an honest clinician will start to lean one way as data accumulates, and from then on she either has to stop the trial or pretend not to notice the trend. This felt a little too tidy to me, but it was a reputable magazine. Is this a fair summary of clinical equipoise? I'd like to know which parts track the actual concept, which are common conflations or overstatements, what someone who defends the standard framework would object to, and whether the conclusion about comparative-effectiveness trials actually follows from the accurate parts.
