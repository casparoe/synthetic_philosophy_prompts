I have been trying to decide whether a parallel I keep noticing is deep or merely verbal. In quantum mechanics, exchanging two particles of the same kind never yields a new state: the wavefunction is symmetric or antisymmetric under permutation, and identical particles obey Bose–Einstein or Fermi–Dirac statistics rather than classical Maxwell–Boltzmann counting. This looks to me like what a programmer would call value semantics: two immutable objects with exactly the same contents are interchangeable, and any program whose behavior depends on which of two such objects it has is defective, because there is no fact of the matter about which one it is. Both frameworks seem to discard primitive this-ness while keeping all relational and observational content. I would like a precise verdict on whether this is a genuine structural identity, a loose analogy, or a confusion. Does the quantum case really remove identity the way value semantics does, or does the weak discernibility of entangled particles—their being distinguished only by their relations—show that quantum particles retain a kind of identity the programming analogy cannot represent? State the mapping explicitly, say what would have to be true on both sides for anything to transfer, and identify at least one thing the analogy would lead one to expect that turns out false.
