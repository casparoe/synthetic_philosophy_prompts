I am a PhD student in decision theory, working on the normative force of the expected utility axioms, and I keep returning to a reconstructed version of the coherence argument for expected utility maximization that I have never seen fully spelled out in one place. I would like you to act as a decision theorist who knows both the formal results and the philosophical debates about them, and to think slowly about this. Here is the argument as I reconstruct it:

(1) If an agent's preferences violate any of the standard expected utility axioms (say, completeness, transitivity, continuity, or independence), there exists a set of lotteries and a sequence of small exchanges, each of which the agent strictly prefers at the time it is offered, that leaves the agent with an outcome strictly worse than the one they started with — a money pump.

(2) At each step in such a sequence, if the agent genuinely prefers one option to another, it is rationally permitted, and perhaps rationally required, to take the preferred option.

(3) An agent who is rationally required, at each step, to take a sequence of trades that leads with certainty to a worse outcome is incoherent in a way that rationality excludes.

(4) Therefore, rationality requires an agent's preferences to satisfy the expected utility axioms.

(5) By the representation theorem, a preference ordering satisfying those axioms (together with the usual structural conditions) can be represented as maximizing expected utility relative to some probability function p and some utility function u.

(6) Therefore, rationality requires maximizing expected utility.

My uneasiness is this: step (5) only gives us the existence of a p and a u constructed from the agent's own preferences, so step (6) looks as if it might be either tautological or normatively empty — it says only that every coherent preference ordering can be re-described as if it maximizes expected utility. On the other hand, I do not want to overcorrect, because the money pump argument itself seems to carry real normative force independently of the representation theorem. Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and under what reading of it. If it is valid, tell me whether the conclusion in (6) earns the substantive claim that rational agents should form their preferences and choices in the way the expected utility story describes, or whether something more modest is the most the argument can establish.
