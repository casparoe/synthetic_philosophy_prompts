I have a standing rule for inferring what people actually want, and I have started applying it to myself: behavior outranks testimony. If a friend says she wants to leave her job but has not updated a résumé in three years while repeatedly choosing to stay late, I conclude the stated preference is not her real one, or at least is not the one that governs her choices. If a company says safety is its top priority but its budget, promotions, and postmortem practices point elsewhere, I trust the allocation, not the slogan. And if I say I value something—exercise, reading, keeping in touch—but find myself never choosing it, I treat the statement as noise unless I can point to a concrete constraint that explains the gap. A colleague who works on inverse reinforcement learning tells me I am doing, informally and badly, what his reward models do; he also warns that the technique has known failure modes—an observer cannot easily distinguish “does not want X” from “wants X but has false beliefs about how to get it,” or “wants X but faces an unobserved constraint,” or “wants X only intermittently and time-inconsistently,” or “behaves strategically because someone is watching.” A friend says my rule is just a bias against first-person testimony dressed up as rigor. Is the rule defensible? If so, state the conditions under which behavior should override self-report, and give the disciplined version of the rule that avoids the naive version’s failures. If not, say exactly where it breaks and what should replace it.
