Hey, I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so forgive me if this is put badly. I've been reading about gene drives — like a selfish gene construct that spreads through a wild population, maybe to make invasive rats on islands infertile or to support some de-extinction project. The thing I can't get past is the irreversability. In my day job, an irreversible change (a migration with no down, rotating a signing key, deleting data you thought you didn't need) is scary but normal: you test it, bound the blast radius, keep backups, roll forward. But a gene drive has no rollback button, and theres no staging environment that accurately looks like production, because the whole point is that it propagates through the wild.

Part of me thinks irreversibility is just one parameter to price in, not a different kind of act. Another part thinks it changes the kind of thing you're doing, because you're not just changing the current state of the system, you're changing the system's own rewriting process, and you're making a choice for future generations who never get a say.

Could you write a dialogue between two people who hold those two sides and have them actually argue it? Like, one says we already do irreversible things to ecosystems all the time — dams, species introductions, extinctions we allow by inaction — so singling out gene drives is status quo bias dressed up as philosophy. The other says it's different because the intervention keeps running after you stop it, and the system you'd roll back to no longer exists; more like modifying the kernel than deleting a column. I don't need a verdict. Just make both characters smart and well-meaning, and make them respond to what the other actually says rather than talk past each other.
