Our philosophy department started a reading group on whether violence has declined, and after a year of meeting every other week it's somehow gotten less productive, not more. One half keeps citing Pinker, Better Angels of Our Nature style: the long-run data from nonstate societies through the World Wars and after shows violence falling, especially when you look at rates per capita. The other half says that's an artifact of counting. If you use absolute numbers, the twentieth century killed more people than any century before, just because there were more people around to kill and to be killed. And if you widen "violence" even a little—to famines, poverty-related deaths, car crashes, or structural violence—the decline can be made to soften or vanish depending on where you draw the line. Everyone has seen the same charts. Everyone grants the arithmetic. And yet nobody has moved an inch in months.

I'm less interested in who's right about Pinker than in what kind of stalemate this is. Is it a verbal dispute about the word "violence"? A disguised moral disagreement about whether per capita rates or absolute suffering is the currency that matters? An empirical dispute about missing data or bad baselines that could in principle be settled? Or is it a case where both sides are asking different questions under one heading—say, "is violence getting rarer" versus "is the modern world getting worse to live in"—so the argument can't resolve until the question is split? Given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would actually count as progress: a sharper distinction, a different metric, a particular piece of evidence, or an honest conclusion that there's no single fact of the matter until a metric is chosen, and choosing it is a normative act rather than an empirical discovery?

I have a hypothesis that the impasse is about whether "violence has declined" is one question or two, but I don't want to just assert that. Give me the diagnosis of the disagreement itself.
