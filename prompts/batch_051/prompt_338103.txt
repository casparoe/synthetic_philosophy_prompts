I've been toying with a principle I call the Leverage Half-Life Principle for thinking about the far future. The idea is that when we compare actions, we shouldn't just sum expected value over all downstream consequences. Instead we draw the baseline trajectory the world would be on if we did nothing, and only count expected improvements over that baseline up to what I call the leverage horizon: the point where the best available guess about whether our action still matters drops to chance, because later agents and institutions will re-optimize, compensate, or wash out the difference. Beyond that point I say we should treat the extra expected value as zero, not as some tiny but astronomically multiplied number. So helping one person today gets counted only as far as we can actually track its consequences, while reducing existential risk counts for much longer because its baseline is extinction and its leverage horizon is enormous. I'm hoping this steers between neartermism and the more wild-eyed longtermist sums without giving up on the far future. I'm aware it might just be cluelessness with extra steps. Is this coherent, is it already something people defend under another name, and what are the sharpest objections? Is there one that actually kills it rather than just making it awkward?
