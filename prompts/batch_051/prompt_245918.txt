Since a cousin of mine cut off her father, I have been trying to think clearly about forgiveness, and I ended up in a discussion group where two members, Amara and Tomas, went back and forth over the question whether forgiveness can be obligatory and whether it can be unconditional. I wrote down the exchange as best I could afterward. I am not taking sides; I want to understand who has which point right.

Amara opened with the case that forgiveness is a duty in many circumstances. She said that to forgive is fundamentally to forswear revenge and to refuse to let resentment harden into malice. On that understanding, she argued, forgiveness cannot be something we are free to withhold whenever we like; failing to forgive means letting a wrong take up residence in one’s character, and we are answerable for that. She added that none of this requires the wrongdoer to have repented, so forgiveness can be unconditional in the sense that it is owed even to the unrepentant.

Tomas replied that Amara had defined forgiveness too thinly. If forgiving is just forswearing revenge and moderating resentment, it collapses into ordinary self-control. Real forgiveness, he said, involves releasing a debt or offering renewed relation, and those things cannot be owed precisely because they are releases. An obligatory release is self-contradictory: if I am bound to cancel a debt, the debt was never really cancelled by my generosity. So on the thicker meaning, forgiveness cannot be obligatory or unconditional.

Amara then brought in Joseph Butler’s two sermons Upon Resentment and Forgiveness of Injuries, from his Fifteen Sermons Preached at the Rolls Chapel. She said Butler’s whole aim was to show that resentment is a natural and useful passion, not a sin to be extirpated, and that forgiveness consists in keeping resentment within its proper bounds: opposing injury without revenge and without malice. If that is what forgiveness is, she said, then it is clearly something we can be commanded to do, because we owe it to ourselves and others not to indulge destructive passions. Butler, she claimed, supports duty without making forgiveness depend on the wrongdoer.

Tomas countered by pointing to the same source. He argued that Butler is careful to say that resentment against present injury can be innocent, even proper, and that what is forbidden is its perversion into revenge. But if resentment remains legitimate whenever the wrong is ongoing or unacknowledged, then Butler has not licensed unconditional forgiveness; he has licensed proportionate disapproval under the name of forgiveness. Tomas said this shows the thicker concept Amara first resisted is doing the real work, and Butler quietly trades on it.

Amara answered that Tomas was conflating two distinct things. To forgive, she said, is to give up the specific claim of personal vengeance and the desire to harm the offender as such. It does not require pretending the offender is safe, approving of what was done, or reconciling. One can forgive unconditionally and still maintain appropriate distance, warn others, and support fair penalties. She called this the distinction between forgiveness as a moral act of the victim and reconciliation as a further relation that legitimately requires confession and change.

Tomas responded that the distinction is unstable. If the victim may still withdraw trust, still pursue just punishment, and still describe the wrong, then what was actually released by this so-called forgiveness? The wrong has two components: the offence and the broken relation. You cannot unilaterally remove the offence, because it is already in the past. The broken relation you cannot unilaterally repair, because it takes two. So unilateral forgiveness, he said, threatens to become a purely mental operation of renouncing feelings, which is neither clearly a duty nor clearly forgiveness.

They then reached a standstill over Butler’s use of the precept to love one’s enemies. Amara took it to support the idea that agapic benevolence can be owed to all, including the unrepentant. Tomas said Butler himself distinguishes kinds of love: willing the other’s eventual good is one thing, while delighting in them or trusting them is another, and only the first can be commanded unconditionally. That, he said, saves the coherence of the command precisely by narrowing its content, which is not the same as commanding reconciliation.

Please go through their exchange move by move and say which specific points each has right, which arguments fail, where an ambiguity in the word forgiveness is doing the heavy lifting, and which of the two has the better case overall on the stated question: can forgiveness be obligatory, and can it be unconditional? As you do so, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, and do not present a contested reading as if it were the established consensus. At the very end, give one line in the form \answer{...} containing one of: Amara is more right, Tomas is more right, or they are roughly equally right.
