I got stuck on this after watching one of those true crime shows where a guy admitted to an assault from the early eighties, and the police basically said there was nothing they could do because the statute of limitations had run out. But for murder, in a lot of places, there is no clock at all. So the mere fact that time passed completely erases one serious wrong and does nothing to another. It's not even just about severity, since some frauds seem to have longer limitation periods than some violent attacks, and the rules vary so much from place to place that it can't be that we've all discovered the same underlying principle.

The more I think about it, the less I can tell whether there's a moral idea underneath this or whether it's just practical compromise stacked on habit. If time really changes the moral situation — because the person has changed, or the harm has faded, or society has moved on — then why doesn't that apply to the murderer who hid for forty years and has been a quiet neighbor ever since? And here's the asymmetry that bothers me most: a person who gets identified the day after the limitation period ends is untouchable, while someone identified the day before faces the full punishment. The only difference is one day. I realize that might sound legalistic, but I keep wanting to say that if the crime still mattered yesterday, it doesn't suddenly stop mattering today.

I want someone to actually defend this, or tell me to give it up. If you defend statutes of limitations, defend them against that one-day case, not just with the usual stuff about faded memories (which dosn't apply when there's a confession). If you'd abolish them, say what we do with very old cases where witnesses are dead and everyone involved has rebuilt their life. Please write in British English, and end with a last line that starts with Verdict: followed by keep, reform, or abolish and a short phrase.
