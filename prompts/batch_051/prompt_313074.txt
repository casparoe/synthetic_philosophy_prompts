I'm running a two-session workshop for first-year mathematics PhD students on how they read proofs, and I want to open by sorting participants according to what they actually take understanding a proof to consist in, before any labels are introduced. Please design a decision tree with seven to nine branch nodes. Each branch node should pose a concrete, answerable case about a proof, never a request for a definition and never using the vocabulary of the positions; a student should be able to answer it without knowing the philosophical labels. Build the tree from the logical space first—let the questions and the combinations of answers carve out the positions—and only then attach names to the leaves. The leaves should be the following positions: (i) the derivation-checking view, on which understanding just is verifying that every step is licensed by an accepted rule of inference; (ii) the explanatory-structure view, on which understanding additionally requires grasping the mathematical reason the theorem is true, typically a structural or conceptual feature of which the steps are the consequence; (iii) the constructive view, on which understanding an existence proof requires being able to extract a procedure or witness, so a non-constructive proof may be accepted but is not understood; (iv) the generative-transfer view, on which understanding is measured by what the proof lets one do, such as modifying hypotheses and proving a novel analogue; and (v) the social-fluency view, on which understanding is the ability to perform the proof competently by the standards of a mathematical community, including explaining it to a peer and seeing where an altered argument would break.

Use at least these cases as branch nodes, reworded as questions about the case: a short proof a student can reproduce perfectly but cannot say why it works; a non-constructive existence proof by contradiction; a proof verified only by machine, for which the student has read only the certificate that no error was found; an elegant proof the student claims to understand but cannot adapt once a hypothesis is weakened; a student who has never read a proof of a theorem but applies it fluently to new problems; and a proof a student insists they understand but cannot explain to a peer. If two leaves cannot be separated by any question the tree reasonably contains, flag the pair and say which further case would separate them. If a logically possible combination of answers is occupied by no named position, mark it empty and note in one sentence whether that is because the resulting view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Each leaf should carry the position's name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
