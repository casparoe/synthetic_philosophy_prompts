Longtime LW reader here. I keep running into the line "history is written by the victors" in rationalist-adjacent arguments, and I'm starting to think it's doing several different jobs at once. Sometimes it's treated like a boring empirical observation about who edits the textbooks; sometimes it's treated like a deep claim about how power shapes what counts as historical knowledge; sometimes it's used as a blanket get-out-of-jail-free card for doubting any standard account of the past. Last week I watched someone invoke it to end an argument about Cold War historiography. The disputed claim was whether statements like "the Soviet Union was always going to collapse" are just the hindsight bias / narrative-fallacy thing doing its usual work, or whether they can be legitimate counterfactual judgments about structural weakness. The person said, roughly: of course it looks inevitable in hindsight, history is written by the victors. I honestly couldn't tell what work the slogan was supposed to be doing there, or whether it actually supports the claim it was being used to support.

So, acting as an actual philosopher of history or epistemologist (whichever framing you find more useful), think carefully and brainstorm first, then seperate out the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be making — from the trivially true to the near-conspiratorial — and for each say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or just false. Then tell me which reading(s) would genuinely support the use in that hindsight-bias argument, and which reading you suspect most people think they're invoking when they drop the line. American English, please.
