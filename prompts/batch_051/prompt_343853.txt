Every year my school runs a semester-long student business competition, and this year I am the treasurer of one of the teams. Last week I gave another team a lecture built entirely on what my economics teacher calls the outside view: almost no student business has broken even in the last decade, the same three mistakes sink most of them, and their claim that a clever marketing plan made them different was exactly the story every failed team tells itself. An hour later I was sitting with my own group, explaining why our case really is different — we have a niche product, a pricing strategy, and an unusually committed advisor. The asymmetry was hard to miss. If someone presented my team's plan to me as another team's plan, I would predict failure; but as a member of the team, I predict we will beat the base rate. I seem to hold myself to a far lower standard of epistemic modesty than I hold other people.

Is there a principle that justifies this asymmetry, rather than merely explaining it? The obvious candidate is that I possess private information about my own team — our effort, our conversations, the details of our plan — that I do not have about other teams, and that this entitles me to give the inside view more weight than a base rate. But every team inside its own situation possesses that same kind of private information, and the base rate is composed of teams exactly like mine, each of which believed its own details mattered. I want the strongest principle you can find that makes it rational to judge one's own plans differently from equivalent plans made by others, and an honest verdict on whether it holds up — or whether epistemic modesty requires that I hold myself to the same base rate I impose on everyone else.
