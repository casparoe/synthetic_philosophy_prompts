I am putting together a syllabus for an advanced high-school elective called "Debts and Devotion," which pairs a work of literature with a problem in moral philosophy each month. Next month we will read King Lear alongside the question of what adult children owe their parents, and I want to frame the unit with Jane English's friendship model of filial duty. On that model, grown children owe their parents nothing merely as repayment for having been raised; being cared for in childhood does not create a standing moral debt. Whatever obligations exist arise, as obligations do between close friends, from the present quality of the relationship—affection, trust, honesty, mutual concern—rather than from gratitude for past sacrifice. The vocabulary of indebtedness, on this view, systematically misdescribes what a good adult relationship between parent and child looks like.

I would like a critical reading of Lear through this lens rather than a summary of the play, written so I can hand it to students before we stage several scenes. Where does the friendship model genuinely illuminate what happens in the play—for example, Lear's opening demand that his daughters convert their love into a competitive public declaration of what they owe him, or Goneril and Regan's later reduction of his household to an accounted number of knights? And where does the play push back against the model or expose its limits? I am especially interested in Cordelia's refusal to perform affection even though she acts steadfastly, in Edgar's care for the blinded Gloucester, and in whether the tragedy is equally hard on children who treat a parent as a burdensome dependent and on a parent who treats love as a debt. Does Lear himself operate with a debt model of filial life, and does the play ultimately vindicate that model, dismantle it, or force us to see what is lost when it collapses? Conclude by saying what the play adds to the philosophical debate about filial duty that an argument alone cannot capture. Please write in American English.
