Consider the golden rule in its familiar positive form: an instruction to treat other people as you yourself would wish to be treated. A long-standing objection is that it takes the agent's own preferences as the standard of good treatment, when what actually matters is what the action is like for the other person. A second, more practical difficulty is that people are often mistaken, sometimes self-servingly, about how their conduct feels from the receiving end.

Now imagine a counterfactual. From the first of next month, every neurologically intact adult acquires a new capacity: whenever a person is on the verge of acting in a way that will materially affect a particular other person, he or she briefly but vividly senses how that action is likely to feel to the other party from the inside — its pleasures, pains, humiliations, reassurances, frustrations included. The impression is generally reliable though not infallible, it arises only during live decisions and is forward-looking, and it carries no motivational force on its own; a person remains entirely free to brush it aside and act as before. Nothing else about human nature, social institutions, or technology changes.

On this supposition, predict in detail how the next twenty years play out for ordinary friendships and family life, for bargaining in markets and workplaces, and for the criminal law and everyday practices of blame and punishment. Set out your reasoning link by link, and indicate at each step whether the effect is robust or depends on a further assumption — for example, about whether vivid insight into another person's suffering reliably moves people, or about how quickly norms adjust. Finally, say whether this capacity would fix the preference-standard problem in the golden rule, or merely make the problem more precise and harder to ignore. Please write your answer in British English.
