I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, currently writing a chapter on idealization in economic and physical modeling, and I'd like a consistency check on the methodological position I seem to have arrived at. My commitments are these: (1) the primary test of a model is its predictive success, not the realism of its assumptions; (2) in line with Friedman's F-twist, whether a model's assumptions are literally true is largely irrelevant so long as the model tracks the behavior it is meant to capture; (3) idealizations are nevertheless not all on a par — ideal gases and frictionless planes strike me as virtuous, while representative-agent assumptions or strong rationality axioms in macrofinance strike me as suspect; (4) explanation requires more than prediction, so a model that predicts well but supplies no intelligible mechanism should not count as explanatory; and (5) robustness of a result across different idealizations is positive evidence for that result. Does this package hang together? I want to know which commitments are in genuine tension, which are only apparently in tension, and what the least costly revision would be. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing all of them as equally serious, and be specific about what has to give.
