So I got into a long argument with a friend last night about whether natural selection is actually explanatory, and I've been turning it over since without finding the exit. He kept pushing a version of the tautology objection, and by the end of the night I couldn't see what was wrong with the way he set it up. We got it down to this. (1) If fitness is just actual reproductive success, then saying the fittest survive just says the survivors survive, which explains nothing. (2) So the propensity interpretation has to be doing the real work: fitness is a probabilistic capacity to survive and reproduce, not the outcome itself. (3) But a capacity like that isn't directly observable—all we ever see are outcomes, births and deaths and offspring counts. (4) So after the fact, whenever a trait spreads we can say those organisms were fitter, and whenever it doesn't we can say their fitness was lower, or the environment shifted, or we were measuring at the wrong level. (5) If that's right, the propensity version of selection makes no risky predictions—whatever actually evolves comes out compatible with the theory once we've redescribed fitness. (6) But a theory compatible with every possible outcome isn't an empirical theory. (7) So the propensity interpretation saves natural selection from being an empty tautology only by emptying it out the back door.

I don't want to accept (7), but I also can't tell which of (1) through (6) is actually false rather than just painful to give up. Which step should I reject, and what do I have to pay? For what it's worth, I'd sooner give up (5) or (6) than (2), since (2) feels like the only non-circular account of fitness I've seen. But I want to know the real cost of each move, not just what I can live with.
