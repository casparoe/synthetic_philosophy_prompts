Use the decision by national governments in late February and early March 2020 about whether to impose COVID-19 lockdowns as a case study for decision under ignorance, analyzed through maximin, minimax regret, and the Rawls–Harsanyi disagreement about choice behind a veil of ignorance.

Set the case this way. In late February and early March 2020, officials had to choose between stringent, economy-wide restrictions and a wait-and-see approach. The decision was made without settled estimates of the infection fatality rate, the asymptomatic fraction of transmission, or the marginal effect of particular restrictions; Italian hospitals were already overwhelmed, and early worst-case projections without mitigation ran from several hundred thousand to over a million deaths in the United States over the course of the epidemic if nothing was done, with similarly large figures for other countries. Do not treat this as a historical research question: work from the facts as stated and from the decision rules themselves, and do not rely on citations.

For each of three decision rules — maximin (choose the policy whose worst outcome is best), minimax regret (choose the policy that minimizes the maximum shortfall from what you could have achieved), and the veil-of-ignorance debate between Rawls's anti-probabilistic maximin and Harsanyi's expected-utility treatment with equal probabilities over positions — do the following. Identify the decision-maker, lay out the options and a plausible payoff matrix on the information available at the time, and state which choice the rule recommends. Then reverse the direction: say which features of the case each rule captures and which it misdescribes or is silent about.

Be explicit about four issues. First, whether this was genuine ignorance rather than ordinary risk, and which facts would have had to be knowable for that distinction to matter in practice. Second, who the positions behind the veil would be, and how telling the analogy between constitutional choice and emergency pandemic policy actually is. Third, how much of the dispute was best understood as moral disagreement about whose losses count and how much rather than empirical disagreement about likely outcomes. Fourth, how a static decision rule should handle the option of waiting for better information when waiting itself has asymmetric and partly irreversible costs.

Conclude with what the case shows about maximin, minimax regret, and Harsanyi's critique of Rawls: does the case confirm each rule, expose it as an anomaly, or fall outside what it was designed to decide? Finally, say which of the rules, if any, you would defend for this kind of policy choice, and under what conditions.
