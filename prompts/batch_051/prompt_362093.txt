An argument that keeps resurfacing in the mixed philosophy-and-machine-learning reading group I'm part of is whether the falsehoods large language models produce are best described as lies, as bullshit, or as straightforward errors, and after two years the three camps have settled into a stalemate. Everyone accepts the underlying facts about how these systems are trained and that they sometimes emit confident falsehoods, but nobody moves. The first camp invokes Harry Frankfurt's distinction, roughly that the liar deliberately asserts what he takes to be false, while the bullshitter is defined less by an intent to deceive than by indifference to truth; on this view a language model that generates fluent text without any concern for truth is a bullshit machine, and calling its output a lie attributes too much intent, while calling it a mere error attributes too much truth-aiming. The second camp replies that this is anthropomorphic: a system that cannot care about truth cannot be indifferent to it either, so it is neither bullshitter nor truth-aimer; its false outputs are errors only in the engineering sense of failing against the product's specification, not in the epistemic sense of a judgment that misses. The third camp says both sides have picked the wrong locus, because the model's output by itself is no more a deceptive utterance than a broken thermometer is a liar, but the people and institutions that deploy a known-confabulating system while presenting its output as authoritative can still be criticized in roughly the way we criticize liars, and focusing on the model lets the humans off the hook.

My working theory is that the impasse is partly verbal — the three camps mean different things by error and even by bullshit, which sometimes labels the model's internal relation to truth and sometimes the way the whole human-machine system treats its users — and partly a substantive disagreement about whether a language model's outputs are assertions at all, and about where in a distributed system speech acts and responsibility should be located. I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is rather than a verdict about which camp is right, together with an account of what would actually count as progress: a distinction that dissolves it, an empirical finding about the system, a decisive case, or an argument that the question is ill-posed.
