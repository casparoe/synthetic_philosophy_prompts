I'm trying to get clear on what actually does the work in arguments about reparations for old wrongs, and I'd like a case built to order rather than the usual hand-wavy ones. The two views I'm pitting against each other are these. Call the first the counterfactual-advantage view: a living person owes reparations just in case she is materially better off than the person she would have been had the original wrong never occurred, and the size of the obligation tracks the size of that gap, regardless of how the advantage got to her. Call the second the traceable-benefit view: a living person owes only for specific, identifiable fruits of the wrong that are still in her hands or still flowing to her—the stolen farm, the inherited lease income, the company shares originally bought with wrongfully taken capital—not for being better off in some diffuse, overall way. Set aside the question of who is owed; assume there are living descendants of the wronged family who are clearly worse off than they would have been, and that any payment would go to them.

What I want you to construct is a matched pair of ordinary, realistic cases growing out of the same original wrong—say, a piece of land taken from one family and granted to another a few generations back. In both cases the current descendant on the beneficiary side is a lot better off than she would have been without the taking, and I'd like the size of that advantage held as close to equal as the story allows, so nobody can say the two views come apart only because one advantage is bigger. The single thing that should differ is the channel through which the advantage traveled. In Case A it runs through a discrete asset that descends directly from the taking and is still in her hands. In Case B the same value was converted long ago, passed through generations of education, business connections, and social standing, and is now baked into her income and opportunities with no identifiable asset left to point to. The intended verdicts: both views say she owes in Case A; in Case B the counterfactual-advantage view still says she owes, while the traceable-benefit view says she owes nothing.

The pair is for a paper on whether traceability is doing any independent moral work in reparations claims, so cheating would be smuggling a discrete asset into Case B after all, letting the two advantages differ in size, or just insisting that the advantage is baked in without making the causal path from the taking to her present position plausible. Give me the two scenarios—a short paragraph each is fine—then run both views over both cases and show exactly why they agree in A and split in B. Close with an honest note: which single feature of Case B does the real work, what happens to the pair if that feature is removed, and what is the strongest attack a defender of the traceable-benefit view would make against Case B—where would they say it smuggles its conclusion or begs the question?
