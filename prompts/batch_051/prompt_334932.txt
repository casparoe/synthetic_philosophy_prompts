Here is Aristotle, listing the markers of democracy in the Politics (1317b): "the following institutions are democratic in character: election of officials by all from all; government of each by all, and of all by each in turn; election by lot either to all magistracies or to all that do not need experience and skill"

In the centuries since, a different sortition proposal has emerged: not merely filling low-skill offices by lot, but constituting a standing citizens' assembly — or a second chamber — entirely by lot, and giving it real authority, such as setting the legislative agenda on long-term risks, triggering binding referendums, or vetoing ordinary legislation. Its advocates claim that randomly selected citizens, supplied with expert testimony and structured deliberation, can competently handle matters elected legislatures handle poorly.

Predict Aristotle's verdict on this proposal. Would he take it to extend the principle stated in the passage, or to violate the clause "do not need experience and skill"? Reconstruct his likeliest position and its reasoning from the Politics, drawing on his account of the deliberative, executive, and judicial functions in a constitution and his case for the collective judgment of the many. Then identify the single premise of his that a modern lottocrat would most need to reject for the proposal to work, and explain what rejecting it would do to the rest of his scheme.
