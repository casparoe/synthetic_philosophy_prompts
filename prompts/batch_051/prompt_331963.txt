A friend and I recently spent an evening arguing about the gender-equality paradox, and I came away from the conversation sure of one thing: neither of us could state what evidence would actually settle our disagreement. The finding he kept pressing on me was that countries ranking highest on standard indices of gender equality — Finland, Norway, and Sweden are the usual examples — also show larger average differences between men's and women's educational and occupational preferences than many countries that score far lower on those same indices. His inference was direct: once legal and institutional barriers are removed, he argued, people become free to express the preferences they already had, so the widening gap is evidence that the differences are at least partly natural rather than socially produced. I resisted that inference, but I could not say what would show him to be wrong — or, for that matter, what would force me to concede he is right.

I would like you to design the strongest possible test of the claim that the paradox reflects natural or endogenous differences rather than residual social causes. Be concrete: specify the comparisons, the measurements, the populations, and the pattern of results that would count for and against the inference. Do not satisfy me with "correlation is not causation" or a list of methodological problems with the existing studies; I want the best version of the test, even if it would be difficult or expensive to run. Then say precisely what the test would and would not settle. In particular, suppose the test came out in favor of a substantial non-social component: what would and would not follow about nature, culture, and justice, and what would remain open even in that outcome? Finally, tell me what would change your mind — which result or consideration would lead you to a different verdict — and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
