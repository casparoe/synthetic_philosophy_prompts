I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest publication, and a few times a year I write pieces that draw on a historical scientific or mathematical text — a seventeenth-century optics treatise, an eighteenth-century algebra paper, a pre-Darwinian natural history essay. I'm usually working on a one-week deadline, with a fact-checker who can verify quotations but no historian of science on staff, and my editor signs off on the final framing. The risk I keep facing is anachronism: using a modern term or framing that quietly credits an old author with a concept, question, or distinction that is actually a later achievement, and so misleads the reader. Please give me a reusable editorial procedure, to run before anything goes to my editor, for deciding when a modern formulation is a legitimate translation for a contemporary reader and when it is a distortion that should be removed or explicitly flagged. Structure it as an ordered sequence of questions I can put to any passage, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure forces a stop or a rewrite, and where it has to hand the decision back to judgment because no checklist can settle it. Then run the procedure on three recurring cases, choosing a concrete historical instance for each: (1) a passage where I'm tempted to say an old author 'anticipated' a modern result; (2) a passage where a modern term seems harmless because it is now ordinary vocabulary; and (3) a passage where the old text's word is the same as ours but its meaning has shifted. At least one of these should come out against the phrasing I would have been inclined to use, and the procedure should finish by stating what it cannot detect.
