Next week I am presenting a paper to an analytic philosophy of religion research seminar on the argument against accepting testimony for miracles in the chapter on miracles in Hume's An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding. My thesis is that the argument is best reconstructed not as the crude inductive claim that uniform past experience of nature's regularities simply outweighs human testimony, but as an equilibrium condition on rational testimony-based belief: one should accept a miracle report only when rejecting the testimony would require a greater departure from one's background beliefs than accepting the reported violation of a natural law would. I argue that this reading is textually better than the crude inductive reading because it explains why Hume sets out a threshold test — no testimony suffices to establish a miracle unless the falsehood of that testimony would itself be more miraculous than the event it reports — rather than simply declaring testimony for miracles always defeated. The paper then draws out an uncomfortable consequence. On my reconstruction, the threshold cannot in practice be crossed by any improvement in the internal quality of the testimony alone, no matter how numerous, disinterested, or mutually corroborating the witnesses are, because any testimonial evidence for a miracle is itself evidence only through the same past experience of human reliability, so testimony cannot by itself purchase a higher grade of warrant than the regularities it is invoked against. The comparison is anchored by a background belief in the uniformity of nature that the miracle report itself puts in question. I concede that consequence rather than soften it, and I claim it is a feature of Hume's empiricism, not a defect of my reading.

The audience: an analytic philosophy of religion seminar, mostly epistemologists of testimony and philosophers of religion, with two Bayesian formal epistemologists who know the main Bayesian criticisms of Hume's argument and are hostile to quick dismissals of it, plus two Wittgenstein-influenced members who will find the formal apparatus distorting. Several members are theists who accept that the Biblical miracle reports fail Hume's test but think the test itself is question-begging.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the objections ordered by how likely they are to be raised and how damaging they are, phrased the way this particular audience would phrase them. For each, give a two-minute reply I can deliver and a note on where that reply is weakest. Include the objection I should most hope nobody asks, and say why it would hurt.

Please write the answer in British English.
