Augustine, in his treatise *On Grace and Free Will* (426/427), writes:

Now He has revealed to us, through His Holy Scriptures, that there is in a man a free choice of will. But how He has revealed this I do not recount in human language, but in divine. There is, to begin with, the fact that God's precepts themselves would be of no use to a man unless he had free choice of will, so that by performing them he might obtain the promised rewards.

The move embedded in the passage can be stated as the claim that a command with an attached promised reward can be a genuine precept for an agent only if the agent has free choice of will with respect to complying; otherwise the command would be useless. This is one of the hinge points on which later Calvinist and Arminian treatments of inability and responsibility turn.

I want to know whether that claim, or its most tractable core, can be tested rather than merely asserted from a prior theological commitment. Design the best concrete test you can of the claim (or of the central psychological component of it). Do not build the conclusion into the test by defining “precept” or “genuinely commanded” as effective only under free choice. Specify what observable result would count in favor of the claim, what result would count against it, and why. Then say clearly what your test could not settle even in principle—for example, whether the divine-precept case is sui generis, or whether empirical findings about human command-following transfer to Augustine’s theological inference. Throughout, state how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of. End with a one-sentence summary as the final line.
