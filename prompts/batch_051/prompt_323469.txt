I have been working with a distinction I cannot find named in the literature, and I would like it assessed candidly. I call it the difference between structural and concessive exceptions to a generic. A structural exception is an instance that fails the predicate but is already built into the kind's own organization. The drakes and ducklings that do not lay eggs do not threaten the truth of "ducks lay eggs," because the duck kind is sexually dimorphic and age-structured; the generic tracks the reproductive structure of the kind rather than how each individual duck behaves. A concessive exception is different: most members of the kind lack the property, yet the property is still assigned to the kind as a capacity. "Mosquitoes carry malaria" is accepted even though only a tiny minority of individual mosquitoes carry the parasite, because being a malaria vector is treated as a property of the kind rather than a statistical fact about its members.

My provisional view is that both patterns are versions of a single rule: a generic is acceptable just in case the property can be attributed to the kind as a structured whole — either because the kind's architecture distributes the property across a designated part (a sex, a life stage, a caste) or because the property is a kind-level capacity, however rarely manifested. If that is right, raw prevalence among members should be neither necessary nor sufficient for the truth of a generic.

I am uncertain whether the distinction is genuine. Do structural and concessive exceptions really come apart, or does the second collapse into familiar ideas about striking properties or principled exceptions? Are there generics that force a third kind of exception-tolerance — and would something like "sharks attack bathers" or "pit bulls are dangerous" be the cleanest counterexamples? And is the underlying rule defensible, or does it overgenerate by letting any striking property be packaged as a kind-level capacity?
