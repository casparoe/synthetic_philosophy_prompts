Interpersonal utility comparisons are the original sin of cost-benefit analysis: you can add up everyone's money numbers, but it's not obvious that my gain is bigger than your pain in any real sense. Sidgwick, in The Methods of Ethics, basically says the satisfactions of different people live in separate consciousnesses and can't be placed directly on one scale, but he doesn't provide a clean way around the problem. Rank these candidate fixes from most to least defensible as a foundation for policy, with a sentence of reasoning for each. (1) Edgeworth-style 'least perceptible pleasure increment' as a common unit. (2) Behavioral proxies like equivalent income, QALYs, or subjective well-being scales. (3) Harsanyi-style empathic preferences behind a thick veil of ignorance. (4) Sen-style partial comparability: maybe we can only say some comparisons are clear, others not. (5) Robbins-style position that science is done once you've listed preferences; the rest is a value judgment. (6) Direct cardinal neural/hedonic measurement, e.g. putting the relevant brain states on a common scale. Tier them and keep it under 250 words.
