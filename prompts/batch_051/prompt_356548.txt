I got into an argument at a local politics discussion group last week. Someone was pushing lottocracy—replace elections with bodies of ordinary citizens chosen by lot, like juries—and he kept saying a lottery-chosen body would "represent the public" in a way an elected one cant, because it's a random sample. I kept pressing him on whether that was an empirical claim or just a stipulative one: was he saying the sortition body would actually make decisions closer to what the public wants, or just that it would mirror the public's demographics and then he'd call that representation? He said "both," which felt like a dodge, but maybe I was being unfair.

I want to know how one would actually test the claim that sortition gives better representation than election. Design a concrete test: what would you compare (an actual citizens' assembly running alongside an elected council in the same city? a survey experiment? a simulation?), what would you measure, and what result would count as sortition beating election versus election beating sortition. Then do the harder part: say what the test would and would not settle. Suppose the lottery body tracked public opinion better than the elected body on some set of issues. Would that show it "represents" the public in the sense lottocrats actually care about, or only that random sampling gives you a good mirror? What about accountability, or the way elected officials anticipate being thrown out of office—if the test can't capture those, does that leave the lottocrat's conceptual claim mostly untouched, or does it show the claim was never cleanly testable to begin with?
