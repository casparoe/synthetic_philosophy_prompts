I'm a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of art seminar, and we have spent the last several weeks on aesthetic testimony. The debate has settled into a fixed shape, and I have begun to suspect that the reason nobody moves is not that the arguments on either side are weak but that the two sides are not engaging the same question. The pessimists hold that an aesthetic judgment cannot legitimately rest on another person's say-so: to judge that a work is graceful, or derivative, or beautiful, one must encounter the work oneself, because the judgment is meant to express one's own responsiveness to the object, and a borrowed verdict provides no such basis. The optimists reply that we constantly and rationally rely on others in aesthetic matters — we believe that a film is worth seeing or that a novel is badly plotted on the word of a trusted critic or friend — and that a rule forbidding this would make nonsense of ordinary aesthetic practice. What puzzles me is that the two sides appear to grant the same examples. Both accept that I can learn from testimony that a gallery opens at ten; both accept that hearing someone say that the aria was moving is not the same as being moved by the aria. Yet the disagreement survives every concession either side makes.

My working hypothesis is that the phrase aesthetic judgment is doing double duty: one side is talking about the act of appreciating or evaluating something for oneself, while the other is talking about the epistemic standing of a propositional belief about an object's aesthetic properties. But I can also see rival diagnoses: a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether aesthetic properties are response-dependent, a hidden empirical question about how people actually form aesthetic beliefs, or a substantive dispute about whether aesthetic knowledge carries a special first-person requirement. I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Say what kind of disagreement this really is, supporting the diagnosis with what each side actually maintains, and say what would count as progress given that diagnosis — whether a distinction, a change of question, a piece of empirical work, or an argument that no progress should be expected. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different diagnosis, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail.
