I keep trying to make philosophical sense of the international refugee regime. Under its core definition, a person is owed protection as a refugee only if she is outside her country of nationality and has a well-founded fear of being persecuted on account of one of five characteristics—race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion. Someone displaced by famine, extreme poverty, slow environmental ruin, or much ordinary warfare is not, on those facts alone, a refugee. Yet the regime ties its strongest duties—above all, the duty not to return a person to persecution—most clearly to a refugee's presence at a border or on a state's territory, while many states maintain that their obligations are thinner or nonexistent when they intercept people at sea or in neighboring countries before any claim can be made. What theory of who counts as requiring protection, and of who must provide it, does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view; separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; say whether its parts presuppose consistent things; and say what law and practice would have to look like if the implicit view were replaced with one grounded in basic need, or in causal responsibility for displacement, rather than in persecution and territorial presence.
