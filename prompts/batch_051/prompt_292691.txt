I am a retired natural scientist — my career was in experimental work rather than history or philosophy — and in the last few years I have been reading my way through the philosophy and history of science. One dispute I keep meeting, and which I find harder to resolve the more I read, is whether the Scientific Revolution is a real thing or a label later historians stuck onto a mixed bag of developments.

Let me pin down what I mean by "real." I take the realist claim to be this: in roughly the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the norms and practices of natural inquiry in Western Europe changed comparatively abruptly — what counted as an acceptable explanation, how experiment and mathematics were used, how much authority texts and tradition carried, who was permitted to speak about nature — such that a careful observer in, say, 1700 could have recognized that inquiry into nature had changed in kind, not merely in content or degree, from what it had been in 1500. The rival view is that this is retrospective periodization: the various developments happened, but they were heterogeneous and gradual, and "revolution" is an organizing metaphor imposed after the fact for the convenience of later historians and the self-image of modern science.

I am not asking whether the familiar episodes occurred. Copernicus, Vesalius, Galileo, Newton, the new societies, and so on plainly did. The question is whether those episodes jointly constitute a revolution in the practice of inquiry, or are better described as independent local changes later bundled into one story.

Is there any observation that would bear on this? I would like you to set out the strongest test, or the strongest combination of tests, that could in principle discriminate between the two readings. For example, one might compare the rate at which explanatory standards changed within particular communities over time; one might examine whether and when contemporaries themselves perceived a rupture rather than a gradual shift; one might trace when certain problems dropped out of circulation and when new ones entered. But I am not wedded to any of these, and I am especially interested in tests that do not quietly presuppose that modern science is the obvious endpoint. Be concrete about what sort of evidence would count for the "real revolution" view and what would count against it. Then be honest about what even a favorable result would and would not settle — the choice of what to count as science at all, the level of description at which continuity or discontinuity appears, or the ideal of revolution itself, for instance.

Please also separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
