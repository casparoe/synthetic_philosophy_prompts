I write about AI and the economics of technology for a magazine, and at a safety-policy panel last week an investor kept saying that safety work is just a tax on the capable players. His claim was that red-teaming, alignment research, audits and slow, staged rollouts all cost money and time, improve the product about as much as a tax does, and put the careful labs at a disadvantage against labs that skip them. He called this the alignment tax, and the conclusion he drew was that voluntary commitments are hopeless: we shouldn't be shocked or moralistic when companies quietly cut safety budgets, because competition punishes the ones that don't. A researcher at the same event pushed back with a different analogy: safety work is more like insurance, an unglamorous cost that only pays off in a tail event, or maybe like paying to dispose of your own chemical waste when your competitor just dumps it.

I want to audit the tax analogy before I rely on it in print. What exactly corresponds to what: the tax base, the rate, the revenue, the government, the taxpayer, the public good the revenue is meant to fund, the shadow economy of evasion? Where does the mapping break — for instance, a real tax raises revenue that can be spent, whereas the "revenue" from an alignment tax seems to be safety itself, which mostly benefits the public or even competitors rather than the firm paying it? Does the analogy quietly assume there is some authority that can assess and enforce the tax, and if that authority doesn't exist yet, is the analogy doing illicit work? And does the investor's conclusion that voluntary safety is untenable survive the disanalogies, or would an externality or insurance model be more honest about what is actually going on?
