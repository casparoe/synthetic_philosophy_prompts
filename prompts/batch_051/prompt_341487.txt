I am an undergraduate taking a course on later Wittgenstein, and we have just spent two weeks on On Certainty and the hinge epistemology that developed out of it. The course readings repeatedly claim that hinge propositions—for instance, that the Earth did not come into existence five minutes ago—cannot be known, because they are the fixed scaffolding that makes knowing and doubting possible in the first place. But I have an argument that seems to me to yield the opposite, and I cannot find the mistake. Here it is:

(1) A proposition is a genuine object of doubt only if there is some possible evidence that would count against it; otherwise the doubt is idle or merely verbal.
(2) For a hinge proposition such as that the Earth did not come into existence five minutes ago, there is no possible evidence that could count against it, since any candidate evidence would need to rely on that very hinge in order to bear on it at all.
(3) Therefore hinge propositions cannot be objects of genuine doubt.
(4) Any proposition is either open to doubt or certain.
(5) Certainty is the highest grade of knowledge.

It seems to follow that hinges are not unknowable but rather are known to the very highest degree, which is the exact reverse of what our readings defend. Each step looks right to me on its own. Please do not hedge by surveying what different commentators might say; give me a committed diagnosis. Is the argument deductively valid, and if not, exactly which step or steps fail, and how should the offending step be corrected?
