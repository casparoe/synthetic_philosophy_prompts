I am writing a paper for a philosophy-of-biology competition on holobionts (hosts together with their microbial partners), colonies, and superorganisms, and I need a thought experiment built to a specific set of requirements. My paper argues that the physiological and evolutionary definitions of an organism are genuinely different, so I want a case that drives them apart.

The two definitions I am working with are these. A living system is one organism in the physiological sense when its parts form a single functionally integrated unit: they share a boundary, a metabolism, and a division of labor, and the parts depend on the continued functioning of the whole to survive. A living system is one organism in the evolutionary sense when it is a single unit of selection: its parts share a reproductive fate, and cooperation among the parts is protected from conflict among them, for instance by a single-cell bottleneck or by a germ line that does all the reproducing on behalf of the rest.

Familiar cases often blur rather than sharpen the difference. A Portuguese man o' war is usually treated as one evolutionary individual even though its body is divided into many physiologically distinct zooids. An aphid and its bacterial endosymbionts, by contrast, are highly integrated physiologically but remain several lineages with different genomes and imperfectly aligned interests.

Please construct a thought experiment in which a single living system is clearly one organism in the physiological sense but clearly many organisms in the evolutionary sense—in other words, one body that is several units of selection. The constraint is that the case must not rely on microbial symbionts; start instead from an insect colony or another familiar social animal, and allow yourself at most one biologically coherent modification to its normal biology, of a kind that could plausibly arise by mutation. No aliens, no futuristic technology, and no stipulating the verdicts. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph.

After you set out the scenario, show the step-by-step reasoning for each definition, identify the feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what would happen if that feature were removed. Finish by explaining how a defender of the evolutionary definition could argue that your system is not really many organisms, and what observation would settle the dispute.
