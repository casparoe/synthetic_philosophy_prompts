A question I keep tripping over when I teach writing systems: I say "an alphabet encodes phonemes," then I say "English spelling is only loosely phonemic," and someone asks what the word is actually tracking. So give me a working defintion of phonemic as applied to a writing system -- or, if you think the term is a muddle, define whatever you think I should say instead. Test it against these:

- Finnish, about as one-letter-one-phoneme as real orthographies get;
- English, where <sign> keeps its <g> because of <signature>, and letters often encode morphemes or etymology rather than surface sound;
- German, where <Rad> is spelled with <d> though the final sound is [t] (compare <Rades>);
- French, with silent final letters that reappear in liaison and a lot of etymological spelling;
- Hebrew and Arabic, abjads that mark the consonants and leave short vowels mostly unwritten;
- Korean Hangul, where the letter shapes are built to code articulatory features, but letters get packed into syllabic blocks and modern pronunciation isn't mapped one-to-one;
- Japanese kana, which write morae instead of phonemes (the small tsu, the moraic n);
- Cherokee, a syllabary;
- Chinese characters, which at least in principle encode words or morphemes rather than sound segments;
- Thai, where several letters now stand for the same sound and the spelling preserves etymological distinctions from Pali and Sanskrit.

Run through each and say where your defintion breaks or forces a shoking verdict. End by telling me which of your verdicts is least secure, and whether "phonemic" is best thought of as a yes/no property, a gradient, or a loose label for two or three different things a script can be doing. Lead with the definition in your first sentence -- no preamble, no restating the question -- and then give the reasoning.
