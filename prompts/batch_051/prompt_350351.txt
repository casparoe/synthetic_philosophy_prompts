A colleague has asked me to lead a two-hour, whiteboard-only seminar for working programmers and data scientists — mathematically literate but with no formal logic beyond a first undergraduate course — on what Gödel's incompleteness theorems do and do not establish when they are exported outside mathematics: to debates about artificial intelligence, the supposed limits of formal systems, and claims about truth. Plan the teaching arc rather than the exposition itself. I need the order in which the ideas must be introduced so that the theorems' fine print — the consistency assumptions, the difference between a system's being incomplete and its being unable to prove its own consistency, and the role of the arithmetization step — is in place before the slogan that there are true but unprovable statements is allowed to do any work; the single toy formal system or worked example that should carry the explanation, and what it must have and must lack; the misconceptions to expect (for instance, that the theorems show any formal system is unreliable, that they rule out artificial intelligence in principle, that they prove the human mind escapes any machine description, or that truth itself is system-relative) and at what point in the sequence each should be defused; and a closing check that would tell me the group has grasped the distinction between what the mathematics establishes and what the popular extrapolations add — for example, whether they can state which conditions in the theorem are doing the work and refuse a bad inference drawn from it. I also want you to specify what should be deliberately left out at this level and how I should mark it as left out, so that the omissions do not get mistaken for the argument itself.
