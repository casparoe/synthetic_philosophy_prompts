Build me a case where a shame-based moral culture beats a guilt-based one at the very thing guilt is supposed to be best at: getting somebody who did a secret harm to voluntarily own up and repair it. The twist that makes this hard is that the culture is delibrately moving from guilt talk to shame talk, so it doubles as a data point on whether a culture can choose its moral-emotion regime. Work with the standard distinction as your backdrop—guilt says "I did a bad thing" and is supposed to push toward confession and repair; shame says "I am the kind of person who does this" and is supposed to push toward hiding or lashing out. I want the shame regime to win, but not because the person is afraid they'll get caught.

Constraints: realistic setting (a workplace, a neighborhood, a co-op, that sort of thing), one paragraph for the scenario, no law enforcement or formal punishment in the story, and the harm itself has to be something the person could conceal forever if they wanted to. The scenario should show, concretely, what the guilt regime and the shame regime each look like in practice, and what the community did to switch.

After the scenario, walk me through it: state the harm, why it's concealable, what each theory predicts about the wrongdoer, and why the shame regime gets more voluntary repair. Then be honest about the machinery: name the single feature of the case that makes shame win, say what happens if you remove that feature, and give the strongest response a guilt-first theorist would have—including how they'd rewrite the case to make guilt win again, and whether that rewrite is cheating.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning; no preamble and don't restate the question.
