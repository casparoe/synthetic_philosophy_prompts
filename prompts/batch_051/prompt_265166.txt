Two of my colleagues, Alex and Jordan, are analysts at a firm that forecasts regulatory outcomes. After a public merger hearing, they argued about the probability that the transaction will be blocked, and I was asked to referee but could not. Their exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Alex said: "We sat through the same hearing, we run the same in-house model, and we both update like Bayesians. Yet I put the probability of a block at 80% and you put it at 50%. Aumann's agreement theorem says rational agents with a common prior cannot agree to disagree, so at least one of us must be making a mistake."

(2) Jordan replied: "You're citing a slogan, not the theorem. What Aumann actually shows is that if two agents share a prior, each conditions by Bayes's rule, and their posterior probabilities for a given event are common knowledge, then those posteriors must be equal. Common knowledge here means not just that we each announce a number, but that I know that you know that I know your number, and so on without end. That condition is not met here. I don't even know whether your 80% is your honest posterior or a bargaining position, and I know almost nothing about the private evidence behind it."

(3) Alex said: "Fine. Then let us make it common knowledge. We will write out all our private evidence and all our modeling choices, post everything where both of us can inspect it, and check that each has read the other's entire file. Once relevant information is common knowledge, if we genuinely share a prior, our numbers should be forced to converge. If they still do not converge, one of us really has made a mistake."

(4) Jordan said: "That only proves my point. The theorem bites only after you have engineered something close to complete disclosure — an antecedent that real disagreements almost never satisfy. Outside toy cases, people do not literally share a prior, they routinely have good reasons not to reveal private information, and common knowledge is never actually achieved. So the theorem cannot be used to brand real disagreements irrational."

Please go through this exchange move by move. For each of the four moves, rate the claim from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify the rating briefly. Then say who is more right overall, and identify at least one important point that both of them are missing.
