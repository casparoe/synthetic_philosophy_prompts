I have spent years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and the lottery paradox and the preface paradox keep haunting my day job, which involves evaluating many individually strong claims that I nevertheless know will not all turn out to be true. Recently an argument has been pushing me toward the conclusion that the preface case is just the lottery case with extra narrative, and I need someone to tell me exactly where, if anywhere, the argument breaks.

You are a professional epistemologist who works on formal and probabilistic epistemology and the structure of rational belief. Treat this as a careful diagnostic task. Before settling on an answer, restate the argument in your own words in the most charitable form you can, then work through it twice: once looking for shifts in the meaning of terms such as specific, statistical, and ground, and once checking whether each transition preserves truth rather than merely preserving plausibility.

Here is the argument.

(1) In a fair lottery with one guaranteed winner and n tickets, the probability that any given ticket loses is (n−1)/n, and by making n large enough this probability can be made arbitrarily close to 1.

(2) For sufficiently large n, it is rational to believe of any particular ticket that it will lose, even though the only ground for this belief is the statistical ground described in (1).

(3) In the preface case, an author rationally believes each individual sentence in her book on the basis of specific grounds: the particular argument, data, calculation, or source behind that sentence, not merely its membership in a large class most of whose members are true.

(4) The same author rationally believes that the book contains at least one error, as she says in the preface.

(5) The author has no specific reason to doubt any particular sentence; her only basis for the preface belief is the general track record of long, carefully checked texts, which shows that such texts usually contain at least one error.

(6) A general track record of that kind is a statistical ground, in the same sense as the ground available in the lottery case.

(7) If the author's only basis for disbelieving the conjunction of all the book's sentences is a statistical ground about the class, while her basis for believing each conjunct is a specific ground about that sentence, then the asymmetry cannot be rationally sustained: doubt about the conjunction must be located in the conjuncts, and if the doubt originates only in class membership, then each sentence's standing with respect to the conjunction is being fixed by class membership rather than by its specific grounds.

(8) It follows that, in forming her preface belief, the author is treating each sentence's claim to be true as having the same kind of basis as a lottery ticket's claim to lose.

(9) Therefore the preface case is the lottery case in all epistemically relevant respects, and the specific grounds in (3) do no work in the author's preface attitude.

(10) So there is no third diagnosis: either the author's preface belief is unjustified, or the closure-denying lesson of the lottery applies to the preface in exactly the same way; the view that the preface is different in kind from the lottery is unavailable.

Tell me: Is this argument valid? If it is invalid, identify the exact step or transition that fails and state exactly how it fails, for example as an equivocation, a scope shift, or an unsupported distribution of doubt from wholes to parts. If it is valid but unsound, identify the false premise and give the strongest defensible replacement for it. If the argument is sound, say so directly rather than softening the conclusion.
