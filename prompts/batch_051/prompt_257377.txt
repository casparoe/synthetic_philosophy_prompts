I am a TA for a small interdisciplinary seminar called "Induction, Learning, and the Problem of Priors," cross-listed between philosophy and computer science. The students have read Hume on induction, the basic supervised-learning setup, and a statement of the no-free-lunch theorem without the full derivation; several of them come from a LessWrong-adjacent background and are comfortable with the vocabulary but sometimes run the argument too quickly. On the final exam I asked the following short-answer question:

"In a paragraph or two, explain the relationship between the no-free-lunch theorem for supervised learning and Hume's problem of induction. State the theorem's main condition carefully, and then say what the result does and does not license us to conclude about learning from experience. In particular, address the claim that the theorem 'formalizes' Hume's insight."

One student wrote:

"The no-free-lunch theorem is often taken to be a formal version of Hume's point that induction cannot be justified by experience alone. What the theorem actually shows is that in supervised learning, if you average over all possible target functions and weight each one equally, every learning algorithm has the same expected off-training-set error. So if an algorithm does better than chance on the problems we actually care about, that advantage has to come from its inductive bias, not from the data. This is Hume's worry restated in the language of function spaces: any finite history of inputs and outputs is compatible with infinitely many underlying regularities, and the data by themselves cannot choose among them.

The stronger conclusion people draw is that an algorithm's success is really a matter of whether its priors match the world. That is why Occam's razor works only when the environment is simple: simplicity is itself an inductive assumption, not a free-standing principle. But the theorem also tells us something about real, fixed problems. On any particular real-world distribution, two algorithms will perform about equally unless one of them happens to encode the true function, because no amount of data can distinguish between hypotheses that fit the past equally well. So Hume was right that, absent some principle of uniformity, induction has no rational foundation, and the no-free-lunch theorem supplies the formal proof that was missing."
