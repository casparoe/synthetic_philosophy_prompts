Someone in a forum I read floated a fix for the standard Makropulos worry about eternal life — the worry that after enough centuries you've ran through every ambition and relationship that made you want tomorrow to come, and you're left just not minding either way. The proposal: in the immortal society (heaven, post-singularity sim, whichever), every 120 years each person's episodic memory gets wiped back to their 25-year-old self. Skills, language, semantic knowledge, and habits survive; what goes is the felt, first-person record of events and people. You wake up knowing the wipe happened, and you get a folder: a few pages you wrote to yourself the week before, plus a neutral administrative summary of what you did and who you knew. Opting out isn't allowed, and the designers claim this is the only stable way to keep paradise from going stale. Treat it as a mechanism, not a moral position: what does it actually do to boredom? Does the post-wipe person have any reason to care about promises, debts, grievances, or multi-century projects from the previous block? How do marriages, friendships, and reputations reorganize around the wipe cycle? Where does it get gamed first — forged folders, reputational arbitrage across lives, people front-loading terrible behavior into week 119 — and can the obvious patches (no notes, mandatory disclosure of pre-wipe identity, a review board) work without gutting the point of the scheme? And underneath all that: does this actually answer Makropulos, or does it just swap one bored immortal for a series of fresh people and declare the problem solved? Answer in American English.
