Write a classification instrument for an undergraduate philosophy of mathematics seminar: a taxonomy of positions in the dispute among logicism, intuitionism, and formalism, cast as a decision tree. Every branch node should be a diagnostic claim or a small case about numbers, proof, or meaning, phrased so that a student can answer it without already knowing which position the answer leads to. I intend to distribute it in the first session, before the students have read the classic statements, so that they are sorted by what they accept rather than by a label they prefer.

The leaves must include the following four positions, with roughly these characterisations:

1. Frege–Russell logicism: mathematical truth is ultimately logical truth, and the objects mathematics speaks of are supplied by logic itself rather than by a separate mathematical intuition.
2. Brouwerian intuitionism: mathematics is a constructive mental activity; a mathematical statement is true only if there is a constructive proof of it, and classical inferences such as the unrestricted law of excluded middle over infinite domains are not generally valid.
3. Hilbertian formalism, understood carefully as a two-tier view: a contentful finitary core (roughly, reasoning about finite configurations and computations) alongside a transfinite or infinitary remainder that functions as an ideal instrument, justified by consistency rather than by reference.
4. Game formalism: mathematics is a system of marks manipulated according to rules, and the marks need not be about anything.

If your tree throws up a robust combination of answers not covered by those four, add a properly named leaf instead of forcing it into the nearest of the four. Combination candidates likely to arise include non-logicist platonism, where mathematical objects exist independently but arithmetic is not reducible to logic, and if-thenism, where mathematics consists of logical truths of the form if the axioms hold then the theorem, with no assertion that the axioms themselves hold. You may conclude that a candidate belongs under one of the existing leaves, but say why.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. First identify the claims on which the positions genuinely come apart, turn those claims into diagnostic questions, and only then place the named positions among the combinations of answers that result. For every combination that no named position occupies, state briefly whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, because competent people find it unattractive, or because it has simply been underexplored. If two leaves agree on every question in your tree, flag that rather than inventing a distinction.

The following distinctions matter to me. Do not let full logicism become the default for any student who merely says mathematical objects exist independently: logicism is a further claim about the reducibility of mathematics to logic, so include a question that tests reducibility separately from existence. Keep Hilbertian formalism and game formalism clearly apart; the working split is whether any part of mathematics is contentful, with the Hilbertian saying yes for the finitary core and only the transfinite part ideal, and the game formalist saying no across the board. Separate questions about bivalence (is every closed arithmetical sentence already true or false before it is proved or refuted?) from questions about admissible proof (is a non-constructive existence proof a genuine proof?) because people follow these in different combinations.

Some sample diagnostic claims you may use, adapt, or replace with better ones: whether quantification over the completed set of all natural numbers is legitimate; whether a proof that applies the law of excluded middle to statements about infinite sets establishes something true; whether seven plus five equals twelve is true in the ordinary sense or merely a string the rules permit one to write; whether the meaning of a mathematical statement is given by its proof conditions rather than its truth conditions; whether every theorem of analysis should be read as a conditional with the conjunction of the relevant axioms as its antecedent.

Keep the instrument usable within one session: no more than nine branch nodes on the longest path and no more than seven leaves.

Finally, mark the status of each characterisation. For each branch and leaf, indicate whether the characterisation is uncontroversial as a reading of the historical programme, or whether it is a live interpretive question on which specialists genuinely disagree (for example, how much mentalism must be attributed to Brouwer, or exactly which methods Hilbert's finitism licensed). Do not present a contested reading as the consensus view.
