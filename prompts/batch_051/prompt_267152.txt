I have a standing rule when I think about organisms: whenever I meet a puzzling feature—a courtship display, a strangely shaped leaf, a metabolic quirk—I take it for granted that it was selected for because it helped ancestors survive or reproduce, and I do not give that up until someone offers me a concrete non-adaptive account. Lately I keep running into the criticism that this is exactly the adaptationist habit that produces just-so stories, and that a great many features are spandrels: by-products of other selected traits or of how bodies develop, not adaptations in their own right. My question is whether my rule is defensible as an epistemic shortcut rather than a bias dressed up as a principle. Under what conditions is an adaptation-first default reliable, when does it systematically mislead, and is there a version of the rule that keeps the benefits without making me see purpose everywhere?
