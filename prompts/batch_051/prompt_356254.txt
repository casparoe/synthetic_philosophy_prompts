I've been arguing with a friend about what it takes for something to count as representing, and I wrote down her rulings to see if there's a stable rule underneath. She's firm that a smoke detector represents smoke (or fire), and that when it beeps because of burnt toast, it's misrepresenting. She's equally firm that a thermostat represents the room's temperature, and a miscalibrated thermostat is wrong about the temperature. So far so boring. But she also says tree rings don't represent anything: they indicate the tree's age, but a tree ring can't be mistaken, it's just there. A frog's snapping reflex, she says, doesn't represent 'fly'; it represents something like 'small dark moving thing over there,' because the frog can't tell flies from beads — so when it snaps at a bead whizzing past, it isn't misrepresenting anything, since a small dark moving thing really is over there. And a heart doesn't represent blood or anything else: a failing heart is broken, not wrong.

The new case: a forester has a wooden stake in the ground that's been there for years just to brace a fence. She's started using its shadow every afternoon to guess the time, and she's actually good at it — she'd be flat wrong if the shadow read two o'clock when its really four. But the stake was never built, chosen, positioned, or adjusted to tell time, and no one ever copied the trick. Will my friend say the shadow represents the time of day, misrepresents it when she's off, or is just an indicator she happens to use, like the tree rings? And what's the rule she's running — plus, what further case would cleanly separate it from the nearest rival rule?
