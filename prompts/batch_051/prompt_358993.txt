In a seminar on personal identity that I have taught more times than I care to count, the core argument of the Buddha’s Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta (Saṃyutta Nikāya 22.59) has always seemed to me almost impossible to fault. The sutta examines each of the five aggregates — form, feeling, perception, determinations, consciousness — and asks whether any of them could be the self. Its decisive move, to my mind, is not the familiar appeal to impermanence but an earlier argument from susceptibility to affliction and the absence of control. Here is Ñanamoli Thera’s translation of the passage:

"Bhikkhus, form is not-self. Were form self, then this form would not lead to affliction, and one could have it of form: 'Let my form be thus, let my form be not thus.' And since form is not-self, so it leads to affliction, and none can have it of form: 'Let my form be thus, let my form be not thus.'"

The same test is then run on feeling, perception, determinations, and consciousness. This looks to me like a genuine metaphysical argument and not merely a meditative suggestion: a self worth the name would be an owner with authority over its own constituents, yet nothing in experience answers to that description, and no such owner is discovered apart from the aggregates. I am therefore drawn towards the view that the person is a useful designation for a causally connected bundle, and that questions about the persistence of a substantial "me" are badly posed.

And yet, when I step back, the intuition that there is a subject here — the thing to which these experiences are given, the one who notices that form leads to affliction — simply will not go away. It does not present itself as a theory I have been taught, but as something pre-reflective: my future flourishing matters to me in a way that the flourishing of a merely connected stream of aggregates would not. I cannot make myself experience that concern as a grammatical artefact without beginning to treat myself as a kind of expository fiction, and I am unwilling to write off believers in a persisting self as merely confused. So I am genuinely in the middle. Give me the strongest version of each side — the reductionist reading of the sutta and the case for taking the persisting subject seriously — and then say which, if either, should yield. In particular, tell me what would justify each of the following moves: debunking the intuition as a product of grammar, habit, and attachment; amending the doctrine so that it denies only a substantial self while leaving the conventional person intact; or reading the passage as therapeutic rather than metaphysical, aimed at undermining clinging rather than stating the ultimate furniture of the world. Each is a live option, and I want a negotiated verdict rather than a one-sided brief. I would also be grateful if the whole reply were written in British English.
