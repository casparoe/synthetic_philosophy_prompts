I keep noticing a parallel I cannot decide about, and I want it tested rather than merely confirmed. In the chapter on Fortune toward the end of The Prince, Machiavelli seems to hold two pictures at once. On one side, Fortune is like a river in flood: you cannot stop the flood, but if you built embankments and channels in the calm years you are far less ruined, which sounds like hedging against a risk whose rough shape is known in advance. On the other side, Fortune is personified as a woman who yields to the impetuous rather than to the coldly cautious, which sounds like the opposite of hedging, as though audacity itself changes the odds. Machiavelli half-reconciles the two by saying that a ruler prospers when his temperament matches the times and fails when it does not, and that no one is supple enough to change his nature at the right moment. I cannot tell whether this is an early and muddled version of something real in modern decision theory, or whether the two images simply do not fit together.

Here is the modern structure I see in it. Decision theory separates risk, meaning a stable enough distribution you can prepare against even when you cannot remove the hazard, from settings where there is no fixed distribution to hedge: either because the probabilities themselves are unknown, or because the environment is an adversary that reacts to your style of action. Under risk, cautious preparation and diversification are sensible; under deep uncertainty or strategic pressure, they can be worse than committing or acting audaciously. The proposed mapping is that Fortune-the-river behaves like risk, Fortune-the-woman behaves like strategic uncertainty, and virtù — adaptable, energetic character rather than Christian virtue — plays the role of a policy for choosing how to act given the regime. Is this a genuine identity of structure, or a loose analogy that papers over the gap between a personified force and a probability distribution?
