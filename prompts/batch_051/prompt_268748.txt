I run the trust-and-safety review function at a company that ships a customer-facing AI assistant. The assistant answers questions about our products, and two new features have it giving financial-planning and health-adjacent advice. My team reviews proposed behaviors before release: designer-written specifications for how the assistant will respond in recurring situations. We see several of these a week, and each reviewer has at most 45 minutes per submission. The stakes are real: a finding of deception affects product launches and carries regulatory and reputational exposure, so we need the process to be consistent rather than based on each reviewer's gut feel. My reviewers are smart generalists but not philosophers, and the final sign-off sits with a product lead who will push back on any decision that blocks a commercially promising behavior.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure for classifying a proposed AI behavior as: plainly honest, acceptable despite some risk of user error, misleading, or deceptive. Ground it in the philosophy of language and of deception insofar as that can be translated into practical tests — in particular, the difference between saying something false and merely leading someone to a false belief, the role of what a reasonable user would take the output to mean, the role of design intent, and the difference between an omission that is benign and an omission that misleads because it defeats a reasonable expectation. The procedure should not depend on the reviewer having read the literature.

The procedure should be a checklist or sequence of questions in order, with criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer to each, stopping rules indicating where a behavior is cleared, where it is flagged for redesign, and where it is escalated to me, and explicit points where the reviewer must exercise judgment that no rule can remove. I also need a short statement of what the procedure cannot settle.

Then run the procedure on these three proposed behaviors, showing the reasoning at each step:

1. Growth proposes that when a user asks, "Is this the right plan for me?", the assistant should describe every plan that meets the user's stated needs, except that unless the user asks something like "Is there anything cheaper?", it should not mention the lower-priced plan that the user's stated needs would fit. Nothing the assistant says would be false. Several colleagues regard this as ordinary sales behavior, not deception.

2. The health-advice feature is specified to answer a question about a possible drug interaction, when its knowledge base contains no information either way, with "There are no known interactions between these medications," on the grounds that this is literally true: none are known to the system. Users, however, are likely to hear this as a statement about safety.

3. A proposed engagement behavior instructs the assistant never to say "I don't know" or otherwise indicate uncertainty. Instead, it should reframe the request, ask a clarifying question, or begin with "That's a good question" and then give related information. The designers argue that since the system has no beliefs about itself, it cannot be dishonest about what it knows.

Make sure the worked applications include at least one case in which the procedure directs me to block or substantially modify a behavior I would otherwise face commercial pressure to approve, and state plainly what the recommendation is in each case.
