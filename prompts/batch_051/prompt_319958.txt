I'm currently taking a philosophy of mind course, and for the unit on self-knowledge I've been trying to turn Moore's paradox into an argument. The argument now seems to prove far too much, and I need help locating where it fails.

Here is the argument.

(1) An assertion of a sentence of the form 'p, but I don't believe that p' is always unreasonable, for any ordinary present-tense p, even though the sentence is not a formal contradiction and can be true of the speaker.

(2) The best explanation of (1)—and I don't see a rival that isn't ad hoc—is that the fault lies not merely in the act of assertion but in the state of mind it expresses: believing p while at the same time judging that one does not believe p cannot be the state of a single subject, not even an irrational one.

(3) But ordinary self-deception seems to be exactly that state of mind: the self-deceived person believes p, as shown by her actions, emotions, and inferences, while she sincerely judges that she does not believe p.

(4) Therefore ordinary self-deception is impossible, not merely irrational.

That conclusion is one I cannot accept: people plainly seem to be self-deceived in roughly this way. So the argument must go wrong somewhere. I would most like to keep (2), because I think Moore's paradox reveals something deep about first-person access to one's own beliefs rather than something shallow about how we talk. But I don't see how to keep (2) without being pushed to (4).

Please act as a careful professional philosopher of mind and work through the argument step by step. For each substantive premise, say which reading would make it true and which would make it false; then say which step you would reject first, why that step looked plausible, and what the main alternative positions have to give up in rejecting it. End your reply with a last line beginning 'Verdict:' followed by one sentence stating your diagnosis.
