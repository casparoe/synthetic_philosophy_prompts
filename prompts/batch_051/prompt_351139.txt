I am a doctoral candidate preparing to present a paper at a research colloquium whose audience will be mostly historians of early modern European philosophy — scholars trained on Descartes, Spinoza, and Leibniz — along with two or three specialists in African and Africana philosophy and one theologian who works on Ethiopian Orthodox thought. My thesis is that Zera Yacob's Hatata, a philosophical text attributed to a seventeenth-century Ethiopian writer though the authenticity of the surviving text is itself a matter of scholarly dispute, should be read as an independent and theoretically serious version of rationalist ethics, one that deserves a place in the early modern canon rather than a respectful nod in a survey of non-Western philosophy, and that its central argument can stand comparison with the reasoning Descartes reports in the Discourse on Method. The reading I will defend is roughly this: the author presents himself as someone driven by religious persecution to withdraw from society and to refuse every inherited doctrine until it has been tested by his own intelligence; from the order and interdependence of created things he infers a single wise creator; he concludes that this creator gave human beings reason precisely so that what is to be believed and done could be known rather than merely received; and he derives from this an ethic requiring honest worship, love of neighbor, refraining from harm, and the testing of every custom and scriptural claim by its agreement with reason — a test that, as he draws it, gives moral standing to every intelligent person regardless of sex, birth, or confession. I need a prep sheet for the question period. I want the objections in rough order of likelihood and damage, phrased as this specific audience would actually phrase them, with a note on what I should concede up front, what I must hold, where each reply is weakest, and the objection I should most hope nobody asks because it is the one I have not yet learned to answer.
