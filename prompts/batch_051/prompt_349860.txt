In my philosophy of mind and AI seminar we have been discussing Dennett's intentional stance, especially the line between treating a system as if it has beliefs and its genuinely having them. I am trying to sort out which of my own attributions are literal and which are merely shorthand, and I keep going in circles. Here are my current verdicts.

I would not say that a thermostat literally believes the room is cold or wants the radiator on, however useful that way of talking is. With a chess engine I am half-inclined to say that it wants to defend its king, but on reflection I take this to be a compact description of its evaluation function and search; I would deny that it literally has beliefs about the position. A self-driving car seems to me a harder case: when it fails to brake because it has treated a pedestrian as a stationary object, I do want to say that the car believed the pedestrian was stationary, and that the belief was false in a way that explains its behaviour, not merely an economical paraphrase. An ant colony that builds a bridge across a gap I would not credit with a decision, though I accept that the colony solved a problem; here too the mental talk feels metaphorical. A corporation, by contrast, I have no hesitation in describing as intending to expand production or believing that demand will rise, even though a company has no single mind in which a belief could sit. And a large language model that produces the sentence 'I believe you may have meant...' I want to deny both knowledge and intention, yet I find the mental idiom almost impossible to avoid when describing what it does. My working principle is that literal belief requires internal states that can misrepresent the world and that flexibly control behaviour rather than merely implement a fixed heuristic. That principle would put the self-driving car in, the thermostat and the chess engine out, and the corporation only metaphorical — but I am unhappy with that last result, and I cannot see what the principle does with the model.

Is there a single principled account that captures all or most of these verdicts, or should some of them be revised? What does the account you would defend say about two cases I have not considered: a home robot that acts on a false belief about the layout of a room and corrects itself when the mistake is pointed out, and an artificial system trained on human behaviour that passes a standard false-belief test? Please be explicit about which of my verdicts your account cannot accommodate, and whether you would simply bite the bullet on any of the others.
