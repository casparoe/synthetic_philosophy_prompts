In my philosophy of religion seminar we are working through the argument from divine hiddenness: roughly, that if a perfectly loving God existed, God would see to it that no one who is capable of a personal relationship with God and who is not resisting God remains in nonbelief; yet such non-resistant nonbelievers exist; so no perfectly loving God exists. In our discussion, one student keeps returning to a single sentence: "God must remain hidden, because a love compelled by overwhelming evidence would not be love." The sentence is being asked to do real work: it is meant to show that hiddenness is not evidence against divine love but a precondition of the kind of love God seeks. The seminar splits into those who find this obviously true and those who find it obviously question-begging, without anyone first stating the precise claim at issue. I would like to sort out the slogan before we meet again. Please separate the distinct claims this sentence could be making—about freedom of the will, about the nature of love, about the epistemic conditions of genuine faith, about what a perfectly loving God would be obligated to reveal. For each reading, say whether it is trivially true, a substantive and defensible thesis, or a thesis that should be rejected or is at least unmotivated. Then identify which reading or readings would actually block the hiddenness argument, which would merely evade it, and which reading the student is most plausibly committed to. I want a catalogue of readings with verdicts, not a single defense of one interpretation.
