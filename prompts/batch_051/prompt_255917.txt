I'm kicking around an essay idea on Quinn's self-torturer and I've conviced myself the usual way people set it up already asks the wrong question, but I want comments before I write the whole thing. Below are my notes, not a draft. What I'd actually like from you: (1) is the indiscriminability/valuation distinction doing real work or am I just restating the standard response with better labels, (2) the second idea near the end — I suspect I'm confusing the Archimedean axiom with something else, so a correction there would be worth a lot, and (3) is the essay interesting enough as pitched, or is it only interesting to people already inside this debate.

The setup as usually given: a guy can turn a dial up one notch, each notch hurts so slightly more than the one before that he can't tell the difference, and he gets paid each time he does it. So for every adjacent pair he'd rather be at n+1 than n, but he'd also much rather be at 0 than at 1000. People then ask whether a rational agent can have intransitive preferences.

I think that question is misposed. What the adjacent comparisons actually involve is indiscriminability, not valuation. Not being able to feel the difference between n and n+1 is a perceptual fact, and its a relation everyone knows is nontransitive — it's a sorites chain. Letting the money decide when two options are pain-wise indistinguishable doesn't reveal a stable ranking; it reveals a tie in a locally unreliable measurement. So the apparent intransitivity isn't in the agent's preferences, it's in his pairwise choice behavior under a non-transitive discrimination relation.

If that's right, the self-torturer isn't a counterexample to transitivity as a constraint on rational preference, because he doesn't have a single intransitive preference ordering. He has a bunch of pairwise choice dispositions that don't add up to any ordering at all. The real lesson would be that pairwise choice isn't a reliable readout of preference when the comparisons are locally unreliable.

But here's the part I can't make work. Even after moving the intransitivity out of the preference relation, the guy is still making a mistake. A rational agent who knows his discrimination is soritical shouldn't march up the dial. So what norm does he violate? You can't answer "compare each option with every other option," because that's impossible and arguably not even what rationality demands. So maybe the real puzzle is about bounded rationality: how non-local does your comparison set have to be before a pairwise choice counts as rational? Is there a non-arbitrary answer that doesn't just relocate the arbitrary stopping point from the dial to the comparison procedure?

The other thought I had was to keep preferences transitive and blame an Archimedean or continuity assumption about how pains and money aggregate. But I can't see how that helps by itself: if you keep every adjacent preference, transitivity forces 1000 ≻ 0, so you'd have to reject at least one adjacent preference anyway, and then you're back to saying the local comparisons were never genuine preferences. I think I might be mangling what the Archimedean axiom actually rules out, though.

So yeah — does the main distinction survive contact with the details, am I wrong about the Archimedean move, and is this worth an essay?
