I spent my working life as a natural scientist, and I have only recently begun reading philosophy in any sustained way. The debate I keep returning to is the one in the theory of well-being about whether hedonism can survive the heterogeneity of pleasure. Let me set out how I understand the lay of the land, and then pose the question that interests me.

Hedonism about well-being, as I understand it, says that a life goes well for the person living it just to the extent that it contains pleasure and is free of pain, and that pleasure is the single intrinsic good. The heterogeneity problem begins from an ordinary observation: the pleasure of solving a puzzle, the pleasure of hearing a piece of music, the pleasure of relief when pain stops, the pleasure of eating when hungry, and the pleasure one might take in another person's misfortune feel almost nothing alike. If the word pleasure picks out one kind of thing, and if that one kind of thing is supposed to carry all of a life's value for the person living it, then there ought to be some single feature all these episodes share. What is it?

I find roughly three positions in the literature I have read. The first holds that there is a common hedonic tone: a distinctive felt quality present in every pleasant episode, however much the surrounding textures differ, somewhat as sight and hearing differ yet each has its own something-it-is-like. The second denies this. It says that careful introspection reveals no common felt element running through the enjoyment of a melody and the relief of a toothache; any candidate common core is either too thin to do the explanatory work asked of it or is simply a disjunction of different feelings assembled after the fact. The third position relocates the common element out of phenomenology altogether: pleasure is not a feeling but a kind of attitude, such as being pleased about something or wanting it to continue, and this attitude can be instantiated in episodes that feel nothing like one another. On this third view hedonism survives, but at the price of conceding that pleasure is not itself a distinctive sensation.

As an outsider with a scientist's habits, I find this debate oddly stalled. Each camp responds to counterexamples by refining its definitions, but I do not see anyone specifying what would count as evidence against their position. So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement about a matter of introspective fact, namely whether the common felt quality is really there? Is it a partly verbal disagreement about how much difference in feel is compatible with the single word pleasure? Is it a disagreement about what a theory of well-being needs pleasure to do for it, about the theoretical role the common element must play? Or are the camps answering different questions under one heading?

My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the argument turns less on conflicting introspective reports than on competing standards for when two experiences count as the same in kind: one side demands a felt common denominator, while the other accepts a common functional or attitudinal role. But I am unsure whether drawing that distinction would end the argument or merely relocate it.

Given your diagnosis of the impasse, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here: a conceptual distinction, an introspective protocol, a formal or empirical result, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why.
