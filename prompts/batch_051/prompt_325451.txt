I am a PhD student working on the moral limits of markets, and this term I have been reading through the commodification literature: the corruption objection, the fairness objection, the reply that if one may permissibly give away or do a thing for free then one may sell it, and the dispute over whether a vote is best modeled as a preference or as a judgment about the common good. I am trying to assemble prompts that force a model to show it actually understands the structure of these arguments, rather than trading in stock anti-capitalism slogans, so I would like something more specific than a broad satire of the idea that everything is for sale.

Please write a parody of a help-center FAQ page titled 'Ethics & Values' for a fictional startup whose platform facilitates three transactions: the purchase and sale of votes from eligible voters who will not otherwise use them, surge-priced emergency water, and what the company calls 'kidney partnerships,' modeled on the real non-cash voucher proposal in which a living donor receives not money but future transplant priority for a designated person. The piece should be roughly 700–800 words of deadpan, warm, metrics-obsessed customer-support prose. The company should treat repugnance as a mere user-experience problem, redefine coercion as something like a mismatch between revealed and stated preferences, describe vote-selling as 'portable, liquid democracy,' and treat each price as a signal that willingness to pay tracks both need and desert rather than asking whether it does. The target is the flat-footed version of the view that any voluntary exchange is presumptively permissible and that anti-commodification bans express nothing but unexamined disgust.

The humor must come from the actual philosophical content. Each answer should read as a garbled or evasive version of a recognizable move in the debate: the fairness objection as applied to poor sellers, desperate emergency buyers, and voters with no stake in the outcome; the corruption objection, on which attaching a price to voting changes what voting is as a collective civic practice; the crowding-out worry that market incentives displace nonmarket motivations; and the gap between formal consent and meaningful choice under severely constrained circumstances. Keep the voice deadpan throughout, and keep the irony legible to a reader who has not read the academic literature, while letting a specialist see that each deflection is pointed rather than random. Avoid naming any real person, work, or paper, and do not have the company state the objections outright; let them show through the evasions.
