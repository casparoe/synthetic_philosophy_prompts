I am taking a module on rational choice and diachronic agency, and one argument from a recent seminar keeps nagging at me. We considered an agent choosing among three flats by three criteria — floor space, commute, and rent — using the rule that one flat is preferred to another when it wins on two of the three criteria. As the lecturer showed, the agent's pairwise preferences can then cycle: flat A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A. A broker could exploit this by offering a series of exchanges, each of which the agent prefers to the last and on each of which the agent pays a small fee, until the agent has paid to end up back in the flat it began with. The lecturer concluded, in the standard way, that money-pump vulnerability shows the agent's preferences are inconsistent, and that any such agent is irrational. I am not sure the argument establishes quite that.

The step that troubles me is the move from there being a sequence of individually acceptable trades that jointly harms the agent to the claim that the agent's preferences are rationally defective at each point. A diachronic sequence can exploit the very fact that the agent's verdicts are local and pairwise; that may show that pairwise preference is the wrong unit for evaluating a whole course of action, but it need not show that each pairwise choice is defective, nor that the agent fails some requirement of synchronic transitivity. Moreover, an agent who foresees the broker's pattern can simply refuse to continue the sequence, thereby avoiding the loss while retaining the cyclical pairwise rankings; the failure to do so looks like a failure of foresight, not of preference coherence. Please make the strongest case you can for the position that a money-pump argument does not by itself show that the agent's cyclical preferences are rationally defective, even when the sequence is foreseeable; at most it shows something about the risks of failing to see one's choices as part of a temporally extended course of action. Engage with the strongest version of the opposing view, namely that rationality is partly a diachronic matter and that preferences which cannot be integrated into one stable ranking are therefore defective by their very nature. Be clear about how far your defence extends: does it protect only the agent who notices the cycle and adopts a policy of refusal, or also the myopic agent who never looks ahead?
