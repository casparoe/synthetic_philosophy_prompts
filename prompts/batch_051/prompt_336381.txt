I'm a retired biochemist (enzyme kinetics, mostly) and have recently started reading political philosophy without much of a syllabus. I keep tripping over this passage from Thoreau's "Resistance to Civil Government":

Can there not be a government in which majorities do not virtually decide right and wrong, but conscience?—in which majorities decide only those questions to which the rule of expediency is applicable? Must the citizen ever for a moment, or in the least degree, resign his conscience to the legislator? Why has every man a conscience, then? I think that we should be men first, and subjects afterward. It is not desirable to cultivate a respect for the law, so much as for the right. The only obligation which I have a right to assume, is to do at any time what I think right.

What's confusing me is the step from "conscience" to "the right." He asks whether majorities should decide right and wrong, and says conscience should decide; but then he ends by saying his only obligation is to do what he thinks right. So is "the right" supposed to be something objective that conscience detects (the way a detector reads a concentration), or is it just whatever an individual conscience says it is? Because if it's the latter, I don't see how this escape subjectivism, with each person as their own final judge and "the right" not really doing any work as a standard. Can you help me see how the two halves are supposed to fit together — or whether "conscience" is doing heavier lifting than I'm reading it as?
