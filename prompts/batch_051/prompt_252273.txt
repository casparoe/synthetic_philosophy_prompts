I've spent the last few weeks going down the s-risk rabbit hole — by which I mean the view that the dominant priority for a longtermist should be preventing futures that contain astronomical amounts of suffering, not just futures where humanity goes extinct. I get the basic expected-value argument: even a small probability of a future with, say, billions of suffering minds running for millions of years swamps nearly everything else on the ledger. What I'm after is a clear statement of the most important practical implications of taking this seriously, for someone currently choosing between technical AI safety, global health, and farmed animal welfare. I don't just want the answer to be work on AI safety with more urgency. I want the places where the s-risk lens actually changes the ranking, or changes what kind of AI safety work you'd do. For instance, I've seen people argue that if you're genuinely unsure whether the future will be net positive or net negative, reducing extinction risk isn't automatically good, and that some work aimed at making AI safe could make a stable totalitarian lock-in more likely — wich might be worse than extinction. Are those the central implications, or is the real upshot something else, like shifting from creating happy future lives to preventing suffering, or prioritising speed over robustness in AI governance? I'd rather you push past the abstract case for the view and tell me what I should actually do differently.
