I work as a software engineer on authorization and policy systems. In a discussion about entitlements, someone pointed me to Bentham’s Anarchical Fallacies, where he attacks the French revolutionaries’ declarations of rights. As I read him, Bentham argues that a right has to be the product of law: a right that is said to exist before or independently of law is not really a right. He makes an analogy that stuck with me: a person’s hunger is not bread, and asserting a pre-legal right is like treating the felt demand as if it were already the thing demanded. I want to use that idea to get clear on what a right actually is, but I have no formal training in this area.

Please give me a working definition of a right that handles the following cases:

- A user account in a system is granted read access to a table by an administrator; the grant is later revoked. Does the user have a right before the grant, during the grant, and after the revocation?
- A constitution states that citizens have a right to privacy, but no statute creates a cause of action, and no court has the power to enforce the provision.
- A software license grants an end user the right to run the software under specified conditions, and the license terminates if the conditions are breached.
- A political movement claims that people have a right to adequate housing even though no legislature has enacted such a right.
- A statute requires the state to provide free primary education, but the relevant budget is empty and no official can be compelled to act.
- A child, without citing any law, claims a right to be fed by their parents.

I suspect Bentham’s view works cleanly for the system and license cases but either fails or changes meaning for some of the others. If your definition draws a line between legal rights and something broader, tell me what those have in common such that both are called rights, or whether one use is just figurative. Then run your definition through all six cases and identify which verdict is least stable.
