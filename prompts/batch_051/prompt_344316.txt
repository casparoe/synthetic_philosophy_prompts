I serve on the longitudinal-programmes review panel of a national research council. Our current case is the renewal of a seven-year grant to a group in social-cognitive psychology. The group's core commitment has been that moral judgment is driven by low-level, easily manipulated features of a person's immediate environment. Their one bold, falsifiable prediction, that brief exposure to cleanliness cues makes people judge third-party transgressions more harshly, has now failed to replicate across three independent, pre-registered studies. Each time, the investigators revised the surrounding assumptions rather than the core claim: the cleanliness cue became a multisensory purity-salience composite, the effect was restricted to participants above a threshold on a disgust-sensitivity scale, a post hoc moderator involving vignette order was added, and in the renewal application the target is now described as a family of cue-action links that emerge only when several enabling conditions are jointly present, to be mapped in an exploratory phase. The panel is split. One faction sees a textbook Lakatosian degenerating problem-shift, kept alive by an ever more adjustable protective belt. The other faction replies that anomalies alone falsify nothing, that most healthy programmes look degenerating from up close, and that the group's measurement work has value independent of the central claim. Draft the panel's decision memo, recommending renewal, termination, or renewal with conditions, that applies Lakatos's distinction between progressive and degenerating research programmes, gives the panel operational criteria it could use again at the next review, and treats the strongest consideration on the losing side fairly.
