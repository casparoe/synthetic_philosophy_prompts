I'm a science journalist — I cover biology and the philosophy of science, mostly — and next month I'm giving a lunchtime talk in a biology department. The talk is called something like 'The selfish gene is a ledger, not a hypothesis', and my line is this: the gene's-eye view of Dawkins and Williams is best read as a bookkeeping convention for tracking what persists and spreads, not as a causal claim that genes literally do all the work or that organisms are mere vehicles. Read that way, the usual refutations — niche construction, epigenetic inheritance, developmental bias, plasticity-led adaptation — don't kill it; they just make the accounting messier by adding feedback loops. The real fight, I think, is whether the mess is better handled by a gene's-eye ledger or by something like multi-level selection or an organism-centred account.

The audience will be mostly evolutionary and developmental biologists, a lot of whom think gene-centrism is basically dead and care more about mechanism than about bookkeeping, plus two philosophers of biology who know the old Dawkins–Gould fight and are tired of it. I know I'll get some version of 'you've moved the goalposts', 'then it's unfalsifiable', and 'epigenetics gives you heritable variation that isn't in the genes'. But I want the full prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actualy get from this crowd, phrased the way they'd put them, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Say what I should concede up front and what I should hold, and include the embarassing question I'm hoping nobody asks.
