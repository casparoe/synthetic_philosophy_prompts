I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I recently read an article in a serious but non-specialist magazine about rational choice. It used a scenario called Death in Damascus, which it summarised roughly as follows. A man in Damascus learns that Death has an appointment with him there tomorrow, but Death is a reliable predictor: Death has made his appointment for whichever city, Damascus or Aleppo, he predicts the man will choose. The man knows all this, and he also knows he would rather die in Damascus than in Aleppo. The article says that evidential decision theory tells the man to stay in Damascus: staying is evidence that Death predicted he would stay, and therefore that he will die in Damascus, which is the preferable death, whereas fleeing to Aleppo is evidence that he will die in Aleppo, the worse death. The article then says this is a reductio of evidential decision theory, because the man would be choosing a course of action merely because it is bad news for him, not because it changes anything, and that causal decision theory avoids this by asking what the act causes rather than what it reveals. It concludes that the rational man should go to Aleppo, because staying in Damascus guarantees death if Death is in Damascus, whereas fleeing gives him a chance if Death is in Damascus.

I am not sure the article has represented the distinction correctly, and the conclusion feels too quick. I would be grateful if you would go through this summary and tell me what is accurate, what is a simplification, and what is simply wrong. In particular, does the Death in Damascus scenario really show what the article claims it shows about evidential and causal decision theory, or has the article conflated two different things under the heading of evidential decision theory? And what should the man in the scenario actually do according to each theory, on the most careful version of the case?

Please answer in British English.
