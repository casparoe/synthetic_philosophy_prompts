The debate between methodological individualism and holism in the social sciences is usually introduced as a single opposition: either social outcomes are to be explained in terms of individuals, or they are to be explained in terms of structures, institutions, and wholes. In practice, however, the label "individualism" covers at least two distinct commitments. One is an ontological or compositional claim: social entities such as markets, classes, and states are exhaustively constituted by individuals and their interactions, so there is no social substance beyond those individuals. The other is an explanatory claim: a satisfactory explanation of any social phenomenon must ultimately terminate in facts about individual agents—paradigmatically their beliefs, desires, decisions, and actions. A third, related commitment concerns modeling and evidence: aggregate variables are legitimate only insofar as they can be understood as shorthand for individual-level processes.

Compare the ontological and explanatory versions of individualism. Can a theorist coherently hold that social entities are nothing more than configurations of individuals, yet deny that every good explanation must bottom out in individual psychology—for example, by allowing explanations that cite relations, positions, endowments, or information asymmetries among individuals, which are structural even though they are facts about individuals? Or does adopting the compositional claim force one toward the explanatory claim as well? To make the comparison concrete, consider a bank run, in which individually reasonable withdrawals collectively produce a collapse. Locate where each version of individualism would situate the disagreement with holism, and say whether holism is best understood as the negation of individualism or as a separate thesis about which level of description can do genuine explanatory work.
