I chair my university's standing committee on conduct in virtual environments, which adjudicates alleged wrongdoing in the shared virtual worlds the university maintains for seminars, design studios, and student life. I am a philosopher by training, and I have come to think our decisions so far have avoided deciding whether we are punishing real-world harms that merely pass through virtual media, or recognizing that some virtual objects, spaces, and encounters can themselves be the site of a real wrong. Next week's case will force that question, and I would like help thinking through what our own record commits us to before I rule.

Our decisions so far:

- We found a student responsible for disruption after he used a world-editing tool to seal a classmate's avatar inside an inaccessible room during a required VR seminar; the classmate missed twenty minutes of discussion and reported feeling panicked while a technician undid the setting.
- We found a student not responsible after he demolished a decorative footbridge that three students had casually built on the public campus building server; the builders rebuilt it in an afternoon, said it had never been treated as theirs, and reported no distress.
- We found a student responsible for destroying virtual property after she deleted a classmate's only working copy of a semester-long digital archive from his private studio in the design world. A backup restored the underlying assets in minutes, but three weeks of annotations and version notes were unrecoverable.
- We found a student not responsible after, in a game-studies course whose rules expressly permitted destroying another player's character within a designated arena, he eliminated a classmate's character; the classmate lost the match and complained only that the move felt cheap.
- We found a student responsible for harassment after he produced a series of degrading scenes featuring a recognizable avatar of a classmate and left them where other residents of a student-run world would encounter them; the classmate stopped entering that world for the rest of the semester.

Next week's case: a student used an undocumented command to transfer to his own account a student organization's stall in the official allocation system of a campus social world. The group had not yet begun to set the stall up, and administrators restored it within minutes. No work was lost; the group's officers say they were unsettled by the fact that someone had briefly exercised control over a space they regarded as theirs.

How ought we rule if we are extending our own record rather than inventing a new standard? Which of the prior rulings is the nearest analogue, and what competing principles are consistent with the decisions above? If those principles diverge on the present facts, what principle would each outcome commit us to, and what further case would settle which one actually governs?
