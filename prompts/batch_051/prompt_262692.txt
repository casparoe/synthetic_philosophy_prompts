I keep loosing an argument with a friend in my reading group, and I can't tell if I'm actually beat or if he's pulling a fast one somewhere. The argument is supposed to show that once you take the meta-problem of consciousness seriously — the problem of explaining why physical creatures like us are so convinced there's a further "hard problem" about subjective experience — the hard problem should lose its force. He's numbered the moves and challenged me to say exactly where it goes wrong:

(1) If the meta-problem can be solved at all, then there's an explanation of our problem intuitions (that there's an explanatory gap, that zombies physically identical to us but with no experience are conceivable, that Mary learns something new on seeing red for the first time) couched entirely in physical and cognitive terms.
(2) An explanation couched entirely in physical and cognitive terms doesn't mention phenomenal properties, so it would explain the same intuitions even in a world where no phenomenal properties exist.
(3) So if the meta-problem is solvable, we'd have the same problem intuitions in a world without phenomenal properties.
(4) An intuition we'd also hold in a world where its content is false is not evidence for its content.
(5) So our problem intuitions aren't evidence that phenomenal properties exist.
(6) Those intuitions are the only evidence we have that phenomenal properties exist.
(7) Therefore, if the meta-problem is solvable, we have no evidence that phenomenal properties exist — and the hard problem turns out to be a trick our own minds play on us.

I like step (7) about as much as a root canal, so I want to find the crack. Steps (1) and (6) feel pretty tight, and (4) just seems like the definition of a debunking explanation. Is the argument valid? If not, tell me exactly which step fails, and how you'd have to rewrite it so that it actually says what it needs to say.
