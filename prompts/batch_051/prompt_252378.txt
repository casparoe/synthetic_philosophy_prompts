Two people in my discussion group got into an argument about a mutual friend who, when deciding between a modest holiday now and a more valuable holiday in retirement, said she discounted the later trip partly just because it is later: a benefit to her future self, she said, matters less to her simply because it lies in the future.

Rhea called this pure time preference and said it was irrational: once uncertainty, inflation, and opportunity cost are accounted for, the bare fact that a benefit is later is no better a reason to discount it than the fact that it would be experienced by someone spatially distant is a reason to discount that person's benefit. Sam replied that time is unlike space, because one's future self is psychologically less connected to one's present self, and concern should track that connectedness; since connectedness fades with time, some discounting is appropriate.

Rhea came back: if connectedness is the real ground, the discount should track measured degrees of connectedness rather than calendar time — so a future self who shares one's values, memories, and plans should be weighted nearly fully even decades on, while a nearby self in the grip of dementia or psychosis might be weighted less. Pure time preference gets this backwards. She added that the same principle should apply to future pains, in which case Sam would have to prefer a much larger pain next year to a small pain today whenever the discount is steep enough, which she finds absurd.

Sam answered that the dementia case actually supports him, since it shows we really do discount future selves with whom we share little; that connectedness and time are correlated enough for practical purposes; and that the pain case is not a reductio, because anticipation and dread are present costs that usually explain preferring to get pain over with, while in other cases postponing a larger pain is not obviously irrational. He added that Rhea was simply assuming temporal neutrality — the view that a benefit's date is irrelevant — rather than arguing for it.

Neither would budge. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say what each move gets right and wrong, identify anything both parties are missing, and then say which position is better supported overall.
