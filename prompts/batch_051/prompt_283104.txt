Ok here's something that's been bugging me. If a kid at my school sees someone struggling and feels bad for them, everyone says that's compassion and treats it as a sign of being a good person — we'd judge someone who felt nothing. But if the struggling kid finds out they're being felt bad for, they usually hate it: they get embarassed or even insulted ("I don't want your pity"), and if they lean into it or throw themselves a pity party, we respect them less. So the same feeling gets a flattering name from the side of the person having it and a demeaning one from the side of the person it's aimed at. Is there anything that actually justifies that asymmetry, or are we just being inconsistent about pity? In class we touched on Schopenhauer (compassion as basically the foundation of morality) and Nietzsche (pity is sneakily condescending and can be bad for the person pitied) — which one, if either, does a better job explaining why we're like this? Give me your honest verdict, then say what argument or consideration would most likely flip you the other way.
