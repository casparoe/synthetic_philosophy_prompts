I keep trying to reconstruct this argument I heard once about why rational people can't 'agree to disagree', and i want to know if it actually works.

(1) Suppose two people are perfectly rational Bayesians and they start from exactly the same prior.
(2) If they have the same prior and each updates on their own evidence, then any difference in their final probabilities is fully explained by a difference in the evidence each has seen.
(3) So if their evidence differs, they should be able to update toward each other by exchanging information. In particular, if each tells the other their final probability, and each knows the other knows, and so on, then each has now seen all the information that was keeping them apart.
(4) Therefore two rational Bayesians with a common prior whose probabilities are common knowledge must assign the same probability. They cannot actually disagree.
(5) So when you meet someone who claims to be rational, shares your basic assumptions, and still disagrees after you've each stated your view and talked it through, at least one of you is being irrational.

The jump from (3) to (4) feels like a magic trick, but I can't tell whether its valid and I'm just not seeing the proof, or whether some hidden assumption sneaks in. Is the argument valid? If not, which exact step fails and whats the corrected version that would actually go through?
