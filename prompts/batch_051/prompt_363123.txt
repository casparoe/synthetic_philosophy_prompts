I've been obsessing over what makes two things two rather than one ever since my kid dumped two identical-looking blocks out of a box and asked how many things were in it. The rule I keep trying to make work is a version of Leibniz's identity of indiscernibles: distinct things always differ in some feature. It handles two identical-looking apples on a table fine, and it handles a perfect, instantaneous duplicate of a coffee mug made an inch away from the original (two mugs for me, not one mug twice over). But I can't make it handle Max Black's thought experiment: two same-sized iron spheres, same material, same temperature, alone in an otherwise empty universe, some distance apart. I count two spheres there, even though I can't point to a feature one has and the other lacks. For the two electrons in a helium atom, where swapping them changes nothing about the atom, I still count two electrons. But here's the case that makes me think I'm missing something: a perfectly uniform rod alone in an empty universe. I do not want to say its left half and right half are two objects; it's one rod with two halves, even though the halves are exactly alike and the setup is symmetric in the same way as the sphere case. And the case I keep going back and forth on is two identical spheres just touching in an otherwise empty universe — sometimes it look like two spheres in contact, sometimes like one dumbbell-shaped thing with two lobes. Is there a single principle about identity, discernibility, or counting that gets all of these, or close? What does it say about the just-touching spheres, and which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? And is it cheating when I reach for properties like being that very sphere?
