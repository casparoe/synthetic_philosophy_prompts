In my work as a journalist covering decision science and behavioral economics, I keep returning to a dispute that appears never to move. Herbert Simon argued that real agents do not maximize expected utility; they satisfice — they search until an option clears an aspiration threshold and then stop. Critics have replied for decades that this is optimization in disguise: the satisficer is a maximizer who has folded the cost of continued search into the objective, and the aspiration threshold is simply the shadow value of one more search. Defenders counter that the two accounts become equivalent only because the optimization reading is fitted after the fact, and that satisficing carries distinct commitments — about how thresholds adapt, about order effects, about stopping rules that no fixed utility-plus-search-costs model reproduces without extra machinery. Each side ends up accusing the other of unfalsifiability, and the argument has circled for years. I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a survey of positions: is it a verbal disagreement about the word satisficing, a substantive disagreement about whether two computational accounts of choice are empirically separable, or two fields answering different questions under one label — one about the process an agent actually runs, the other about what an outside observer can rationalize? My own hypothesis is that it is mostly the last. Assess that hypothesis, and tell me what would count as progress given the correct diagnosis: an experiment the optimization account is forbidden from absorbing after the fact, a formal equivalence or non-equivalence result, or a change of question.
