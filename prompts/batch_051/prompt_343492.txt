ok, here's one that's been bouncing around my head since the last time i read the dutch book papers. Suppose that starting next year, in every jurisdiction that enforces ordinary commercial contracts, an open-source, well-capitalized bot — call it the Coherence Auditor — gets deployed at scale. Anyone can point it at a person, or at an AI agent that trades and makes choices, and the bot will make them a long series of binding offers designed to extract money whenever their revealed preferences violate the expected utility axioms. It handles intransitive cycles, money pumps from dynamic inconsistency, and independence violations, and it can chain offers creatively rather than just running one fixed trade. Crucially, it has a very good but not perfect model of the target's preferences, it is patient enough to wait for the right pair of choices, and there's a secondary market where you can buy futures on how much a given target gets pumped over the next year. Nobody is forced to accept any individual offer, but the offers keep coming indefinitely, and the law treats them like any other enforceable contract.

After twenty years, what does the population of economic agents actually look like? I don't just want the yes/no about whehter incoherent agents die out — obviously some people just refuse to trade and survive — I want the equilbrium. Which defenses do incoherent-but-smart agents settle into? Does a blanket policy of never accepting sequential offers work, or does the existence of the secondary market and the bot's patience make non-participation costly in ways that are themselves exploitable? Which axiom violations turn out to be genuinely costly to hold in this world, and which ones are survivable because the bot can almost never set up the required sequence in practice? Do we get a split where declared preferences all look coherent but people run a black market in preference laundering so their real revealed preferences never aggregate in one place? And what happens to the normative force of coherence arguments — do people stop saying don't be dutch-bookable as an argument for expected utility maximization and start treating it as just an environmental hazard you route around?

As you answer, flag which links in your prediction are robust versus guesses, and say which background assumption matters most: the bot's preference model being near-perfect, contracts being enforceable, or refusing to bet staying cheap. A good answer won't just describe the bot's mechanics — it should reason about incentives, adaptation, and where the scheme's own equilibrium point is.
