I'm a phd student in moral psychology, and I've been collecting my own blame judgments because they don't fit any clean view I was taught. Could you tell me what principle, if any, they're tracking?

1. Two drivers drink the same amount and both run a red light while texting. One crosses an empty intersection; the other kills a cyclist. I blame the second much more and think he deserves much more punishment, even though the only difference is luck.

2. Two people decide to shoot someone. The first's gun jams and nobody is hurt; the second's gun fires and the victim dies. Here I don't think the first deserves less blame, and I see the different legal sentences as a practical compromise, not a moral fact. So my gut treats these "bad outcome vs. no outcome" pairs differently from Case 1, and I can't say why.

3. Two managers each embezzle a few thousand dollars. One gets caught right away; the other gets away with it for years and only stops when the accounting software changes. I blame them about equally for the first theft, but something in me still treats the second as worse overall—even though continuing to have the chance to steal is itself luck, and he didn't go looking for new opportunities.

4. One person grows up in an abusive household, one in a stable one. Both commit the same serious assault under similar provocation. I blame the first somewhat less, because his self-control is worse, but I also think you dont normally get a discount for traits you didn't choose.

Is there one principle about when luck of outcome, opportunity, or constitution should change blame that delivers all four verdicts? If not, which verdicts should I drop, and what does the best candidate principle say about a case I didn't list: two bystanders both fail to save a drowning child they easily could have saved, but in one case a wave pushes the child to shore at the last second and in the other the child drowns—do I blame the second bystander more?
