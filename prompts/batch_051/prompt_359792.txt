I've been going in circles on an argument that came up in my metaethics group, and I want someone to tell me where it snaps. It starts from a pretty standard internalist thought about reasons and then extends it in a direction I'm not sure is allowed. The steps, roughly as she gave them:

(1) A consideration is a reason for me to do something only if, after sound deliberation from what I already care about, I could be moved by it. Otherwise it can't guide me, and a reason that can't guide me isn't a reason for me.

(2) Sound deliberation can do a lot, but all of it works on the motivational set I already have. It can correct factual mistakes, spell out means-end connections, resolve conflicts among my desires, and make vivid what satisfying a desire would actually feel like. What it can't do is conjure up a brand new ultimate concern out of nothing.

(3) So, when no deliberative path of that kind runs from my present motivational set to my being moved to do the thing in question, I have no reason to do it.

(4) Now take someone whose only ultimate concern is counting blades of grass. He isn't mistaken about anything; he just cares about exactly that. A stranger is drowning in front of him, and saving the stranger would cost him a minute and a wet shoe. Given (2) and (3), there's no path from grass counting to saving: saving doesn't advance the count, isn't a means to more counting, and doesn't resolve any conflict among his desires, since he has none.

(5) So he has no reason to save the stranger.

(6) That already feels like a stronger conclusion than anyone should want. But my groupmate says to sit with it, because the real trouble comes next. She says the same test applies to epistemic reasons. If someone's motivational set contains no concern for believing the truth—she isn't hostile to the truth, just indifferent—then by parity, no deliberative path from what she cares about leads to her wanting to scrutinize evidence or update on it.

(7) So she has no epistemic reason to believe what the evidence supports, however strong the evidence is.

(8) But believing what the evidence supports doesn't seem conditional on what you happen to want. Wanting the truth to be otherwise, or wanting not to know, doesn't seem to cancel the reasons the evidence gives you.

(9) So something has to give. But nearly every candidate hurts somewhere, and the argument gets its force from how obvious (1) and (2) looked before they started doing work. I'd most like to keep the guiding idea in (1), but I'd also like to keep the categorical force of evidence in (8).

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
