Back when I first read the eliminative materialist stuff, the pitch really did feel like a threat: ordinary talk about beliefs, desires, hopes, and intentions is just a theory (folk psychology), it hasn't improved much in thousands of years, and a mature neuroscience will probably replace its categories rather than reduce them, the way earlier sciences dropped things like phlogiston. The obvious comeback was to say we have direct introspective access to our beliefs, but that never felt super satisfying to me.

Anyway, I've been trying to reasses this now that computational neuroscience keeps talking about predictive processing and active inference. Crudely, the idea is that the brain is a hierarchy of generative models that carry around priors about hidden causes in the world and try to minimize prediction error; on the active inference version it also encodes preferred sensory states and acts to bring the world into line with them. That sounds like the framework has reinstated belief-ish and desire-ish constructs as load-bearing parts of the explanation. So my question is whether the original eliminativist argument survives this development. Can the eliminativist just say these are modeling shorthand or engineering abstractions, not folk concepts, and that they will get eliminated once we look at actual neural detail? Or is that an ad hoc retreat, and the real damage is to a specific premise of the old argument—say, that folk psychology is stagnant, or that nothing belief-like will survive in a mature science?

Put differently: if your best current theories of the brain end up positing structures that do roughly what beliefs and desires were supposed to do, is eliminative materialism dead, does it just need repair, or is the resemblance too shallow to matter? I'm less interested in the sociology of who calls themselves an eliminativist than in whether the argument itself still has teeth.
