I've been going back and forth with a friend who works on safety for autonomous systems about the responsibility gap — the classic worry that once a system learns and acts on its own, it gets hard to point at a designer, trainer, or operator and say that person was reckless, but if nobody's liable then victims are left holding the bag and manufacturers don't feel much pressure. My friend has a proposal he calls a guardianship market, and I want you to take it apart for me like an institutional design problem, not a moral one.

The scheme: every autonomous system above a certain capabilty threshold (can operate in public, move money, or take consequential actions without a human approving each step) must be registered to a licensed guardian firm. The guardian is the exclusive civil defendant for harms the system causes; the manufacturer or deployer chooses its guardian through an auction at deployment, where guardians bid by offering to take on that liability in exchange for an annual premium. Manufacturers who keep paying their premiums are immune from civil suit even for design defects, though criminal law still applies to humans who intentionally cause harm or deliberately disable safeguards. Guardians need a license, a minimum reserve, and a bond scaled to the total liability in their portfolio. The pitch is that guardians would then have every reason to audit systems, demand safer designs, and price risk well, because they eat the losses.

Walk me through how this actually behaves. What does it incentivize among manufacturers, guardians, and users once the auction is over? Who do guardians pressure, and what do they pressure them to do? Where does it get gamed first — shell guardians, reinsurance chains, manufacturers quietly owning their guardians, guardians offloading portfolios right before harms surface, collusion between guardians and deployers, or something else entirely? What happens when a guardian goes insolvent after a mass-casualty event? Does the scheme close the responsibility gap or just move it around, and can the failure modes be patched (capital requirements, clawbacks, mandatory rotation, public rating) without gutting the immunity that makes the whole thing attractive? Put yourself in the mindset of someone who does law-and-economics plus the philosophy of actionable responsibility; start by mapping the players and their payoffs, then think through the second-order effects carefully.
