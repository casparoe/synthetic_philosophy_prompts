I teach an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of mind seminar with fourteen students, in one seventy-five-minute session per week. In two weeks we start a unit on conceptual slippage in a microdomain, and I want the students to feel the phenomenon in their own reasoning before I name it, rather than hear me describe it.

By conceptual slippage I mean this: a concept that is stable and confidently applied inside a small, rule-governed domain slides into nearby, incompatible concepts as soon as the domain is stretched. A target sits at the edge of the rule's normal range; an input can be grouped in more than one natural way; two rules make all the same predictions on the easy cases and diverge only on a hard one. The person does not simply make an error. They revise the concept's boundaries midstream, switch which feature of the input counts as the relevant one, or redescribe the problem so that the old rule still seems to cover it—and they sincerely report that they applied the same idea throughout.

Design a runnable seventy-five-minute activity built around letter-string analogy problems: short strings of capital letters, a solved example such as abc mapping to abd, and a target such as ijk. Construct a fresh set of items; do not presuppose any named system from the analogy literature, and do not require any prior reading. The session should first let small groups converge on a confident rule for a small set of easy items, then confront them with probe items that force the rule to slide, while a scoring and recording system keeps their successive redescriptions publicly visible. The students should run into the slippage themselves: the rules, incentives, and record-keeping must push them to commit to a single stated rule before seeing the probes, and then to keep an unedited public record of every patch, exemption, or redescription they make after each probe. No answer should be treated as canonical, and no real money should be used.

They should come away seeing that the feeling of applying the same concept all along is compatible with the concept's actually sliding under one, and that the boundaries of a concept are partly a product of which hard cases it has been forced to handle—not something the easy cases reveal.

Include all of the following.

1. An exact materials list, including printable content for cards, worksheets, and any shared record sheet, sized for fourteen students working in groups of three or four.
2. A minute-by-minute timeline for the seventy-five-minute session, with clear roles within each group—for example, a rule-writer, a challenger, and a record keeper—so that no single student controls the record.
3. The full set of strings: six to eight seed items on which the groups train, and six to eight probe items, each probe accompanied by a one-sentence rationale naming the kind of slippage it is meant to induce, whether boundary of the rule's normal range, grouping ambiguity, competition between two rules that were equivalent on the seeds, or a shift in the level of description from individual letters to groups of letters.
4. The scoring rules. The scoring should reward groups for committing before the probes and for accurately predicting what a different group will answer, but it should not punish any particular answer as wrong. It should also make silent changes costly: a group that quietly alters its rule should lose points unless it records the change before giving the new answer.
5. A debrief. It should begin by reconstructing the sequence of moments from the run rather than lecturing, and should map specific moments to specific parts of the phenomenon: the initial overconfidence, the first patch, the first time a group denies that a probe still counts as the same problem, the switch from letter-level to group-level description, and the moment a group borrows another group's rule and loses its own.
6. A troubleshooting section saying what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure. Include at least the student who insists the whole exercise is just ambiguous instructions, the group that freezes rather than patch, and the student who announces the moral of the exercise halfway through.
7. An honest closing note on which feature of conceptual slippage the letter-string activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself after the debrief.
