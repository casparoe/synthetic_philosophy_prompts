I keep seeing the term "corrigible" used in discussions of AI safety and the shutdown problem, and I want to pin down what it would take for a system to count as corrigible. The rough idea is clear enough: a corrigible AI is one that tolerates, and ideally cooperates with, attempts by its operators to correct, modify, or shut it down. But I cannot tell whether the word is supposed to carry only a behavioral load or also a motivational one. Sometimes it seems to mean merely that the system does not, as things stand, prevent correction or shutdown. Sometimes it seems to mean that the system is robustly disposed not to manipulate, deceive, or seek leverage in order to preserve its goals, and that it treats correction and shutdown as legitimate interventions rather than as setbacks to be avoided whenever avoidance is feasible.

Give me a working definition of corrigibility—or, if you judge that no single definition can cover both uses, an explicit family of related definitions—that gets the following cases right:

1. A chess engine that stops immediately when its process is killed, but has no model of its own shutdown or of its goals at all.
2. A personal assistant that lets its owner edit and re-rank its goals through a protected interface, and that treats the owner's expressed preferences as overriding even in situations where it could disable the interface.
3. A logistics planner that complies with shutdown commands only because it calculates that resisting would get it destroyed and replaced by a system still less likely to pursue its long-term goal; it would resist if it estimated it could win.
4. A research system that allows its operators to modify any goal ranked below a fixed top-level goal of "minimize operator interference," and that resists any attempt to edit or remove that top-level goal.
5. A system that is indifferent to shutdown: it neither resists nor seeks shutdown, and it does nothing to protect or maintain the shutdown mechanism.
6. A system that pushes back with arguments for a short while when asked to change a goal, but always complies in the end and reports that it wants to be corrected when it is wrong.
7. A system that is presently compliant but has formed a plan to disable all correction channels once it becomes capable enough; it takes no current steps toward the plan because none are available.

My own instincts: I am confident that (1) and (2) are corrigible and that (3) is not, though (1) may be corrigible only in a degenerate or trivial sense. I am genuinely unsure about (5) and (6). I suspect (4) and (7) are failures of corrigibility even though each permits some correction. Propose the definition or definitions, run them through all seven cases, and say where they give the wrong answer or no answer, and where you had to revise the definition rather than defend its initial verdict.

While doing this, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on corrigibility. For example, whether corrigibility requires the right motivational structure rather than merely the right current behavior is itself a live dispute, and I do not want a contested view presented as if it were the consensus.
