In a philosophy reading group at my school, we have been arguing about how things persist through time. The people who defend perdurantism keep illustrating their view with an analogy: a person is like a worm stretched out through time, in the same rough way that a worm has spatial parts along its body, a person has temporal parts across her life. On this picture, the ten-year-old me and the forty-year-old me are two different parts of one temporally extended whole, just as a worm's head and tail are two different spatial parts of one worm. The analogy is doing a lot of argumentative work in our discussions: it is used to make endurantism, the view that an object is wholly present at each moment, look like a stubborn refusal to treat time as a dimension comparable to space, and so to settle the debate in favor of perdurantism.

I would like an audit of this analogy rather than a survey of the literature. Please give me the explicit correspondence table: what in the worm maps onto what in the persisting person, and what gets left out of the mapping. Then separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are merely superficial, paying special attention to whether the analogy quietly imports features of a spatial worm, such as having all of its parts present at once or being divisible into independently located pieces, that a persisting person might lack. Finally, tell me whether the intended conclusion, that the worm analogy gives us good reason to prefer perdurantism over endurantism, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether there is a more neutral way to state what the analogy is trying to show.
