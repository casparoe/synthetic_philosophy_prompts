I've been teaching a graduate seminar on twentieth-century critical theory, and we're about to do the culture industry. The students always come in with the standard reading — that Adorno and Horkheimer thought mass culture was bad because it was formulaic, repetitive, and turned listeners and viewers into passive consumers — and I dont think that reading survives contact with streaming, where everyone's feed is personalized and the catalog looks endless. I want to try out a different reconstruction, and I'd like you to make the case for it as forcefully as you can.

Here's the thesis: the real object of the culture industry argument isn't the standardization of cultural products but the industrialization of receptivity itself. What gets manufactured is not so much the song or the show as a particular way of attending to it — pre-scripted cues, genres that do the anticipating for you, the background sense that your response is already included in the product. On this reading, algorithmic personalization doesn't refute the culture industry; it completes it, because the management and prediction of attention becomes the actual product. Argue that this is the more faithful and more useful version of the thesis, deal with the obvious objection that Adorno's own examples (jazz, radio, the assembly-line film) were backward-looking and medium-specific, and end by saying what would count as evidence against your interpretation.
