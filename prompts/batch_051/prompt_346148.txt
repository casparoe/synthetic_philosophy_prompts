I've been rereading George Eliot's early essays alongside a few of the novels, and I keep running into a move that gets treated as obvious: the claim that once you stop believing Christian doctrine literally, you can keep what matters by treating religious language as poetry or expressive myth. On this view, God is a symbol for the collective human capacity for sympathy and fellow-feeling, prayer is an imaginative discipline of attention, and the Incarnation is a story that dramatizes how the ideal can appear in the finite — none of which requires supernatural facts. People in my reading group use this to argue that Christianity can be fully naturalized without losing its ethical core. Eliot's translation of Feuerbach's The Essence of Christianity is often cited in support, since Feuerbach treats theology as a disguised anthropology: the predicates believers give to God are really estranged predicates of the human species, and the task is to recover them for humanity. But then I read her long critical essay on Edward Young, Worldliness and Other-Worldliness: The Poet Young, and she seems to be saying that the otherworldly, heaven-directed side of Christian poetry is not harmless expression at all — it trains people to devalue the finite, concrete lives where the actual moral work is done.

I would like you to audit the analogy that religious language is poetry as a salvage operation for Christianity after literal belief has gone, using Eliot as the test case. Give me the correspondence table: what corresponds to the poet, the poem, the audience, the reading experience, and truth-in-fiction when religious language is moved over to poetry? What does the analogy get right, and what does it quietly import from the aesthetic frame that religion lacks — for instance about suspension, optionality, and the absence of commitment? Where does it break most decisively when the religious language is not only expressive but prescriptive, communal, or history-claiming? And does Eliot's attack on Young show that she thought the poetry analogy was insufficient, or only that she thought some Christian poetry is ethically bad poetry? Finally, tell me how much of the project — keep the affects and ethics, discard the metaphysics — actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table.
