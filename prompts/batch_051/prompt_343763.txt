I am a former museum collection manager now writing a historical study of how taxonomic categories get stabilized, and I keep noticing a pattern I do not have a stable term for. When a group of organisms is classified by two independent evidential bases—say, a molecular phylogeny and a morphological one—the resulting groupings sometimes line up in a striking way. Where they agree, researchers routinely treat that agreement as evidence that the group is real, not merely a filing convenience. Where the two bases disagree, the taxonomy becomes unstable, and people argue about which evidential standard should have priority. I can also think of practical classifications made for two unrelated purposes that agree for trivial reasons, for instance because both inherit the same historical or institutional bias, and nobody takes that agreement as evidence of anything deep. So the pattern is specifically this: agreement across independently motivated classification schemes is treated as evidence of a mind-independent division, even before anyone can explain why the two schemes should agree. Is there an established name for this inference, or for the principle that such convergence licenses a claim about the world rather than about our conventions? I know it is somewhere near older ideas of natural classification and near talk of carving nature at the joints, and somewhere near consilience in the history of science, but I want the exact conceptual edges. How does the taxonomic case differ from consilience, where independent lines of evidence support a theory rather than a grouping? What would a good answer need to say about the trivial-agreement cases, and where does the inference fail? If there is no established name for exactly this, what would you call it, and how would you define it so that it does not collapse into the nearby concepts?
