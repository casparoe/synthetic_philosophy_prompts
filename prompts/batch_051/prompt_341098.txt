Last night a friend and I argued for hours about Hempel's raven paradox, and we both got stuck on a variant that came up near the end of the argument. Please act as a philosopher of science with solid working knowledge of formal confirmation theory. Before you commit to a conclusion, briefly brainstorm at least two different ways one might model the added noise, then choose the approach you judge most faithful to the original puzzle and work through it carefully, step by step.

Here is the standard setup we started from. Let H be the hypothesis that all ravens are black, and let H' be its contrapositive, that all non-black things are non-ravens. Nicod's criterion says that a generalization of the form All F are G is confirmed by its positive instances, the things that are both F and G. A red apple is a positive instance of H'. So, granted the equivalence condition—that logically equivalent hypotheses are confirmed by the same evidence—a red apple should confirm H. But intuitively a red apple seems to tell us nothing about ravens, or at most something negligible. That tension is the paradox.

Now here is the variant we could not settle. Suppose an observer never sees objects directly and has access only to a noisy classifier that outputs two labels: color, black or non-black, and species, raven or non-raven. The classifier is applied to a single object drawn uniformly at random from a finite population of N objects. The color output is flipped with probability ε: a black object is reported black with probability 1 − ε and non-black with probability ε, and symmetrically for a non-black object. The species output is flipped with probability δ. The two error channels are independent, the error rates ε and δ each lie strictly between 0 and 1, and the observer knows all of this.

The observer also knows the population composition: exactly R of the N objects are ravens, and among the N − R non-ravens exactly B are black. Let A be N − R − B, the number of non-black non-ravens. The observer is choosing between two hypotheses:

- H0: all R ravens are black.
- H1: exactly one raven is non-black, and the other R − 1 ravens are black.

The classifier reports the output pair non-black, non-raven for the drawn object. Call this report E.

Please work through the following questions.

1. Derive the likelihood ratio P(E | H0) / P(E | H1). It may help to set out the four possible true states of the drawn object—black raven, non-black raven, black non-raven, and non-black non-raven—and the probability that each state gives rise to report E. Does report E confirm H0 over H1, disconfirm it, or leave it unchanged? Does the direction of the evidential effect depend on ε, δ, N, R, B, and A, and if so how?

2. Compare this with the noiseless analogue, ε = δ = 0, where the observer directly sees that the drawn object is non-black and non-raven. Does that observation confirm H0 over H1 in your setup? What does the comparison reveal about the role Nicod's criterion is being asked to play in the original paradox?

3. Consider Hempel's equivalence condition in the noisy version. H0 and its contrapositive, that all non-black things are non-ravens, are still logically equivalent, so any shift in support for one must be a shift in support for the other. Is the equivalence condition doing real work in this variant, or has the noise moved the pressure somewhere else in the original argument?

4. Step back and explain how this variant bears on the original raven paradox. Does adding known measurement noise merely weaken the standard conclusion, preserve it, or reverse it? Which of the familiar diagnoses of the raven paradox, if any, looks strongest in light of your result?

Be explicit about any auxiliary assumptions you need beyond those stated, especially about how the alternative H1 is understood, and flag any point where a different auxiliary assumption would change the answer.
