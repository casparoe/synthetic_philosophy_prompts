I spent my career as a natural scientist, and have only recently begun reading philosophy seriously. Lately I have been working through the debate about repugnant transactions and commodification—the idea that some goods or relationships are corrupted by being bought and sold, with stock examples including kidneys, votes, and emergency goods after a disaster. I would like to read Kazuo Ishiguro's novel Never Let Me Go through that lens. In the novel, children are cloned and raised so that their organs can be removed in a series of operations until they die, something the book euphemizes as completion. No money is offered for these organs, and the practice seems to be socially accepted. Does the novel condemn commodification as such, or does its horror lie elsewhere—in instrumentalization, coercion, deception, or the creation of a class of donors? Point to specific scenes or features of the book, and say where the novel complicates or resists the anti-commodification reading rather than merely illustrating it. Conclude with what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not capture.
