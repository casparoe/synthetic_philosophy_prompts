I spent Saturday evening arguing with a friend who works in health policy, and who could not see why a public research council should fund a next-generation cosmic microwave background survey — or cosmology at all — while the same money could run clinical trials. I found myself retreating to familiar slogans: knowledge is worth having for its own sake, spin-off technologies arrive unpredictably, basic research is a public good that markets underprovide. He pressed back that none of this tells us what to do with this proposal, on this afternoon, when the alternative is a hospital ward rather than another telescope. I sit on the astronomy panel of a public research council, and we make exactly these decisions six times a year under real constraints: roughly twenty minutes of discussion per proposal, a panel of working scientists rather than philosophers, a ranking list that commits public money, and a main board that signs off but does not re-argue the merits. The argument with my friend exposed that we have no explicit procedure — only instincts, rhetoric, and a vague deference to "curiosity-driven research". I would like that fixed.

Please turn the best available thinking about the value of knowledge without application into a decision procedure my panel could actually run when a proposal has no foreseeable use. Ground it in whatever philosophy of science and value theory can genuinely defend, but keep it usable by scientists with no philosophical background. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "no", and where it hands the decision to a person or another body because the question is one of values rather than facts. Mark each step at which judgement cannot be eliminated. Then apply the procedure to at least two representative cases: a large ground-based cosmic microwave background survey whose science case concerns only the early universe, and a space-based gravitational-wave observatory with substantial engineering novelty but no application on any horizon; make one of the applications a case where the procedure tells me or the panel something we would rather not hear.

Two requests. First, separate what is genuinely settled about the value of pure research from what remains contested among people who actually study this, and do not present a contested philosophical position as the consensus. Second, begin with the one-sentence spine of the procedure, then give the full procedure and the applications, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
