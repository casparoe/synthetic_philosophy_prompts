I've ended up defending a view I used to think was obviously stupid: that brain size is morally relevant, at least across species. Not as a cutoff, but as evidence about how much mental life an animal probably has. The clean version: if I have to choose between a species with baseball-sized brains and a species with poppy-seed-sized brains, and I know nothing else, I should expect far more going on in the first, and that expectation should affect how I treat them. People always bring up crows and parrots, but I take those as showing size is not everything, not that it is nothing, and anyway what's doing the work might be neuron count, or cortical neuron count, or encephalization, not raw mass per se. So the position is: brain size is morally relevant evidence. Tell me what would count against it. I don't just want the standard bird counterexample; I want the full map of exposure. Which observations or results would be fatal, which would merely force me to qualify the view, and which should a defender rightly shrug off? What does the view predict about a newly discovered species that a pure demonstrated-capacity view would not? At what point would I have to admit it's been padded with 'other things equal' and 'as a weak prior' until it can't be wrong, and is being unfalsifiable in that way actually a problem for a view whose job might just be to set priors rather than decide cases? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which a committed defender would concede as counting against them.
