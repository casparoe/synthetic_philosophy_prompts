I'm a journalist who covers biology and AI, and I keep running into a claim that sounds either like the deepest idea I've heard this year or a category error. A honeybee researcher told me insects probably have valenced, pain-like states and that refusing to call it sentience is just chauvinism; an AI-safety person told me reinforcement-learning systems have tiny slivers of something like valenced processing; and someone on a philosophy podcast argued that even a thermostat doing error-correction sits on the same scale as a mammal in pain, just at the faint end. The through-line is: sentience is a graded property — a dimmer, not a switch — and there's no natural joint between "just homeostatic regulation" and "real feeling."

I want to know whether that claim is testable and what a test would actually settle. Act as an expert in philosophy of mind and comparative cognition. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming at least three different things "graded" could mean here (graded intensity? graded integration? graded behavioral flexibility? something else?). Then design the strongest empirical test you can think of that would tell the dimmer view apart from the switch view. Be concrete: which systems would you compare (thermostat-like controllers, invertebrates, vertebrates), what would you measure, and what pattern of results would favor a continuum versus a threshold? Then do the philosophical post-mortem. If the continuum side won on the data, has sentience been shown to be graded, or only the capacities you measured? Could a thermostat redesigned to be flexible and re-prioritizing pass your test while still feeling nothing? Does the test smuggle in a behavioral criterion for sentience that the threshold theorist is allowed to reject? In short: which parts of the claim are empirical, which parts are a decision about how to use a word, and is the original dimmer claim one of the empirical parts?
