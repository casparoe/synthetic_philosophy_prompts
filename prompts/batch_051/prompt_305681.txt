I’m preparing a seminar discussion on Rousseau’s social criticism, and I want to build the session around an apparent tension in the Letter to M. d’Alembert on the Theatre. Early in the letter, Rousseau argues that a theater cannot improve a people’s morals because it must please the taste it finds: dramatists study what an audience already approves and mirror it back, so the stage flatters rather than reforms the inclinations it depicts. Later in the same letter, however, he describes the theaters of Paris as institutions that occupy the idle, draw fashionable young people away from occupations he considers more dangerous, and supply a comparatively harmless pleasure in a corrupted city. The first line of argument seems to say theater is at best morally inert and at worst a reinforcer of existing vices; the second seems to assign it a protective or palliative function, at least in a corrupt metropolis. Are these two claims consistent? Reconstruct the argument that carries Rousseau from one to the other, and say whether the consistency depends on a distinction between the function of theater in a republic like Geneva and in a monarchy like Paris, a distinction between direct moral instruction and indirect social control, or on some other distinction he may not have drawn explicitly. Give a verdict on whether the apparent conflict dissolves, and at what cost: does the consistency require treating one of the passages as a concession rather than a genuine theoretical claim about theater? Please answer in American English.
