I've been trying to get straight what I should even be asking for from an account of what an optimizer is optimizing. "Objective", "utility function", "reward", "search space" all get thrown around like they're obvious, and I keep noticing people in alignment conversations talk past each other because they're smuggling in different criteria. So before I read any more of the specific proposals (revealed preference, inverse RL, intentional stance, whatever), I want the desiderata. If I meet a theory that claims to infer, from behavior, what a system is optimizing and over what search space, what should I require it to get right before I trust it? The use-case I care about is deciding what I'm allowed to say about a system after watching it: whether it's an optimizer at all, what objective I should attribute to it, and what search space it's actualy searching, as opposed to the one I'd project onto it.

Make the list concrete: for each desideratum, give the case or setup that forces it, and mark which requirements are load-bearing and which are negociable. The list has to arbitrate messy cases, not wave at them. A few I suspect belong on it, but I want your version rather than just mine: separating objective from search space from competence, because a system can be searching the right space with a fine objective and still lose if its search is too small or too shallow; the proxy case, where on all the training behavior "it was optimizing the real target" and "it was optimizing a correlated proxy" make identical predictions and only diverge out-of-distribution, so the account has to say what evidence would tell them apart; nested optimizers, like evolution building a brain that plans for status while the brain's reward circuitry is doing its own thing, so whose objective is "the" objective; the degenerate case, where the account needs a rule that decides whether a thermostat is in and a river is out; and the cheat where someone defines a utility function that assigns 1 to the actual history and 0 to everything else and then says the behavior was optimal. If any two requirements can't be jointly satisfied — and I'd be surprised if at least one pair can't be — flag it and say which one you'd drop. Then, briefly, run two or three existing approaches against the list (revealed preference, "the objective is the literal reward signal", "objectives are just compressed descriptions of the policy", or something adjacent) so I can see the list doing work.
