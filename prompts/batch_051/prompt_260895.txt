I work as a programmer rather than a philosopher, and much of my own thinking runs through a vocabulary of recursive definitions, base cases, termination proofs, inference rules versus premises, well-founded orderings, and the difference between productive co-recursive definitions and plain non-terminating loops. I have never formally studied philosophy. I recently read Lewis Carroll's short dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles," and I can feel that it is making a precise point about infinite regresses, but I cannot map that point onto anything in my own toolkit. The passage that especially troubles me is this:

"You should call it D, not Z," said Achilles. "It comes next to the other three. If you accept A and B and C, you must accept Z."
"And why must I?"
"Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"
"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"
"You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."
"Very good. I'm quite willing to grant it, as soon as you've written it down. We will call it
(D) If A and B and C are true, then Z must be true.
Have you entered that in your note-book?"

Philosophers apparently distinguish vicious from benign infinite regresses, and it seems to be a live question which feature of a regress makes it vicious: that it would require infinitely many steps to finish, that it deprives a chain of justification of a starting point, that it makes an explanation circular, that it is structurally impossible, or something else again. I would like you to restate that whole issue as systematically as you can in the vocabulary I know from programming: recursive definitions and their base cases, inference rules versus terms and premises, termination checking, productivity of co-recursive structures, lazy infinite data, induction versus coinduction, and well-founded orderings. Then give me a residue report: which candidate accounts of viciousness translate cleanly into that vocabulary, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all. In particular, tell me whether the Tortoise's regress, on the best programmatic translation, behaves more like an ordinary non-terminating loop, a productive co-recursive stream, a missing base case, or something more subtle. I want to know whether my working intuition that "vicious" more or less means "non-terminating" survives contact with the actual debate, and whether the move of treating modus ponens as an inference rule rather than a premise is really just the distinction I already know as that between rules and terms. Please write your answer in British English.
