A friend and I have just spent a long evening arguing about a question that runs back to Plato's Meno: if a true belief and knowledge direct action equally well — the correct turn guides you no less surely than the explained turn — why is knowledge prized so much more, and is that heightened value warranted? By the end my friend's position was that the extra value is largely institutional cosmetics. What courts, investors, and voters really want, he argued, is decisions that come out right; the further demand that a person also be able to produce reasons is a gatekeeping habit that would wither if true beliefs became cheap and reliable. I do not think it would wither; I think it would reassert itself in a sharper form.

The argument eventually became a thought experiment, and I would like your reasoned prediction of how it plays out. In 2035 a safe, inexpensive neural implant reaches the market with the following property. Whenever a wearer must settle a factual matter with a determinate answer — whom to hire, what caused a bridge to fail, whether a drug is effective, what a contract obliges, whether a model's forecast is sound — the implant generates a firm belief in whichever of the wearer's live alternatives is in fact true; if the true proposition is not among those alternatives, the implant remains silent. The belief arrives as a bare hunch: nothing like evidence or inference accompanies it, there is no explanatory narrative, and the wearer cannot say why she holds it except that the implant prompted it. By stipulation the implant is always correct. Early adopters are dismissed as credulous, but by 2060 the device's reliability has been publicly established, and by 2085 it is used as unreflectively as corrective eyewear in much of the world.

Please reason through the fifty-year arc in terms of the differential value attached to knowledge as against mere true belief. Trace the likely effects on at least these sites: the rules of evidence and expert testimony in courts; scientific publication, peer review, and the training of researchers; education from primary school through professional certification; everyday relations of trust, deference, and accountability; and the way people assign intellectual credit and blame. For each effect, distinguish what is robust from what is conjectural, identify the mechanisms and incentives that produce it, and state what would have to be different for the prediction to fail. Also consider whether the device's known reliability would transform its bare hunches into knowledge of a new kind, or leave them as true beliefs that now compete with knowledge on unequal terms, and say whether, by 2085, the old Meno problem has been dissolved or given a harder institutional form.
