I'm a grad student in a practical-reason seminar and we spent an entire meeting on Buridan's ass. You know the standard version: a donkey exactly halfway between two identical bales of hay, equally hungry, with no more reason to go left than right, so it can't decide and starves. The usual replies are (1) it's fine to just pick, since either option is permissible when they're tied, and (2) the puzzle only works if you sneak in the false premise that a rational chooser needs a uniquely best option. But it struck me that the setup builds in *equality*, and equality isn't the same as a hard choice. So here's the variation I've been toying with and would like you to actually work through, not just gesture at. Make the two bales non-identical: the left one is much bigger but dry and old, the right one is small but fresh. The donkey isn't indifferent between them; the two dimesnions (volume and freshness) just don't sit on a single scale for it. In Ruth Chang's terms the bales might be 'on a par' — comparable in some loose way, but neither is better, neither is worse, and they aren't equal — or maybe they're flatly incommensurable. My question: do the two standard replies to the original Buridan case still work in this version, or does something break? In particular, is 'just pick one' doing the same thing here as it does in a tie, or is the donkey committing to a value rather than breaking a symmetry? And does the variation end up showing that the original ass is a bad model for the phenomenon of hard choices, because it was about indifference all along? I'd like the answer to state, for each of the standard replies, what it's forced to say in the modified case and whether that's plausible.
