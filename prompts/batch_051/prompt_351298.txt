Our philosophy of biology reading group has been stuck on the function debate for three weeks, and I'm starting to think the two sides aren't actually contradicting each other. The etiological (selected-effects) people say the function of a trait is the effect it was selected for: a heart's function is to pump blood because pumping blood is why hearts got selected, so a trait's function is an inherently historical property. That's why they'll say a vestigial organ can have no function even when it's doing something now, and why an exaptation's current use isn't its function. The causal-role people say that gets the actual explanatory practice wrong: biologists assign functions by asking what a part contributes to a system capacity here and now, with no selection history needed. A molecule-for-molecule identical creature that popped into existence with no evolutionary history would still have a heart whose function is to pump blood, and they take that to be decisive. Everyone agrees about normal hearts; the fights only start with vestiges, exaptations, novel mutations, artifacts, and the no-history creature. My working theory is that these are two different jobs — explaining why a trait is there versus explaining how the organism works — being run under one word, so no amount of biological evidence will settle it. But that could be to quick, and maybe there's a real factual dispute underneath about whether function is historical. What kind of disagreement is this actually, and given your diagnosis, what would count as real progress?
