I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and in my reporting on machine reasoning I keep meeting a claim that sits ambiguously between mathematics and doctrine. The claim is that there is a Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents. Informally, Löb's theorem says that in any formal system strong enough to represent its own provability, if the system can prove, for some proposition P, that its own proof of P would entail P, then the system can already prove P. The advertised moral is stronger: a reasoner cannot establish the general reliability of its own reasoning without assuming what it is trying to show or collapsing into triviality, so a self-verifying agent must take its own soundness as an unproven article of faith; self-trust cannot be earned from inside. I have seen this used to explain everything from the difficulty of aligning powerful systems to why human reasoners need not regard themselves as rationally required to prove that they are not systematically deluded. Yet I have never seen a satisfying account of how one would know whether the formal theorem is doing the explanatory work, rather than serving as a vivid analogy that imports a proof-theoretic result into settings where the derivability conditions may not hold.

I would like to press on that gap. Design the best concrete test you can of the claim that a Löbian obstacle really constrains a class of real reasoners. The test may be computational, behavioural, or a hybrid: for instance, you might take a theorem prover or a language-model agent and ask it to certify its own conclusions, comparing a condition in which it is supplied with an explicit self-soundness rule against one in which it is not; or you might train an agent to monitor its own errors and measure whether its performance shows a sharp ceiling that tracks its representational strength. Specify the setup in enough detail that a research group could implement it without further conceptual decisions. Then state which outcomes would count as evidence that a principled Löbian obstruction is at work, which outcomes would instead suggest that the apparent obstacle is an artifact of a particular formalization or engineering choice, and, finally, what even a clean positive result could not settle about self-trust in natural reasoners or in agents whose inference is probabilistic, bounded, or poorly modelled by a proof system. I am not asking for a literature survey or bibliography; I want the protocol and a careful account of what it can and cannot show.
