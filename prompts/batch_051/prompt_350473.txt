I've been going back and forth with my roommate about the dark forest hypothesis — the idea from Liu Cixin's science fiction that the universe is quiet because any civilization that gives away its position gets wiped out in a preemptive first strike. The way he sets it up, it runs off a couple of axioms: survival is the first need of any civilization, civilizations keep expanding while matter stays fixed, trust across light-years can't be established (that's the chain-of-suspicion part), and technology can explode suddenly, so when you detect anyone the only safe move is to shoot first and stay hidden. That's supposed to explain the Great Silence, and it gets wheeled out whenever people argue about whether we should be doing METI.

The part I can't get past is this. Over the last decade or so we've gotten good enough at atmospheric spectroscopy of exoplanets that detecting biosignatures isn't hypothetical anymore — and that makes an old fact newly relevant: Earth has been putting out an oxygen-rich atmosphere since around the Great Oxidation Event, well over two billion years ago. Any hunter advanced enough to sterilize a rival would presumably be able to read planetary atmospheres first, so they'd have spotted us long before we had radio, let alone spacecraft. If dark-forest logic were right, shouldn't Earth have been preemptively silenced back in the Proterozoic? We're still here. So does the hypothesis die, or does it just have to retreat to saying only technosignatures count while biosignatures get a pass? And if it retreats that far, does it still explain the Great Silence, or has it quietly stopped doing the work it was advertised for? I'm trying to figure out exactly which premise the biosignature point bites on, and whether the argument can be salvaged in some narrower form or needs to be replaced by a different answer to the Fermi paradox.
