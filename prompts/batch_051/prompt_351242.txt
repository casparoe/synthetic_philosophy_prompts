Suppose a medium-sized global health fund adopts an explicit scoring rule for grants, heavily inspired by threshold negative utilitarianism. The committee's rule is: (1) any intervention whose main effect is to reduce or prevent suffering below a "severe" threshold — a fixed list of conditions like agonizing chronic pain, certain kinds of severe depression with no access to treatment, end-stage illness without palliative care — automatically outranks every intervention that doesn't, no matter how much measured wellbeing the other one produces; (2) above the threshold, reductions in pain and gains in pleasure are treated as commensurable and traded off on a shared scale; (3) when two proposals both work below the threshold, they're ranked by some weighted combination of how many people they bring below the line and how far below the line they bring them; and (4) the board revises the "severe" list every three years by majority vote.

Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position. Walk me through how it actualy plays out. What does it do to the kinds of proposals applicants write and how they describe their beneficiaries? Where does the scheme get gamed first — gaming of the list, gaming of the measurement, gaming of the bundling? What happens over several review cycles to the boundary between "severe" and "not severe," and to the committee's own behavior? Pay attention to the hard cases: uncertainty about which side of the line someone's in, one person far below the line versus a very large number of people just above it, and interventions that partly reduce severe pain and partly produce pleasure. Does the lexical character actually survive contact with a working bureaucracy, or does it slowly turn into ordinary cost-benefit analysis with more dramatic grant-writing? And can the failure modes be patched without gutting the rule? Write the answer in American English.
