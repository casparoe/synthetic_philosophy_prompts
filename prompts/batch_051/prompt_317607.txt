The position I want examined is constitutive micropsychism about consciousness, and it is one I hold. Stated positively: the fundamental constituents of the world have experiential states of some kind; the experiences of ordinary subjects — yours, mine, a dog's — are not caused from outside by non-experiential matter; and there is no macro-experiential fact over and above the micro-experiential facts together with whatever arrangement relation binds them. I deliberately leave that arrangement relation unspecified; specifying it is, on my view, the central unfinished task, not a trivial detail to be waved away.

I grant that the combination problem is the obvious first-pass objection, but I am not asking for it to be simply replayed. I want a complete map of what would count against the position as I have stated it. Separate, where they behave differently, the subject-summing problem (how could many subjects yield one subject?), the palette problem (how could a limited range of micro-phenomenal qualities yield the full variety of macro-experience?), the structural mismatch problem (why does unified macro-experience not mirror the discrete structure of the micro-level?), and the boundary problem (what fixes which micro-subjects constitute which macro-subject?). Then go beyond those standard pressure points: which possible cases, or unnerving variations on actual cases, would strain the position most; which results in neighbouring areas — mereological composition, personal identity, the unity of consciousness, grounding and the metaphysics of emergence — would damage or strengthen it; and which empirical developments outside philosophy would make it worse off even though it is not an empirical theory.

For each candidate defeater, tell me whether a committed defender of the position should concede that it counts against them or should dismiss it as presupposing the very picture of subjects the view was introduced to replace. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say what the position predicts or explains that its chief rivals do not, and finish with an assessment you would be willing to defend: is the position, in the version I have stated, specified well enough to be exposed to anything, and if not, does that insulation matter the way it would for an empirical hypothesis?
