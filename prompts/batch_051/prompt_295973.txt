I've been trying to get clear on how much I should read into the fact that someone wants to trade, and my gut is all over the place. Six situations keep coming up. First, if a vintage guitar dealer offers me a specific guitar for noticeably under market, I treat that as evidence something is wrong with the guitar, even though I know he might just need cash. Second, if a grocery store puts milk on sale, I don't think the milk is bad; I think they're managing inventory. Third, if a CEO sells a large block of her own shares out of the blue right before an earnings call, I treat it as a bad sign, even while telling myself she might be buying a house. Fourth, if she sells the same amount every quarter under a pre-scheduled plan, I barely update. Fifth, if a friend is desperate to sell me his crypto holdings, I assume he suspects a crash, but I might still buy if I think he's just panicking and my horizon is longer than his. Sixth, if an index fund sells a stock because the index dropped it, I don't revise my view of the stock at all.

My working principle is something like: a person's willingness to trade is evidence about hidden value only when the trade is more likely if the asset is bad than if it's good, so I should check whether they have an independent reason to trade that doesn't depend on hidden information. But that principle seems to make the CEO case messy, because a scheduled sale is also an independent reason, yet I still want to treat the unscheduled one as informative. And the poker-table version of this, where a person's eagerness to bet could mean a strong hand or a bluff, doesn't fit the principle at all, since I'm not checking for hidden asset value.

Is there one defensible principle behind these verdicts, or a repaired version of my working principle that gets them nearly all? I'd like you to state the best candidate principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it forces me to revise or reinterpret, and then tell me what it predicts about a case I keep going back and forth on: a colleague who offers to sell me her house below market because she says she wants it to go to a friend. And please, no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables in your answer; just write it in continuous prose, the way you'd write an email.
