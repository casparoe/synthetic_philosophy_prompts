I'm setting this year's final exam for an upper-level undergraduate metaphysics course on grounding and fundamentality, and I need you to draft one question plus a model answer and a marking scheme. The exam is 90 minutes, and the question should test whether students can do more than recite the stock distinction between grounding and causation or the slogans that the fundamental facts form a "complete" and "minimal" base. I want the question to turn on a specific tension, with enough material laid out that a strong student and a careful marker both have what they need.

Here is the tension. On a standard grounding-theoretic picture, the fundamental facts are required to satisfy two constraints: completeness, the thesis that every non-fundamental fact is grounded in fundamental facts; and minimality, the thesis that no fundamental fact is grounded in any further fact. A familiar further idea is purity: fundamental facts should be statable in purely fundamental terms, without mentioning composites such as tables. Apply this to the ordinary fact that there is a table in the room. The natural grounds are facts about certain microphysical simples and their arrangement. But the fact that the simples are arranged tablewise seems impure, because "tablewise" characterises the arrangement in terms that already appear to involve tables, the very things to be grounded. If we instead demand a purely microphysical specification of the arrangement, it is hard to see how that specification secures the existence of exactly one table rather than several overlapping table-shaped collections or none. The existence fact also sits uneasily between the specific and the generic: "there is a table" appears to be grounded less by one particular arrangement of named simples than by the generic fact that some simples or other are arranged tablewise, which puts further pressure on the idea that the fundamental facts are a fixed, minimal set.

I want the exam question to present this tension and force the student to take a side: should we keep completeness and weaken purity, allowing facts such as "the simples are arranged tablewise" into the fundamental base; or keep purity and revise completeness, accepting that not every ordinary fact has a complete pure base; or keep the base intact and revise minimality; or something else, provided the revision is stated precisely? The question should ask for a definite verdict, not a survey.

Please write up: (a) the exam question itself, polished and suitable to hand to students; (b) a model answer of about 900 words that argues for a specific response rather than merely rehearsing the options; and (c) a marking scheme.

For the marking scheme, give exactly five numbered must-hit points, each one or two sentences, each worth four marks out of twenty, and each followed by a one-line note on how candidates typically get that specific point wrong. Do not add an introduction or conclusion to the marking scheme. Write the model answer and marking scheme in British English, and make the model answer committed: it should state its position early, defend it with reasons, and say plainly what that position gives up.
