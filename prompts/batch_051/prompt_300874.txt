I keep seeing 'the banality of evil' dragged into arguments where it does a lot more work than I think it can support. Last week someone used it to describe a mid-level manager quietly cooking sales numbers — the punchline being the scary thing is he isn't a monster, just bland. Another time someone on a forum wrote that Arendt's account of Eichmann showed the Holocaust wasn't really about ideology or hatred, just paperwork and people not thinking. I'm sure the phrase isn't just 'evil is boring' or 'everyone is capable of evil', but I don't know what it actually says once you stop using it as a slogan.

Could you seperate the distinct precise claims hiding in that phrase? What kind of 'banality' is being asserted: that the person is ordinary, that their motives are shallow, that the deeds come to feel routine, that the thinking is absent, or something else? Does the phrase say ideology and fanatical hatred played no role at all, or only that they're not the whole story? For each candidate claim, could you say whether it's a platitude, defensible but contentious, or probably false — and which reading actually supports the 'just following procedures' use of the slogan? I want to know which readings are commitments a careful Arendt-style account of Eichmann would actually need, and which are just things people now mean by the phrase.
