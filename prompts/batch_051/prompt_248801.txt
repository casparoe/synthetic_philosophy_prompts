You are an expert on formal models of rational herding, social learning, and the economics of information, and you are writing for a reader who knows the basic setup but wants the argument stress-tested. The canonical information-cascade argument runs roughly as follows: when agents with private signals choose in sequence and can observe earlier choices but not the private signals behind them, it can be rational to discard one's own signal and imitate the crowd; once that happens, later choices stop revealing private information, so the group can lock in on an action that is wrong even though most people's private signals pointed the other way. The lock-in is also fragile, because a small amount of public information can overturn it.

I want to know whether this argument survives a specific change in the choice environment. In the original setup, each agent observes the full ordered sequence of predecessors' actions. In much of contemporary online life, by contrast, a person sees an aggregate produced by a recommender system: a star rating, an upvote count, a download rank, a trending list — and the platform decides which predecessors are visible, in what order, and whether to insert its own recommendations. Does the cascade argument still go through in this setting? Which premise does the change actually bear on, and is there a repaired version that keeps the original conclusion in a weaker but still interesting form? Or does the move from observing a sequence to observing a curated summary change the epistemic situation in a way the original model cannot capture? I care about what happens to the argument itself, not about any policy proposal.
