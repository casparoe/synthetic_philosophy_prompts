I'm in a reading group on the ethics of belief, and we've been going back and forth about whether Clifford's principle in The Ethics of Belief is actually defensible. My friend keeps pushing the voluntariness objection: you can't just decide to believe something, so how can you be morally on the hook for a belief? She thinks that kills the whole position because ought implies can. I suspect that's too quick, but I can't tell where exactly it breaks down. The passage we're stuck on is this one, from W. K. Clifford's essay:

The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him.

So here's what I want. Give me your actual credence on the claim that a defensible version of Clifford's principle survives the voluntariness objection. When you decompose it, I'd like probabilities for at least these sub-questions: (a) whether Clifford's core argument is really committed to direct voluntary control over belief, as opposed to voluntary control over inquiry, attention, or evidence-gathering; (b) whether there's a purely epistemic wrong involved in believing on bad evidence that can ground a moral duty without first needing a harmful outcome; (c) whether a reformulated principle can handle ordinary trust—like believing a friend's report, or a doctor's advice—without becoming so demanding that it's practically absurd; and (d) whether the ought-implies-can worry is fully dissolved by saying that blame attaches to the way you form belief through things you do control, rather than to the moment of belief itself. Give a number and a one-line justification for each, then show the combination. Also tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move you most on that term. I'd rather have your honest decomposition than a confident-sounding number.
