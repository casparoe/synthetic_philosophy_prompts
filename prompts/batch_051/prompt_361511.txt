I have been trying to settle what I actually think about the treatment of animals, and I keep half-endorsing a broadly Kantian line: we have no direct duties to animals because they are not rational, and whatever duties we have concerning them are indirect, because acts that harden us toward animal suffering also harden us toward human beings. The trouble is that my verdicts on cases keep pulling away from it. Swatting a mosquito: fine. Eating factory-farmed pork: I suspect it is wrong, but I do it anyway. Keeping a pet cat and caring for it: fine, even obligatory once one adopts it. Setting off fireworks that panic the neighbor's dog: obviously wrong, even though it is thoughtlessness rather than cruelty. Torturing a stray cat for amusement: clearly wrong, but I cannot tell whether it is wrong because of the cat or because of what it does to the torturer. Using dogs in medical research that could save children: acceptable with regret, if there is no alternative. Shooting a healthy stray dog because it is a nuisance: I think wrong, though I cannot say why that differs from euthanizing it in a shelter. A child slowly burning insects with a magnifying glass: more wrong to me than swatting a mosquito, not because insects matter much but because of what taking pleasure in the burning reveals. Is there a single principle that captures these verdicts, or nearly all of them? In particular, can the indirect-duty view be repaired to cover the fireworks case and to distinguish swatting from the magnifying glass without conceding direct duties to animals? What would the repaired view say about cases I have not listed, such as humanely raised meat or pest control in the home, and which of my stated verdicts, if any, would it force me to abandon?
