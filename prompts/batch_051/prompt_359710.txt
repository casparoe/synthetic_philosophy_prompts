We're doing laws of nature in my metaphysics module and I've been trying to work out what I actually think by collecting my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases. When I look at them together I can't tell whether there's a single principal behind them or just vibes.

Here's where I've landed:

- All electrons have the same charge. Law.
- All the coins in my pocket right now are copper. True, but not a law.
- Suppose there's no sphere of pure gold more than a kilometre across anywhere in the universe. True (let's say), but not a law.
- No sphere of enriched uranium-235 can be more than a few tens of centimetres across, because anything bigger would go supercritical. My gut says that's a law, or at least law-like.
- But then: no sphere of enriched uranium-235 can be more than a kilometre across, and the same physics rules it out. Yet I'm noticeably less happy calling that a law. The threshold feels arbitrary, even though the impossibility is just as real.
- Pure water at one atmosphere boils at 100 °C. Law-ish, even though I'm hedging with conditions.
- All bachelors are unmarried. True, but not a law; it's just meaning.
- Smoking causes lung cancer. I want to call this a law-ish causal generalisation even though it isn't exceptionless.

The rough principle I've been testing is something like: a law is a true generalisation that isn't just an accident of what happens to exist, because it supports counterfactuals and holds with some kind of necessity. But I can already see the uranium pair and the smoking case straining it.

Is there a principle (or a small set of candidate principles) that captures most of these verdicts, with the cases that motivate each clause? Which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? I half-suspect this collapses into the Humean/anti-Humean split we've been reading about, but I want the principle first, not the label. Also: what would the account say about (i) all living things on Earth use nucleic acids for heredity, and (ii) no signal can travel faster than light in a vacuum?
