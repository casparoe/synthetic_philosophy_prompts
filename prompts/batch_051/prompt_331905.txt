I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been thinking about a pattern that keeps appearing in toy agents I build that reason about their own future choices. I've given it the working name the Open-Option Constraint: an agent currently deliberating about an action cannot at the same time assign a well-defined probability to that action, because prediction and choice are competing uses of the same internal state. On this view, 'what will I decide?' and 'what should I decide?' are not two questions about a single quantity but two incompatible stances. In the modelling stance, my future action is treated as an exogenous variable with a base rate; in the deliberative stance, the alternatives must be kept without probabilities. I've tried to formalise this as follows: an agent's unconditional credence in its own future action is well-defined only once it has fixed a policy or committed conditionally to one; before that, a self-prediction is either conditional on a policy or is an intervention that partly settles the decision rather than an observation of it. I suspect this is either trivially true or fundamentally confused. Give me an honest assessment. Does the distinction survive sharpening, or does it reduce to something already worked out under a cleaner name? What are the strongest counterexamples involving intermediate credences, such as being 90% sure one will order the fish while still deliberating, and do they show the constraint applies only at probabilities 0 and 1? Where does it break? Please answer in British English.
