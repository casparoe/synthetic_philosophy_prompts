For most of my working life I was a physical chemist, and only in retirement have I begun reading decision theory with any care. I recently worked through Warren Quinn's self-torturer, and it has unsettled some of the habits I used to bring to sequential optimization problems. In the familiar setup, a person can move a device through a large number of numbered settings; each one-step increase brings a cash payment and adds an increment of pain too small to distinguish from the previous setting, though the difference between the starting and final settings is severe. Adjacent settings are phenomenally indifferent, yet the series seems to lead someone who always accepts one more step to a state he would have refused from the start, and the question is whether the preferences driving each local choice can be rationally intransitive.

I would like you to reason through a counterfactual. Suppose that a government, impressed by this puzzle but persuaded that adults should be free to make such choices, licenses private self-torturer contracts under these conditions: the device has five hundred settings; the payment schedule is fixed and disclosed; each setting increase must be voluntarily initiated with a twenty-four-hour delay after the previous one; a one-year cooling-off period applies before the first increase; the contract cannot be assigned, sold, or used as collateral; and any adult who passes a comprehension test may enroll, with free medical and psychological monitoring throughout. Payments are taxable income. After twenty years, what does this market look like, and how has it changed the broader social treatment of voluntary suffering for pay? Give me a visible chain of reasoning about who enrolls and who exits, how regret and intransitivity interact with time-inconsistency and social pressure, whether secondary markets or informal analogues arise despite the restrictions, and how politicians, employers, insurers, and families respond. Tell me which predictions are robust, which depend on assumptions about rational intransitivity, and what would have to be true for the main prediction to fail.
