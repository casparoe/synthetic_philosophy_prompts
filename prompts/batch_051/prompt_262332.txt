Consider the question of when counting and record-keeping became mathematics. Start from these proposed criteria for counting a practice as mathematics rather than mere numeracy:

1. Generality: the object of study is classes of numbers or quantities, not a particular field, account, or consignment of goods.
2. Systematic method: results are produced by an explicit, repeatable procedure rather than by ad hoc tallying or copying.
3. Structural interest: the work shows interest in relations among numbers themselves—patterns, equivalences, unknown values—not only in practical outputs.
4. Standard of correctness: claims are checked against the procedure or against internal numerical relations, not merely against physical measurement.
5. Theoretical aim: the work is at least partly pursued for the internal problem rather than only as a ledger, a mensuration table, or a school exercise.

Now consider Plimpton 322, a Babylonian clay tablet usually dated to around 1800 BCE. It contains a table of fifteen rows and four columns written in sexagesimal (base-60) notation. On the standard reading, the numbers in each row relate to a Pythagorean triple—a triple of whole numbers (a, b, c) satisfying a² + b² = c²—and the values are consistent with generation by a regular procedure; some of the triples are too large to have been found by ad hoc methods. Apart from brief column headings, the tablet contains no statement of a theorem, no derivation, and no explanation of its purpose. Scholars dispute whether it was a number-theoretic investigation, a geometrical exercise, a teacher's aid for setting problems, or a practical table for surveying or administration.

Assess whether Plimpton 322 counts as mathematics under the five criteria above. Work criterion by criterion; where a criterion underdetermines the verdict, say so and explain why. Then say whether the case shows that the criteria themselves need revision—in particular, whether any criterion smuggles in a later ideal of proof, and whether the line between "mere numeracy" and "mathematics" is really as sharp as the criteria imply.
