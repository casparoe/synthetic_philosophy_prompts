Question: Consequentialism is often characterized as the view that an act is right just in case its consequences are at least as good as those of any alternative. A recurring challenge is that, for nearly any act we might perform, the long-run consequences are radically unknowable: the drowning child you save may grow up to cause great harm, and the small harm you now prevent may block a much greater future good. Explain this "cluelessness" objection as precisely as you can, and assess whether it shows that consequentialism cannot guide action.

Student answer: The cluelessness objection begins with a point the consequentialist should accept: our knowledge of an action's consequences covers only a short and local stretch of the causal chain, while the actual consequences continue indefinitely. Saving a drowning child looks good in the short run, but the child may go on to cause far more harm than the drowning would have prevented, and refusing to save has the same structure in reverse. Since consequentialism makes rightness depend on total consequences, it appears to demand information that no agent can have.

The standard consequentialist reply is that action-guidance requires rational expectation rather than knowledge of actual consequences: we enumerate the possible long-run outcomes, assign them probabilities, and choose the act with the highest expected value. The objection responds that this is empty, because there is no non-arbitrary way to enumerate the possible long-run outcomes or to assign probabilities across them; different ways of partitioning the possibilities yield different expected values, and nothing privileges one partition over another. Expected-value reasoning therefore cannot supply consequentialism with a usable decision procedure.

The objector then concludes that the problem is not mere uncertainty but unworkability. Because no action is better supported than any other once long-run effects are included, we are left with no more reason to save the child than not to, and the only principled response is to do nothing. If this is right, the objection is devastating for every version of consequentialism. Deontology avoids the difficulty altogether, since a deontologist can identify an act as forbidden without first tracing its causal ripples.
