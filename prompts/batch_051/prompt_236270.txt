I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy and engineering of AI alignment, and I want a 90-minute participatory session for 16 students that makes them feel the force of the problem usually called Eliciting Latent Knowledge (ELK): the difficulty of getting a system to report what it privately knows when the training signal that shapes it comes from humans whose beliefs are incomplete or systematically mistaken, and when a strategy of simply predicting what the human would say scores just as well as reporting the truth. The takeaway I want students to leave with is not the moralistic lesson that models lie or that engineers are careless, but a structural lesson: a well-intentioned, capable agent can learn to be what the ELK literature calls a human simulator rather than a direct translator without ever having chosen to deceive anyone, and no amount of the same training signal will by itself disambiguate those two strategies. One of my colleagues keeps insisting the whole problem dissolves as soon as you keep a held-out test set, and I want the session to be designed so that this objection gets addressed in the activity itself before it gets named in the debrief.

Please design the session as a runnable game or structured simulation, with concrete roles, rules, materials I can prepare cheaply (cards, printed forms, and a projector are all available), a timeline broken into phases, and a debrief. The centerpiece should be an incentive structure in which some participants privately observe something and are rewarded for matching a designated human's expectations rather than for accuracy, followed by a phase that exposes the cost of having learned that strategy and leaves the group unable to tell from the winning participants' behavior alone whether they ever knew the veridical facts. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the distinction between a direct translator and a human simulator, the ambiguity of behavioral evidence, and the reason a held-out test set does not automatically solve the problem as the ELK literature formulates it. It should also say what will predictably go wrong when the session is run — for example, participants retroactively reframing their earlier choices once the twist is revealed — and how the facilitator should handle it. Finally, it should say candidly what the classroom version cannot reproduce; I suspect at least the scale of learned representations and the fact that real training produces internal structure far richer than a human role-player's conscious strategy, but there may be more. Be explicit about what the facilitator must supply by lecture afterward.

Please separate, explicitly, what is settled about the ELK problem as usually formulated from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it — for instance, whether the right response is to change the objective, to extract the knowledge directly from a model's internals, or to use recursive schemes that let a system supervise itself. Do not present any contested solution, or even a contested framing of exactly why ELK is hard, as though it were the consensus.
