I'm giving an invited seminar next month to a research group in philosophy of physics and metaphysics, and I want to be ready for the question period. My thesis is that causation is neither fundamental nor eliminable: nothing in fundamental physics answers to a causal relation, but causal talk in the special sciences is disciplined, projectible, and indispensable enough that eliminating it would cost us explanatory and practical capacities we have no replacement for. I defend what I call anti-fundamentalism without eliminativism, meaning causation is a real but non-fundamental structure that appears only once systems are described with coarse-grained, contrastive, intervention-relative variables. The argument has three moves: first, that the fundamental level fails to supply relata or a relation with the right modal profile; second, that this failure is compatible with truth-evaluable causal claims at higher levels, given a deflationary account of higher-level reality; and third, that eliminativism faces a practical-elimination problem, since there is no usable non-causal vocabulary for explanation, prediction, and control. The audience includes two committed causal eliminativists who regard all special-science causation as pre-scientific talk, three metaphysicians who think causal relations are part of fundamental reality and are drawn to powers or dispositional essentialism, and several interventionists in philosophy of science who are suspicious of appeals to fundamentality altogether. Please write me a Q&A preparation sheet: eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do if I handled them poorly, each phrased the way the relevant camp would actually put it, noting which figure or camp would raise it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is softest. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks, and mark clearly where I should concede rather than defend. Write the sheet in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write an email or a letter.
