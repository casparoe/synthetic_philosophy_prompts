Two people in my cohort (I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology) got into it at happy hour about Van Fraassen's Reflection principle, and I still can't make up my mind about the argument, so I'd like an adjudication.

The principle, as they were using it, is roughly: for any proposition A and any later time t', your current credence in A, conditional on your credence in A at t' being r, should equal r. You're supposed to treat your future self as an expert.

Mira opened by saying the principle is false as a rational requirement. She gave this case: I know that tonight someone will slip me a pill that will make me .99 confident that the Earth is flat, without giving me any new evidence. Right now I'm .0001 confident it's flat. Surely I shouldn't now raise my credence to .99 just because I know the pill will make future-me believe that? So Reflection fails.

Sam said that's to quick, because it ignores the diachronic Dutch book. If you violate Reflection, a bookie who knows your credences can offer you a bet now and another bet later, each of which you'll regard as fair at the time it's offered, and the pair guarantees you a sure loss. If each bet is fair by your own lights at the time, taking both is incoherent. The pill case just means you expect future-you to be crazy; it doesn't get you out of the incoherence.

Mira pushed back that the Dutch book is circular. The second bet is fair only by the lights of the future credences. From where I stand now, I don't endorse those credences, so I don't regard the later bet as fair. A diachronic Dutch book isn't like a synchronic one, where all the bets are assessed from one point of view; here the supposed sure loss is sure only relative to a future self I already think is wrong. So the argument smuggles in exactly what Reflection was supposed to earn: future-me's authority over present-me.

Sam answered that without Reflection you can't deliberate. If learning that you'll later believe A to degree r gives you no current reason to move toward r, you can't treat your own future judgments as evidence, and planning collapses. If I expect that tomorrow a proof will convince me of a theorem, that expectation is a current reason to think the theorem true; Reflection registers that. The pill case is a degenerate scenario where you now expect a known cause to make you radically wrong later, which isn't a future self you can count as yourself for purposes of rational agency. So it falls outside the principle's domain.

Mira replied that the appeal to a domain restriction is doing all the work. Either Reflection is a fully general coherence norm, or it's a helpful rule that applies when my future self is a good barometer. If it's the latter, we're really saying: defer to future credences insofar as you trust future reasoning. That's substantive, empirically hostage, and can lapse; the Dutch book never marked the boundary. And in the pill case the rational thing seems to be to expect my future self to be wrong and to precommit against him now (hide my wallet, give instructions to friends). That's not incoherent; it's the opposite.

Please adjudicate this as a committed referee, not a survey: for each turn, say which specific moves survive and which don't, then give a bottom-line verdict on who has the better overall position, and flag anything important both of them are missing.
