I am drafting a paper for a philosophy class on the claim that some of what we owe one another are attitudes—standing patterns of attention, feeling, and interpretation, like respect or the refusal to take someone seriously—and not only actions. So far I have collected the following considerations in favor of this claim:

1. The reactive attitudes: emotions such as resentment, gratitude, and forgiveness are central to holding people accountable, and we assess them as fitting or excessive. That makes at least some attitudes answerable to standards that are not standards for acting.

2. The case of hidden contempt: a person can privately hold others in contempt while giving them faultless outward treatment. Many people judge that he still wrongs them. If that judgment is sound, faultless conduct is not always faultless.

3. The constitution of relationships: part of what it is to be someone's friend, colleague, or equal is to be regarded by them as one, and not merely treated in a way that resembles it. Since such relationships generate obligations, some obligations seem to concern the attitudes that go into constituting the relationship.

4. The virtues of feeling: several recognized moral failures—callousness, envy, arrogance—are primarily patterns of feeling and attention rather than patterns of behavior. If these are genuinely moral failures, moral assessment already reaches into attitudes.

5. The second-person demand: recognizing another person as a person means treating them as an addressee to whom one is answerable, not as an object to be managed or predicted. That stance of being answerable seems to require a kind of regard that is present before any particular action is chosen.

These five seem to me to be the standard moves, and I am trying to go past them. I would like one more consideration in favor of the same conclusion that is genuinely different in structure from all five. It should not be a rewording, a special case, or a combination of items on this list, and it should not rest on the thought that a bad attitude matters only because it risks producing bad actions. It may remain neutral about which specific attitudes are owed. Once you have given the new consideration, please tell me how confident you are in it and in your judgment of its strength, and single out the point in your reasoning that you are least sure of.
