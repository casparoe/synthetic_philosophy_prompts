I've been rereading Adam Smith's *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* for a reading group with some LessWrong/ACX types, and I got stuck on one sentance that seems to be doing way more work than I remembered. The opening line goes: "How selfish soever man may be supposed, there are evidently some principles in his nature, which interest him in the fortune of others, and render their happiness necessary to him, though he derives nothing from it, except the pleasure of seeing it." Smith starts from sympathy as something like mirror-neuron/emotional contagion — you imagine yourself in another's situation and feel something analogous — but by Part III he's talking about "the man within the breast," an impartial spectator who judges our own conduct. The relation between these two things is the whole puzzle: if sympathy just is matching the feelings of actual people, where does the authority of an imagined spectator come from? Can its verdicts diverge from every actual person's, or is it just internalized prediction of social reward and punishment?

I'd like you to build a "which account of the impartial spectator do you actually hold?" sorting quiz for the reading group. Make it 8-10 diagnostic questions, each a concrete case (e.g. someone does a charitable act while genuinely mistaken about its effects; a person feels remorse for an action nobody will ever discover; a community approves of revenge; a hermit acts on a principle that no actual other endorses; a person with a brain lesion loses concern for reputation but keeps "conscience"). Each question should be answerable by someone who has never read Smith and doesn't know the labels. The scoring key should sort people into positions like: the spectator as internalized social mirror / expected punishment; the spectator as a genuinely ideal observer with fuller information and no partiality; the spectator as a construction that aggregates actual affected parties' sentiments after correcting for distance but adds nothing normative of its own; the spectator as a real self-standpoint with authority independent of public opinion; and a deflationary reading where the impartial spectator is basically Smith smuggling in norms he can't derive from sympathy. Build the tree from the logical space if you can: first the questions and combinations of answers they allow, then locate actual positions in that space, and flag unoccupied combinations and whether they look incoherent or just unattractive. Also flag any two positions your quiz cannot separate well, especially the boundary between "internalized social mirror" and "aggregated affected parties." I care about the diagnostic questions being genuinely separating, not just asking people which label they like. Be blunt if Smith's text underdetermines some of these readings.
