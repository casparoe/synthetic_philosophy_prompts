I'm putting together the reading for a moral philosophy module I teach, and I want to anchor the unit on speciesism and moral standing around Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go, which most of the students have already read. My working thought is that the novel is almost an inverted speciesism: Kathy, Tommy and Ruth are, in every empirically inspectable respect, ordinary human beings — they fall in love, get jealous, make art, remember their childhoods — but the society around them has drawn a line that puts them outside the sphere of full moral concern, and treats them as sources of organs rather than as lives that matter for their own sake. That looks like the structure of speciesist exclusion with the species line moved, since the exclusion doesnt track any actual capacity; it is just the boundary the powerful need in order to use the excluded. I keep thinking the book also complicates the analogy in ways I can't quite articulate, because the donors are not marginal cases, they mostly accept the arrangement rather than fighting it, and the whole story is told from inside by one of them.

I'd like you to read the novel through the lens of speciesism and the argument from marginal cases the way a careful critic would. Where does the analogy actually illuminate what the book is doing — I am thinking of Miss Lucy's outburst about the students being told without being told, and of Hailsham collecting their art as if it could prove something about their souls — and where does the book resist the reading, precisely because the clones are human beings who can narrate their own subordination? Is the donors' passivity a problem for treating the novel as a work about animal ethics, or is that passivity itself part of what the book has to say about how exclusion works? And what does the novel do for a discussion of speciesism that the standard philosophical papers tend not to do?
