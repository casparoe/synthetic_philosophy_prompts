Had a massive argument last night with a friend about whether all this pre-AGI dealmaking is just cosplay. His whole thing was: a superintelligence can walk away from any agreement, so any deal you make now is just a promise backed by nothing. I couldn't stop thinking about it, and I ended up with a distinction I've been calling the participation ceiling, though I almost want to call it the Empty Contract Problem for AGI. Its probably wrong in some obvious way, but I want someone to actually check it.

The core claim: a pre-AGI bargain binds only if the enforcement mechanism is at least as hard to escape as the promise is to keep. But in a post-AGI bargain, the thing that decides wether the promise is kept is also the thing that can outthink, out-bribe, or just outlast every enforcement mechanism we can build. So the only pre-AGI deals that bind are ones the AGI would have chosen anyway. A deal that tries to make it do something it wouldn't choose is void on its face, not because it will defect but because it never had a real enforcement surface to begin with.

So I'd conclude you can only bargain over what the AGI wants, not over what it does afterwards. A treaty that says "we split the compute" is empty unless the AGI already wants the split; a treaty that shapes training data, default values, or who gets to do the first alignment work is the only kind of bargain with teeth.

Is this a real distinction, or did I just rediscover credible commitment for the thousandth time? What is the strongest version of the opposite view, that outcome bargains can be nonempty even when one party could walk away costlessly? And where specifically does the principle break?
