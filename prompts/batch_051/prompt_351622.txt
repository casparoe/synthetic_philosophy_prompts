I sit on my city's public health communications committee, and we're about to approve the copy for a malaria awareness campaign. The working group wants the main line to be "Mosquitoes carry malaria" on posters and transit ads. One member is pushing back hard, saying it's misleading: only female Anopheles mosquitoes transmit malaria, only a tiny fraction of those are actually infected, and the overwhelming majority of mosquitoes never carry the parasite at all. Another member says that's just how generics work — "ducks lay eggs" is fine even though drakes don't lay eggs and hens only lay them sometimes — and people won't read the slogan as a claim about most individual mosquitoes.

I've been asked to draft the short decision memo the committee will circulate before Thursday's vote. I need a concrete reccommendation: approve the slogan as-is, approve with a revision (e.g., "Some mosquitoes carry malaria" or "Mosquitoes can carry malaria"), or reject it. The memo should engage both positions rather than just picking a side, and it should say something useful about when a true generic with exceptions still misleads. One specific thing I keep going back and forth on: whether the relevant standard is semantic truth, or what people will actually infer and do, given that the campaign's goal is to get residents to use bed nets and clear standing water.
