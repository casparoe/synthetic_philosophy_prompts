I'm a CS undergrad taking a decision theory course this term, and there's a parallel I can't get out of my head but don't fully trust. We just covered the Ellsberg paradox and the usual risk/ambiguity distinction: risk is when you know the probabilities (the urn with 50 red and 50 black), ambiguity is when the probabilities themselves are unknown (the urn where you're not told the mix), and people seem to dislike the second in a way expected utility theory has trouble accomodating. Meanwhile, in my ML classes I keep running into the aleatoric/epistemic uncertainty distinction: aleatoric uncertainty is the irreducible noise in the data, epistemic uncertainty is uncertainty about your model that would shrink with more data. The mapping feels almost too clean: risk = aleatoric, because both are randomness you can quantify with a single well-defined distribution; ambiguity = epistemic, because both are about not knowing which distribution you're in.

What I want to know is whether this is a real structural identity or just a nice vocabulary overlap. If it's real, does anything actually transfer? For instance, would the Ellsberg result give us a reason to treat epistemic uncertainty with different decision rules than aleatoric uncertainty, instead of collapsing both into one predictive distribution? Do ideas from the ambiguity-aversion responses to Ellsberg (maxmin expected utility and similar) transfer to ML concerns like distribution shift or model misspecification? Or does the analogy break down because epistemic uncertainty in ML is usually parameter uncertainty inside a fixed model class, whereas Ellsberg ambiguity is supposed to involve not even having a single agreed prior? I'd like a careful diagnosis of where the two distinctions line up and where they come apart, and whether there's anything ML practice should actually take from the ambiguity literature or whether the parallel is mostly superficial.
