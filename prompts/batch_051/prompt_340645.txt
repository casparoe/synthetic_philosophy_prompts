After a long discussion with a friend about whether a rational agent can ever reasonably pay to remain ignorant, I find myself torn between a theorem I cannot refute and an intuition I cannot dislodge. The theorem is Good's theorem on the value of information: for an expected-utility maximizer with a fixed utility function, costless information can never have negative expected value. Whatever you learn, you are free to ignore it, and the proof is little more than the convexity of the maximum — having the option to condition on a signal can only improve the best choice available in expectation, so the value of choosing after seeing the signal is at least the value of choosing without it. This has the texture of a proof rather than a heuristic, and it seems to imply that a large class of "I would rather not know" judgments are simply errors.

And yet some of those judgments feel like the sanest parts of my psychology. I can construct cases where I would decline a free, private, perfectly reliable source of truth: the date of my death; whether a close friendship will eventually end; whether the work I am most attached to will be remembered; whether I would, under enough pressure, choose to hurt someone. In each case I can tell the standard story about why the theorem's assumptions fail — the information would change what I want, or make a course of action psychologically unavailable, or leak into my face where others could read it — but I can also imagine versions of each case in which those standard escapes are stipulated away and the reluctance remains. I keep half-forming a distinction between information that merely changes my beliefs about fixed options and information that changes the chooser, but I am not sure it does the work I want it to do.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. What exactly would have to be true for the reluctance to know to count as a bias that should be debunked and dropped? What exactly would have to be true for Good's theorem to be restricted to a narrow class of belief-updates rather than treated as a general principle for living? And is there a reading of "better off" on which the proof and the unease are both right?
