Locke's memory criterion holds that a later person is the same person as an earlier one just in case the later consciousness can reach backward and appropriate the earlier person's thoughts and actions; where consciousness cannot extend, sameness of person does not obtain, regardless of any continuity of soul or body. Thomas Reid pressed the brave-officer objection against this view. A boy is flogged for stealing from an orchard. Years later, as an officer, he remembers the flogging, so on Locke's criterion he is the same person as the boy. Still later, as an old general, he remembers his own act of bravery as the officer, capturing an enemy standard, but has entirely forgotten the flogging; so he is the same person as the officer, yet by the memory criterion he is not the same person as the boy. Since identity is transitive, Reid concluded that Locke's account yields a contradiction.

Now modify the case. Keep the sequence of boy, officer, and general exactly as before, except that the general's loss of the flogging memory is not permanent. During episodes of fevered sleep, the memory of the flogging returns to him with complete first-person vividness: he can describe the orchard scene from the inside and unhesitatingly affirms that the flogging happened to him. When each episode ends, the memory vanishes again; the general sincerely denies any recollection of it and can no longer appropriate the boy's experience. These episodes recur unpredictably for the rest of his life.

How should a Lockean analyze the modified case? Must he say that the general is identical to the boy only during the episodes, so that personal identity flickers on and off as the memory becomes occurrent and then lapses? If that conclusion is unacceptable, which commitment should give way: the memory criterion, the transitivity and stability of identity, or the claim that the episodic memories are genuine memories reaching back to the boy? Is there a Lockean reading on which the same consciousness persists through the intervals in a dormant or dispositional form, and does that reading covertly reintroduce a continuing self of just the sort Locke meant to avoid? Finally, say whether Reid's contradiction reappears in this variant, or whether the recurring memory supplies exactly the link from general to boy that Reid's original case withheld.
