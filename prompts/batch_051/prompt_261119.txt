Here is a line of thought I keep returning to, and I cannot find the flaw. Suppose I know some proposition p. Because knowledge is factive, p is true. Now take any possible piece of evidence against p. Since p is true, that evidence would have to be misleading; it would point away from a truth. It seems to follow that, in knowing p, I already know that any evidence against p is misleading. But if I already know that a piece of evidence is misleading before I even encounter it, then when something appears to be evidence against p I should simply discount it as a false lead and keep my confidence in p unchanged. Generalize this, and everything I know becomes immune to counterevidence: I would be rationally entitled, even required, to ignore any apparent counterexample to any belief that counts as knowledge. That cannot be right if fallibilism is true — that is, if I can know things on evidence that does not guarantee their truth. So it looks as though fallibilism collapses into dogmatism, and the only escape is to say that knowledge requires infallible certainty. I suspect this dilemma is too tidy, but I cannot locate the precise mistake. Which step in the reasoning should a fallibilist reject, and how should that person describe the difference between evidence that is misleading and evidence that merely appears misleading?
