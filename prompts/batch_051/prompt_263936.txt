After retiring from a career in physical chemistry, I have been reading my way through moral philosophy, and I find myself caught between a methodological view that impresses me and a particular judgment I cannot surrender. The view is a form of moral generalism: a verdict about a case is justified only if it follows from a general principle that specifies which features count and how they combine. Absent such a principle, the verdict explains nothing, cannot be extended to new cases, and makes consistency a merely autobiographical fact about me. The rival position, particularism, seems to me to be in exactly the bind that made me distrust sterile model-building as a scientist: a curve can be fitted to any finite set of data points and tells you nothing about the next one, and a moral method that merely re-describes my case-by-case intuitions seems equally non-predictive. The familiar argument from supervenience presses the same way: since I reject the possibility that two cases identical in every non-moral respect could differ morally, I feel there must be general features in virtue of which a case has its verdict, and that commitment looks hard to honor without principles.

Yet I have a firm intuition that no general principle I have found captures. I promised a dying colleague that I would go through his papers after his death and destroy certain drafts he was ashamed of—drafts of an industrial-safety review he never published. When I read them, I found that the drafts contained the only clear statement of a serious and plausible failure mode in a widely used procedure, and circulating them would likely save lives. I judge that I should break the promise and circulate the drafts, and I would do it without hesitation. An unqualified principle of promise-keeping misses this. The softer principle that one should keep promises unless a stronger duty overrides merely postpones the difficulty until the overriding duties are specified. Every specification I have attempted either undergenerates, failing to capture other promise-breakings I also regard as plainly wrong, or degenerates into a broad consequentialist license I cannot accept. The considerations that move me here—preventing serious harm and serving what I take to be my colleague's deeper wish—are familiar general considerations, but they are present in different strengths across the whole range of promise cases, and I cannot state in advance the conditions under which they defeat a promise.

I am not asking what I should do, nor which side wins. I want a negotiated account of the collision between the systematic pull of generalism and this single judgment. Please respond in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two long, with no introduction or conclusion. The points should specify, concretely, what would justify treating the intuition as a moral illusion and dropping it, what would justify amending or abandoning generalism, and what a reading of generalism on which both survive would have to establish.
