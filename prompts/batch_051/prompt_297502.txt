I got roped into adjudicating a long-running disagreement between two people in my building, and I want a professional philosopher's take on it. One of them, Sana, is basically an orthodox welfarist—she thinks all value has to bottom out in how lives go for the creatures living them, and that "prosperity" is only ever shorthand for stuff that tends to make lives go better. The other, Ezra, thinks abundance can matter in itself, or at least in ways that don't reduce to the well-being it causally delivers. The argument happened in our building's shared courtyard, so I'm reconstructing it as best I can from memmory.

Ezra opened with a twin-world case. In world A, everyone has a decent baseline and total well-being ends up exactly equal to world B. But world A is lean: just enough capacity, no slack. World B has enormous surpluses—unread libraries, spare hospital wings, empty trains running, unexercised legal rights, tools nobody ever picks up. He said world B is plainly better, and that shows abundance has value over and above what it buys. Sana answered that the case can't separate the two kinds of value, because surplus changes expectations, security, and opportunity. If we truly stipulate that the slack will never affect anyone's life in any possible way, she said, it's just expensive rust, and nobody values that. Ezra said the stipulation is doing all the work in her favor: being a society with slack just is a different fact about people's lives, so you can't stipulate it away while keeping the world the same. Then Sana pressed the options point. An unexercised option, she said, can have value only in three ways: it is felt as freedom by the person who has it; it functions as insurance against future uncertainty; or it eventually gets exercised. Strip all three away and nothing remains. Ezra replied that this was a bait-and-switch: she keeps the label value only for welfare, but an unused passport still makes a person free, and a free person is better off even if equally cheerful. Sana said that either unpacks, again, into the person's subjective experience of their situation or into future possibilities, and there is no third thing of the sort Ezra keeps gesturing at. At the end Ezra shifted to future generations. A society that bequeaths its descendants far more capability than they will ever use—extra knowledge, redundant infrastructure, room to veer—gives them something even if their well-being would be identical either way: it leaves them not locked in. Sana said "not locked in" is itself a welfare-relevant fact, so he has only discovered a further component of what makes lives go well, not a value beyond welfare. Ezra said at that point that she was relabeling his point and refusing to take it on its own terms.

They both turned to me and asked me to say who had won each step and overall. I couldn't do it cleanly. Can you act as an expert moral philosopher and adjudicate their exchange move by move? Please first state the strongest charitable version of each person's position, then identify exactly where each is right, where each overstates or begs the question, and finally say who has the better all-things-considered case. Please take your time and walk through the arguments step by step, and write the whole answer in American English.
