For a seminar exercise in philosophical theology, consider the following asymmetry in the practice of petitionary prayer. A theist who affirms that God is omniscient, immutable, and outside time will readily ask God that an operation succeed, that a sick child recover, or that a journey be safe. These are requests about events not yet past. The same theist will not, after the fact, ask God that the operation did succeed, that the child did not die, or that the journey had been safe; the natural comment is that the past is finished and cannot be undone. But from the standpoint of an atemporal God the asymmetry is not obvious. What counts as already settled and what counts as still open is a feature of our temporal perspective, not of divine knowledge or willing. If God responds to a prayer at all, it is by eternally willing to include both the prayer and its answer within a single act; on that picture God could bring about a past state of affairs precisely because the prayer was made later, in just the way critics of petitionary prayer say God brings about the future. Yet religious practice overwhelmingly treats prospective petition as reasonable and retrospective petition as superstition or incoherence. I do not want the standard observation that prayer cannot change God's mind; assume that petitionary prayer makes sense on the best account you can give of it. What I want to know is whether anything justifies the asymmetry between praying forward and praying backward. Take seriously the reply that the future is open and the past is fixed: does it commit the theist to a temporal and mutable God, and is that a cost a classical theist should be willing to pay? And test your answer against the case of an outcome that already lies in the past but is wholly unknown to the one praying, as with a flight that may have landed safely an hour ago. Say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or abandoned, and on what grounds.
