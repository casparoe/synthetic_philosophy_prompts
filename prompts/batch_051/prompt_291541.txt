Consider a proposal meant to combine universal suffrage with the epistemic ambitions of epistocracy. In a jurisdiction where major decisions — bond issues, land-use plans, tax changes, social policies — are made by popular referendum, every registered voter casts a ballot with a numerical weight. Each voter starts at weight 1. Every year the electoral commission invites each voter to forecast a panel of policy-relevant quantities over fixed time horizons: unemployment, housing completions, hospital waiting times, local air pollution, school test scores, and similar outcomes. Forecasts are reported as probabilities and scored under a strictly proper scoring rule — a rule under which one's expected score is uniquely maximized by reporting one's honest probability. A civil-service statistical office assigns each panel question to one of several policy clusters and likewise assigns each upcoming referendum to a cluster. A voter's weight on a given referendum is then 1 plus a bonus between 0 and 2, computed as a concave, pre-published function of the voter's average score over the preceding three years on that cluster. Voters who decline to forecast, and newly registered voters, vote at weight 1. Individual forecasting scores are published.

Treat this proposal strictly as a mechanism, not as a moral position about who deserves political power. Analyze how it would actually behave. What would it incentivize among ordinary voters, highly engaged partisans, campaigns, advocacy organizations, media outlets, and the officials who choose the panel questions and cluster assignments? How would it be gamed — for example, by forecasting outcomes one then tries to bring about, by specializing in only the most predictable questions, by copying the forecasts of a coordinated faction, or by choosing strategically which referenda to vote on? Where would the scheme fail, and which failure modes could be repaired by adjusting the scoring rule, the bonus cap, the cluster definitions, the publicity of scores, or the baseline, without abandoning the core idea that demonstrated predictive accuracy should partly determine vote weight? Pay particular attention to second-order effects: whether the scheme selects for genuine policy judgment or for something narrower, how it changes the relationship between voters and the policies they evaluate, and what happens to important issues that resist short-horizon forecasting.
