Last night I got into a long argument with a friend who is convinced that the fear of death is always irrational. He gave me what he called the Epicurean argument. As he summarized it, you can only be harmed by something you experience, and death is simply the absence of experience, so being dead cannot be bad for the person who dies. He added that anyone who says a death at twenty is worse than a death at ninety is making a logical error, because there is no moment at which the person and the badness of being dead overlap: while you exist, you are not dead, and once you are dead, there is no you left to suffer the loss.

When I objected that a death at twenty is worse because it deprives you of sixty years of life you would otherwise have lived, he dismissed this as sentimental. He said the deprivation account just invents a ghostly subject standing outside life, after death, noticing what it is missing. He granted that the process of dying can be painful and in that way bad, but he insisted that this is a separate question from whether being dead is bad. He also said the same reasoning shows there was nothing bad about my not having been born earlier, and that this symmetry confirms the argument. I kept feeling he had conflated two or three different claims, but I could not say exactly where. I would like to know which parts of his summary a careful Epicurean would actually accept, where the argument goes wrong if it does, and whether his conclusion that a death at twenty is no worse than a death at ninety follows from the parts that survive correction.
