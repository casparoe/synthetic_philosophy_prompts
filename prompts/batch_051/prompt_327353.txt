I'm a novelist, and I've started to distrust one of my own favorite tools, because I can't tell whether it tracks anything real. Here's the asymmetry. If I give a character a grievance—say she was promised a job and it went to the boss's nephew—readers treat her anger as righteous: she was wronged, of course she's furious. But if I give a character the same heat over a colleague who got every promotion ahead of her, and I have her want the colleague to fail, readers treat her as small and bitter. And its not just readers; I'd write those two women as different moral types without noticing I was doing it.

The thing is, the second woman could say the first woman's grievance and her own envy are responses to the same underlying fact: an unearned advantage, a rigged queue, luck wearing a tie. The difference between being wronged and being envious might be a decision I make as the narrator—a word choice—rather than a difference in the world. Resentment is supposed to be about a wrong done to you; envy about wanting what someone else has. But when the thing someone else has is itself the wrong, the distinction starts to blur.

So here is my question. Ordinary judgment treats resentment as tracking an actual injustice and envy as a flaw in the person who feels it, even when both are aimed at the same unequal state of affairs. Is that asymmetry justified, or is it just a trick of framing? Give me the strongest principle you can find that seperates legitimate grievance from mere envy, test it against the hard cases—envy aimed at unearned advantage, resentment over harms nobody intended, a person who resents someone who has done nothing wrong but enjoys an unjust luck—and tell me honestly whether the principle survives. In particular: should I, as a writer, keep sorting my characters into wronged and envious, or is that sorting itself the bias?
