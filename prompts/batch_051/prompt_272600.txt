I'm a retired physicist who only recently got into philosophy, and I keep tripping over the same question: when should a compelling theory override a firm intuition, and when should it be the other way around? I've got my gut verdicts on a pile of cases, and I'd like to know whether one principle captures them or whether I'm just being inconsistent.

- Special relativity says there's no fact about what's happening now on Alpha Centauri, and my intuition says of course there is. I go with the theory, and I don't even feel I'm losing much.
- Bell-type results say the world isn't locally real; my intuition says its impossible, but I go with the theory.
- Monty Hall: after the host opens a goat door, my gut says the two remaining doors are 50-50, but I switch because the conditional-probability argument is sound. Theory wins.
- Utilitarianism says a doctor should carve up one healthy person to save five. My gut says that's morally monstrous, and here I don't set the intuition aside; I think the theory is missing something. Intuition wins.
- Rational choice theory says voting is irrational because one vote basically never decides an election. I don't conclude voters are irrational; I conclude the model is wrong about what's going on. Intuition wins.
- From my old life in physics: when a mathematically lovely model got one robust, replicated phenomenon wrong, I almost always trusted the anomaly over the model until someone explained it. But if the misfit was small and the model kept making new correct predictions, I'd mostly trust the model and wait for the anomaly to go away.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? And which verdict would it force me to give up or sharpen? I'd also like to know what it says about the case I keep going back and forth on: a future theory of consciousness that entails my clearest introspective judgments, like that I'm in pain right now or that I'm deciding freely, are illusions. Do I treat that like special relativity or like the organ-harvesting case, and why?
