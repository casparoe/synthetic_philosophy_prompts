In many discussions of social media's effects, someone reaches for Kierkegaard's critique of the daily press and treats the two cases as near-perfect matches. The idea is that the nineteenth-century press did to public life what algorithmic feeds do today: they call into being an anonymous, envious, leveling public—an abstraction rather than an assembly of actual persons—and they trap individuals in endless reflection by giving them a place to chatter about everything while committing to nothing. On this view, the categories Kierkegaard used to analyze the press map straight onto a modern platform: the press corresponds to the media company and its algorithms, the public corresponds to the trending feed or the aggregated audience, leveling corresponds to metric-driven flattening of qualitative distinctions, anonymity to pseudonymous posting and the way no one in particular is responsible, envy and comparison to the engine of engagement, idle chatter to content produced for attention, and the single individual's task to the user who resists the feed rather than performing for it. The intended conclusion is that Kierkegaard's verdicts and his remedy transfer almost unchanged, and that anyone who accepts his analysis of the press is committed to something similar about social media.

I want this analogy audited rather than simply elaborated. Please give me a correspondence table, breaking out what maps to what and any elements on either side without a counterpart. Then identify the points where the mapping breaks or quietly imports features that the digital case lacks; for each, say whether it is incidental or whether it undermines the intended conclusion. In particular, consider whether algorithmic selection and platform incentives play the role the press played in constituting an anonymous public, whether the distribution of responsibility actually matches Kierkegaard's picture of the public as an abstraction, whether the comparative and envy-based machinery is a close enough fit to his account of leveling, and whether his remedy answers to the user who has no obvious exit rather than to the individual who could choose to stop reading the paper. Then say what, if anything, a better analogy would be. End with a line of the form \answer{...} containing exactly one of the following: survives, survives in modified form, fails.
