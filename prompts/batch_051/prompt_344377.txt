I'm currently taking a course on the philosophy of medicine, and I've been trying to work out what principle, if any, captures my own judgments about when someone is dead. Here are my verdicts.

A ventilated patient who meets the standard clinical criteria for whole-brain death but whose heart is still beating in the ICU: dead.

A patient in a permanent vegetative state who breathes spontaneously and shows sleep-wake cycles but no awareness of self or environment: not dead.

A fully conscious patient with locked-in syndrome and almost no voluntary movement: not dead.

A patient on ECMO whose heart and lungs have permanently ceased all function but whose brain is intact and who is awake: not dead.

A donor in a controlled protocol for donation after circulatory death, whose ventilator is withdrawn, whose heart stops, and who is declared dead after five minutes without a pulse, even though no test has shown irreversible loss of brain function and the heart might have restarted if anyone had attempted resuscitation: dead.

A hypothermic patient found pulseless who might be revived with rewarming and CPR: not dead.

A brain-dead pregnant woman ventilated for weeks so that a fetus can develop: dead, though her body is being maintained.

My working principle is roughly that death is the irreversible loss of the organism's capacity to maintain itself as an integrated whole, with the brain as its chief integrator. But that principle fits the circulatory-death donor poorly, and I cannot tell whether my verdict in that case tracks irreversibility or merely permanence — that nobody will try to reverse the loss. Is there a single principle that captures all or most of these verdicts? If not, which should I revise, and what does the resulting account say about an anencephalic newborn with an intact brainstem?
