i'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, but I read a lot of the EA forum and keep chewing on the measurability bias critique: interventions with one clean per-dollar metric get funded and defended partly because they're easy to compare, while diffuse, preventive, long-horizon stuff gets underfunded even if it might matter more. I've been playing with a mechanism for an EA-adjacent grantmaking body and want it stress-tested before I pitch it anywhere.

Two tracks. Track A is "measured": you submit exactly one primary metric and a projected cost per unit of that metric, with a margin you're willing to be held to. Track B is "diffuse": you're actually forbidden from giving any quantitative projection, but you have to name one concrete sign that a skeptical outside observer would be able to notice within five years if the thing worked, even if it can't be measured cleanly.

The mechanism. Every Track A grant pays a 15% "measurement levy" into a common pool. The pool is spent on Track B proposals, but the people who decide which Track B proposals get funded are the Track A grantees themselves: each Track A grantee gets votes proportional to how much levy their grant paid in, and they rank the Track B proposals. A Track A grantee can avoid the levy entirely by volunteering ten hours a month helping one funded Track B project build any measurement capacity it can. Finally, if a Track A grant misses its projected cost-per-unit by more than 30%, 25% of the grant is clawed back into next year's pool.

I designed this to correct for measurability bias: the people who benefit from having a clean metric are forced to subsidize the people who can't have one, and the clawback makes them put some money behind their projection instead of just saying a number. But I also know I'm an engineer and I'm probably missing all the weird second-order stuff. Walk me through how this actually plays out. Who applies to each track, and who stops applying entirely? How do Track A orgs game it: sandbag the projection, pick a metric that's easy to move but not the real goal, clawback-insure each other, vote-trade on Track B? What does the Track B committee dynamic look like—does it end up funding the least-threatening diffuse work, the stuff that looks like cheap PR for Track A grantees, or something else? Does this actually reduce measurability bias or just relocate it into a committee of people selected for measurable wins? And where do earning-to-give people fit, since they don't produce a metric until years later when they donate: do they become Track B, game Track A, or just route around the whole thing?
