Please evaluate the following argument, which I find plausible but suspect achieves too much too easily.

(1) Patients with pain asymbolia can detect and describe painful stimuli — they may say that a stimulus hurts — yet they do not mind the pain: they do not withdraw from it, complain of it, or try to stop it.
(2) If the badness of pain were an intrinsic property of the pain sensation itself, then anyone who had that sensation would thereby have an experience that is bad for them and would find it aversive.
(3) Pain asymbolia patients have the pain sensation, but the experience is not bad for them: they show no aversion, distress, or negative motivation.
(4) Therefore the badness of pain is not an intrinsic property of the pain sensation; it must be a separate affective-motivational component, which asymbolia happens to strip away.

Is this argument valid as written? If not, identify exactly which step fails and why, and state what the strongest corrected version of the argument would establish.
