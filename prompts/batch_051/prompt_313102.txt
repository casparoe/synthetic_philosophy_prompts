My friend has this running project about whether the state itself can do wrong, as opposed to just the officials in it. She's pretty firm on these:

- When the government signed a contract with a small supplier and then refused to pay, she said yes, the state did wrong — it broke its word.
- When a border guard, purely out of personal spite and against agency rules, detained and humiliated a traveler, she said the guard did wrong but the state didn't.
- When the justice ministry adopted and followed a written policy of holding asylum-seekers past the legal limit to deter future arrivals, she said the state did wrong; the policy was the wrongdoer, not any one officer.
- When the state weather service's forecast, made with the best available models, was wrong and a fishing boat sank, she said the state didn't do wrong; an honest mistake isn't a wrong.
- When a public hospital followed a ministry rule that blocked a second-opinion referral for 30 days, and a patient died waiting, she said the state did wrong; the rule, not the physician, did it.
- When an off-duty soldier beat someone up in a bar fight, she said the soldier did wrong but the state had nothing to do with it.

Next she's going to hear about this case: the central bank, under an official policy, deliberately keeps inflation above its announced target for years, knowing that will erode the savings of pensioners on fixed incomes, and no law promises them a stable currency or any particular purchasing power. I want to know whether she'll say the state itself has done wrong here.

I'm less interested in the one-word answer than in this: what rule or rules is she implicitly running, which other rules fit all her earlier verdicts just as well, which earlier case is the closest analog to the new one, and what principle we'd be attributing to her if she says yes versus no. Then tell me what further case would seperate the surviving rules. And if you think the whole question is badly set up — for instance because "the state" isn't the kind of thing that can literally do wrong, and all this is just a metaphor — say that straight instead of playing along with a prediction.
