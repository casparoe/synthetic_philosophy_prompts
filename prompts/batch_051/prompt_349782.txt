It struck me that there is a structural parallel between one influential line of pessimism about moral testimony and a familiar failure mode in machine learning, and I cannot tell whether the parallel is deep or only a tempting figure of speech.

On the moral side, the worry is not that moral testimony is usually false. It is that someone who simply accepts another person's moral verdict — for example, that a particular hiring policy is unjust — without working through the considerations that make it unjust ends up with something less than the competence to judge. The person can replay the verdict they were given, but they do not possess the principle that generates it; so when the next case differs in a small but morally relevant way, they are no better placed to assess it than they were before. The received verdict functions like a memorized answer rather than an acquired capacity.

On the machine-learning side, something similar happens in shortcut learning. A model can achieve high accuracy on its training distribution by tracking features that are only spuriously correlated with the target label, without learning the causally relevant rule. It then performs well on test sets drawn from the same distribution and collapses on inputs that lie outside it, because it never acquired the underlying function that produces the labels.

The parallel, then, is that moral deference and shortcut learning are both cases in which a system can reproduce correct outputs for the examples it has been given while lacking whatever would allow it to generate correct outputs beyond them. If that is right, the pessimist's complaint is not just a piece of moral psychology; it is an epistemic point about generalization, and anyone who adopts verdicts without the generating rule will be brittle, whether the learner is a neural network or a person.

I want to know whether this analogy amounts to more than a shared slogan about memorization versus understanding. State the mapping precisely. Say what would have to be true of both sides for results from machine learning — concerning distribution shift, adversarial examples, or generalization bounds — to transfer to the moral case. Identify at least one thing the parallel appears to predict that is false on the moral side. Then conclude with a verdict: is this a genuine identity of structure, a merely loose analogy, or something in between?
