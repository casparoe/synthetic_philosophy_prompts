After a seminar discussion left several of us unconvinced that Heidegger’s tool-analysis has anything to say beyond carpentry, I went back to Being and Time, Division One, Chapter Three, section 16. The passage I have in mind is the one where he distinguishes the ways equipment loses its transparent readiness-to-hand: a damaged or unusable tool becomes conspicuous; something missing makes the rest of the equipment obtrude; and something standing in the way becomes obstinate. On his account, these privative modes of un-readiness-to-hand are what first reveal the referential whole of the workshop, the equipmental context that had been silently absorbed in use. I want to test whether that description is genuinely illuminating when scaled up from a hammer to a disaster.

The case is the Chernobyl accident of April 26, 1986. I know the chronology and the basic facts: a low-power test, disabled safety systems, the power drop, the attempted shutdown, the graphite-tipped control rods, the runaway power surge, the explosion. What I want is a disciplined case analysis. First, map the episode onto the three modes: what became conspicuous, what obtruded, and what was obstinate during the sequence? Then reconstruct what the accident made visible, in Heidegger’s sense, about the referential whole of the Soviet nuclear establishment, the RBMK design, the grid, the test protocol, and the reactor crew’s practical world. Then turn critical: which features of Chernobyl does the readiness-to-hand and breakdown account explain well, and which does it misdescribe or simply leave out? In particular, can organizational hierarchy, production pressure, report falsification, design flaws hidden from operators, and the scientific knowledge gap about the positive void coefficient be handled within the equipment-analysis, or does the theory fall silent on precisely the factors that mattered most? Finally, tell me what the episode shows about Heidegger’s threefold taxonomy: is it confirmed, stretched, broken, or perhaps aimed at a different phenomenon altogether?

Keep the answer structured around the mapping, and do not pad it with historical narrative. Where the theory is silent, say so explicitly instead of forcing it to explain everything. As you go, separate what is settled in Heidegger interpretation from what is contested, and likewise distinguish the uncontroversial facts of the accident from the explanatory claims about its causes; do not present a contested reading of the passage or of the disaster as if it were the consensus.
