Here's the argument, which a friend in my logic group calls airtight:

Take a fair six-sided die. Let A be "the die came up even" and C be "the die came up six." Then P(C|A) = 1/3, since P(A) = 1/2 and P(A∧C) = 1/6. The thesis (call it ST) says the probability of the indicative conditional "if A then C" equals the conditional probability P(C|A).

1. By ST, P(if A then C) = P(C|A) = 1/3.
2. By the law of total probability, P(if A then C) = P(if A then C | C)·P(C) + P(if A then C | not-C)·P(not-C).
3. ST applied to the probability function I get by conditioning on C: P(if A then C | C) = P(C | A∧C) = 1.
4. ST applied to the probability function I get by conditioning on not-C: P(if A then C | not-C) = P(C | A∧not-C) = 0.
5. Then P(if A then C) = 1·(1/6) + 0·(5/6) = 1/6.
6. But that contradicts step 1, since 1/6 ≠ 1/3.

My friend says this forces the usual moral: either you give up ST for arbitrary evidence, or you deny that conditionals are the kind of thing that obeys total probability — i.e., they don't express propositions.
