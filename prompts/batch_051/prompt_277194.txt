I have no formal training in mathematics beyond the usual school sequence and none in philosophy, but I have spent a good deal of time on my own trying to pin down the difference between following a proof and understanding one. I can reproduce several standard arguments — that there is no largest prime, that the square root of two is irrational, that a set of n things has two to the power n subsets — and I can check each step and notice when one is missing. Yet there are proofs I can reproduce and check in this way without having any sense of why the theorem is true, and there are proofs where, partway through, the pieces lock together and the theorem comes to seem unavoidable. I no longer trust that feeling as a guide, and I also no longer trust step-by-step verification as a substitute for it.

Before I read any more proposals about what it is to understand a proof, I want a specification of what an adequate account must deliver — a working account, good enough to guide my own study, that tells me what I am aiming at when I say I want to understand a proof rather than merely get through one. Please lay out the desiderata: the cases such an account must get right, the distinctions it must respect, and the demands that are genuine requirements rather than the preferences of one school. To make the list concrete, consider cases like being able to repair a proof when a step is damaged, being able to adapt the argument to a nearby problem, being able to explain it to a beginner, having the moment of seeing why it works, and being able to certify a long or computer-assisted proof that no one holds in their head all at once. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that earns it a place on the list, and say which requirements you regard as negotiable and which are not. Then flag any pairs you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which requirement or two does the most to rule out inadequate accounts. If useful, close by briefly indicating how the common candidate views — understanding as the ability to explain, as the ability to transfer, as the possession of an articulate reason why, or as an experience of insight — fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves.
