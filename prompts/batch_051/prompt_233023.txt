I retired a few years ago from a career in ecology, and I've only recently gotten into philosophy of science, so sorry if I mangle the terminology. There's a habit of mine that's starting to worry me, and I'd like an honest verdict on it.

The habit shows up whenever a long-run prediction from a theory I respect misses its deadline. Instead of counting it against the theory, I tell myself the theory was right, just early. The clearest case is Malthus. He argued that population tends to grow geometrically while the food supply can at best grow arithmetically, so population will eventually outrun food and be held in check by famine, disease, or war. Two centuries on, the world feeds several times more people than lived in his time, and the global catastrophe he predicted hasn't arrived on his schedule. My reflex is still to defend him: the underlying mechanism is sound, and technology merely postponed the arithmetic. I do the same with collapses of fish stocks that didn't happen by the forecast date, and I used to make this exact move with modelling projections in my own work that blew their deadlines.

A friend recently sent me a piece declaring Malthus simply falsified, and I found myself drafting a long reply about how the logic remains valid, just delayed. That made me notice the policy I'm apparently running: a theory with a plausible mechanism gets its deadline extended when a specific prediction fails, and I don't treat it as falsified until... well, I honestly cant say until when.

Is that defensable, or is it just a way to make a theory unfalsifiable while flattering myself as open-minded? Under what conditions is 'right but early' a legitimate verdict on a failed prediction rather than an ad hoc rescue? Does it matter whether the mechanism was independently supported, whether the original theory said anything about acceptable timing, or how much auxiliary machinery has to be bolted on to keep it alive? And is there a restated version of my policy that keeps the good part — not throwing away a sound mechanism just because the timing was off — without giving every failed prophecy an unlimited line of credit? I'd like a sharper rule than the one I'm running on, if one exists.
