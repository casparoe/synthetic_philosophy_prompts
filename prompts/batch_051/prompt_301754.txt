I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and logic, and this term we have been working through what Gödel's incompleteness theorems actually show when they are exported outside mathematics — in particular, what they imply about the powers of human minds as against computing machines. One of my students has submitted a term paper built around the following argument, which the student claims shows that the computational theory of mind is not merely false but self-refuting. Each step seems plausible to me when I read it, but I find the conclusion far too strong, and I have not been able to say exactly where the damage is done. I want a rigorous diagnosis I can give the student.

The argument, with the technical claims left as the student stated them:

(1) By Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, for any formal system F that contains enough arithmetic to represent basic facts about the natural numbers, if F is consistent, then there is an arithmetic sentence G_F such that F can prove neither G_F nor its negation, and yet G_F is true on the standard interpretation.

(2) Any computing machine that does mathematics proceeds according to a finite body of rules that can be represented as a consistent formal system F_M containing enough arithmetic.

(3) Therefore, for any computing machine M, there is a true arithmetic sentence G_{F_M} that M cannot prove.

(4) I, as a human mathematician, can carry out Gödel's construction for any such machine M whose description I am given: I can write down G_{F_M}, and I can see, by the very reasoning of Gödel's theorem, that G_{F_M} is true.

(5) Hence for every computing machine M, there is an arithmetic truth that I can prove but M cannot prove.

(6) Therefore there is at least one arithmetic truth that I can prove and that no computing machine can prove.

(7) Hence no computing machine can match the mathematical powers of a human mind, and the computational theory of mind is false.

(8) Worse for the computational theorist: if my mind were a computing machine, then by (3) and (4) there would be a true arithmetic sentence — my own Gödel sentence — that I can both prove, because I can see it is true, and cannot prove, because I am that machine. So computationalism is self-refuting, not merely false.

I would much rather keep the incompleteness theorems than this conclusion, but I do not want to reject the argument merely because I dislike where it lands. Please give me a committed diagnosis, not a survey of possible escape routes. Name the first numbered step, or the first inferential transition, at which the argument fails, and explain why it looked as though it could be taken. Then tell me what Gödel's theorems do and do not license here: what would have to be true of a human mind for the move from (3) and (4) to (5) to succeed, what assumptions about a machine's consistency and about my knowledge of that consistency are doing unnoticed work, and whether any weaker anti-mechanist conclusion survives once the argument is corrected. If the first failure is already in the student's statement of the mathematical premise, I want that said plainly, with the premise restated in a form the theorem will actually support.
