I've argued myself into a corner over Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles," and I'd rather not leave it until I know which thing to give up. The part I keep staring at is this, where the Tortoise has already accepted A and B, and has accepted C (the first "if A and B are true, Z must be true"), and Achilles is trying to get her to accept Z:

> "You should call it D, not Z," said Achilles. "It comes next to the other three. If you accept A and B and C, you must accept Z."
> "And why must I?"
> "Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"
> "If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"
> "You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."

The lesson I want to draw is upsettingly tidy: whatever you add to close the gap — another "if P then Q," another rule, another line in the notebook — just becomes a new premise she can accept while still refusing Z. So whatever takes you from premises to conclusion can't itself be a premise. It's a rule you act on, not a proposition you believe. Every chain of reasons has to bottom out in a rule that gets applied, not justified; otherwise you regress forever. That view feels like a clean hard stop, and for a while it felt like the whole problem just dissolved.

But my other hand won't budge. If the rule is "just what we do" — a brute piece of machinery, a habit, a convention — then the Tortoise isn't making an error, she's just playing a seperate game with the same sentences. Deduction doesnt feel like that to me. When I see that Z follows, it feels like I'm tracking something real about how A, B, and C are related, something that's binding on her whether or not she writes down another line. If all I can say is "the rule of inference can't be a premise," I worry I've renamed the gap, not crossed it. Saying "we just apply modus ponens" — the if-then move — sounds like a fact about us, not an answer to her "why must I?"

So I want help deciding which of these should yield and what each option costs. Is my intuition just a confused craving for a justification of the wrong type — asking for a premise where only a rule can go? Or does the "rules aren't premises" view quietly help itself to the very thing it refuses to explain — the force that makes the conclusion follow — as if "we apply the rule" did work it hasn't actually accounted for? Or is there a way both can be true, and what would that leave the Tortoise?
