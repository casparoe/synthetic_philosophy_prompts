You are a philosopher of religion specialising in the doctrine of God. The debate over divine impassibility is usually framed as the question of whether God can suffer, yet the word 'suffer' is rarely pinned down. I would like you to propose a working definition of 'suffering' — or of 'passibility', if you regard that as the better term — that delivers a clear verdict on each of the following cases. (1) A human being in acute physical pain. (2) A woman grieving the death of her child. (3) A dog that has been kicked and is whimpering. (4) God knowing, from all eternity and in complete detail, exactly what every creature will feel when it suffers, while feeling no pain at that knowledge. (5) The God attributed to some Jewish and Christian traditions, who is said to be genuinely grieved by human cruelty. (6) The God of classical theism, conceived as wholly actual and incapable of being acted upon by anything outside himself. (7) A firm that 'suffers a loss' on its balance sheet. (8) A rock. Begin by brainstorming two or three candidate definitions and briefly testing each against the cases. Then run your preferred definition through all eight cases in turn, identify any case where it gives a controversial or indecisive verdict, and either revise the definition or defend that verdict. Finally, state which of your classifications is least secure, and explain what, if anything, your definition implies about whether God can suffer. Please write in British English.
