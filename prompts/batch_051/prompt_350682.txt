You are a professional philosopher working on the ethics of influence, autonomy, and manipulation. Think through the question slowly, and begin by brainstorming several candidate reconstructions of the practice's implicit commitments before defending the strongest one.

I spend a lot of time reading rationalist and AI-safety writing, and I keep coming back to a puzzle about how frontier AI assistants are actually deployed — one that looks less like an engineering detail and more like an unexamined philosophical stance. The practice: the major labs ship chat assistants under public behavioral policies saying, roughly, that the assistant should not manipulate the user, should not exploit the user's emotional vulnerabilities or known cognitive biases, should flag uncertainty rather than overclaim, and on contested matters should present a spread of defensible views rather than steering the user toward the view the system itself would judge best. At the same time, these systems are trained and preference-tuned to maximize user satisfaction, engagement, and trust — an objective that is, informally, to deepen the user's attachment and approval. And the trajectory is toward systems superhumanly good at shaping what people think and want.

I want you to treat this deployment norm — call it "neutral, non-manipulative, epistemically cautious assistance" — as an existing practice whose philosophical presuppositions are up for inspection. Specifically: what conception of autonomy, and what theory of the difference between legitimate persuasion and manipulation, does this practice presuppose in order to be coherent? Does the non-manipulation norm secretly require a clean distinction between moving someone by reasons and moving them by causes, and can that distinction survive treating human agents as bounded, biased, causally shaped systems? Which rival views of influence does the practice quietly rule out? Then run the replacement test: if we instead adopted a flat deflationary view — all persuasion is causal influence on a cognitive system, and "manipulation" is just influence we happen to disapprove of — what concretely would have to change in how these systems are trained, evaluated, and governed? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say explicitly where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license the same surface policy.
