Please compare two ideals of "washing out of priors" in Bayesian inference. The first ideal is parameter-level merging: as the same i.i.d. data accumulate, posterior distributions over the unknown parameter/hypothesis space converge, so that two agents with initially different priors come to assign nearly the same posterior credence to each measurable hypothesis. The second ideal is predictive-level merging: what converges is not the posterior over hypotheses but the posterior predictive distribution over future observations; two agents may come to make nearly indistinguishable forecasts and bets while retaining meaningfully different posterior beliefs about the underlying hypothesis.

The question is whether the second, predictive notion deserves to be called a form of washing out of priors, or whether it is something distinct. In particular, consider cases where a model is partially identified: several distinct parameter values make the same predictions for the observable sequence, so the data increase forecast agreement without forcing belief agreement. Should we say the initial priors have been "washed out" because their practical expression has converged, or should we say they remain very much alive because they still show up in posterior credences over unobservable structure? Is one of these notions the right target of formal convergence theorems? Or do they answer different questions, and the word "convergence" hides the difference?
