So in my Kierkegaard class we've been going through his essay about what he called the present age — the one with all the stuff about the press and the public. The professor keeps zooming in on a couple of ideas: that the press manufactures a kind of phantom public where everyone has an opinion and nobody is answerable, that the age replaces passionate commitment with endless reflection and chatter, and that the only genuine ethical life is becoming what he calls the single individual who actually decides and owns the decision. I can follow that in the abstract, but I want to know whether these categories actually do any work on a concrete case.

The case I keep going back to is Justine Sacco in 2013. Right before a flight to South Africa, she tweeted an offensive joke along the lines that she wouldn't get AIDS in Africa because she's white, and then got on the plane. While she was in the air with no internet, the tweet blew up; by the time she landed she'd been fired and was basically the most hated person on Twitter, all without ever getting to respond. On one hand, that seems like a perfect illustration of the stuff Kierkegaard was complaining about: an anonymous crowd, outrage operating for its own sake rather than as any real response to her, and a weird asymmetry where one particular person had to bear the entire weight of what the public did while the public itself didn't have to own anything. On the other hand, the fit isn't clean. She wasn't exactly a passionate single individual; the crowd was definitely acting and choosing, just destructively; and there was no single press doing the amplifying — the platform made everybody into press and public at once.

What I'd like is a careful two-direction analysis. First, map Kierkegaard's categories onto the episode: which features play the role of the public, the press, leveling, and the single individual, and what would his account have predicted about how the whole thing would end? Second, flip it around: where does the episode confirm his diagnosis, and where does it strain or break it — do the categories from 1846 still cut the way he thought they did, or does a case like this expose something the framework can't see? I'm not after a summary of either the theory or the story; I want the actual mapping and an honest account of where it failes. And please write the answer in British English — my TA marks down American spellings.
