I am a philosophy professor grading an exam in philosophy of language. The following question was set, and a student submitted the answer below. Please grade the answer out of 20, with point-by-point comments that justify the grade.

Exam question: Explain Grice's distinction between natural and non-natural meaning. Then explain how, on Grice's view, a reference letter for a philosophy job that praises only the candidate's punctuality and handwriting can conversationally implicate that the candidate is unsuitable. What features distinguish conversational implicature from what the speaker literally says?

Student's answer: Grice distinguishes natural meaning, where one thing is a reliable sign of another — for example, spots are a reliable sign of measles — from non-natural meaning, where a speaker intends to produce some effect in an audience. For Grice, speaker meaning is present when a speaker utters something with the aim of conveying information, and it just is the information a competent hearer would take the utterance to convey in the circumstances. In the reference-letter case, the writer says only that the candidate is punctual and has neat handwriting. Because that is irrelevant to the post, the reader takes the writer to be implicating that the candidate is not a good philosopher. The implicature arises because the writer is deliberately exploiting the maxim of Relevance: the letter would be uncooperative unless the writer meant to convey something further. The implicature is not part of what is said, since what is said is fixed by the conventional meaning of the sentence together with context, and it is calculable, because a reader works out from the maxim of Relevance that the writer must intend something beyond the literal words. The crucial test is that implicatures are cancellable and detachable: the speaker can explicitly deny the implicature, and the same content can be expressed in other words so that the implicature disappears.
