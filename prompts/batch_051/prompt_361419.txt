I am a novelist, and I have run into a problem about moral testimony. My current book is set in a moral community I do not belong to, and to render that community faithfully I have accepted a large body of its moral judgments on the word of its members: that a certain form of lending is usury and wrong, that children owe their parents a particular deference, that refusing a guest certain things is dishonourable. Within the manuscript I apply these judgments as an insider would — extending them to new cases, letting them conflict, letting them move my characters — but outside it they do not move me, and I would not give them as reasons for my own conduct. Call this representational moral deference.

The criteria I have been given for acquiring moral knowledge from testimony are these: (1) the recipient comes to hold the moral proposition itself, not merely the proposition that the community holds it; (2) her holding rests on the testifier's word rather than on her own independent grasp of the reasons; (3) the proposition is true; (4) she grasps the moral concepts involved well enough to apply them to novel cases on her own.

Give me a committed answer, not a survey of options. Take representational moral deference through the four criteria one by one, say where they underdetermine the verdict, and then take a definite position: does a novelist in my position know these judgments, by testimony, in the morally relevant sense? If not, say whether the fault lies in my case or in criterion (1), and whether criterion (1) quietly demands that moral knowledge answer to the knower — a demand my case may show is doing work the other criteria are not.
