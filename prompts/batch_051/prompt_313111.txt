Suppose you were trying to give a principled account of what makes a characteristic a protected ground of discrimination rather than merely a way in which people differ without legal or moral consequence. Below are eight candidate criteria that theorists, courts, and advocates have invoked for this role:

1. Immutability, or at least the absence of any genuine choice in possessing the trait.
2. Salience: the trait is cheaply observable or inferable, and therefore readily available as a device for quick social sorting.
3. Historical subordination: the group has endured sustained, often legally enforced disadvantage.
4. Political vulnerability: the group cannot reliably defend its interests through ordinary political channels.
5. Moral irrelevance: the trait carries little or no information relevant to legitimate decisions.
6. Stigma: the trait is socially coded as a mark of lesser worth.
7. Insularity: the group is relatively cohesive and bounded, so membership is not easily entered or left.
8. Pervasiveness: the disadvantage attached to the trait recurs across many domains of life, not just one or two settings.

Keep some awkward cases in view. Religion is widely recognised as protected though it can be changed; age is protected though it changes of its own accord; handedness is salient, morally irrelevant, and largely unchosen but is rarely treated as suspect.

Please rank these eight criteria from most to least fundamental, or sort them into tiers, and give a sentence or two for each explaining why it lands where it does. Where two criteria seem to run together, say which, in your judgement, is doing the real work. Please write your answer in American English.
