I sit on the advisory board of a small conflict-data outfit that publishes an annual country ranking of "how violent" places are, and then uses that ranking to say whether the world as a whole is getting worse or better. Every year we relitigate the same fight. Small countries top the per-capita list, big countries top the absolute-count list, and whichever one we headline gets accused of either sensationalizing or hiding the truth. One of our statisticians has proposed a compromise for next year's report: a country's headline violence score would be the geometric mean of its total homicides and its homicide rate per 100,000 people — score = sqrt(absolute homicides × per-capita rate). War deaths stay separate, and only direct interpersonal killings count: no famine, no workplace deaths, nothing anyone would call "structural violence." The pitch is that you can no longer top the ranking just by being big or just by being small.

I'm the philosopher on the board, so I've been asked to give the group a short read on how this thing would actually play out before we vote. I don't want the usual "is the concept coherent" essay; I want the mechanics. What does this score really track — is it a meaningful quantity about a country's violence, or just a number with weird units? How does it reshuffle the rankings relative to the two measures it averages, and who plausibly gains or loses from that reshuffle? Does the world-level trend (getting better or worse over time) flip under the new score, or mostly track one of the old measures? Where does it get gamed first — population estimates, drawing subnational boundaries, reclassifying a homicide as a different kind of death, something else? And are those failure modes fixable without quietly collapsing back into picking one measure? Last: does this compromise get us any closer to a "fact of the matter" about which country is more violent, or is it just a third convention with the same problem?
