I'm a novelist, and the next chapter has a scene where a campaign volunteer tries to talk a friend out of voting for a third-party candidate, and it got me staring at a weird asymmetry in how we treat two nearly identical gambles. If my neighbor spends a whole evening filling out sweepstakes entries and his chance of being the one who changes the result is one in several million, everyone agrees he's wasting his time. But if the same neighbor stands in line to vote in a statewide election where his chance of being the decisive voter is, by any realistic estimate, about one in several million, we call it civic duty — and anyone who says 'my vote won't matter' comes off as cynical or lazy. Structurally I cant see the difference: a small cost, a tiny chance of changing an outcome the person cares about. I know this is adjacent to what people call the paradox of voting. What I want is the strongest principle that could justify the asymmetry, stress-tested on some hard cases: voting in a safe district, voting for a candidate with no chance at all, buying a lottery ticket whose payout goes to a charity instead of yourself, and attending a protest where your individual presence certainly changes nothing. Finally, give me an honest verdict on whether we should keep treating voting as special in kind, or admit the halo is doing the work. Along the way, separate what's genuinely settled among people who study this from what's actually contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. Keep the whole answer under 700 words.
