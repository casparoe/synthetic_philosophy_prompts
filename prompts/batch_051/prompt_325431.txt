Over a decade on a state commission reviewing education proposals, a colleague of mine cast a distinctive set of votes. I have her record on six matters:

1. She voted to let home-schooled students enroll part-time in public schools for laboratory science, music ensembles, and interscholastic athletics, at local discretion.
2. She voted against a universal voucher usable at any private school, including sectarian schools, with no testing or portfolio requirement attached.
3. She voted for a tax-credit scholarship restricted to students assigned to the state's lowest-performing schools, usable at private schools including religious ones, with annual state testing for recipients.
4. She voted to require homeschooling families to submit an annual portfolio and have their children sit for the state test in grades 4, 8, and 10.
5. She voted against a charter expansion in a high-performing suburban district, and voted for a specialized arts charter in a low-income city.
6. She voted to exempt private schools from state curriculum mandates except in health and safety.

Now a bill is before the legislature that would create a universal education savings account. Any family, regardless of income or school performance, could direct a per-pupil amount to any accredited private school or state-approved online program, including religious providers. Students using the account would take the state exam each year, but participating schools would face no new admissions, curriculum, or tuition constraints, and the account would be funded by a per-pupil transfer from the public school formula.

What is she most likely to decide on this bill, and what rationale would best cohere with her recorded votes? I want the candidate rules that fit her record, where they part ways on this bill, which prediction you would defend as most consistent and why, which past vote is the closest precedent, and what further case would do the most to separate the remaining rival rules.

Please respond as a numbered list of exactly six items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, in this order: (1) the best-fitting candidate rules; (2) what each rule predicts about the bill; (3) the verdict you would defend and the chief reason; (4) the closest precedent and whether the bill is more like it or unlike it; (5) the single feature of the bill doing the most analytic work; (6) a further case that would cleanly distinguish the rival rules.
