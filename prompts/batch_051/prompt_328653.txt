You are a philosopher of risk and public policy. Consider two existing institutional practices in the current energy transition. Germany, after the Fukushima disaster, accelerated the permanent shutdown of its nuclear fleet, often justifying this through precaution: even if a severe accident is unlikely, the harm would be catastrophic and partly irreversible, and the choice is made under deep uncertainty rather than reliably measurable risk. France, by contrast, has retained and is now seeking to expand its nuclear fleet, treating reactors as a low-carbon, dispatchable backstop while other sources scale up; on this approach, nuclear risk is one expected harm among many, to be weighed against the harms of climate change, intermittency, and industrial decline.

Begin by identifying the most plausible decision-theoretic frameworks available to each side, then reconstruct the implicit theory of decision under uncertainty that each practice presupposes. Does the German practice presuppose a distinct precautionary principle, or can it be represented as ordinary expected-harm reasoning with different probability and severity inputs? Does the French practice presuppose that nuclear risks are quantifiable enough to trade off, or can it answer a precautionary challenge by arguing that climate change is the more certain catastrophic harm? Distinguish what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, say whether the two practices are genuinely inconsistent or differ mainly in empirical assumptions, and tell me what a precautionary but climate-sensitive policy would have to change about both practices.
