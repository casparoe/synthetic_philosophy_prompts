Compose a short story of about 2,000 words whose plot is driven by the phenomenon sometimes described as the unilateralist's curse. The situation is this: a number of actors each have the power, acting alone, to take a step that will irreversibly change things for everyone concerned; once any one of them acts, the others cannot undo the step, and the cautious have no symmetrical power to prevent it. Each actor forms a private judgement about whether the step is, on balance, worth taking, using shared evidence plus their own hunches and their own threshold for how strong the evidence ought to be. The curse lies in the arithmetic: as the number of actors grows, the probability that at least one of them will act on a mistaken positive judgement grows as well, even if every individual is sensible and well-intentioned. The group's effective decision is made by its least cautious member, while its most cautious members can only abstain.

The story should make that mechanism vivid rather than didactic. Show the private reasoning of at least three characters before the decisive act, including how their estimates of the costs and benefits differ and how their standards of evidence differ. Then show the aftermath, in which the act has turned out, by the standards of most of the group, to have been a mistake; the reader should see that the mistake was produced by the structure of the situation and the distribution of judgements, not by villainy or simple foolishness.

Set the story in a concrete, plausible context of your choosing. It might, for example, concern a distributed network of researchers deciding individually whether to publish a dual-use method, a community of engineers deciding individually whether to deploy an irreversible geoengineering intervention, or a group of biologists deciding individually whether to release a gene-drive construct into the wild. The story need not use the term unilateralist's curse; the plot should embody it.
