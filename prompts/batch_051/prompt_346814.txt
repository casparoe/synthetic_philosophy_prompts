Okay so here's the situation. I teach a philosophy of biology seminar, and every term I get students who arrive already knowing the labels — "I'm a pluralist," "I'm Mayrian" — and then I hand them the asexual rotifers and their positions fall apart. So this year I want to open the species-problem unit with a sorting exercise instead of a survey lecture. About fifteen undergrads, fifty minutes, one prior philosophy course and roughly no biology background. The quiz has to sort them by what they actually think about hard cases, not by which label they like. That means every branch node should be a concrete case or claim a student can answer without knowing what a "phylogenetic species concept" is; the jargon only shows up at the leaves.

Build me either a decision tree or a short scored quiz with a key, whichever gives cleaner sorting, using these four cases, which I'll put on the handout in this simplified form: (a) a ring of populations where neighbors interbreed all the way around a geographic loop but the two end populations, where they meet, do not; (b) an ancient, exclusively asexual lineage that has radiated into several ecologically distinct forms; (c) bacteria that swap genes sideways often enough that their ancestry is reticulate rather than tree-like; and (d) a conservation dispute where a distinctive-looking population hybridizes with a more common one, and whether it gets protected as its own species turns on which concept you adopt. Leaves I need at minimum: biological species concept, at least one phylogenetic species concept (say whether your tree can seperate the diagnosability and monophyly versions, and if it can't, flag that), ecological species concept, morphological concept, and pluralism. Aim for under a dozen questions and whatever number of leaves that yields.

Two more things. For any combination of answers no leaf occupies, tell me whether it's empty because the position is incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to develop it. And I want the tree to notice whether "pluralism" hides two different routes — species talk is purpose-relative, versus the evidence currently underdetermines a single concept — and either split them or say explicitly what extra question would. If two leaves turn out to give the same verdicts on all four cases, say how we'd ever tell them apart.
