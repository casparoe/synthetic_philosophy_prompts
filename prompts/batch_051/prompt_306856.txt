Ok here's one I keep coming back to when I teach Kant. Whenever I, or the textbook, or a student, tries to make the universalizability test intuitive, we all reach for the same move: imagine your maxim were to become a universal law of nature, as if the will were legislating for nature itself rather than just for you. That law-of-nature bit is doing a lot of quiet work, and I'm starting to think it smuggles in more than it's licensed to. I want you to audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence table first: what is supposed to map onto what — the maxim onto a law, the agent onto a legislator, the kingdom of ends onto nature or a system of laws, maybe your inclinations onto causal forces. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and seperate the disanalogies that actually matter for the test from the ones that are just surface. For example: laws of nature are discovered, exceptionless, and morally inert, while a self-legislated moral law is none of those. If a first-year turns the test into the question what if everybody did that, and then cashes it out as what if this were a law of physics, have they gotten the categorical imperative or a different test entirely? And does Kant's distinction between a contradiction in conception and a contradiction in willing still track anything real once you strip the nature-picture away, or was it riding on the analogy all along? Be honest about whether the analogy is doing more harm than good, and if so, give me a better one.
