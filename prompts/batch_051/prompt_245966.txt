I keep tripping over the opening of A Scandal in Bohemia: "To Sherlock Holmes she is always the woman." A few lines on, Watson describes her as "the late Irene Adler, of dubious and questionable memory," which is strange on two counts — in the episode he goes on to tell, she is very much alive, and "memory" is doing a lot of work. I'd like a reading of the story through the philosophy-of-language debate over empty names and truth in fiction. Here's the wrinkle that interests me: the story turns Adler into a towering figure mostly through Holmes's memory of her and through traces — she's on the page only briefly, sees through the fake-fire trick, outsmarts him, and then she's gone, leaving a letter and a photograph that Holmes keeps. That's almost exactly what the Holmes stories do with Holmes himself: there never was any such man, yet we talk about him the way Watson talks about Adler, as if the interesting question were whether the claims are true, not what makes them true. Do you think the story is aware it's doing that? I want a reading that stays close to the scenes I named, and that says honestly where the Frege/Russell/Kripke-ish machinary helps with this particular story and where it distorts it — and, if you think the story adds something to the debate that the theory papers can't, say what it is. Not after a summary of the plot or the theories.
