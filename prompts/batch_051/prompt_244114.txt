As a retired natural scientist who has come to philosophy rather late in life, I have been rereading Samuel Butler's 'Darwin among the Machines' (1863), a letter to the editor of The Press in Christchurch. I should like the selectionist and the exceptionalist about machine evolution to produce the document of an adversarial collaboration. By the selectionist camp I mean the view that competitive selection among artificial systems can become an autonomous causal process, one that may in time favour machine interests over human interests even though no designer intended that outcome; by the exceptionalist camp I mean the view that artefacts remain embedded in human intentions, demand, and maintenance, so that selection among them is at most a loose metaphor and cannot systematically displace human beings. Butler's letter contains the following passage:

"We have used the words “mechanical life,” “the mechanical kingdom,” “the mechanical world” and so forth, and we have done so advisedly, for as the vegetable kingdom was slowly developed from the mineral, and as in like manner the animal supervened upon the vegetable, so now in these last few ages an entirely new kingdom has sprung up, of which we as yet have only seen what will one day be considered the antediluvian prototypes of the race."

Please begin by stating, in terms both camps can accept, what Butler is claiming in this passage and what would have to be true for the claim to be correct. Then set out: (a) the claims both camps can sign; (b) the residual disagreement, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own view; (c) the cruxes on which the disagreement actually turns; and (d) for each crux, a concrete case, test, or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Please also say whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal. Format the result as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.
