I have been drafting a novel in which a minor antagonist—a self-taught lecturer with a gift for grand synthesis—insists that all human institutions are "just Darwinism." Whenever he is challenged, he falls back on a line I have since heard repeated in other forms: "survival of the fittest is a tautology." On his telling, fitness is defined by what survives, so the phrase simply says that the successful succeed; it cannot be tested, cannot be evidence, and can therefore be bent to any ideological use he likes. I have also come across a more respectable version of the worry in Karl Popper, who once refused to call Darwinism a testable scientific theory and treated it instead as a broad metaphysical framework, though I believe he later revised that position.

What I need, for the novel and for my own thinking, is a careful disambiguation of the one-liner. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that "survival of the fittest is a tautology" might be making—what 'fit' could mean, whether 'survives' refers to actual or expected outcomes, whether the charge is about definitions, logical form, or the availability of evidence—and then say for each reading whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false? Please also separate what is settled among philosophers of biology from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
