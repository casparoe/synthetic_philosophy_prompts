A friend of mine corrected me the other day: I'd said the storm "decimated" the downtown strip, and he said the word can only mean "kill one in ten," because that's what it meant originally — some Roman army punishment, I think. When I objected that words change, he said "the original meaning is the real meaning" and looked pleased with himself. I'm sure there's something wrong with that move and something right lurking inside it, but I can't seperate them. Lay out the different precise claims that slogan could be making: about where a word's meaning lives, about whether an older sense stays the "real" one after speakers have moved on, about whether ordinary speakers can be wrong about the meanings of the very words they use, about whether a word can have several senses of which one is oldest and none is "more real." Give each as a separate reading, say for each whether it's a platitude, a defensible substantive claim, or false, and then tell me which readings would actually support his correction about the storm, if any. End with the strongest true thing hiding behind the slogan, stated carefully, so I can grant him that without granting the correction.
