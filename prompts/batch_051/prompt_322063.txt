I recently left a research role to join my lab's external-affairs team. The move happened after we came within days of shipping a safety-critical model ahead of a rival, and I have now been assigned to evaluate a proposed mutual-transparency agreement with that rival. A colleague with a background in political science gave me the following argument against the agreement. I find its conclusion too strong, but I need to say precisely where it goes wrong before I write my memo, and I want to know both which step to reject and what I give up by rejecting it.

(1) If two rational actors persist in a costly conflict even though there is some bargain both would prefer to fighting, then at least one of three things must hold: (a) private information — they are uncertain about each other's capabilities or resolve, and each has reason to misrepresent; (b) a commitment problem — a bargain available today would become unenforceable or unstable as the distribution of power shifts; or (c) indivisibility — the thing at stake cannot be divided in a way both can accept. This is the rationalist account of war associated with Fearon, with its three mechanisms.

(2) The AI race is such a conflict. Both our lab and the rival would do better under a jointly verified bargain in which neither deploys until an external auditor has certified safety; racing makes each of us spend more and accept more tail risk than that bargain would.

(3) Therefore the race persists because of at least one of (a), (b), or (c).

(4) The transparency agreement I am evaluating provides only mutual audits of model evaluations, compute, and safety procedures. That genuinely narrows (a), private information and the incentive to misrepresent, but it creates no enforcement capacity, so it does not directly address (b) or (c).

(5) And (b) is plainly present here. Transformative AI looks winner-take-most, so the lab that deploys first may lock in a durable, perhaps permanent, lead. Because both sides can foresee that power shift, neither can credibly commit to a pause; any promise to pause is worth little once the other lab is about to deploy, since honoring the promise would mean handing away a decisive advantage.

(6) Therefore the transparency agreement cannot, by itself, end the race; only something that solves the commitment problem — for instance, an enforcement body with the power to punish defection — could.

(7) The same reasoning seems to apply to any contest with four features: two or more actors, rivalry that is costly relative to an available bargain, a durable first-mover advantage, and a transparency mechanism that reveals private information without adding external enforcement. Patent races, academic priority races, electoral primaries, and startup fundraising all have these features.

(8) So, by (1)–(6), transparency cannot by itself end any of those races either; only externally enforced commitment devices could.

(9) But that is doubtful. Patent disclosure has shaped the direction and timing of invention without any enforcer punishing racing as such; academic priority races are moderated by publication norms rather than by a central enforcement body; and campaign-disclosure rules change candidate behavior even though no institution prevents an aggressive early campaign as such.

(10) So the argument proves too much. Where does it go wrong? If forced to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (5): (1) is the framework I am expected to work within, and (5) matches what I see inside the lab. Which premise or step should I reject, why did it look acceptable, and what does rejecting it cost me in the memo I have to write? Please end with a final line, beginning with "Verdict:", that states in a single sentence which step or premise to reject — or, if the argument is valid, which premise I should abandon.
