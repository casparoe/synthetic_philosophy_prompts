I'm a retired earth scientist (geochemistry and ocean drilling, mostly) and Ive gotten into philosophy of science late in life. Lately I've been reading about inference to the best explanation, and I keep wanting to stress-test it against an episode I saw the later part of up close: the plate tectonics revolution.

I dont need the history summarized for me—I know the timeline and the evidence. What I want is the philosophical mapping, with the details kept in view. Treat the move from "continents are fixed, and the odd fossil and rock distributions are explained by land bridges or sunken continents" to "continents and ocean floor are moving plates" as a case study in inference to the best explanation.

Walk me through it at three stages: (1) Wegener's drift, with the jigsaw fit of the continental shelves, the matching fossil belts, and the Permo-Carboniferous glaciation evidence, but with no physically plausible mechanism for moving continents; (2) the postwar period, when paleomagnetism gave apparent polar wander paths that made little sense unless continents had moved, and when symmetric magnetic stripes on the seafloor suggested seafloor spreading; and (3) the late-1960s synthesis of ridges, trenches, transform faults, and deep earthquake zones.

For each stage, tell me what the live competing explanations were, what the evidence favored, and whether inference to the best explanation, as philosophers describe it, would have said the community was rationally switching allegiance or rationally dragging its feet. Use the loveliness-versus-likeliness distinction (the loveliest explanation need not be the likeliest) to diagnose what was actually going on. Was Wegener's drift the loveliest explanation in the 1920s but not the likeliest, so the rejection was not just sociological stubbornness? And did the eventual vindication of drift confirm that inference to the best explanation describes real scientific change, or does the story mostly reveal how much background beliefs and mechanism requirements were doing the work?

I want the analysis to run in both directions: what does the episode reveal about the theory, not just what the theory says about the episode. Where does inference to the best explanation break down or need stretching here—underdetermination, the rationality of dissent, the role of prior probability, the decades where the best explanation had no mechanism? I'm less after a clean verdict than an honest account of which parts of the episode the model fits and which parts fit it badly.
