Okay so I TA an upper-level undergrad class called “The Idea of a Writing System”—about 16 students, 75-minute section. Next week we’re reading Henry Sweet’s preface to A Handbook of Phonetics (1877), and I keep coming back to this line:

“Without a knowledge of the laws of sound-change, scientific philology — whether comparative or historical — is impossible, and without phonetics their study degenerates into a mere mechanical enumeration of letter-changes.”

I want a 75-minute activity in which Sweet’s worry gets inverted: not “why you need phonetics to avoid treating letters as language itself,” but “why you can’t and shouldn’t write everything phonetically, and why a practical orthography is always a theory of what the language’s real units are.” The students should do most of the arguing, and the concept should come out in the game before I name it.

Design a runnable structured game with: a small artificial language (maybe 30 words with root-final consonants that assimilate, a couple of vowel reductions in unstressed syllables, and two “dialect” pronunciations), roles or teams (phonetician, etymologist, scribe, new reader, language committee), a rule that after the committee approves a spelling they have to teach five new readers to pronounce novel forms from the spelling alone, and a debrief that maps their failures back to that Sweet passage and to the difference between phonemic, morphophonemic, and lexical spellings. Include a point where the activity will force a vote that one faction loses, and tell me how to make that loss productive rather than just annoying. Also say what real-world process this won’t capture and where I’ll have to supply the bigger picture myself.
