I'm the Year 12 rep on our student council's constitution commitee, and we've got ourselves into a proper argument about a rule that looked totally boring until last week. Our old governing document says, in Section 7:

'Every member of the Sixth Form who has attained the age of sixteen years shall be entitled to vote at an election for the Student Council, and shall be required to do so unless the Returning Officer certifies that they were absent from school on the day of the election or otherwise excused.'

That's copied exactly from the document, so the wording is a bit legal-ish. The thing is, the constitution was written in 2009 by people who, as far as anyone can tell, never imagined two things that are true now. First, the school has started a year-long exchange with a lycée in Dijon, so nine French pupils are fully enrolled in Year 12: they're on the register, have IDs, come to registration, all of it, but they aren't British citizens and most will leave after the summer term. Second, a boy in Year 12 called Asif skipped a year earlier on, so he's doing A-levels with us but won't actually turn sixteen until a week after the March election.

The council has to issue a ruling on whether the French pupils count as members who are entitled (and actually required) to vote, and whether Asif is allowed to vote at all. I'd like you to work through what Section 7 gives under each of the usual ways of reading it: the plain wording, the purpose behind the rule, what the 2009 drafters would have said if they'd been asked, and what a reasonable sixth-former reading it today would take it to mean. Then tell me which interpretation you'd write into the ruling and why.

Please structure the answer with these sections in this order: (1) Plain wording (about 150 words), (2) Purpose (about 150 words), (3) The drafters' likely view (about 100 words), (4) The reasonable reader (about 150 words), and (5) My ruling (about 200 words). In each section cover both the French pupils and Asif, and if one approach just doesn't decide one of the cases, say that outright rather than stretching it.
