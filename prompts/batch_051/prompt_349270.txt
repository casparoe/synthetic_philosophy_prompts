I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but a question about graded moral status keeps coming back to me. I'm drawn to a systematic view: the properties that seem morally relevant—sensitivity to harm, the capacity to learn, the integration of information, goal-directedness—come in degrees, and a system's moral status should track where it sits along those dimensions. The view has real virtues for me. It avoids drawing a single bright line between things that matter and things that don't, it lets me take insect welfare seriously without pretending a bee's life counts anywhere near a human's, and it provides a clean procedure: locate the system on the relevant axes and scale concern accordingly.

The difficulty is that I also have a firm intuition that a thermostat has no moral status whatsoever—not very little, but none. Disassembling one, overwriting its firmware, or tossing it in a landfill isn't wrong in even the smallest degree, holding aside effects on people. Yet a thermostat is not inert: it senses a variable, compares it against a set point, and acts to close the gap, which makes it goal-directed in at least an engineering sense, and it therefore scores above zero on at least one of the dimensions I listed. My work probably feeds the intuition: I regularly build systems that steer toward target states without there being any felt perspective to the system, so I am reluctant to treat that kind of goal-directedness as morally weighty. But if the underlying features run on a continuum from thermostat to insect to mouse, and I have no non-arbitrary place to draw a threshold along that continuum, then I seem forced into one of two positions I want to reject: either the thermostat has some nonzero amount of moral status, or there is a sharp threshold—some discrete ingredient present in insects but absent in thermostats—that I cannot currently specify and that would undermine the gradualism I found attractive. I can see the obvious escape routes: my thermostat intuition might be a scope-insensitivity artifact or a category mistake, and the graded view might simply need an additional necessary condition such as valence or a point of view. But I cannot tell whether these are real resolutions or just ways of not facing the conflict.

I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the thermostat intuition to be the thing that gets debunked, what it would take for the gradualist picture to be the thing that gets amended or abandoned, and whether there is a reading of "moral status" on which the continuity thesis and the zero-for-thermostats intuition are compatible.
