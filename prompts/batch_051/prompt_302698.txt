I have a standing policy about how much to trust my own unaided judgment, and I want to know whether it is defensible. I treat rationality as a trainable skill rather than a fixed trait, and I train it roughly the way a martial artist trains: through regular isolated drills, katas, designed to sharpen moves I hope will carry over to live reasoning. Each morning I write down calibrated probabilities for a batch of settleable claims from the next few weeks (sports outcomes, election results, verdicts in high-profile trials, and the like) and later score myself by whether the events I assigned 70% happened about 70% of the time. I also run a pre-mortem before major decisions: I imagine the decision has already failed and write out the most plausible causal story of how it failed. Once a week I argue the opposite of a belief I hold, for ten uninterrupted minutes.

The policy I want examined is this: because I have kept up these drills, I now lend my fast, high-confidence intuitions more weight than I used to, including in domains far from the drills—political economy, reading other people, personal decisions. In particular, after a few weeks of consistent practice, I let a strong snap judgment override a slower and more uncertain analysis, even on topics where I have never drilled. I am treating the drills as having improved my reasoning in general, not merely my performance on the drills.

Is this policy defensible? Please take a committed position rather than laying out options. Address the transfer problem directly: what would have to be true about transfer of training—near transfer to tasks that resemble the drills versus far transfer to unstructured, high-stakes domains—for practice on scored calibration and pre-mortems to justify greater trust in untrained snap judgments? Distinguish improvements in scoreable metacognitive habits (assigning probabilities, noticing overconfidence) from improvements in the substance of first-order reasoning. And if the policy as stated fails, give the strongest defensible restatement of it: which components of rationality can plausibly be trained this way, and what would genuine evidence of far transfer look like? In particular, is the asker's increased self-trust a trained improvement or a bias dressed up as a rule?

Structure your response as follows:

1. The policy, restated (about 150 words): state the habit and the transfer claim it depends on.
2. The case for the policy (about 250 words): the strongest reasons to think these drills improve general reasoning and entitle the asker to greater self-trust.
3. The case against the policy (about 250 words): the main ways this goes wrong, especially the transfer problem and the risk of miscalibrated self-trust.
4. What practice can and cannot train (about 200 words): separate the trainable sub-skills from the parts of judgment the drills plausibly leave untouched.
5. Verdict (about 150 words): give a definite overall judgment on the stated policy, and state the restated policy you would actually endorse.
