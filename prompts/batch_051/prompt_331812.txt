Proposal for our department's world-history survey, which i've been asked to poke holes in. We scrap the usual chronological narrative. Each week, the instructor draws a region and a fifty-year window at random and has to lecture on that region in that period using only categories people in that place actually used at the time — so no "Middle Ages" or "feudalism" for Japan or India, no "Renaissance" outside the places where it makes sense, no "early modern" unless the source culture had some analogue. Lucky draws exist: if you get Florence 1490–1540 you're fine, if you get central Asia 850–900 good luck. Comparisons across regions are banned until a single end-of-term capstone session. Walk me through what this course actually becomes: what students learn and quietly stop learning, how instructors will game the lottery and the vocabulary rule (by redefining terms, by picking sources that just happen to fit, by smuggling European period labels in through supposedly neutral economic or social categories), where the scheme breaks first, and whether "telling the past from everywhere at once" is being approximated or just replaced by a pile of disconnected local stories with the old center still hiding in the syllabus design.
