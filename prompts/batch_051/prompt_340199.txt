Consider the following argument for the thesis that satisficing, as a standard for rational choice, is not a genuine alternative to expected-utility maximization. Assume throughout an agent with limited time, information, and computational capacity, choosing among options whose values she assesses under uncertainty. By a “satisficing criterion” I mean a decision rule of the form: stop searching and accept the first option whose expected value meets or exceeds some aspiration level, fixed in advance or adjusted adaptively. By “maximizing” I mean: choose the option or rule with the highest expected value among the feasible alternatives.

(1) An agent’s satisficing criterion must include some aspiration level determining what counts as good enough; otherwise the rule does not tell her when to stop searching and accept an option.
(2) A satisficing criterion is rationally justified for an agent only if, in light of her beliefs about the choice environment, the expected value of acting on that criterion is at least as high as the expected value of acting on any feasible alternative criterion.
(3) The expected value of acting on a criterion includes both the value of the options it tends to select and the costs of search and deliberation incurred under it.
(4) Therefore, a satisficing criterion is rationally justified only if it has the highest expected value among feasible criteria.
(5) Choosing the criterion with the highest expected value among feasible criteria just is an expected-utility maximization, carried out over criteria rather than over first-order options.
(6) Hence, any rationally justified satisficing criterion is simply the output of maximizing expected utility at the level of criteria.
(7) So satisficing is not an alternative to the maximizing conception of rationality; it merely relocates maximization from options to decision rules.

Is this argument valid? If it is valid, say so, and identify the premise a defender of genuinely bounded rationality should reject. If it is not valid, identify the first numbered step at which it fails, and say whether the failure is a false premise, a non sequitur, or an equivocation in a key term. In either case, give the strongest reply available to a defender of satisficing.

Structure your response in four sections, in this order:
(1) “Verdict”: one or two sentences stating whether the argument goes through.
(2) “The case for validity”: about 200 words, explaining how each step could be defended, including the step you ultimately reject (if any).
(3) “The case against”: about 300 words, isolating the exact point where the argument fails or where a defender of satisficing must dig in, and stating precisely the nature of the failure or the contested premise.
(4) “My final judgment”: about 150 words, answering the strongest counterargument and saying what you think the argument really establishes, even if that is weaker than its stated conclusion.
