In a reading group and a couple of forum threads I frequent, I have repeatedly seen the two-word slogan "all is one" used to end arguments about ontology, and each time it appeared to be doing different work: once to dismiss the question whether a pile of sand is one thing or many as merely verbal, once to claim that boundaries between selves are illusions, and once to claim that physics already describes a single object, the universe. I want a disambiguation, because I suspect the slogan is being borrowed at very different levels of ambition. What precise theses could "all is one" be expressing? I can think of at least these: that every apparent individual is numerically identical to every other; that there is exactly one substance and everything else is a modification of it; that the universe is one physical object whose parts are arbitrary; that exactly one thing is fundamental and everything else derives from it; and that the slogan is only the banal point that everything is connected or part of one aggregate. For each candidate reading, or any better-stated one you can add, say whether it is trivial, substantive, or false, and say which of my three encounters it would actually license. Throughout, separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
