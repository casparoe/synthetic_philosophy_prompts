A friend laid out the following argument and I cannot find the exit from it.

(1) The value of a single conscious experience is the product of two factors: its degree of consciousness — the intensity, richness, or wakefulness of the awareness in play — and its hedonic quality, that is, how good or bad it feels. In symbols, v = c × h.

(2) The value of a whole life, and of a whole world, is the sum of the values of the conscious experiences it contains. Once each experience has its value, the value of a set of them is fixed by addition.

(3) From (1) and (2), quantity can substitute for quality without limit. Consider, on one side, a world containing a billion lives of full consciousness and very high happiness; on the other, a world containing only faint, brief, barely pleasant flickers of experience, each just slightly above zero on both c and h. Since each flicker contributes a positive amount and nothing caps the total, there is some sufficiently large number of flickers whose summed value exceeds the value of the billion flourishing lives.

(4) But that cannot be right: no heap of barely conscious, barely pleasant flickers could be better than a world of a billion flourishing human lives.

(5) So at least one of (1) and (2) must go. The natural place to break the argument is (2): make one of the two factors lexically prior. A lexical view says that below some threshold on the consciousness dimension, or on the happiness dimension, no amount of experience can compensate for the loss of even one experience above the threshold. With the threshold set sensibly, one fully conscious, happy life outweighs any number of faint flickers, and the conclusion of (3) is blocked.

(6) But lexicality merely replaces one absurdity with another. A threshold on a continuous dimension must sit somewhere; suppose it sits at h = 0.800. Then one experience at 0.800 is judged better than any number of experiences at 0.799, however astronomically large, even though 0.799 and 0.800 are experientially almost indistinguishable. If you respond that the threshold is vague, or try to relocate it, you either reintroduce the kind of quantitative tradeoff that led to (3) or simply push the same discontinuity elsewhere.

(7) Therefore the two-factor view, together with any way of aggregating consciousness and happiness, either collapses into the unacceptable conclusion in (3) or must treat an unnoticeable difference in quality as an infinite difference in value. Since (4) excludes the first alternative and ordinary judgment excludes the second, the view appears unable to compare lives or worlds at all — which would mean either that such comparisons have no determinate answer or that one of these individually plausible steps is wrong.

I want to push back, but I cannot locate the mistake. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to abandon? I would most like to keep (4), but I suspect that may be the one that has to give.
