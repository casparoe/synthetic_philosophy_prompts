I'm a retired physicist who came to philosphy of mathematics late, mostly through arguments with my daughter who teaches high school math. She swears by motivation-first teaching — start with an interesting problem or picture, build intuition, hold off on definitions and proofs. I've been trying to turn my resistance into a clean argument, and I've got something that feels right but also feels like it proves too much, so I suspect theres a skipped step. Here it is:

(1) Genuine mathematical understanding requires rigor: knowing that the derivative of x squared is 2x is not the same as knowing why, and the why has to bottom out in exact definitions and gap-free inference. (2) Motivation-first teaching engages students exactly by relaxing that rigor — intuition, pictures, examples, computational templates, with proof either postponed or mostly left out. (3) So the form of the mathematics a student acquires in that setting is non-rigorous. (4) But a person whose grasp of a piece of mathematics is non-rigorous fails the condition in (1), so they don't genuinely understand it. (5) Therefore motivation-oriented teaching cannot produce genuine mathematical understanding, and the more it motivates, the more efficiently it produces a confident imitation of understanding.

I can't tell whether this is valid and the conclusion just seems harsh because I like her teaching, or whether one of the steps is doing something fishy — my own suspicion is the move from 'acquired non-rigorously' to 'grasp is non-rigorous', but I can't make the objection precise. Wich step, if any, fails, and what would a corrected version of the argument say? Also, could you write your answer in American English?
