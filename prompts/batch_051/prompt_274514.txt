I am a retired geochemist, and after four decades of thinking about phase equilibria and reaction pathways, I have lately been reading contemporary metaphysics for the same reason I once read thermodynamics: because the structure of dependence relations is satisfying to think about. The idea I would most like to share with my former colleagues is the notion of grounding—the non-causal relation expressed by locutions such as 'in virtue of' and a non-causal 'because.' I have been asked to lead a ninety-minute session for the philosophy discussion group at our local scientific society, which comprises about fifteen retired scientists and engineers. Most of them have never read analytic metaphysics, though they are comfortable with necessary and sufficient conditions, with the direction of causal explanation, and with the idea that some explanations are mathematical rather than causal. I want them to come away with a felt sense of three connected points: that grounding is an explanatory dependence relation distinct from causation; that it is asymmetric and transitive, so the order of dependence matters and reversing it changes the content; and that there is a substantive question, which metaphysicians call well-foundedness, about whether chains of such dependence must eventually terminate in fundamental facts or may descend without end or circle back on themselves.

Please design a runnable classroom-style activity for this group, with no lecture up front. The activity should force participants to sort, negotiate, or generate chains of 'in virtue of' claims—perhaps using cards or tokens representing facts—and thereby run into the pressure to posit a bottom level, the discomfort of a loop, and the difference between making something happen and making something what it is. Give me concrete rules, simple materials, and a timeline ending in a debrief that maps what happened in the activity onto the philosophical distinctions. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and end with an honest note about which part of the idea the activity cannot itself produce and therefore must be supplied by the facilitator.
