I follow a policy for the revisionist history I get sent by relatives and acquaintances: an Atlantis documentary from a cousin, an ancient-aliens explanation of the pyramids, a long post arguing that the received chronology of the ancient and medieval world is an early-modern fabrication. I call the policy the coordination-burden test. Before reading whatever evidence is presented, I ask what the world would have to be like for the claim to be true, and specifically how many independent people, institutions, and physical lines of evidence would need to be forged, suppressed, or brought into line through coordination rather than ordinary mistakenness. If the answer is a large number, I set the claim's probability low enough to ignore and do not inspect the evidence further.

So I dismiss the claim that the pyramids were built by a lost civilization not because I have checked the engineering, but because accepting it would require the archaeological record — excavated settlements, quarries, inscriptions, radiocarbon dates — to be pervasively misread or concealed by people with strong incentives and capacity to correct one another. I treat a new-chronology claim as effectively false because it would require the coordinated fabrication or recalibration of archives, chronicles, languages, and material remains across unrelated cultures and centuries, with no plausible mechanism and no one breaking ranks. I would not dismiss, by contrast, a revision that merely required a field to have been wrong about something: the test is triggered specifically by the need for broad coordination or concealment, not by disagreement with experts.

My question is whether this shortcut is defensible, or whether it is simply consensus bias wearing a probabilistic costume. My estimate of how independent the lines of evidence are is itself the product of the very mainstream picture I am trying to test, and a determined fringe theorist can always redescribe requires the field to be wrong as requires the field to be suppressing the truth. Is there a version of the coordination-burden test that keeps the benefit — I am not prepared to spend a weekend on every claim sent to me — without making me structurally unable to recognize a genuine revolutionary revision that does imply extensive error by existing experts? Under what conditions would the rule fail, and how would I tell from the inside whether I am applying it or hiding behind it?
