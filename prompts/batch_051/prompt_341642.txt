In a reading group on Hume's Dialogues a couple of my cohort went at it over Part 11, where Philo lays out what he calls the first circumstance that introduces evil: roughly, the way animal life is contrived so that pains, as well as pleasures, are employed to excite creatures to action and self-preservation, when pleasure alone could in principle have done the job. One of them, Mara, said this is still one of the strongest versions of the evidential problem because it attacks the design argument at its own level—not just the amount of suffering, but the apparent gratuitousness of pain as a motivational architecture. Julian replied that the premise that pleasure alone would have sufficed is an empirical claim about the space of possible creatures, and no one can know that; a design theodicy can say pain is necessary for finite creatures with the kind of agency God wanted. Mara came back that this is special pleading: if appeals to unknown necessity can save theism, they can justify any horror whatsoever, and the whole problem of evil is designed to close that escape hatch. Julian said theism only needs a logically possible reason, not a known one, and that Mara's confidence about what could have been is just the kind of causal overconfidence Hume himself spent his career dismantling. They went a fourth round on whether Philo's argument is local—only undercutting inference to a benevolent designer—or a global case against theism, and at that point I wasn't able to follow the thread.
