A friend of a friend has been pushing a governance scheme for stratospheric aerosol injection, and I keep going back and forth on whether it's a real safety device or just a way to make unilateral deployment look manageable enough that someone actually tries it. The basic idea: before any state or coalition can deploy, they have to keep a "termination escrow" on deposit with a treaty secretariat. Each year the required balance is recalculated as (projected cost of phasing out the aerosol layer gradually over ten years) + (estimated damages from the fast catch-up warming if the program stops) − (a discount proportional to the verified, additional cuts in long-lived greenhouse gases the deployer has made below a negotiated baseline). If the deployer walks away, the escrow funds an orderly phase-down, and countries can file claims for damages from the temperature overshoot, paid out of whatever remains. To keep the claims process from being a rubber stamp, half the panel is appointed by states classified as net losers under the aerosol regime itself.

I'm not asking whether this is a good idea in the abstract. I want to know how it would actually behave. Who deploys under these rules, and who stays outside them and deploys anyway? What does the emissions-cut discount actually do—does it offset the moral hazard problem (deployment lets everyone drag their feet on decarbonization), or does it just give the deployer a new variable to game, and what does that gaming look like? How do the deployer, other countries, and the claims panel each respond once the escrow exists? What happens if the escrow is badly underestimated, or if the phase-down cost guesses turn out wrong mid-deployment? And which of the failure modes could be fixed without gutting the scheme, as opposed to being baked in because termination shock is inherently a sudden, non-marginal event that no pot of money can really smooth over?
