I'm in an Indian epistemology course this term, and I also took a databases class last year, and I cant stop seeing a parallel that I can't decide is deep or just pattern matching.

On the Mīmāṃsā side: a cognition is supposed to be intrinsically valid (svataḥ prāmāṇya). You don't establish the validity of a piece of testimony by checking the speaker and then accepting it; the understanding itself already gives you something that counts as knowledge, and it stays standing unless a later cognition specifically contradicts it. Error is handled after the fact, by conflict, rather than prevented in advance. The Veda is the pure case: it has no author (apauruṣeya), so there's no fallible source whose defects could form a defeater, and its testimony just remains authoritative.

This looks, structurally, a lot like optimistic concurrency control in databases. Ordinary concurrency control locks things up front to check before you proceed. OCC instead lets each transaction run on the assumption that conflicts are rare, and only at commit time does it check: if two transactions conflict over the same data, the later one gets rolled back. Default to validity, correct only on conflict.

The mapping I can't get out of my head: cognition ↔ transaction, understanding testimony ↔ executing without pre-checking, a contradiction ↔ a detected conflict, sublation ↔ rollback, and the authorless Veda ↔ a transaction that never finds a conflicting write.

Is this a genuine identity of structure, or is the word conflict doing all the work? If it is deep, what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer? For instance, OCC degrades badly under high contention — would that mean a svataḥ-prāmāṇya epistemology should fail in adversarial or contradiction-rich environments, and does it? And can you name something the parallel predicts that turns out false? I want to know exactly where the mapping breaks before I write this up for class.
