I write novels, so I've spent alot of time thinking about the shape of a life, and I have a professional bias towards event — give a character too many unchanged days and a reviewer calls it plotless. So when I'm trying to work out whether novelty is actually part of a good life, or just a tool for stopping pleasure from decaying and attention from flatlining, I don't trust my own instincts. Here are my gut verdicts on some cases, and I want to know what principle they add up to, if they add up to anything.

Case 1. My uncle has read the same fifteen or so novels in rotation for thirty years and says each pass shows him something new in them. I don't think he's missing out.

Case 2. A couple I know have spent the same fortnight every year in the same damp cottage, walked the same loop to the same pub, and driven the same lane home, for forty years. They're the happiest people I can name. I don't think something is absent from the middle of their life.

Case 3. A friend of a friend can't bear an hour with nothing in it. New podcasts, new people, new plans, new city, always the next thing. He says he's terrified of being bored. I think he's badly off, and I don't think the newness is helping him — it's more like living on stimulants.

Case 4. Here's the one that pulls the other way. An artist I know spent three years painting the same corner of her kitchen, and I don't think that was a mistake. But when she switched to a new subject each month, the work got more alive in a way I can't put down to skill or pleasure or output. I want to say the newness itself was doing some of the work.

Case 5. I know a man who's been to forty countries and can barely say a true thing about any of them, and a woman who has never left her county and knows the light at each hour on each footpath. I think the second life is at least as good — actually, I think it's better. If novelty were an ingredient of well-being, that should jar me more than it does.

The hard pair is Case 2 and Case 4. Most of my instincts say novelty is a means — it keeps the machine from gumming up. Then Case 4 makes me think newness can be part of the good itself, and I can't get the two to sit together.

And there's one more I keep going back and forth on. A colleague of mine has just turned fifty and has gone back to university to read archaeology, not for any job. She's already done pottery, chess, Spanish, and now this. She says the beginner feeling is the only time she feels properly alive. Is she the painter or the terrified man?

What principle, if any, captures the pattern here? Answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no conclusion. I'd like the five to be: (1) the principle you think best fits my verdicts, stated plainly; (2) how that principle handles Case 2 and Case 4 together; (3) the verdict it gives on my archaeologist colleague; (4) which of my gut verdicts it forces me to drop or reinterpret, if any; and (5) the rival principle I'm probably flirting with and why it's worse.
