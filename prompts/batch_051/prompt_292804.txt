I spent my career in the behavioral and neural sciences and have only recently begun reading academic philosophy. The paradox of fiction has occupied me for the past few weeks, and I find that I am more puzzled by the paradox than by the phenomenon it purports to explain. Let me set out the deflationary argument I keep wanting to give, and ask you to tell me exactly where it fails, if it does.

(1) The paradox of fiction is supposed to be that experienced readers and viewers are genuinely moved—that they feel fear, pity, grief—by characters and events they know perfectly well are unreal.

(2) The puzzle is generated by the assumption that a person cannot have an emotion such as fear or pity toward an object unless she believes the object exists and believes that the represented situation is real.

(3) But that assumption is empirically false. In humans and other animals, affective systems respond to perceived, remembered, and imagined threats and losses through appraisal-like processes that run in an as-if mode: threat conditioning, fear-potentiated startle, and autonomic preparation can be driven by cues the organism consciously knows are not the real threat, and by imagined scenes toward which there is no existential commitment at all.

(4) Fiction, on this picture, is a way of feeding such as-if appraisal machinery with vivid, internally generated representations. The tear at the cinema and the quickened pulse over a novel are real physiological outputs of a simulation system, not effects of a belief that the depicted events are occurring.

(5) Therefore step (2), read as a causal claim about emotional responding, is false.

(6) If step (2) was the only thing that made the phenomenon look paradoxical, then the paradox of fiction dissolves into a mistaken piece of folk psychology, and the special explanatory apparatus philosophers have proposed for our emotional responses to fiction is unnecessary.

I half-suspect that the weak step is (3): that it quietly substitutes reflex-like or subpersonal affective responses—a racing heart, a startle—for what philosophers mean by fear and pity, and in doing so changes the subject. But if that objection is right, I want to know precisely where the substitution invalidates the move from (3) and (4) to (5). Can the objection be stated without smuggling in a belief requirement whose falsity is exactly what is at issue? Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails on the most charitable reading, and what is left of the paradox once the distinction against step (3) has been made explicit?
