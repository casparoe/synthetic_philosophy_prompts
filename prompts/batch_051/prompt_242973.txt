While working through the muddy children puzzle in a course on epistemic logic, I have been struck by how quietly the standard solution assumes that each child is a perfect logician. I would like to examine a variant in which that assumption is relaxed, and I would be grateful for a careful step-by-step treatment.

As background, take the usual set-up: a father announces to his children that at least one of them has a muddy forehead; each child can see every other child's forehead but not her own; and the father repeatedly asks whether any child now knows that she herself is muddy, with any child who knows stepping forward. In the canonical treatment, if exactly k children are muddy, the muddy children all step forward on the kth asking and not before. The proof relies on common knowledge and on the idea that each child draws all the logical consequences of what she knows, including arbitrarily nested consequences about what others know.

Now consider a variant in which the children are depth-bounded reasoners. Suppose a child can carry out any piece of reasoning whose derivation requires at most d nested steps of the form 'consider what another child would know or do in such-and-such a situation', but cannot carry out a derivation that requires d+1 or more such steps. The children all share the same bound d, and this fact is announced to them along with the father's original announcement. First, work through the case in which exactly k children are muddy and all have the same bound d: for which values of d does the canonical result survive, and where precisely does the standard induction break down when d is too small? Which public facts, such as nobody stepping on a given round, remain usable to a child whose bound is below the threshold, and which conclusions become permanently unavailable? Second, trace a heterogeneous case: there are exactly three children, all three are muddy, one child has bound 1 and the other two have bound 3; determine who steps and on which round, and whether anyone is left stranded. Finally, say what this variant reveals about how much of the original puzzle's lesson depends on the idealisation of logical omniscience, and whether the usual morals about common knowledge survive once that idealisation is removed. Please answer in British English.
