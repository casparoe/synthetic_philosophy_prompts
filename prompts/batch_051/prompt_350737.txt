Here's an asymetry that bugs me. Everyone treats "This sentence is false" as a genuine paradox—a problem a theory of truth is supposed to handle—but "This sentence is true" gets shrugged off as a curiosity: maybe true, maybe false, maybe ungrounded, but not a threat. Structurally the two sentences seem to be doing the same trick, just a sentence pointing at its own truth value. I see that in plain classical logic the liar rules out both truth and falsity, while the truth-teller is consistent with either. But once you allow sentences to be neither true nor false, the liar no longer forces a contradiction; it just gets classified as ungrounded, which is also what standard fixed-point treatments do with the truth-teller. At that point the difference looks more like a matter of which one scared us first than a real logical divide. So: is there a defensible asymmetry between the liar and the truth-teller, and what rule separates them? And if the truth-teller is true, what fact makes it true beyond its saying so—shouldn't its truth floating free bother us just as much as the liar's failure to settle?
