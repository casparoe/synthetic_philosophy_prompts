I'm stuck on an argument a friend gave me after our professor pulled a surprise quiz on us. He says the professor's announcement couldn't have been true, and he's got numbered steps to prove it:

(1) On Sunday the professor announced: "There will be a quiz one day next week, and you won't be in a position to know which day until class meets."
(2) The students know the announcement is true: the professor is honest and already has the quiz date set.
(3) If Friday were the day, then once Thursday night passed with no quiz the students would know it's Friday before class, which contradicts the announcement. So Friday is out.
(4) With Friday out, if Thursday were the day, then once Wednesday night passed with no quiz the students would know it's Thursday, which contradicts the announcement. So Thursday is out.
(5) The same reasoning, run backward, eliminates Wednesday, Tuesday, and Monday. So no day can work and a truthful professor could of never made the announcement in the first place.
(6) But on Wednesday the professor gives the quiz and nobody sees it coming. So the announcement was true after all.

He says the argument also shows something bigger: nobody can ever be told in advance of a planned surprise, so surprise parties are impossible. I don't believe that for a second, but I can't find the step where the logic overreaches. Which premise or step should I reject, and what do I have to give up by rejecting it?
