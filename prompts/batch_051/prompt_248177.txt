I'm on the board of a small US state that's overhauling it's ballot-initiative process, and I'd like you to stress-test a proposed rule before we float it. Here's the rule. Any initiative that gets between 50% and 55% of the vote is "provisionally passed": it doesn't go into effect, but it's automatically placed on the next general election ballot. If it wins a second time by any margin, it passes for good. If it loses the second time, it's dead, and materially similar initiatives can't be refiled for four years. If it loses the first time with under 50%, it just dies and can be refiled next cycle like normal. Anything that clears 55% the first time passes immediately. And at any point between the first and second vote, the legislature can adopt the initiative by simple majority as an ordinary statute, which cancels the rerun; the statute can then be amended later like any other law.

Walk me through how this thing would actually behave, not whether it's a good idea in the abstract. What does it incentivize for initiative sponsors, for the legislature, for interest groups on both sides, and for ordinary voters? Where does it get gamed first? I'm especially curious whether the 50–55 band creates weird strategy around turning out or demobilizing voters, or whether a sponsor could ever end up preferring a narrow first-round loss to a provisional win that then gets barred. And say whether the worst failure modes can be fixed without giving up the basic rerun idea.
