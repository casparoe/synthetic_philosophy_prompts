I teach an undergrad seminar on rationality and cooperation — 16 students, one 75-minute block. I want a classroom game that makes them feel the pull of the Luring Lottery / Platonia dilemma: each student privately chooses wether to claim a prize; claiming has a small cost (a token or a fraction of a point); if exactly one person claims, that person gets the prize; if nobody claims or more than one claims, nobody gets the prize and the claimants are out their cost. I don't want to lecture about superrationality up front — I want the weirdness to come out of the choices themselves, and I'll name it in the debrief.

Design the activity for me. Give me exact rules, materials, and a payoff scheme that doesn't use real money (extra credit, raffle tickets, or something else a 16-person seminar will actually care about). I'd like enough structure to fill 75 minutes, probably two or three rounds so they can adapt — maybe vary the group size or add a cheap-talk phase in a later round. Then give me a debrief that maps what actually happened onto (a) plain expected-utility reasoning, (b) the mixed-strategy equilibrium given the cost, and (c) superrationality and the symmetry problem. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students colluding beforehand, someone claiming every round because it looks like a free win, half the class not taking the stakes seriously — and how I should handle each. Be honest about what the classroom version can't reproduce: I can't put an actual fortune on the line, so the part of the dilemma that depends on the prize being genuinely irresistible and on it being common knowledge that everyone is in the exact same situation will have to be supplied or faked.
