In my high school philosophy class we have been reading the Meditations, and a friend and I built the following argument, which now strikes us as proving far more than either of us can accept. (1) Descartes's evil demon can make me wrong about the external world, but he cannot make me wrong about the claim I exist while I am actually thinking it, because even being deceived requires a subject that exists. (2) So the cogito's certainty belongs only to a claim in the first person and present tense: the claim I exist now certifies itself whenever I make it. (3) Claims about my earlier existence, such as I existed a minute ago, lack this self-certifying character, since I can doubt them without contradiction; an omnipotent deceiver could have brought me into existence just now together with a false memory of having thought earlier. (4) Therefore the cogito proves at most the existence of a subject at a single instant of thought, not the continuing self to which the later stages of the Meditations appeal. (5) The same reasoning applies to every later thought: each new thought certifies only its own momentary subject. (6) So the meditator who draws the next inference is, by his own standard of certainty, not entitled to assume that he is the same subject who took the previous step, or even that there was a previous step; the rest of the project comes to depend on a continuity that his standard cannot supply. (7) Thus the cogito, far from founding a body of knowledge, seems to prove that no chain of reasoning longer than a single present thought can be carried out with certainty, and a subject that lasts only a moment is not the I Descartes needs elsewhere. That conclusion strikes us as absurd, since we are plainly the same people who began the exercise. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I would rather keep steps (1) and (2) than accept (7), but I want to know what rejecting any of the other steps would cost.
