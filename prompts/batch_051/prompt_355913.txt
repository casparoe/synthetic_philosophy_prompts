I'm writing a section of my thesis chapter on whether saying the genome codes for the organism is more than a loose way of talking, and I've been collecting the standard reasons for thinking it isn't a code in any literal sense. So far I've got: (i) a real code needs an encoder and a decoder who share a convention, and nothing in the cell plays that role — the codon-to-amino-acid mapping is just chemistry; (ii) even if DNA carries information, it's only Shannon information (correlation, reduction of uncertainty), not semantic information, so there's no aboutness or content there; (iii) code-talk covertly imports a program or blueprint picture of the genome, which makes gene expression look unidirectional and hides how much the 'reading' depends on cellular and environmental context. I keep thinking there's a fourth argument I've seen around that genuinely isn't a restatement, special case, or combination of these three. What is it, what does it add that (i)–(iii) miss, and is it actually any good?
