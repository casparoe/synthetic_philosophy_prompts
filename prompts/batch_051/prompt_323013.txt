Lexicography and word-sense annotation share a striking design choice. A printed dictionary lists, under a headword such as "run" or "open," a sequence of numbered senses, each with its own definition and example. The word-sense-disambiguation datasets used to train NLP systems inherit the same layout: for each occurrence of a target word, an annotator must assign it to exactly one sense from a pre-existing inventory, often WordNet-style, and the model is then trained to reproduce those assignments. Yet lexicographers openly treat sense boundaries as editorial conveniences—lumpers and splitters disagree about where one sense ends and another begins, and many uses seem to fall between or across the listed senses. Annotators report the same resistance from actual tokens: a verb used metonymically ("enjoy the book" for enjoying its contents), coerced by an aspectual verb ("begin the book" for begin reading or writing it), or extended ad hoc leaves them unsure which box to tick, but the interface offers no "none" and no "several."

What theory of word meaning does this pair of practices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit semantics: does the practice commit its users to the view that words have stable, discrete, context-independent senses—that a lexicon is a finite list of meanings and context merely selects among them—or is it compatible with richer views on which meanings are dynamic, continuous, or constructed in context? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with, and flag any inconsistency between the fixed menu and the lexicographers' own admission that the menu is arbitrary. Finally, say concretely what would have to change in lexicographic or annotation practice if we adopted a use-based or meaning-potential view of polysemy instead. I want a committed answer: take a definite position on what the practice actually presupposes and defend it, rather than canvassing options and leaving me to choose.
