I got put on my hospital's ethics commitee last fall — we're rewriting the triage rules, and I've been going in circles the whole time. So I did the obvious thing and read some philosophy, and it turns out the professionals have been stuck on the exact same fight for decades. The anti-utilitarian side says you can't add up small benefits across many people until they outweigh a serious harm to one person, because each person is a separate life and nobody else's good news can compensate me for my bad news — the standard example being that no number of other people's mild headaches outweighs one person going blind. The utilitarian side says that intuition falls apart the moment you have to actually make a decision: if each affected person's claim matters at all, there has to be some finite number of moderate claims that outweighs one severe claim, otherwise you get a moral mathematics full of jumps; and anyway everyone already accepts this kind of tradeoff whenever we build roads or approve drugs with side effects. Each side thinks the other is just rigging the examples, and nobody has moved in my lifetime.

I'm not asking you to settle whether the utilitarians are right. I want a diagnosis of the impasse itself. Is the disagreement substantive or partly verbal? Are they disagreeing about a factual question (say, how deep the boundary between lives really is), about what the right unit of moral accounting is (lives? time-slices? experiences?), about what counts as evidence (a vivid case versus a consistency argument), or just about bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for? My own suspicion, offered as a hypothesis, is that the thing everyone treats as fixed — that a person is one unit — is doing an enormous amount of the work here, because inside a single life we already do the same cross-time adding-up: I accept pain now in exchange for avoiding much more pain later, and nobody wants to say I'm a different person at 50 than at 30. So does the separateness of persons give us a real fact the debate could turn on, or is it a covert disagreement about how much a fuzzy boundary should matter? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a reframing of the question — or wether I should stop expecting the discussion to move at all.
