I am a novelist. In the novel I am now writing, two philosophers—one a committed internalist about practical reasons, the other a committed externalist—are required by a foundation to produce a joint statement under the rules of an adversarial collaboration, and the document will appear in full as a chapter. I want it to be good enough that neither character is made to look like a fool or a straw man.

By internalism I mean the view that an agent has a normative reason to φ only if a sound deliberative route leads from the agent's present motivational set—her desires, projects, loyalties, and patterns of emotional response—to φ-ing. The route may correct false belief, supply missing factual information, trace instrumental means, and exercise the imagination, but it may not simply install new final ends wholesale. By externalism I mean the denial of that existence condition: at least some reasons apply to an agent whether or not any such route could bring her to care about them, and the fact that the agent cannot be brought to care is a defect in her, not an absence of the reason.

Draft the statement they publish. It should contain the following.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These should be substantive enough that arriving at them was real work—claims about normative versus motivating reasons, about false belief and ignorance, about instrumental transmission, about which ordinary cases are not in dispute—but none should smuggle in a commitment either side would reject.

Second, a precise statement of the residual disagreement, given once in wording the internalist would accept as accurate to her own view and once in wording the externalist would accept as accurate to his.

Third, a disagreements table of cruxes, where a crux is a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one party. For each crux, specify the case or argument both parties agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts the case shows or what each side would have to concede if it came out the other way.

The statement must address at least these pressure cases: the amoralist for whom no deliberative route leads from his motivational set to any moral concern; the agent entirely indifferent to her own future welfare; the choice whose value can be grasped only from inside an experience the agent cannot antecedently imagine; and the apparent analogy with epistemic reasons for belief, which seem not to require a route from the believer's prior doxastic motivations. If any part of their quarrel turns out to be verbal, say which part; if some part is genuine but has no agreed test, say what both would count as progress.
