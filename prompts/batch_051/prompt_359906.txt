After three decades as a working scientist, I have taken up philosophy in retirement and have been trying to bring my old instinct for controlled cases to a topic in the philosophy of fiction. The topic is this: when a story asks me to accept some stipulation about its world, I sometimes comply effortlessly and even extend the world with details of my own; at other times I find myself unable to do the required imaginative work, even when I understand perfectly well what I am being asked to imagine. I have been recording my responses to such cases, and I would like to know whether a single principle captures the pattern, or whether some of my verdicts have to go.

The cases all involve a stipulation inside an otherwise realistic novel.

1. The novel stipulates that in that world the force of gravity is somewhat weaker on Tuesdays, and that all engineering has adjusted accordingly. I comply without difficulty. I catch myself picturing bridge designs and train schedules.

2. The novel stipulates that water in that world is a basic element rather than a compound, though its observable behavior is exactly as here. I feel a slight scientific itch — part of me wants to say "then it is not water" — but I can set the itch aside and follow the story.

3. The novel stipulates that its society treats offering one's left hand as a grave insult and regards certain ordinary foods as ritually polluting. I comply completely and can reason about the characters' shame and avoidance from the inside.

4. The novel stipulates that in its moral code, betraying a close friend for a promotion is a minor lapse, while wearing the wrong color to a funeral is a much more serious wrong. I can follow the re-ranking, though it feels more like arithmetic than like moral understanding.

5. The novel stipulates that the suffering of an innocent child at the hands of a torturer is, in that world, the highest beauty, and the reader is meant to find it uplifting. I cannot do this. I can read the sentences, but the world does not form; I find myself diagnosing the narrator instead of entering the fiction.

6. The novel stipulates that in that world, for creatures anatomically like us, the taste of feces is delicious — not because their tongues differ, but simply because the fiction says so. I can make partial progress, but the imagining stays hollow: I can reason about the fact without making the experience available to myself.

7. The novel stipulates that an empire which enslaves conquered peoples is, in the fiction, just, and that "justice" is to have its full ordinary meaning. I can follow the story so long as this is carried by the characters' speech and institutions; but as soon as an omniscient narrator asserts it, I balk.

8. The novel stipulates that in its world adding a particular herb to soup is intrinsically heroic — not in virtue of its effects, but simply as such. This is, to my surprise, nearly unintelligible to me; the moral quality refuses to attach to the act.

9. The novel stipulates that white lies are wrong in exactly the way murder is wrong here, and that the characters treat the two with equal gravity. I can manage this; the weight of a white lie is stretched, but the fiction still works.

I am struck by two things in this list. Cases 4 and 5 both ask me to accept a revised moral ranking, yet I accept one and cannot accept the other. Cases 5 and 6 both ask me to feel something as good that I actually find repellent, yet the gustatory case is partly manageable while the moral case is not. My provisional hypotheses have failed. The first — that resistance attaches to evaluative matter and never to descriptive matter — dies on cases 4 and 9, which are evaluative and pass. The second — that resistance attaches to what a narrator asserts rather than to what characters believe — explains case 7 but does not explain cases 5 or 6.

So my question is this. Is there one principle, stated precisely, that captures which stipulations I comply with and which I resist? Please test the principle you propose against all nine cases, and also against a tenth that I remain undecided about: a story that stipulates that gratitude is a vice and that the proper response to a great benefit is contempt. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which, if any, should be revised.
