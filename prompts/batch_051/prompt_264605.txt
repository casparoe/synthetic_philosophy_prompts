I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who came late to constitutional theory, and I have been puzzling over the idea of gaps in a legal order. My entry point has been Locke’s discussion in Chapter XIV of the *Second Treatise of Government*, “Of Prerogative,” where he describes prerogative as the power to act according to discretion for the public good, without the prescription of the law, and sometimes even against it. The passage seems to concede that any finite system of rules has states it does not cover, and that the executive then supplies what the rules cannot. Colleagues used to tell me that near a critical point the usual perturbative expansions break down; I wonder whether constitutional order has something structurally similar.

Please give me your actual credence for the following proposition: in a mature constitutional democracy with a written constitution, when a genuine emergency or interregnum occurs for which the written text supplies no operative answer, the legitimate default is for the executive to act for the public good without textual warrant—and sometimes even contrary to standing statutes—subject to retrospective ratification by the people or their representatives. To keep the question sharp, I ask you to treat “legitimate” as a normative notion, meaning what may permissibly be done, not as a prediction about what a given supreme court would uphold.

I would like the reasoning decomposed into at least these sub-questions: (i) that any finite system of rules, including a written constitution, has genuine gaps where no rule dictates an answer; (ii) that legal orders are not automatically closed by something like a Kelsenian postulate that every situation is already legally determined; (iii) that in such a gap the executive, rather than the legislature or the courts, is the least-bad default actor; and (iv) that retrospective ratification can convert an initially unwarranted act into a legally valid one rather than merely excusing a wrong. Assign a rough probability to each, along with any further sub-questions you think carry weight, and then combine them into an overall number. I do not want a bare conclusion; I want an argument with its weak points marked. Finally, state how confident you are in your overall assessment, which term you are least sure of, and what evidence or argument would most move that specific estimate.
