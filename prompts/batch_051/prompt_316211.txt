Retired field geologist here (so if my philosophy lingo is off, that's why). I joined a monthly reading group after retiring and we've been working through the American pragmatists—Peirce, James, that circle. One guy in the group reaches for the same line to end every argument: 'truth is what works.' Last month we were talking about scientific models, and I said something that seems just obvious to me—a model can be false and still work beautifully for prediction. Treating the coastline as a fractal, or using a point-particle ideal gas, or running Newtonian gravity after general relativity: none of those are literally true, but they work. He smiled and said 'truth is what works,' which didn't touch what I'd said, but a couple of people nodded like it did.

So here's what I want. Take 'truth is what works' as a slogan that could mean a bunch of different precise things, from a modest claim that usefulness is one fallible sign of truth, to the radical claim that there's nothing more to being true than being useful, and whatever positions are hiding in between. Lay them out as a menu. For each reading, say whether it's trivially true, substantive and defensible, or just false, and say which of those readings would actually support using the slogan to shut down my point about false-but-useful models. And since I'm told Peirce and James actually mean very different things by this, even though a newcomer probly can't always see it, flag for each reading whether it's more Peircean—truth as where inquiry would settle in the long run—or more Jamesian—truth as what it pays to believe. Write your answer in American English, and don't worry about citations; I can chase the primary texts myself.
