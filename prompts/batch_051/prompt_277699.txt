I've been turning over a question that keeps surfacing when my friends and I argue about whether history has laws — by which we mean genuine regularities that could, in principle, ground predictions about the rise and fall of societies the way physical laws ground predictions about an orbit. One friend always reaches for the same retort: history is like the weather. The weather has underlying laws, he says, but the system is so layered and so sensitive to small differences that forecasts break down past a fairly short horizon. From this he concludes that the repeated failure of large-scale historical predictions — stages of development, coming epochs, ends of history — does not refute the existence of laws of history, any more than a failed long-range forecast refutes meteorology.

Another friend thinks the analogy is doing all the argumentative work and breaks exactly where it matters. Weather systems do not read the forecast; societies do. A published prediction becomes a new fact for the people it is about, and their reaction — joining, fleeing, suppressing, accelerating — can bring about or prevent the predicted outcome. He adds a deeper disanalogy, which he attributes to Popper (I have not read the original): what most shapes the future course of history is what people come to know and invent, and we cannot predict our future discoveries, because predicting a discovery in any useful detail would already be to have made it. So a law of history would have to cover a future that includes the law's own influence and discoveries that cannot yet be known, which he says is not merely hard but incoherent.

I want the weather analogy audited properly, not just dismissed. Please work through what corresponds to what in the two systems — the forecast, the underlying regularities, the variables, the horizon of prediction — and then separate the places where the analogy is merely loose from the places where it is load-bearing. Is the second friend's objection a disanalogy that only limits predictability, or does it undermine the very suggestion that history could have laws? If the first friend's conclusion is meant to be "there might still be laws of history, just ones we could never use to predict," does that survive the disanalogies, or is it a face-saving retreat? And if it does not survive, should the conclusion be that history has no laws at all, or only that the word law can mean something thinner in history — regularities that hold locally until our knowledge changes them?
