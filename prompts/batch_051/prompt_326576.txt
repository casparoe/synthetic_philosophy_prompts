I teach an upper-level philosophy of science seminar, and we have just spent three weeks on the question of whether evolutionary psychology is a science or a machine for generating just-so stories. My students can recite the standard criticisms—unfalsifiability, post hoc storytelling, the problem of reverse-engineering a trait's function—but they struggle to explain what, concretely, would separate a testable adaptationist hypothesis from a merely plausible one. I want a task that forces them to build the distinction themselves rather than repeat it.

Give an example of a single, reasonably specific human behavioral or cognitive trait for which an adaptationist account and a rival non-adaptationist account (for instance, cultural learning, or a by-product/developmental constraint) can both be made to fit the same evidence. Use the example to isolate the underlying conceptual issue: what must be added—in the structure of the hypothesis, the kind of evidence, or the predictions made in advance—before an adaptationist explanation counts as more than a just-so story? Please avoid the standard textbook examples of sex differences in mate preferences, language acquisition, and incest avoidance; choose something less rehearsed so that the answer has to do original work.

Please provide your response under these headings, in this order:

1. The trait and the evidence (about 100 words)
2. The adaptationist account (about 150 words)
3. A rival non-adaptationist account (about 150 words)
4. The diagnostic problem (about 200 words): explain exactly why the two accounts are hard to separate from the evidence as given.
5. What would decide between them (about 150 words): specify the additional evidence or theoretical constraints that would make one account more credible than the other.
6. Takeaway (one sentence): state the conceptual lesson about the scientific status of evolutionary psychology.
