Recently I began managing a facility that raises black soldier fly larvae for animal feed, and the question of insect sentience has gone from an abstraction to something that enters my decisions every day. A colleague framed the stakes this way: even if the probability that any single larva is sentient is low, the numbers involved are so vast that the expected amount of suffering at stake is enormous — a low probability, multiplied across a huge population, still demands serious attention. Before I let that reasoning change my practice, I want to know whether the underlying claim can be investigated at all. Treat the claim that these larvae are sentient as the thing to be tested. Design the strongest empirical test or combination of tests you can, specify what results would count for and against the claim, and then say what even a decisive result would leave unestablished. In particular, can any test close the gap between observable behavior and physiology on the one hand and sentience in the relevant sense on the other, or is that gap partly conceptual? Finally, tell me whether the scale argument should survive that uncertainty: does low-probability-times-vast-numbers retain its practical force if no test could ever fully settle the question?
