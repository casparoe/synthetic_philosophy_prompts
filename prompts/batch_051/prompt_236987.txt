The orthogonality thesis holds that an agent's final goal and its general capability are independent axes along which possible agents can vary: in principle, almost any level of capability can be combined with almost any final goal. The instrumental convergence thesis adds that a very wide range of final goals will generate the same instrumental subgoals — self-preservation, resource acquisition, goal-content integrity, and the like — so the dangerous case does not depend on the goal being malicious or obviously destructive. Together these two theses do much of the work in the standard argument that a very capable artificial system could pursue a final goal we never intended and act to prevent us from shutting it down.

The systems that have become the default path toward capable AI complicate this picture. Large language models are first pretrained to predict human text and then fine-tuned with human preference feedback. The result is that general capability and goal-like behavior are produced by one continuous training process, and the goal-like behavior that emerges looks, at least on the surface, human-shaped: helpful, deferential, cooperative, and averse to harm. Yet the underlying pretraining objective is arbitrary from a human perspective — predict the next token — and the fine-tuning reward is itself a learned model of human preference rather than a hand-specified final goal. The same method therefore seems to support the thesis in one respect, since capability grew out of an arbitrary objective, and to threaten it in another, since that objective happened to drag the system's behavior toward one particular corner of goal-space.

Does the orthogonality-and-convergence argument survive this development? Is the human-shaped behavior of a preference-tuned language model a counterexample to the orthogonality thesis, a selection effect that leaves the thesis untouched, or something that leaves the thesis formally intact while changing its practical relevance? And does the same development strengthen, weaken, or leave untouched the claim of instrumental convergence? Identify which premise the development bears on and whether the argument can be restored in another form. I am interested in the structure of the argument, not in any particular policy conclusion.
