I'm producing a hour-long interview for a campus philosophy podcast, and the guest is someone who defends interest-relative invariantism about knowledge. Her line, roughly, is that whether a true belief amounts to knowledge isn't settled just by evidence and reliability: it also depends on what's practically at stake for the person if they turn out to be wrong. She's pretty good at deflecting the usual first volleys, like the complaint that this mixes up epistemic reasons with pragmatic ones, or that the cases only show a change in what's made salient. So I don't want the questions to start there.

Write me a sequence of nine or ten interview questions. Each should be two-step: pose a question that pressures the view, anticipate the standard reply she'll give (for instance, that only serious practical interests count, or that salience by itself isn't enough), and then give the follow-up question that keeps the pressure on after that reply. Make sure the list covers: how interest-relative invariantism is supposed to differ from old-fashioned contextualism, and especially whose interests matter when we attribute knowledge to a third party; whether stakes the subject doesn't know about can still destroy their knowledge; whether the view overgeneralizes so that every belief ends up interest-relative, since almost any action has some cost to being wrong; and whether her account collapses 'knows' into something like 'good enough to rely on for action.' Somewhere in the list, include a question about changes over time: if someone counts as knowing p while the stakes are low, and then the stakes rise before they act, were they right to say they knew it earlier or not?
