I’m reading Moore’s *Principia Ethica* for a class on early analytic ethics and I’m stuck on the opening of Chapter VI, right before he starts arguing that personal affection and the appreciation of beauty are among the most valuable things we know. The passage goes:

"When we call a state of things ideal we may mean three distinct things, which have only this in common: that we always do mean to assert, of the state of things in question, not only that it is good in itself, but that it is good in itself in a much higher degree than many other things. The first of these meanings of 'ideal' is (1) that to which the phrase 'The Ideal' is most properly confined. By this is meant the best state of things conceivable, the Summum Bonum or Absolute Good."

I get that he wants to reject Hedonism by showing there are other great goods besides pleasure, and that friendship and beauty are the main candidates. But this passage throws me. He says the three senses of "ideal" only have in common that the thing is good in itself "in a much higher degree than many other things," then says the chapter will be mainly concerned with the third sense, where "ideal" just means good in a high degree. What I don't understand is what the first two senses are doing for his argument about beauty and friendship. Is he saying that when he later calls personal affection and aesthetic enjoyment "ideal" goods, he is not claiming they would be part of the Absolute Good or the Human Good? Or is the shared feature supposed to carry the weight, so that ranking something as ideal in the third sense already presupposes we can compare intrinsic values in a way he hasn't justified? Also, can he really say something is good in itself to a "much higher degree" without first explaining what makes one intrinsic good greater than another? That seems to assume exactly the kind of value commensurability I thought Moore was suspicious of. Could someone unpack how this passage sets up his later claims about friendship and beauty?
