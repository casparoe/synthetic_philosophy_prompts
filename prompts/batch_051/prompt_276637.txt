Yesterday I spent the better part of an evening in a long argument with a close friend about a proposal to scrap a long-standing requirement at our university: every undergraduate, in every department, must pass a course in formal logic before graduating. The proposal would simply delete the requirement, without replacing it. My friend opposed the change by appealing almost entirely to Chesterton's fence — the image of a fence erected across a road, which one should not pull down until one knows why it was put there — together with a picture he called Burkean conservatism, according to which inherited practices carry an accumulated, largely tacit reasonableness that no single reformer can reconstruct on demand. His conclusion was strong: the mere fact that the requirement has survived so long is positive evidence that it serves some function we have not yet articulated, so the burden of proof lies entirely on those who would remove it, even when nobody can say what the function is.

I want an audit of that analogy, not of the logic requirement specifically, though you may use the requirement to test it. Give me the correspondence table: the builder, the road, the fence, the repairs and maintenance it has received, the would-be remover — what plays each role when the fence stands for the logic requirement, or for an inherited institution more generally? Then identify where the mapping breaks. Say which disanalogies matter for the conclusion my friend was using it to support and which are merely cosmetic. In particular, I want to know whether the analogy imports the idea of a single intentional builder with a determinate reason, and whether it matters for the argument if no such builder exists; whether it quietly treats the institution as a mere obstruction when many inherited practices are enabling rather than obstructive; and whether it presupposes that the only options are preservation or demolition, rather than piecemeal revision or replacement.

Then tell me how much of my friend's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table: the strong claim that longevity is positive evidence of hidden function, the intermediate claim that the reformer always bears the burden of proof, and the practical caution that one should understand an institution before destroying it. Which of these, if any, does the analogy actually support, and what would a more honest analogy be, if there is one?
