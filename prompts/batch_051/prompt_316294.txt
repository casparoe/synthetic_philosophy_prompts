I cover cognitive science and philosophy for a general audience, and I'm working on a short column about hedonism. The essay below is rough (its not ready for my editor) but I want a tough read before I pursue it. I'm least confident about the closing fork.

Draft, working title: What do pleasures have in common?

Hedonism about value sounds simple: pleasure, and only pleasure, is good. The simplicity is suspicious, because the things we call pleasures don't look much alike. The pleasure of a genuinely good espresso, the pleasure of finally understanding a proof, the relief after a near-miss in traffic, the mean little buzz of schadenfreude, the post-run ache that somehow feels good — these don't share an obvious felt texture. If you press me on what the espresso-pleasure and the proof-pleasure have in common, all I can come up with is that they 'feel good', which is exactly the phrase that was supposed to be explained.

The usual ways of making this ragged bunch into one thing go three directions. First, the phenomenal view: there is a single hedonic tone, a pleasure-feeling, smeared across every case. Second, the attitudinal view — which I'd associate with Fred Feldman, though I don't want to put words in his mouth — says the value-relevant kind of pleasure isn't a sensory tone but a type of attitude, roughly enjoying that something is the case. Attitudes get typed differently than feelings: belief is one type whether it's about breakfast or black holes, so enjoyment can be one type whether its object is a proof or an espresso. Third, the functional view: what makes a state a pleasure is what it does — we'd like it to continue, we're drawn toward it, it reinforces and smooths attention.

Here's the fork I want to push. The phenomenal view is false to introspection: relief and schadenfreude do not wear the same qualitative coat. The attitudinal view is the best fix, but it only relocates the question. What makes all those heterogeneous episodes instances of one attitude-type? Either the answer is "nothing deeper — enjoyment is primitive," in which case the heterogeneity problem hasn't been solved so much as renamed and filed under 'fundamental.' Or the answer is that they share a characteristic role in the agent's conative economy — a kind of wanting-to-be-in-that-state — in which case hedonism has quietly become desire-satisfaction theory with a friendly face. So hedonism gets squeezed: phenomenally unified pleasures are a myth, and functionally unified pleasures aren't pleasures-and-goodness; they're wantings-and-gettings.

My comment request, and I want a committed take, not a survey: is this fork actually tight? Does treating enjoyment as a primitive attitude defuse it, or is a hedonist entitled to say "no, my fundamental value simply is that attitude, and it's fine that it doesn't decompose"? And do the functionalist justice: is there a version of functionalism that isn't secretly desire theory? If you think I'm strawmanning the attitudinalist, say so and show me how the attitudinalist could answer the "what unifies the attitude?" question without going functional. Be blunt — if you think the closing paragraph overclaims, tell me how you'd redraw the arc.
