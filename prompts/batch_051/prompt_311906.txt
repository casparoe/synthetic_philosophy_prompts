I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but a while ago at a party someone described a puzzle to me that I cant stop turning over in my head. A man is hooked up to a device with a dial. Every time he turns it one notch he gets a small amount of money, and each notch adds an amount of pain so tiny he genuinely cant tell the difference between one setting and the one before. At each individual step it seems completely rational to turn the dial, since the money is worth more than an imperceptible difference, but after hundreds of notches he's in real agony for a total sum he would have refused without a second thought if you had offered it to him at the beginning. What gets me is that the scariest version isn't a torture machine at all — its the way a person drifts into a life, a job, a city, a marriage, through a thousand tiny decisions that each look fine from one step away.

So here is what I'd like. Write me a short story, maybe 2500 to 4000 words, set in a near-future midwestern city where a company has quietly built this logic into its employment contracts: workers wear a small implant that lets the company make micro-adjustments to work conditions — heat, noise, mandatory overtime, surveillance — each change so small you can't feel the difference from yesterday, each paired with a tiny bump in pay. The protagonist is a woman in her late fifties who took the job to pay for her daughter's medical debt, and she has a neighbor, a retired actuary, who keeps trying to get her to look at the whole sequence at once instead of just the next step. The story should make the reader feel the trap of this drift without ever naming the underlying idea or using words like intransitive or rational in the narration; let it come through entirely in what the character chooses, notices, and refuses. End somewhere that doesn't settle whether she was rational to stay.

And one formatting request: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — just continuous prose, the way a person would write an email or a letter.
