My friend and I got into it last night about whether consciousness is a user illusion — the idea that your conscious self is like the desktop on a computer screen: a tidy little interface of icons and folders that hides the absolute mess of machine code underneath, and we mistake the interface for the thing actually doing the work. He kept saying the science is basically settled: change blindness shows you don't have the rich picture you think you have, split-brain patients invent reasons for actions the other hemisphere performed, and the feeling of deciding can arrive after the neural processes that predict the action are already underway. I kept saying sure, those show the interface is lossy and sometimes confabulates, but they don't show the interface is all there is — that the what-it's-like part is a useful fiction doing no causal work. He said I was moving the goalposts; I said he was smuggling in the conclusion. We went round and round because neither of us could say what would actually settle it.

So take the strong version of that claim: not just that introspection is unreliable, but that the conscious self is a constructed, radically simplified representation of what the brain is doing, that its apparent role in deciding and acting is largely post hoc storytelling, and that the whole felt scene is a dashboard, not the engine. How would you bring evidence to bear on that, concretely? What would the experiment or observational program look like — subjects, measures, what pattern of results would push you toward thinking yes, this really is an interface perched on top of deeper machinery, and what pattern would push you toward thinking the metaphor has run its course? Then tell me what part of the claim no test can reach, and whether a thoughtful defender of the user-illusion view should be worried that the core of it survives every possible result.
