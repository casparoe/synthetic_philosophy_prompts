A friend of mine who does constitutional law and another friend who's a behavioral-econ guy spent most of dinner last week argueing about whether entrenched constitutional provisions are best understood as Ulysses pacts. The background, if you don't know it: Ulysses had his crew tie him to the mast and ignore his later orders, so he could hear the sirens without steering the ship onto the rocks. (1) The econ guy opened with: an amendment clause requiring a supermajority is exactly that—we foresee that we'll panic in the future and pass a bad law, so we bind ourselves to make that harder. (2) The law friend said no: Ulysses bound himself, but an entrenched constitution binds people who never consented, and at the extreme you get eternity clauses that make a provision flat-out unamendable—so it isn't self-binding, it's the dead hand of the past. (3) The econ guy came back that a whole polity can be a single agent that binds itself, and except for those rare eternity clauses there is an amendment procedure, which is the release condition; he conceded eternity clauses are probably indefensible on his model but said they're not whats doing the real work. (4) The law friend said the word "self" in "self-binding" is doing even more work than "dead hand": a polity's membership turns over, so by 2050 the people bound won't be the set who ratified. Even ordinary entrenchment, he said, is other-binding dressed up as self-binding. (5) The econ guy's last move was that without that dressing-up you can't have credible commitments at all—rights, treaties, central-bank independence—so the choice isn't precommitment versus freedom, it's precommitment versus being unable to make any collective promise at all. The law friend answered that maybe a polity being unable to make promises is just the price of self-government, at which point we were too drunk to continue. They asked me to referee and I couldn't. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and then say who is more right overall and what, if anything, they're both missing.
