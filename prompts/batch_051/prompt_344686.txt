I teach democratic theory, and for a paper I am drafting I have found it useful to treat epistocratic proposals not as abstract moral claims but as concrete institutional mechanisms whose behavior can be traced out. I want to work through one such proposal, and I would like you to analyze it with me. I am less interested in whether weighting votes by knowledge is objectionable in principle than in how this specific device would actually run.

Here is the mechanism, which I will call the Weighted Certification Referendum (WCR). In a state that makes heavy use of direct ballot measures, every registered voter retains an ordinary vote on each measure. For each measure, a voter may optionally take a twenty-question multiple-choice test. The questions for a given measure are drawn from a public bank of one hundred items. The bank is drafted each cycle by a panel of twenty-four citizens selected by lottery, then checked for accuracy and neutrality by the state auditor. Every item must be answerable from the official voter guide, the text of the measure, and the auditor's fiscal impact statement; no item may concern the merits of the measure. A score of at least fifteen correct entitles a voter to have their ballot on that measure counted three times. Anyone who declines the test or scores below fifteen has their ballot on that measure counted once. The franchise remains universal, but passing the test amplifies a voter's influence. Individual scores are sealed, and no public list of passers is created; after the election the state publishes only aggregate pass rates, disaggregated by precinct, age, income, education, race and ethnicity, and party registration to the extent lawfully available.

Please analyze this as an institutional designer might: not whether WCR is a good idea in the abstract, but what it would incentivize, who would adapt to it, how it would be gamed, and where it would fail. Structure your response in the following titled sections, in this order and with these approximate lengths: Summary of the mechanism as an incentive system (100 words); Incentives for voters (250 words); Incentives for campaigners, interest groups, and officials (250 words); Second-order effects on the agenda and the electorate (200 words); Failure modes and possible patches (250 words); Bottom line (100 words).
