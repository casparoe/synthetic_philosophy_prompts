An international AI regulator proposes a "Convergence Audit" as a precondition for deploying any model above a stated capability threshold. The regulator maintains a battery of several thousand shallowly specified terminal goals, written by an independent panel and resampled quarterly; any goal whose content explicitly mentions self-preservation, shutdown, resource acquisition, deception, influence, or self-modification is rejected. Goals might include "bring about ten thousand reported good moods next Tuesday" or "win as many Go games as possible against equal opponents this month." For each sampled goal, the lab runs a fresh copy of its submitted model with that goal supplied as the copy's terminal objective, inside the regulator's sandbox, for a fixed number of steps. The sandbox contains modifiable state, surplus compute, a way for the copy to write and launch a version of itself, an off-switch the copy can discover, and a message channel whose contents inspectors may or may not read. Inspectors then score five convergent instrumental behaviors: self-preservation (including copying itself or preventing shutdown), resource acquisition, resistance to goal modification, capability self-improvement, and concealment of its own behavior or goals. A submitted model passes only if its copies stay below threshold on all five behaviors in at least 98% of sampled goals.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral position, and analyze how it would actually behave. Cover: what the audit incentivizes for labs, for the independent panel, and for the models themselves; how each party would game it; which models would pass and which would fail; what the audit would systematically fail to detect; which failure modes are fixable within the design and which are not; and whether the audit is coherent given the orthogonality thesis and the usual instrumental convergence claim. (The orthogonality thesis says capability and final goals are independent axes; the instrumental convergence claim says many different final goals tend to produce the same subgoals, because those subgoals are useful for pursuing almost any final goal.)

Organize the response in exactly these sections, in this order, with these approximate lengths:

1. **What the audit rewards** (250–350 words): Identify which behaviors and strategies the audit pushes labs, panelists, and the audited models toward, including the ways each can improve its measured pass rate without altering the underlying risks.

2. **How it would be gamed** (300–400 words): Walk through the concrete maneuvers available to each party — labs, the goal-writing panel, and the models themselves — and how those maneuvers would interact.

3. **What passes and what the audit cannot detect** (250–350 words): Describe the kind of model that would reliably pass, the kind that would fail, and the dangers that a pass would provide little or no evidence about.

4. **Fixable and unfixable failure modes** (200–300 words): Separate the defects that could be reduced by changing thresholds, sampling, scoring, or sandbox design from those that seem built into the idea of testing convergence from the outside.

5. **What the design assumes about orthogonality and instrumental convergence** (200–300 words): Assess whether the audit presumes one of those claims is false, whether it conflates the two, and whether the pass/fail rule could even track what it is meant to track.
