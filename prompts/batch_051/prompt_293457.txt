In a running argument, a colleague defends a version of the doomsday argument — that our species is likely to die out soon — by invoking an urn picture. Humanity is compared to an urn containing every human who will ever live, and each of us is likened to a single ball drawn blindly from that urn; a typical draw falls near the middle of the ordering, so finding yourself with roughly a hundred billion humans born before you should make you uneasy about being early, and that unease is then taken as evidence against a future with trillions more people. I want an audit of this analogy, not a restatement of the surrounding probability calculations. Please answer in exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, covering: (1) the correspondence table — what plays the role of the urn, the balls, the blind draw, the ordering, and the prior over possible urn sizes; (2) the disanalogies, and which ones matter for the inference being drawn rather than being merely cosmetic; (3) the assumptions the picture quietly imports about random sampling from a population that is not yet fixed and about the correct reference class; (4) whether replacing the self-sampling assumption — reason as if you were a random draw from the reference class of observers like you — with the self-indication assumption — your own existence is itself evidence that the class is large — changes the verdict, and how the presumptuous philosopher thought experiment, in which someone treats their own existence as confirming a theory that posits vastly more observers, bears on the exchange; (5) a bottom line on whether the doomsday conclusion survives if the urn mapping fails, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along, and what, if anything, would be a better analogy for the work it is meant to do.
