The no-miracles argument for scientific realism, a paradigm application of inference to the best explanation, runs roughly as follows: the predictive and instrumental success of mature science would be a miracle unless the relevant theories were at least approximately true, so approximate truth is the best explanation of that success. The argument was shaped by a picture of science in which the successful objects are interpretable theories whose central terms can be assessed for approximate truth. A later development complicates that picture. Contemporary machine learning produces systems that are genuinely successful at prediction, forecasting held-out data and recognizing objects, yet these systems are often treated as black boxes: their internal parameters are not naturally read as representing entities, mechanisms, or laws, and their competence can fail abruptly under distribution shift or adversarial perturbation in ways that do not resemble the failures of an approximately true theory.
