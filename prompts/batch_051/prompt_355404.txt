For a piece I'm planning on why some technologies and institutions become hard to reverse, I find myself caught between two competing convictions. On one side, the path-dependence framework gives the most satisfying account of outcomes that would otherwise look perverse: once a standard, platform, or institution acquires an early lead, increasing returns, network externalities, switching costs, and installed-base effects can amplify small initial advantages into durable equilibria that no one would have selected from scratch. It offers a unified explanation for why keyboard layouts, railway gauges, video formats, and even the side of the road we drive on display so much apparent arbitrariness, and why appeals to the 'best' technology or institutional form simply winning out seem naive. On the other side, I find myself resisting the stronger conclusion that history is locked in and that the same outcomes would have arrived anyway. If a locked-in arrangement is genuinely inferior by a wide enough margin, shouldn't entry, modularity, political turnover, and long-run selection pressure find a way around it? And isn't it suspicious that many celebrated cases of lock-in turn out, on closer inspection, to involve standards that were not so inferior as the stylized stories suggest?

I am not asking which slogan is right. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, and what it would take for the theory to be amended or abandoned. Present the strongest version of each side: is the stubborn faith that efficiency eventually wins just a refusal to take the mathematics of self-reinforcement seriously, or is the theory of lock-in a collection of just-so stories that survive because they flatter a taste for contingency? Is there a defensible middle position—say, one that distinguishes fine-grained technical standards from coarse institutional forms, or short-to-medium timescales from genuinely long ones—or is compatibility here just the implausible claim that both views are true because each is true somewhere? For any proposed compromise, explain what would justify it: what concrete evidence in a given case would show that a system is genuinely locked in rather than merely persisting because it is good enough, and what would count as evidence that it is poised to escape.
