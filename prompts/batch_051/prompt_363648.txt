I got into one of those internet arguments about whether being rational means never paying to change your mind, and now I want a clean thought experiment to test the idea, not the same tired example everyone uses. I'm trying to figure out whether the money-pump problem is really about the preferences themselves or about how the person handles a sequence of choices. Build me a case to order, with these specs. A person starts out holding item A. They have preferences over three ordinary items — coffee-related stuff, junk-drawer items, whatever — that go in a circle: they'd pay a small amount to switch from A to B, from B to C, and from C back to A, when each switch is considered on its own. The amounts should be small enough that a normal person would actually pay them, like a dollar or two. The case has to do three things. First, if the person is offered each trade in isolation, one at a time, they will sincerely take every one. Second, a trader who understands the circle can run them through the full cycle and leave them holding there original item minus a few small fees — no lies, no pressure, no exotic trickery. Third, a more savy version of the same person, same underlying preferences, who anticipates the trader's plan before taking the first trade and can stick to a simple policy, refuses at the very start and pays nothing.

Then show your work: set out the three items and the exact pairwise preferences, walk through the naive reasoning trade by trade, walk through how the savy version blocks the pump, and identify the one structural feature of your construction that is doing the work. Finally, tell me what a defender of the naive version would say: does the pump cheat by exploiting a failure to see ahead, and if the whole sequence were written out for the person in advance, would this stop being a problem about their irrational preferences and become just a plain swindle?
