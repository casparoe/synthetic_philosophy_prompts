ive been trying to make sense of something my reading group kept calling the "presumption of independence", and I wrote out what I think is the argument behind it. It feels a little too smooth, but I cant actually locate the problem. Here it is.

Say H is the proposition that the dog bit the mailman. Two neighbors independently report seeing it: neither talked to the other, neither has any general bias about the dog, and the reports E1 and E2 were produced through no common cause except, possibly, H itself. The claim is that this kind of independence between the reports justifies treating their agreement as very strong evidence.

(1) Since the reports were produced independently—no collusion, no shared bias, no common cause other than H—E1 and E2 are probabilistically independent.
(2) If E1 and E2 are probabilistically independent, then the conditional probabilities factor as well: P(E1 & E2 | H) = P(E1 | H)P(E2 | H), and P(E1 & E2 | ¬H) = P(E1 | ¬H)P(E2 | ¬H).
(3) From (2), the likelihood ratio for the conjunction is the product of the individual likelihood ratios: P(E1 & E2 | H)/P(E1 & E2 | ¬H) equals [P(E1 | H)/P(E1 | ¬H)] × [P(E2 | H)/P(E2 | ¬H)].
(4) If each neighbor is more likely to report the bite given that it happened than given that it didn't, each ratio on the right is greater than 1, so their product is greater than either factor alone. So the agreeing pair confirms H more than either report alone.
(5) Therefore, whenever two sources are independent in the ordinary sense, we are entitled to multiply their likelihood ratios, and the presumption of independence is formally vindicated.

I suspect the slip is around (1) or (2)—maybe "independent" is doing different work in different steps—but I can't make the objection stick. Is the argument valid? Which premise fails on its most natural reading? If you give the premises their strongest defensible reading, does the argument go through, or does the conclusion stop following?
