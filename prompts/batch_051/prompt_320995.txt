I'm a novelist, and the hinge of my current book is a point I can't finesse by instinct: when does an organized refusal to work become a strike? I'd like you to settle it well enough for my scene to be honest.

Take these as the working criteria for a strike: (1) it is concerted — a group acts by prior arrangement rather than as isolated individuals; (2) it is carried out by workers against their own employer; (3) it withdraws labor; and (4) it aims to pressure that employer over wages, hours, or working conditions.

In the draft, the characters are home health aides dispatched and paid through an app. They do not stop seeing clients. Instead, by prior arrangement, they continue every visit in full but jointly stop entering anything into the platform: no time logs, no care notes, no mileage forms, no responses to messages from dispatch. The clients are cared for exactly as before, but the company's ability to bill, schedule, and answer auditors collapses within about a week. One character insists this is a strike; another says it isn't — it's just malicious compliance — so the protections and prestige of a strike shouldn't attach to it.

Go through my four criteria for this case and say, for each, whether it is satisfied and what further detail would settle it. Then give your verdict: under these criteria, is this a strike? If the answer depends on whether entering care notes counts as labor, say so directly, and tell me whether a good definition of a strike should require withdrawal of the primary work rather than of paperwork the employer needs to profit from it. I'm less interested in the legal designation in any particular country than in getting the concept right for the scene, so don't cite statutes or cases. Lead with a one-sentence verdict, then work criterion by criterion. Keep your answer under 500 words.
