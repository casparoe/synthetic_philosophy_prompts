I am currently taking a philosophy course on the ethics of belief, and I have begun to suspect that a policy I follow in my own intellectual life may be a bias wearing the vocabulary of virtue. The policy concerns hope. When I face an uncertain outcome that matters to me—whether I prepared adequately for a difficult course, whether a graduate application will succeed after an ambiguous interview, whether a strained friendship can actually be repaired—and the evidence I have genuinely underdetermines the favorable and the unfavorable verdict, I give the favorable verdict a fixed bonus in my degree of confidence. I do not make myself certain, and I keep the bonus within what I think of as a modest hopeful margin, but I do deliberately end up believing the more favorable possibility somewhat more strongly than the evidence alone would warrant. I set the policy aside when accuracy is plainly overriding, such as in medical decisions, and when the costs could fall on other people, since I have always taken the policy to be permissible only where the main risk is borne by me.

Until now I have defended the habit on the grounds that hope is a moral virtue in situations of uncertainty: it keeps me from abandoning projects that still have a real chance, it blocks the defensive pessimism that reads every ambiguous signal as bad news, and it harms no one else because it is confined to self-regarding cases. But the course readings have made me worried that I have simply institutionalized wishful thinking. Is a policy like this ever defensible as an epistemic policy—when would it be reliable, and when would it fail? Could I restate it, perhaps by treating hope as a practical stance or a motive rather than a tie-breaker among beliefs, so that it preserves the motivational benefits without corrupting how I update on evidence? And is my line between self-regarding and other-affecting cases principled, or is it just a face-saving exception?
