Okay, for next week I want you to construct a hard-choice case from scratch, not borrow one from the literature. The target is this. Take two options, A and B. I want a case that forces a trichotomist — someone who thinks that for any two options, either A is better than B, or B is better than A, or they are exactly equal in value — to classify A and B as equal in value, while a Chang-style parity theorist insists they are not equal but on a par. That disagreement is the whole point.

Constraints: no life-and-death stakes, no science fiction, no world-historical choices. The options should be ordinary and specfic enough that a normal person could face them. The case has to earn the verdict: you can't just write "A and B are incommensurable" into the setup. The features of the options have to make "better," "worse," and "equal" each look wrong in its own particular way. In particular, build in the standard parity trap: the case should be one where, if A and B were really equal, then improving B a little in one of its good-making respects should make it clearly better than A — but it doesn't. That's what pushes the trichotomist toward "equal" and then makes "equal" untenable.

After the scenario: (a) run the trichotomist's reasoning and say why she lands on "equal" (or, if she fights it, which horn she ends up impaled on); (b) run the parity-theorist's reasoning and say why "on a par" handles the case better than "equal"; (c) identify the structural feature that does the real work — what exactly keeps the options from being equal — and say what happens if you remove it, for example by making B a strict improvement over A in one respect while holding everything else fixed; (d) give the trichotomist her best comeback. Then close by saying how confident you are in your analysis overall, and single out the specific point you're least sure of.
