I'm working on a piece about whether math is just the language science uses or whether mathematical facts can actually explain things in nature, and I keep getting stuck on what people even mean by a mathematical explanation. So here's what I want: give me a working definition of a distinctively mathematical explanation of a natural phenomenon, and run it through these cases.

1. Periodical cicadas: suppose the ecological story is right that prime-numbered life cycles (13 or 17 years) evolved because a prime cycle shares no factors with shorter predator cycles, so the brood and its predators sync up as rarely as possible. The mathematical fact that primes are coprime to all smaller numbers is doing real work there. I lean yes: this counts.
2. Honeycomb: bees build hexagonal cells, and the usual line is that a hexagonal tiling minimizes wax for a given area. I lean yes here too.
3. A thrown rock tracing a parabola: the parabola is a mathematical object, but is it explaining the trajectory, or is gravity doing the explaining and the parabola just describing it? I honestly don't know whether this is the same kind of thing.
4. Regular pentagons can't tile the plane, and that's sometimes given as the reason floors use squares or hexagons. Is that a mathematical explanation of a physical fact, or just a constraint on what's possible?
5. The rainbow forms at around 42 degrees because that's the angle of minimum deviation for light in spherical water drops. Sounds mathematical, but it might just be optics.
6. The 7:30 bus comes every 15 minutes because the transit agency decided to run four buses an hour. That's obviously not a mathematical explanation, but I want to see what your definition does with it.

Run the definition through all six, tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then either fix the definition or try to talk me out of my intuitions. Also tell me which of your verdicts you're least sure about.
