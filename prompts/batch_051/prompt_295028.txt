A political theorist and a historian of political rhetoric have agreed, after repeatedly talking past each other in public, to produce a single co-signed text about the phrase "Western values" rather than another pair of rebuttals. The political theorist holds that, beneath its rhetorical abuse, the phrase tracks a genuine normative inheritance — individual rights, equality before the law, rule of law, free inquiry, limited government — and that the remedy for abuse is to tighten the term, not retire it. The historian holds that the phrase does its real work neither by naming that inheritance accurately nor by merely misnaming it, but by selecting among facts: it assembles a flattering record while suppressing exclusions, colonizations, and borrowings from elsewhere, and it functions in argument as a pre-authorization rather than as a description. The two signers refuse to fudge the difference, and each refuses to sign any formulation of their own view that sounds like the other side's caricature of it.

Draft the statement they can both sign. Structure it as a numbered schedule of agreed claims about what the phrase is used to do — in policy speeches, institutional mission statements, and accusations of betraying "the West" — followed by a table of the residual disagreements, each row stating the disputed claim twice, once in wording each side endorses. Then isolate the genuine cruxes: the sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side, as opposed to the differences that are merely verbal. For each crux that survives that test, write the shared evidentiary protocol both sides would adopt in advance: a concrete case, comparison, or kind of text that both accept bears on the question, and a prediction from each side about what the case will show. Do not adjudicate; the test is whether the document would be signed, not whether either side is right.

At the end, append a note in your own voice stating how confident you are that each side would sign the document as drafted, and identifying the single sentence or table-row you are least sure would survive contact with one of the signers.
