I spent my career as a physical scientist, and lately I have been reading Sidgwick's work on psychical research. In his first presidential address to the Society for Psychical Research, he makes a point that has stayed with me: roughly, that the question whether certain extraordinary events occur should not be settled by an antecedent conviction about what is physically possible, but by examining the testimony for them as carefully as one would examine testimony in an ordinary scientific dispute. Suppose a well-funded research institute adopts the following mechanism in response to that thought. Any investigator who wants to publish evidence that an anomalous phenomenon—say, apparent telepathy or an apparition—is real must first deposit a detailed case protocol with a standing adversarial evidence board made up of a cognitive psychologist, an experimental physicist, a statistician, and a professional mentalist. The board can block publication only if it can produce a non-anomalous explanation for the full set of reports that survives at least one independent replication attempt; otherwise the case is published with the board's dissent on record. Analyze how this mechanism would actually behave: what it incentivizes among researchers in the area, among the board members, and among the people who supply the initial testimony; where it would be gamed or break down; and whether it would select for stronger evidence, weirder evidence, or just better-packaged evidence. Also say whether Sidgwick's principle survives contact with the mechanism, or whether the mechanism shows that the principle was never the real issue.
