I am a novelist at work on a book set in a near-future in which a one-time service, whose mechanism I am deliberately leaving unexplained, can tell a client the objective chance that a chosen event will occur within a fixed span of time. My protagonist uses this service at a hinge moment: she is deciding whether to attempt a border crossing, and she asks for the chance that she will survive the crossing if she attempts it. I want to get her reasoning right and not merely her psychology, and I have become interested in what philosophers call the Principal Principle: roughly, that upon learning that the objective chance of an outcome is p, a rational agent should assign degree of belief p to that outcome, at least when she has no information that the chance calculation already screens off.

Please describe the most important implications of the Principal Principle for how I should handle this character's reasoning. In particular, I would like to know when she should treat the number she receives as a settled fact about the world and simply align her belief with it, and when she should treat it as more fragile: for instance, if the chance she is given is itself sensitive to the degree of belief she adopts after hearing it, or if she possesses private information about her own resolve and intentions that the calculation cannot incorporate. I would also like guidance on how to dramatize the distance between the calm, rational response the principle describes and the way a frightened person actually relates to a number that seems to be telling her whether she will die. I am after a careful conceptual map I can use scene by scene, not plot suggestions.
