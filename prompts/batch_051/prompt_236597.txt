I just got back from an hour-long argument with a friend who teaches history, and I keep turning it over. I was defending something like Hempel's covering-law model: an explanation really explains only if it shows the event was to be expected given laws plus initial conditions, where the laws can be strict or statistical. My friend kept hitting me with cases where the explanation clearly does work but cites nothing lawlike. The one we kept circling was why the Schlieffen Plan failed in 1914: Moltke weakened the right wing, logistics broke down, the French exploited the gap at the Marne. That narrative seems to genuinely explain the failure, but it doesn't look like a deduction from laws. I'm drawn to the covering-law model because it's the only account I know that gives a clean criterion seperating explanation from mere description or storytelling. But I also can't shake the intuition that the historian's narrative explanation is doing real explanatory work, not just making me feel informed. And I know the model has a famous asymmetry problem — you can derive the length of a shadow from the flagpole's height and the sun's angle, but the reverse derivation doesn't explain the flagpole's height — which makes me worry the clean criterion is too clean.

What I want is a negotiated verdict, not a side. Give me the strongest case for saying my intuition should yield: that narrative explanation is the feeling of understanding produced by coherence, familiarity, or dramatic fit, not genuine explanation. Give me the strongest case for saying the theory should yield or be loosened: that explanations may track mechanisms, contrast classes, or causal processes without subsuming the event under laws. Then tell me whether there's a reading of "explain" on which both survive — for example, narratives supply the contrast class and the mechanism, while laws stay in the background as ceteris-paribus regularities — and what would justify each resolution rather than the others. End with a line beginning "Verdict:" giving a short label (intuition yields, theory yields, amend the theory, or compatible reading) plus one sentence.
