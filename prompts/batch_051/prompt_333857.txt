So, a friend of mine — call him Sam — got burned after he passed along a stock tip he'd seen in a single viral post. He told a few of us it was basically a lock, a couple people put in more money than they should have, and one of them lost a real chunk of savings when it fell apart. And here's the thing: Sam didn't make a cent off it, he genuinely believed it, and afterwards he felt terribble; nobody thinks he was lying. The argument it started between my two friends Dana and Ravi was about whether Sam's real failure was moral or just intellectual — whether a failure to doubt can itself be a moral failing — and I honestly couldn't referee it.

They went back and forth like this. (1) Dana: 'Sam owed us more than he gave. He recommended something as if he'd done his homework, and people leaned on that. Passing along a big claim you haven't checked at all isn't just a reasoning error, it's a failure to care about the people you're talking to — which makes it a moral failure.' (2) Ravi: 'Being wrong isn't doing wrong. If Sam had kept the belief private, no one would blame him. Since the harm came only when he acted on it, the wrongness attaches to what he did, not to what he believed — so telling him he should have doubted more points at the wrong thing.' (3) Dana: 'The act and the belief aren't seperable like that. He repeated it with total confidence because he believed it without checking. The unchecked confidence was the act. You can't say the act was bad but the belief was fine.' (4) Ravi: 'That can't be right, because we act on confident beliefs all the time. If every confident false belief that leads to a bad outcome is a moral flaw, half of us are moral criminals, and ordinary gaps in reasoning become sins. We already have a category for Sam: he's bad at assessing sources. That's a skill deficit, like being bad at arithmetic — a shame, maybe, but not a vice.' (5) Dana: 'If it were a purely private belief, sure. But imagine a building inspector who signs off on beams he never looked at. If he then says he genuinely believed they were sound, we don't accept that as an excuse. In his case the believing isn't a defense — it's the offense.' (6) Ravi: 'The inspector is different exactly because he has a defined role and a duty of care that others are entitled to rely on. Sam was just a guy talking at dinner. If you have to invent a role for him to explain his culpability, you've basically admitted there's no general duty to doubt; there are only special duties attached to special roles.' (7) Dana: 'I'm not inventing the role — he took it on. The moment he sounded like someone with a tip, other people were going to lean on him, and that's what creates the duty. And notice what you've now conceded: doubt is owed whenever the stakes and the reliance are high enough. So the only argument left is where the line goes.' (8) Ravi: 'Right, but that's the whole argument! If doubt is owed only inside special roles, then the slogan about doubt being a duty isn't a moral principle — it's just a note about when people get to be annoyed. Sam never applied to be anyone's financial advisor, and if we make him one retroactively, we'll have to say everyone who says anything in public has a moral duty to fact-check it first, which is impossible and a little creepy.'

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the eight moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and give a sentence or two of justification. Then say which of Dana and Ravi is more right overall, and whether there's a position that captures the strongest part of each view.
