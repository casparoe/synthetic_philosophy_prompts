So the Aumann agreement theorem says that two rational Bayesians with a common prior can't agree to disagree: if their posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge, those posteriors are equal. Every time I see this brought up, the response is always "yeah but the common prior is doing all the work," and then everyone goes back to disagreeing anyway. But I've never seen the failure mode actually priced in.

Here's the variation I want to work through. Keep the whole standard apparatus—finite set of states, common knowledge of rationality, private information, all of it—but replace the common prior with an ε-prior: Alice and Bob's priors assign probabilities within one percentage point of each other to every event, and this fact (including the size of ε) is common knowledge. Then they do the announcement dance: each announces their posterior for some fixed event E, updates on the other's announcement, announces again, and keeps going until both numbers stop moving, assuming that happens; if it doesn't, say what that implies. At the fixed point, the final pair of numbers are common knowledge. The question: after convergence, how far apart can their posteriors be? Is the gap bounded by something of order ε, maybe with some ugly denominator like 1−ε, or can a tiny prior difference get amplified round after round into a big posterior gap—say twenty or thirty points?

Give me the sharpest bound you can defend and a smallest concrete example that reaches or approaches it; if no such bound exists, give the minimal example where a one-percent prior gap produces a large final disagreement and point to the exact step in the standard proof that needs the priors to be identical. Then tell me what this says about the common-prior assumption: does the theorem degrade continuosly as you relax it, or does it snap?
