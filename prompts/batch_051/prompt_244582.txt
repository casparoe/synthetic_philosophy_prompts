I have been reading Erwin Schrödinger’s 1944 book What Is Life? and keep returning to its epilogue, On Determinism and Free Will. He argues there that consciousness is never experienced as a plurality, only ever as one: that what we call separate minds are really only separate sets of memories and bodily histories through which a single awareness looks out at the world. On this view, the boundary between self and other is not a raw datum of experience but something like a practical illusion, generated because each body carries its own remembered past and its own immediate sensations. Schrödinger ties this to the Vedantic idea that the many selves are ultimately manifestations of one underlying consciousness, and that seeing through the illusion of separateness would transform our relation to other beings.

Yet I find myself resisting this conclusion in a way I cannot fully justify. It seems to me that my consciousness is not merely a particular collection of memories with a special first-person access to itself, but something privately and irreducibly mine. If I picture a stranger in pain, or my mother, or even someone I dislike, it seems almost absurd to say that behind their eyes there is the very same I that now reads this sentence. The distinction between self and other feels to me as firm as anything I know, not like a by-product of forgetting or a trick of perspective. But I also see why Schrödinger’s view is attractive: it is simple, it dissolves the problem of other minds, and it offers a deep metaphysical backing for the intuition that harming another is somehow harming oneself.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition of separate selves to be the thing that goes, and what it would take for the unity-of-consciousness theory to be the thing that goes. Could one debunk the intuition as a product of how each life’s perspective is insulated by its own memories, while preserving the theory? Or could one weaken the theory until it no longer claims literal oneness but only a kind of deep continuity or ethical solidarity? Is there a reading on which both survive?
