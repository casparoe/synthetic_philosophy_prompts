Bit of an odd one for the group. I've been going back over Parfit's hitchhiker scenario: you're dying of thirst in the desert, a driver stops and says he'll take you to town but only if you promise to hand over £5,000 once you get there. He's a good enough reader of character that he can tell whether you genuinely intend to pay, rather than just mouthing the words, and there's no way he can enforce the promise after you're safe. The usual rationalist take is that this is the poster child for rational irrationality: you should make yourself into the sort of person who really will pay, even though, at the moment of handing over the money, paying looks like a pure loss, because the rescue is worth far more than the money.

Here's the tweak I've been stewing on alot. Suppose the driver isn't a perfect lie detector. Say he helps iff he reads you as sincere; if you actually intend to pay, he reads you as sincere with probability 0.95; if you don't intend to pay, he reads you as insincere with probability 0.8, so a decent bluff gets you saved 20% of the time. Once you're in town, keep the original background unchanged: you'll pay iff you genuinely intended to pay, because nothing enforceable has materialised.

Run the expected-utility numbers for the two clean strategies—actually form the intention versus just plan to renege—and then tell me whether the standard self-modify-into-a-promise-keeper story survives the noise. Does the problem collapse into a boring expected-value calculation about the driver's error rates, or is there still a distinct commitment problem left over? Is the advice to cultivate the disposition still always right when self-modification is free, or does the 20% free-riding chance drive a wedge between the two accounts?
