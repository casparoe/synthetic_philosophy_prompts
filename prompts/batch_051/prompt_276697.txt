I am trying to map the live positions in the debate over what rights are and what normative work the concept does, and I want to be sure I am not missing a family of views. Below are the three accounts I already have; treat them as the standard options, at least as far as I have been able to survey.

1. The side-constraint view, associated with Nozick. A right is an agent-relative constraint on action. It forbids a certain manner of treating a person — for example, intentionally killing one innocent even when doing so would prevent more violations of the same or other rights. Respect for rights is not a good to be maximized; violations are excluded, not minimized.

2. The trump view, associated with Dworkin. A right is an individual claim that defeats the sort of background justification that normally suffices for collective action, centrally the appeal to aggregate welfare. A right does not settle every conflict: it may be outweighed by a genuinely competing right, and perhaps by an emergency of the most extreme kind, but it cannot be traded away merely because more interests would be satisfied overall by trading it away.

3. The instrument view, standard in rule-consequentialist writing. A right is a device — a rule or a way of thinking — justified because complying with it, and treating certain interests as specially protected, tends to produce better outcomes than case-by-case calculation would. The underlying normative furniture consists only of interests, harms, and value-based reasons; a right has whatever force it has because of the results of assigning it that force. In principle the concept might be eliminable, even if it is indispensable in practice.

Give me a fourth account of what a right is or of the normative work the concept of rights does, one that is genuinely different from all three and not a mere variant or combination of them. For the sake of this exercise, do not offer: (a) an outright error theory that simply denies that anything answers to talk of rights; (b) a hybrid that says rights are side-constraints up to a threshold and trumps beyond it, or any other two-of-the-three blend; or (c) a restatement of one of the three under different vocabulary, such as "rights are extremely strong interests," unless you can show that your account does explanatory work the three cannot.

Then apply your fourth account, together with the three above, to a single concrete case: a policy that would knowingly disregard one person's right not to be subjected to a mildly painful non-consensual medical test in order to spare a very large number of people a brief but real annoyance each. Show how the four accounts yield different verdicts or different kinds of reasons in this case. Conclude by saying whether adding your fourth account makes the question "Do we need the concept of rights?" easier or harder to answer, and why.
