I am scheduled to give a research talk next month at a school of government, presenting a proposal for what I call a bounded advisory futarchy pilot at the municipal level. The audience will be mostly public administration and public policy faculty, plus one empirical political scientist who works on accountability, one participatory democracy theorist, and the budget director of a mid-sized city. None of them are prediction-market specialists, though several are fluent in program evaluation, cost-benefit analysis, and principal-agent problems. My thesis is this: a city should stand up a small, continuously-traded decision market over three to five pre-registered welfare indicators (for example, emergency-room wait times, traffic injury rates, or air quality under alternative policy bundles), receive the market's expected-value ranking of the candidate policies, and permit the council to depart from that ranking only by publishing a written justification. No funds are bound and the market never holds decision rights; it functions as a standing epistemic instrument, not as a legislature. My argument, in compressed form, is that the market aggregates dispersed information better than hearings and staff forecasts, that splitting the value question (set once, democratically, in the welfare index) from the belief question (delegated to the market) preserves majoritarian control rather than undermining it, and that keeping the market advisory sidesteps the strongest objections to full futarchy.

Please write my Q&A preparation sheet for this talk. Identify the objections this specific audience will actually raise, not the ones prediction-market insiders would raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way a public administration scholar or a city budget director would phrase them, with a two-minute reply for each and a note on where that reply is soft. I particularly want the institutional details they will seize on: how the welfare index is chosen and revised, what happens when markets are thin or manipulable, whether an advisory ranking can simply be ignored until it becomes a headline, and how a city attorney will think about gambling and securities law. Include the question I should be most afraid someone will ask, and tell me what I should concede up front rather than defend.
