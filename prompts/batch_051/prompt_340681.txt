I am a high school student, and I have been trying to understand a view in the philosophy of economics that is sometimes called the F-twist: the claim, usually associated with Milton Friedman, that we should judge a scientific model by whether it predicts successfully and not by whether its assumptions are realistic. I would like you to reason through a counterfactual that tests this idea.

Suppose that, starting in 2025, the main public science-funding agency of a large, scientifically advanced country (in all other respects similar to the United States today) adopts an assumption-accuracy rule. It will fund no model-based research unless every assumption of the model can be defended as literally true, or at least true of the particular system being studied, before any results or predictions are considered. Frictionless planes, point masses, perfectly rational consumers, infinite populations, representative agents, and ideal gases all become grounds for rejection, no matter how well a model containing them has predicted in the past. A defense of the form that the model predicts well even though the assumption is false is treated as a methodological error.

Predict, with a step-by-step chain of reasoning, what this country's science and science-based policy look like fifty years later. In particular:

1. Which disciplines are hit hardest, and which barely notice?
2. Which idealizations quietly survive by being renamed, relocated, or absorbed into definitions, measurement procedures, or the mathematics rather than stated openly as assumptions?
3. Does the rule actually make funded theories more truthlike, or does it mostly redirect research toward questions that were already tractable without idealization?
4. What happens to applied work such as engineering, climate policy, epidemiology, and macroeconomic policy, where the standard models are known to be false in their details?
5. How does this country's science perform, after fifty years, relative to comparable countries without the rule?

I am not asking for a story; I am asking for mechanisms, incentives, and constraints. For each major prediction, tell me how confident you are, distinguish the links that follow almost mechanically from the links that are guesses, and identify the assumption that, if it failed, would overturn that prediction. Please explain any technical terms as you go; I am still learning this material, but do not simplify the structure of the argument.
