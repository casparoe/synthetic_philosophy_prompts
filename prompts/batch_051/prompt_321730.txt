I've been turning over an asymmetry in how we treat the emotions, and I can't tell whether it is a defensible distinction or just an inconsistency we live with. On the one side, the same harmful act is judged very differently depending on the feeling behind it: a killing or assault committed in sudden rage is, in most legal systems and in much of everyday blame, treated as less culpable than the identical act done coolly and deliberately. The "heat of passion" mitigates, as if the emotion partly unseats the person's agency or judgment, and we extend the same attitude informally when we say of someone's outburst that "they weren't themselves." On the other side, we treat the capacity for strong feeling—grief, love, moral indignation, even anger at injustice—as part of a full human life, and we would think a person who could switch anger or grief off at will had lost something rather than gained it. So the same phenomenon, emotion, gets treated as a kind of noise that undermines responsibility when it drives action, and as a constituent of the good life when it is present or when we imagine its absence.

The passage that sharpens the problem for me is from Seneca's On Anger, Book II: "Our (the Stoics') opinion is, that anger can venture upon nothing by itself, without the approval of mind: for to conceive the idea of a wrong having been done, to long to avenge it, and to join the two propositions, that we ought not to have been injured and that it is our duty to avenge our injuries, cannot belong to a mere impulse which is excited without our consent." If Seneca is right that anger—and by extension the passions generally—never operates without the approval of the mind, but always involves assent, judgment, and something like choice, then the mitigating asymmetry becomes suspicious: the enraged offender has, on this view, endorsed the wrong he does, and it is unclear why an endorsement made in anger should count for less than one made in calm. Yet I am not sure the legal and social practice is simply a mistake; it may be tracking something that Seneca's sentence misses.

Is there a defensible justification for treating the passionate offense as less blameworthy than the identical deliberate one, without collapsing into the view that emotions are mere noise? If you defend the practice, defend it against the strongest version of the Stoic charge—that the difference between the two offenders, stripped of everything except that one assented in anger and the other assented in calm, is no difference at all. If you reject the practice, say what we should do instead: treat them alike, or treat the emotion as making the offense worse—and explain how either answer fits with the value we place on emotional life.
