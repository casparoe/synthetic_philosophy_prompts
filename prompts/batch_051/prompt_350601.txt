I’m taking a course on meaning in life, and I keep noticing a pattern I don’t have a term for. A friend of mine helps run a food bank and says she almost never asks what the meaning of life is; the question only shows up when she is already miserable and disengaged. Then, when things are going well, the question doesn’t feel answerable so much as unnecessary, like asking what the meaning of dinner is. The same move came up in seminar: one student said he can list things that make his life meaningful, but the moment someone asks about the meaning of his life as a whole, he wants to say the question is a symptom, not a request for information. The weird part is the inverse relation: the more adequate the answer to the little questions, the less the big question seems to need an answer.

I know this is somewhere near Wittgenstein’s idea that some philosophical problems are dissolved rather than solved, and also near the absurdist advice to stop asking and get on with things. But those are views about the question. What I’m after is the specific claim that the presence or absence of the question is itself evidence about the state of a life: asking the big question in earnest is treated as a sign that you are alienated from the everyday sources of meaning, and the absence of the question is treated as the thing that shows the life is going fine. Does that configaration have an established name? If so, what distinguishes it from plain dissolutionism and from the idea that meaning is just a byproduct of engagement? And if there isn’t a name, can you coin one and give its edges — for example, can someone be reflective and ask the question without that counting against their life, or does the view quietly turn an epistemology of meaning into a diagnostic tool for mental health?
