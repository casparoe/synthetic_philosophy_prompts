I have been reading my way through older works on logic, and I keep running into a limitation that I can see clearly but cannot find a name for. The standard apparatus is built around sentences of one shape: a subject, a connecting verb, and a predicate. That works beautifully for arguments about one thing falling under one class, such as moving from every horse is an animal and every animal is mortal to every horse is mortal. But the apparatus seems to stall on arguments that are just as safe: from every horse is an animal it follows that the head of a horse is the head of an animal; and if A is taller than B and B is taller than C, then A is taller than C. Those conclusions do not say that a single thing has a single property; they say how one thing stands to another, and the standard forms have no natural slot for that. I can force the examples in by treating being the head of a horse or being taller than B as a property, but the forcing feels artificial, and it breaks down as soon as two relations cross in the same step.

My question is whether this limitation has an established name, and whether it is the real difference between the older logic and the modern formal logic that replaced it, or only one symptom of a deeper change.
