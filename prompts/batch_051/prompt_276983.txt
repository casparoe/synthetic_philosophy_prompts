I write about philosophy and the behavioral sciences for a general-interest magazine, and for the past several months I have been following an adversarial collaboration between two moral philosophers who disagree about whether Robert Nozick's utility monster is a serious problem for aggregative ethics. One of them, the critic of aggregation, maintains that the monster exposes a structural defect in any view that treats total well-being as the quantity to be maximized: such views, she argues, ignore the separateness of persons and will in principle license reducing everyone else to misery, or worse, for the sake of one being whose capacity for welfare never satiates. The other, the defender of aggregation, maintains that the thought experiment is not a reductio but a clarification: if a being really did have a welfare function with non-diminishing marginal utility for scarce resources, then transferring resources to it would simply be what a beneficence-maximizing morality requires, and the horror of the story is an intuition we should distrust rather than a premise. After a year of work, they have produced a joint statement that my editors want to publish in full.

Draft that statement. It should be the kind of document both camps could sign without embarrassment and a sophisticated reader could use to locate exactly where the argument is still live. Include: (1) a numbered section of claims both can affirm, covering the structure of the thought experiment, the precise conditions under which redistribution to a monster would be utility-maximizing, and what neither side is entitled to infer from intuitive revulsion alone; (2) a disagreements table stating the residual disagreement, with each side's view phrased as its holder would phrase it, including whether they differ about the weight of welfare across lives, about what counts as utility, or about how much work a thought experiment can do; (3) the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — for example, whether the intensity of a preference or experience carries the welfare weight that the aggregative view requires, whether within-life and across-life aggregation are relevantly similar, and whether a being with truly non-diminishing marginal utility is coherent or is just a disguised series of successive selves; and (4) for each crux, a concrete variant of the monster, an actual case, or an empirical finding that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Conclude with a short section separating the disagreements that are substantive from the ones that were merely verbal.
