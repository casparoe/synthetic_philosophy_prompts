I have been working through the infant baptism controversy in its Reformed version, and what puzzles me is not that paedobaptists and credobaptists disagree, but that the disagreement has remained almost perfectly immobile for four centuries despite the two sides sharing a remarkable amount of ground. By "Reformed version" I mean the debate between confessional paedobaptists, who baptize the infant children of at least one believing parent, and confessional credobaptists, who baptize only those who make a credible profession of faith. Both hold that Scripture is the final authority and that salvation is by grace alone; both deny that the mere performance of baptism regenerates; both treat baptism as the initiatory rite of the New Testament church; both grant that the New Testament reinterprets Old Testament institutions; and each side, once its wider system is granted, can offer a coherent reading of nearly every relevant passage. The argument nevertheless keeps bottoming out in two inverse starting assumptions. The paedobaptist begins from covenant continuity: in the old administration the covenant sign was applied to the infant children of covenant members, and if the new covenant is the same covenant of grace differently administered, then the sign belongs to believers' children unless Christ expressly repealed it. The credobaptist begins from covenant redefinition: the New Testament presents God's people as disciples who profess faith, so baptism belongs only to professors unless a command to baptize infants can be shown.

What I want is a diagnosis of the structure of this standoff, not another proof-text survey. In particular, is the real disagreement best understood as (a) a hermeneutical one about whether covenant continuity or covenant redefinition ought to be the default assumption, and what non-question-begging evidence could show one default to be correct; (b) an ecclesiological one about whether the visible church is a mixed body of believers and their children or a gathered body of professing believers; (c) a conceptual one about what baptism essentially is — a sign and seal of covenant membership, an instrument through which God confers or confirms grace, or the believer's public pledge — such that the two sides are in part using "baptism" to name different acts; or (d) a pre-exegetical difference in intuition about how God ordinarily deals with families and households? Are any of these reducible to the others, are any merely verbal, and which of them would further exegesis actually resolve? Given your diagnosis, say what real progress would look like: a shared hermeneutical criterion, a conceptual distinction between covenant membership and eligibility for the sign, a model of old-to-new covenant continuity both sides could accept as a neutral middle term, or the honest conclusion that progress should not be expected. My working hypothesis is that the deepest layer is (c): the two sides operate with different concepts of what a sacrament is, so the textual evidence can only be weighted after a concept of baptism has already been chosen, which is why exegesis alone has never moved the debate. Evaluate that hypothesis; do not merely restate it.
