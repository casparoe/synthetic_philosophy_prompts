I have been reading, on and off, about the view that conscious experience is real but never does any causal work: the brain produces experience, but the experience itself never causes a word, a wince, a flinch, or a belief. I realize the view itself has a name, and I am not asking for that. What I keep failing to find a label for is a narrower difficulty the view seems to land me in, one that strikes me as importantly different from the familiar complaint that the view makes experience a useless byproduct or a decoration. The usual ground we have for believing that conscious experience exists is that people are directly aware of it and say so: someone reports being in pain, or that there is something it is like for them to see red, and we treat these reports as first-person evidence. But on the view in question, the utterance I am in pain is produced entirely by neural events, and those neural events would produce exactly the same utterance even if the pain were absent—in a physical duplicate of me with no experience at all, the same words would come out just the same. So the one kind of evidence the view rests on, the testimony of our own minds, appears to be causally insulated from the very thing it is evidence for. The view does not deny the experience; it denies that the experience ever reaches back into the machinery that speaks and writes about it, and yet the speaking and writing is precisely the evidence on which the view was built.

I can see that this resembles generic self-undermining charges, such as the complaint that a deterministic account of reasoning cannot be right because then no one ever reasons; but it feels like a different species, because the concern here is not that the judgments are false, but that the mechanism producing them would be exactly the same whether or not the experience they describe existed. Is there an established name for this specific difficulty, and what is its precise formulation? I would also want to know how it should be classified: is it best understood as a self-stultification argument, as an undercutting defeater for the introspective evidence, or as something with its own logical shape? If the standard name covers only a narrower version than the one I have described, I would rather hear how the versions differ and where the boundary cases fall than be handed a label and left with it. If there is no established name, what would you call it, and how would you define it so that it stays distinct from the broader charge that the view treats experience as an idle shadow?
