I have recently become the get-out-the-vote coordinator for my district in a municipal election, and the paradox of voting is suddenly a practical problem rather than a seminar topic. Voters I try to recruit tell me that one ballot will never decide the outcome, so the evening spent voting is a bad investment. But I have also observed a local first-voter pattern: when someone in a social circle votes and says so publicly, it sometimes brings one more like-minded person to the polls who would otherwise have stayed home. I want to know exactly how much this should shift the canonical calculation before I start arguing with my neighbors.

Set up the standard instrumental-voter model as follows. There are two candidates, A and B; among n other voters, let M be the margin of A-votes over B-votes that would occur if neither you nor anyone you influence votes. You prefer A to B by utility difference V, and voting costs you C. If you vote, your ballot adds one A-vote. If the final margin is exactly zero, the election is decided by a fair coin. The standard analysis says your single vote matters only when M is -1 or 0; define p = [P(M=-1)+P(M=0)]/2, so the standard expected instrumental benefit of voting is pV, and the self-interested rule is to vote only if pV + D > C, where D is any expressive utility from the act of voting itself.

Now change one thing. Suppose that with independent probability q, your public act of voting causes exactly one additional A-preferring citizen — someone who otherwise would certainly have abstained — to vote; with probability 1-q, no one follows. Your action therefore adds 1 + X votes to A, where X is Bernoulli(q), while your own cost remains C. Work through the exact expected instrumental benefit of voting under this variation. Then apply the usual local near-tie approximation for a large, closely divided electorate, under which P(M=-2), P(M=-1), and P(M=0) are approximately equal, and express the new benefit in terms of p and q. From this, state the condition under which a purely self-interested voter (D=0) should vote. In a realistic large-race setting where p is minuscule, does the first-voter effect rescue rational-choice instrumentalism, or merely reparameterize the paradox? Finally, judge whether the leading non-instrumental resolutions — expressive utility, altruistic weighting of the aggregate benefit, and rule-consequentialist or universalizability accounts — remain just as necessary, or whether the variation makes any of them less central to explaining turnout.

End your response with a single line of the form \answer{<factor>; vote iff <condition>}, where <factor> is the multiplicative factor by which the first-voter effect scales expected instrumental benefit relative to the standard one-vote calculation, and <condition> is the resulting threshold for a purely self-interested voter.
