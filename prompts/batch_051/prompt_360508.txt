I am a novelist writing a book set in a community that treats small commitments — arriving at the hour one named, sending the note one promised to send, returning the borrowed book on the agreed day — as matters of serious moral record, while treating one-off large failures with unusual forbearance; the community is a deliberate inversion, and I want to know precisely what it inverts. Act as a moral philosopher working on promising and character, and excavate the following real-world pattern for me. In ordinary life, a person who is habitually late, or who repeatedly fails to do the small things they said they would do, comes to be regarded as unreliable: we stop counting on them, stop inviting them, and take the pattern to reveal who they are. Yet ask whether any particular lateness or small breach was itself a wrong, and the question feels almost absurd — no single instance mattered. Meanwhile, someone who breaks one important promise in a moment of weakness, with real consequences, is blamed for the act but is not thereby branded unreliable. The practice thus looks strict at the level of character and lax at the level of the individual act. What theory of promising — and of reliability as a trait — does this practice presuppose? Think carefully, and begin by briefly brainstorming the candidate views of promising and character that could license the pattern before you argue for the best reconstruction. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with and where it is philosophically underdetermined, and say whether the two levels are genuinely consistent. Then tell me what would change if we judged each small breach only by its actual consequences, and whether anything of moral importance would be lost. Finally, identify the single feature of the practice that a fictional inversion would need to retain in order to remain intelligible as a morality of small promises.
