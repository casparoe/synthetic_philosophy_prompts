I keep going back and forth on this and want someone to push it around properly. Davidson's famous line on metaphor says there's no such thing as metaphorical meaning: the words in a metaphor mean exactly what they literally mean, and everything else the metaphor does (the image, the seeing-as, the little jolt) is a causal effect of the utterance, not part of what it says. He'll grant that metaphors make you notice things, but he thinks that's no more semantic than a slap making you notice you're awake. The premise doing the real work is that meaning has to be rule-governed and learnable, and there's no finite rule for unpacking a live metaphor.

Then along comes conceptual metaphor theory — Lakoff and Johnson and the rest — claiming metaphor isn't a deviant use of language at all. Its the ordinary cognitive machinery: a small number of systematic cross-domain mappings (argument is war, time is space, theories are buildings) structure how we think and how we talk, and a huge slab of everyday English is conventionalized metaphor rather than fresh poetry. If anything like that is right, a lot of metaphorical interpretation does look systematic and rule-governed, and those mappings are arguably part of what a competent speaker has learned.

So here's what I want worked through: does Davidson's argument survive that development? Could he just say the conceptual-metaphor machinery is all still 'effects', just very reliable ones — and if he says that, what does 'meaning' still pick out that's worth arguing about? On the other side, does conceptual metaphor theory actually refute him, or does it only refute him if you already count conventional mappings as semantic meaning, which he'd deny? Which premise of his is actually under pressure, and is there a repaired Davidson-style position that concedes the mappings are real and systematic but still denies a metaphor adds metaphorical meaning to an utterance? I want the argument-level diagnosis, not a survey of the literature.
