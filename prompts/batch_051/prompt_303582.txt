I am a PhD student in ancient philosophy planning a dissertation on Plato's Meno. I want to decide which line of argument it would be most valuable to pursue over a three-year project, and I would like a defended agenda rather than a literature survey. The pivot is Meno 97e–98a, in the English translation from the Perseus Digital Library:

"For these, so long as they stay with us, are a fine possession, and effect all that is good; but they do not care to stay for long, and run away out of the human soul, and thus are of no great value until one makes them fast with causal reasoning. And this process, friend Meno, is recollection, as in our previous talk we have agreed. But when once they are fastened, in the first place they turn into knowledge, and in the second, are abiding. And this is why knowledge is more prized than right opinion: the one transcends the other by its trammels."

Please rank the unresolved questions this passage raises for Plato's answer to the Meno problem — why knowledge is better than true belief — in order of which is most worth settling first for a PhD-length research project. At least consider these candidates, but add or replace any you think is more upstream.

1. Exegetical: What exactly is the relation expressed by "makes them fast with causal reasoning"? Does it mean explanatory understanding of why the opinion is true, or a capacity to give an account, or some form of calculative stability? And is "causal reasoning" here momentary justification or an ongoing retention-and-recall mechanism?

2. Value-theoretic: Does the passage actually answer the value problem, or does it only explain why knowledge lasts longer? Does the fact that true opinions "effect all that is good" undercut the need for a distinct epistemic value in knowledge, leaving only an instrumental value of reliability over time?

3. Collision with recollection: If the process that ties down true opinions is recollection, and recollection is also supposed to be how anyone originally acquires knowledge, then why are some true opinions unanchored? Does the appeal to recollection explain the difference between knowledge and true belief, or merely restate it?

4. Systematic scope: Does the answer generalize beyond ordinary practical examples like the road to Larissa? The text claims true opinions are already effective; so what precisely is the superior value of knowledge supposed to consist in when both states guide action equally well?

5. Later epistemological development: Does the Meno's distinction between knowledge and right opinion anticipate, or conflict with, the Theaetetus's attempt to define knowledge as true belief plus a logos? If the Meno treats the tie-down as external to the belief, is it offering a reductive account or a different state altogether?

For each candidate, I would like you to say what would count as progress toward settling it, which other questions depend on its answer, and how likely it is to be settled from the text itself plus philosophical argument. Then defend the ranking. End with a calibration note: how confident you are in the overall ranking, and the single point you are least sure of.
