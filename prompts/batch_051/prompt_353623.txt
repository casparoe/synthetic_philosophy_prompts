I teach a graduate seminar in non-Western metaphysics, and this term I have been reconstructing the Advaita Vedānta doctrine of māyā and the identity of self and Brahman as a numbered argument, so that students can engage it as argument rather than as a report of tradition. The reconstruction is below. Each step initially struck me as faithful to the tradition's own commitments, but the final steps appear to make the teaching defeat itself, and I cannot tell whether the fault lies in my reconstruction, in the doctrine, or in an unexamined assumption I am bringing to what a teaching must do. I do not want an exegetical survey; I want a diagnostic judgment on the reasoning itself.

(1) Whatever appears to awareness — a body, a thought, a perception, a world — appears as an object, and objects are always objects for a witness that is not itself an object.

(2) This witness is the self, ātman.

(3) Brahman is the sole ultimate reality, which is never an object because it is that which makes all objects possible.

(4) The tradition's central claim is that ātman is Brahman: witness and ultimate reality are one.

(5) Since Brahman alone is ultimately real, whatever appears as an object is not ultimately real as an object; this is what I mean by māyā.

(6) But the teaching expressed in (1) through (4), when it is heard, read, held in mind, or asserted, appears as an object of awareness.

(7) Therefore, by (5), the teaching is māyā.

(8) If the teaching is māyā, it has the same epistemic status as the misidentification of the self with body and world that it is meant to dissolve, and no practice of study or reasoning can be rationally preferred over any other.

(9) Yet the tradition presents this same teaching as the instrument by which that misidentification is removed. The teaching therefore cannot do what the tradition says it does, and the doctrine refutes itself.

Where does the argument go wrong? Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost the surrounding position? I would prefer to keep (1) through (4), the identity claim, and to revise my gloss of māyā in (5) rather than concede that the teaching is merely another appearance with no authority. But I want to know whether that preference is stable, and what further concessions it forces.
