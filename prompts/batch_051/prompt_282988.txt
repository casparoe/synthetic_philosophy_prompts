For a 90-minute upper-level undergraduate seminar on political economy (16 students), design a classroom game that makes participants feel, before any lecture names it, the Austrian claim that a central planner cannot assemble the dispersed, local, and often tacit knowledge that a market price system transmits. The game should place different privately held information in different students' hands and have one group attempt an allocation by central command using only reports, while another group handles the same allocations through bids, offers, and posted prices; use a non-monetary scoring scheme so the two mechanisms can be compared. End with a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto Mises's original calculation objection, Hayek's point that relevant knowledge is scattered, local, and often inarticulate, and Lange's trial-and-error pricing reply, saying what Lange's reply can and cannot fix. Also say what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run and how to handle it, and be explicit about which aspect of real economic calculation the game cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply.
