An argument I keep hearing in discussions of simultaneous discovery runs like this. When Newton and Leibniz arrived at the calculus, or Darwin and Wallace at natural selection, at nearly the same moment, that supposedly shows the result was by then a piece of ripe fruit waiting to be picked. Once the background concepts and techniques had ripened, the discovery was available to any competent investigator who looked; the person who actually picked it came first only by luck, so priority disputes are mostly vanity and convention, and taking individual credit for what had become inevitable is a mistake. I have been repeating a version of this fruit-picking picture, and I want it audited. Please work, in continuous prose, through an explicit mapping from the analogy to science: what corresponds to the fruit, the tree, the ripening process, the orchard, the act of picking, and the fact that one picker gets there first. Then set out the disanalogies that matter, including anything the ripe-fruit image quietly imports that scientific inquiry lacks, and anything about inquiry that the image leaves out. Along the way, it would help to ask whether the cases we call simultaneous are always even the same fruit, and whether picking is really the right word for what happens when a result becomes formulable. Finally, say how much of the original conclusion survives: does the analogy establish that such discoveries were inevitable and that priority is intellectually and morally arbitrary, or does it fail exactly where that conclusion needs support? If a better analogy would do the work more honestly, describe it with the same explicit mapping, and say whether it supports a different verdict about priority. I would like the whole reply as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
