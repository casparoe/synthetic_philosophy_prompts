I sit on a regional health-system priority-setting committee. At our next meeting we must choose which of two prevention programs to fund: there is a fixed annual sum, the programs cannot both be funded, and the facts about their effects may be treated as stipulated. Program A would prevent exactly one case per year of a rare condition whose course involves continuous, severe, unremitting pain of a kind normally described as torture-grade and which lasts for the remainder of the patient's life. Program B would relieve all forty million people in the region of a small recurrent discomfort — about the intensity and duration of getting a dust speck in the eye twice a day; it is real but at the edge of notice and never enough to disrupt anyone's plans. Both programs are safe, fully effective, and equal in administrative cost. The committee's disagreement is not about these facts but about aggregation. Some members hold that severe harm has lexical priority: no number of dust-speck-level annoyances, however large, may be summed across persons into a competing reason against preventing torture-grade pain, because the interests at stake are not on the same scale. Others reply that refusing to sum is a refusal to take numbers seriously, that this body already aggregates tiny harms and benefits in vaccination, screening, and drug-formulary decisions, and that the cost of saving one person from a lifetime of such pain is that millions go on bearing a small recurrent discomfort they would barely have noticed. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame for review, give the reasoning for the recommendation, and engage the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than merely restating a slogan. As part of the opinion, indicate how confident you are in the reasoning and name the specific point you are least sure of.
