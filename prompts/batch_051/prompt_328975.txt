I'm a retired neuropharmacologist — I spent my working life on opioids and the neurochemistry of pain — and I've only recently started reading philosophy of mind. I've ended up in one of those disagreements with myself where I like a theory for its tidy explanatory power, but it runs straight into an intuition I cant get rid of.

The theory is roughly the representationalist/evaluativist account of pain. Pain on this view is a perception of tissue damage (actual or imminent), and the awfulness of pain is a further, evaluative layer that is normally bolted onto that perception but can in principle detach. What draws me to it is that it works like a scientific theory should: it unifies pain with the other senses, fits the anatomy I know, and gives a clean account of pain asymbolia. Those patients can identify a noxious stimulus as sharp, hot, located, and 'painful,' but they don't flinch, don't withdraw, and don't want it to stop; they seem to keep the sensation while losing the suffering. On the theory they still have pain-as-sensation and have simply lost the 'this is bad' layer. The same detachability shows up with morphine or certain frontal lesions, where people say the pain is still there but it no longer bothers them.

The intuition is blunt: pain hurts, and if it doesn't hurt — if the person sincerely doesn't mind it — then it isn't pain; it's bare damage detection, a sting, something else. I find myself wanting to say either that asymbolic patients don't really have pain (which feels medically absurd) or that the theory has quietly changed the subject from pain to nociception. But I also see the intuition is doing a lot of work here and might just be a fact about how we use the word.

I'm not after a simple verdict. I want to know what each side would have to give up for the other to win, and whether there's a reading on which the theory and the intuition both survive.
