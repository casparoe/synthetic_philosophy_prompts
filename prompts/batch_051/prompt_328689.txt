I curate acquisitions for a mid-sized public arts organization: three or four times a year a committee of five or six of us chooses, from a docket of works by living artists, one or two pieces to purchase for a permanent collection intended to outlast us. The budget and wall space are small, so a bad purchase is costly both in money and in credibility, and the final decision has to go to a board that will only approve purchases accompanied by a documented rationale. But the committee members are not philosophers, and we meet for a single afternoon.

Hume's essay on the standard of taste has always seemed to me to be a decision procedure struggling to be born. I want you to write it out for me. The procedure should translate, without flattening them, the ideas of delicacy of taste, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, and the verdict of the true judge, and it should be usable in plain language by non-philosophers under time pressure. It has to handle what I find hardest: works the committee finds immediately moving but suspects are shallow; works that are difficult, ugly, or offensive but may be important; works from traditions none of us knows well; and artists without a long track record, for whom the test of time cannot be applied literally. It should also say what to do when our best-qualified members disagree, including cases where Hume would count the disagreement as blameless rather than as a failure of judgment on one side.

Give me the procedure as a sequence of questions or checks, with a one- or two-sentence rationale at each step. Then apply it to three cases: (1) a technically brilliant but sentimental painting that the visiting public plainly loves; (2) a formally austere and currently off-putting video work that the two members with the most relevant experience defend strongly; and (3) a competent but derivative work that would fill a gap in how well our collection represents a particular tradition. Make sure at least one application gives a result my committee will not like. Be honest about where judgment cannot be eliminated, where the procedure will probably misfire, and what the procedure cannot tell us.
