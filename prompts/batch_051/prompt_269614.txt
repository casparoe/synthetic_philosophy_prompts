In my graduate seminar on scientific method, one student has taken to ending methodological disputes by invoking Paul Feyerabend's slogan "anything goes." Most recently, when I asked whether her evidence could discriminate between her hypothesis and an obvious rival, she replied that since anything goes, she was under no obligation to justify her particular approach. I want to give her a rigorous disambiguation of that slogan rather than another retort. What are the distinct precise claims that "anything goes" might be used to express? Present them as a menu, ordered from the least to the most radical, and for each say whether it is a platitude about the history of science, a substantive and defensible epistemological claim, or so strong that it becomes false or self-undermining. Then say which reading is most plausibly Feyerabend's own, and which reading, if any, would actually do the work the student needs it to do when she uses the slogan to deflect a specific methodological objection. The aim is not to decide on a single correct interpretation, but to display the space of possible precisifications and show what each would license.
