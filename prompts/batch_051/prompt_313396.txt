I'm in a moral philosophy course on promises and we keep circling the same puzzle: why does saying some words put you under an obligation you can't just opt out of later? We've done the usual moves — the convention story, the knowingly-raising-expectations story, the trust story — and I got into a long argument with my roommate about whether the real problem is just that promising has no teeth. So me and my roommate invented a system on the spot, and now I'm trying to figure out how it would actually behave, not just whether it sounds nice.

The proposal is a platform (we're calling it PromiseSure) where you can back any promise with escrow. The promissor writes the promise in their own words and then has to lay out, in advance, a condition under which the promise counts as broken and the money automatically transfers to the promisee. Disputes go to binding arbitration, with a default rule that any vagueness in the break-condition gets interpreted against the promisor, since their the one who drafted it. The platform also maintains a public completion score for every user. The pitch is that this finally gives promising real teeth: you make your promise credible by attaching money to it, and the other person can trust you without personally knowing you.

I'd like a proper mechanism-design take from someone who knows both incentive design and the philosophy of promising: think carefully, maybe start by brainstorming the actors and their incentives, and then walk me through how this thing would behave once real people start using it and gaming it. Some specific worries: people promising only the letter of what they'd otherwise have promised and quietly dropping the spirit of it; using deliberately narrow break-conditions to look credible while keeping loopholes; building up a high score on cheap promises and then cashing out on one big broken promise; rich people being able to make impressive collateralised promises while everyone else's promises look shakier by comparison; and the worry that putting a price on breach turns it into a fee you might willingly pay rather than a wrong. Am I missing the more dangerous failure modes? And could any of these be fixed without turning the whole thing into ordinary contract law?
