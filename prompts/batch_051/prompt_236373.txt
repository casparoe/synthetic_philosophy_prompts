I have just come away from a long argument with a friend who is a committed utilitarian and who spent the evening resisting, with surprising success, the suggestion that she is in fact a lexical-threshold negative utilitarian. The argument turned on a single question: whether pleasure and pain are commensurable in the way total utilitarianism assumes, or whether some pains stand outside the trade in the way that lexical priorities do.

To keep the discussion tractable, we confined ourselves to a rough common scale. Any experience is assigned an intensity between 0 and 100, where higher numbers on the pain side mean worse pain and higher numbers on the pleasure side mean better pleasure. A trade is then a choice in which a specified episode of pain is accepted in exchange for a specified episode of pleasure. We agreed to set aside side-effects, questions of distribution, alternatives, and the difference between one person and many; the point was simply to map her implicit exchange rates.

Her verdicts, in the order in which they arose, were as follows.

1. Pain at intensity 10 for 10 minutes, in exchange for pleasure at intensity 60 for 60 minutes: accepted without hesitation.

2. Pain at intensity 30 for 5 minutes, in exchange for pleasure at intensity 80 for 60 minutes: accepted, though she paused.

3. Pain at intensity 35 for 1 minute, in exchange for pleasure at intensity 90 for 120 minutes: accepted, but only after hesitating, and she said it felt near the limit.

4. Pain at intensity 35 for 1 minute, in exchange for pleasure at intensity 90 for 10,000 minutes: accepted, without much further comment.

5. Pain at intensity 45 for 1 minute, in exchange for pleasure at intensity 90 for 10,000 minutes: refused.

6. Pain at intensity 70 for 1 minute, in exchange for pleasure at intensity 100 for 100,000 minutes: refused.

Throughout, she denied that there is any intensity beyond which the two sides simply stop trading at any rate. But her denials sat very uneasily with her refusal of case 5, since case 5 offered exactly the same pleasure she had accepted in case 4, for a pain only marginally worse.

The new case I want you to adjudicate is this. Suppose she is asked to accept a pain at intensity 45 for one minute, and the pleasure on offer is effectively unbounded: take it to be the equivalent of ten thousand people each enjoying a full year of pleasure at intensity 50, with no other costs. Treat this purely as a quantity of pleasure, not as a question about motivating ten thousand lives.

Should I predict that she accepts or refuses, if she is to remain consistent with the verdicts above? Which earlier case is the closest analogue, and what is she committed to in each direction? In particular, could someone who accepted cases 1 to 4 and refused cases 5 and 6 coherently be running a merely steep, continuous disvalue curve for pain, with no genuine lexical threshold? Or does the pattern force a threshold somewhere between 35 and 45, as a lexical-threshold negative utilitarian would claim? If the present case does not settle the matter, specify the further case that would: a case in which the two readings predict different verdicts, and which therefore tells them apart.
