Lately I've been chewing on a puzzle that came out of an argument in my family. My brother-in-law bought a used car from a private seller, and my sister said he had walked into an information trap: the seller knew the car's history, and the very fact that he wanted to sell at that price was itself a bad sign. My brother-in-law replied that if that reasoning were right, no one could ever buy anything, since every voluntary sale would be a confession by the seller that the item wasn't worth the price. I've been trying to turn their exchange into a clean argument so I can see where the reasoning actually breaks, and I can't tell.

Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it.

(1) Suppose someone offers me an asset at price P, and suppose the seller is under no immediate pressure to sell, is at least as good a judge of the asset as I am, and has the same attitude toward risk that I do. If the seller judged the asset to be worth more than P, keeping it would be the better option, so the offer itself is evidence that the seller privately judges the asset to be worth less than P.

(2) Since the seller is no worse a judge than I am, I should lower my own estimate of the asset's value as soon as the offer is made, before learning anything else about the asset.

(3) The seller, knowing I will lower my estimate this way, should not expect me to accept. So if I nevertheless accept the seller's price, my acceptance is itself evidence that I have private information that the asset is worth more than P. That should make the seller raise his estimate and become reluctant to sell.

(4) Thus any proposed trade undermines itself: each side's action sends a signal that should make the other side less willing to go through with it. Therefore no trade should ever occur between two rational people who are simply trying not to be on the wrong side of the deal, even when each has private information the other lacks, and this should extend to things like the stock market, where the same reasoning would apply on a much larger scale.

(5) But trading happens all the time, including among people who are not obviously irrational. So either one of these steps contains a hidden mistake, or most trading is irrational.

I half-suspect the problem is in step (1), because real sellers often sell for reasons that have nothing to do with an asset's value: they need cash, or they are rebalancing, or their risk tolerance differs from mine. But when I explicitly set those reasons aside in step (1), the argument still seems to prove that mere differences of information can never make trade rational. Is the argument valid when restricted in that way? If not, which step fails, and what exactly is the mistake?

Please answer in American English.
