In the welfare systems of many developed countries, the treatment of different kinds of support is visibly asymmetric. Pensions and disability benefits are paid as of right: once a person qualifies, no one requires them to keep demonstrating that they are old or impaired, and no one cuts their pension for failing to try to work. Working-age unemployment benefits, by contrast, are surrounded by conditions: claimants must document job applications, attend work-focused interviews and training courses, accept suitable offers, and sometimes complete mandatory work placements; missing an appointment or declining a job can trigger a sanction that stops payments for weeks. Benefit fraud is pursued with unusual moral energy—the benefit cheat is one of the most recognizable villains in the politics of welfare—while tax evasion by businesses and the well-off, which usually costs the public far more, tends to be handled as a regulatory matter and often settled without prosecution. At the same time, the state pays out large sums in tax credits and wage subsidies to low-paid workers, apparently on the principle that trying, even when the job still leaves you poor, is what matters.

I am trying to work out what theory of deservingness, responsibility, or the causes of poverty this pattern presupposes. Does it presuppose that need alone is never a sufficient claim for the able-bodied; that continued support must be earned by demonstrated effort; that noncompliance reflects choice rather than circumstance; that benefit fraud is a graver wrong than tax evasion, or simply easier to punish? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, say whether its parts presuppose consistent things, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and indicate what would actually have to change if the system were rebuilt on a purely needs-based or unconditional view.

Please present the answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
