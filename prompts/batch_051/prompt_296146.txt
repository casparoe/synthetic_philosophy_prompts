Here's a pattern I keep seeing in arguments about the Nordic model, and I cant tell if its just motivated reasoning. People cite Denmark or Sweden as proof that a big universal welfare state doesn't have to wreck the economy—look, high taxes and still rich, low corruption, high trust. Then the moment someone says "great, so lets copy it," the same person says no, it can't be exported: it only works because of high social trust, or a small homogeneous population, or a cultural norm like Janteloven that stops people from resenting the taxes. So the model is being used as evidence for the viability of the policies and as evidence that those very policies would fail anywhere else. That looks like a contradiction, or at least like the speaker is helping themselves to a hidden variable. The one halfway defense I can think of is that trust is a precondition rather than an output: the Nordic welfare state works because the trust was already there, and you can't import trust by importing the tax code. But if that's true, then citing the Nordics as proof that the welfare state "works" is basically useless for any low-trust country, since it doesn't identify the active ingredient. So: is there a principled distinction that lets someone use the Nordics both as a success story and as a non-exportable special case, or should one of those uses be dropped? Pick whichever side is weaker and see if it survives, or tell me whether the whole move should be retired.
