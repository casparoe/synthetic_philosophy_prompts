A course in the philosophy of mind is working with the following four-way classification of cognitive agents, often called the tower of generate-and-test.

- Darwinian creatures: the behavioral repertoire is generated and fixed by natural selection over generations. Variation arises through mutation and recombination, and the environment tests whole phenotypes across generations. The individual creature does not revise its behavioral repertoire within its own lifetime.
- Skinnerian creatures: in addition to the Darwinian base, individuals can revise their own behavior during their lifetime, but only through overt trial and error. The creature emits behavioral variants into the real environment, and the environment selects among them through reinforcement and punishment. The actual world is the only testing ground, and errors carry their real-world costs.
- Popperian creatures: in addition to the lower levels, individuals can test candidate actions before performing them. They have a private inner environment — a simulator, an imagination, or a representation of the world — in which candidate behaviors can be generated and eliminated without being enacted. At least some errors are discovered without the creature actually making them.
- Gregorian creatures: in addition to the lower levels, individuals can import mind tools from the cultural environment. Language, imitation, instruction, and external artifacts such as notation, diagrams, written plans, and algorithms allow the creature to borrow candidate behaviors and testing procedures from the experience of others, and to expose its own candidates to criticism before they meet the environment.

For an undergraduate tutorial on this scheme, produce a sorting instrument with the following structure.

1. First, lay out the logical space. State the independent yes/no diagnostic questions your instrument will use, list the combinations of answers that make sense given the definitions, and assign each of the four named kinds to one of those combinations. Note any combinations no named kind occupies, and briefly diagnose each: is it incoherent, empirically unattested, or merely outside the four-way scheme?

2. Then present the decision tree itself. Phrase every internal node as a question a student could answer from a behavioral description alone, without already knowing which named kind the answer points to. Do not use the labels "Darwinian," "Skinnerian," "Popperian," or "Gregorian" in any internal-node question. Ensure all four kinds appear as leaves, and add at least two further leaves for agents that fall below the Darwinian level or cannot be cleanly classified.

3. For each leaf, give a sentence or two on what sort of evidence would justify moving an agent at that leaf up to the next level of the tower.

4. Run the following cases through your tree and report the result: (i) a bacterium swimming up a chemical gradient; (ii) an Aplysia sea slug showing habituation of its gill-withdrawal reflex; (iii) a rat learning to press a lever for food; (iv) a New Caledonian crow bending a wire into a hook to retrieve food; (v) an adult human writing a pros-and-cons list before a major decision; (vi) a current large language model. Where a case breaks one of your questions rather than cleanly answering it, say exactly which node breaks and why.

5. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate on behavioral evidence alone, and state what further information would be needed to settle the boundary.
