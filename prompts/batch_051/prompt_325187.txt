I'm a back-end dev with no real philosophy background, so excuse me if this is a tired question. Lately I can't stop thinking about whether being excellent at something is good even if nobody ever benefits from it. I've been collecting my gut verdicts on made-up cases, and I want to know if there's a single principle behind them.

(1) A friend spent six months writing a emulator for a long-dead computer, purely for the challenge. Nobody uses it, the repo never goes public, but the code is elegant and he says he's a better programmer now. I think that's genuinely good. (2) A painter produces something that everyone whose seen it in progress called a masterpiece, then destroys it the same day because she only wanted to prove she could. I also call that good, though it makes me a little sad. (3) A runner with world-class potential trains alone for a decade, hits what would be a national record, times it on his own watch, then quits without telling anyone. Here I keep flip-flopping: part of me admires it, part of me thinks an unseen record isn't really a record. (4) A teammate wrote a beautiful little module for a feature that got cut; it was never merged, never run outside a test. This feels like waste to me, not good the way the emulator was, even though everything I just said about the emulator seems to apply. (5) Someone is born with a freak memory but leads a life where they never once need to remember anything hard. I don't think the unused talent is good — it feels neutral, maybe a pity. (6) A duffer at chess slowly improves for years, never gets past club level, but plays a handful of games he alone sees as beautiful. I think that's good.

My half-baked theory is something like: excellence is good when it's actually exercised and makes the person more than they were, but not when it only produces an object nobody uses. But then the burned painting breaks my own rule, since no object survives and no third person is made better — unless the painter herself counts as the beneficiary, which feels like cheating. Is there a cleaner principle that captures most or all of my verdicts, and which ones does it force me to drop? And what should I say about the case I keep chewing on: a mathematician proves something genuinely new, writes it down, and dies before anyone reads it; the proof is found a week later. Is it good the week before it's found, or only after someone reads it?
