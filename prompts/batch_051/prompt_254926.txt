So my wife and I just finished the home study to adopt, and it hit me how completely backwards the screening logic is: months of interviews, background checks, financial disclosures, references, parenting classes, a social worker poking through our house — but nobody asked me any of that before I had my biological kid. I basically signed a form at the hospital and walked out with a human being. The state will fine me if I drive without a license, but there's no license to be a parent, and you only get vetted if you're taking on some other person's child. I can't figure out what picture of parenthood this asymmetry rests on. Does the law presuppose that biological parents are competent by default, or that they have a kind of authority over their own children that the state isn't allowed to condition, or that a child's interests are protected well enough by pediatricians and teachers and relatives who see them? And then why does the whole system flip to deep suspicion the moment a child moves between adults — doesn't that commit it to the weird view that the biological tie itself is doing all the moral work, since that's the only variable that changed? I'd like to know what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it's merely compatible with, whether the two halves (no screening for birth parents, heavy screening for adoptive ones) even presuppose consistent things, and what would have to change if you instead started from the idea that a child is a separate person whose future options shouldn't be closed off by the luck of who birthed them. Sorry if this is rambly — the home study people were very nice, but the whole process left me with more philosophy than I planned on.
