Two kinds of ecological intervention now sit side by side in the same institutions and public debates. The first is precautionary: regulators demand that genetically modified organisms be containable and their release reversible, species are rarely moved across landscapes, and restoration proceeds by slow degrees. The second is engineered to be irreversible: serious proposals call for releasing gene drives — genetic elements designed to bias inheritance so that a trait sweeps through an entire wild population and maintains itself there — and well-funded projects aim to resurrect extinct species or install engineered proxies, such as mammoth-like elephants, into habitats that have not seen them for millennia, all under the heading of ecosystem restoration. What philosophy of nature, value, and responsibility do the gene-drive and de-extinction programs presuppose, given how abruptly they depart from the precautionary norm — and is that departure an inconsistency, or can a single underlying theory license both? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can: address whether these programs commit their proponents to treating ecosystems as steerable machines, to valuing species or ecosystems while discounting the welfare of individual animals, or to treating present ecological states as fungible with some preferred future state. Then mark what is strictly presupposed versus what is merely compatible, identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and say how the programs would change if they were rederived from a radically precautionary view, or from a view that takes the welfare of individual wild animals as the primary unit of value.
