His line is that if somebody built a working hypercomputer—say, a machine that could decide the halting problem or compute a non-Turing-computable function—that would just falsify the thesis, full stop. I keep feeling like that's to fast, but I have trouble saying exactly why. I want you to make the best possible case for the opposite view: that the standard Church-Turing thesis (the one about the informal notion of an effectively calculable function coinciding with Turing computability) is not the sort of thing that any single physical device could refute. Steelman it properly: engage with the obvious objection that a hypercomputer looks like a textbook counterexample, and explain why someone could still resist that conclusion. Along the way, it would help if you separated the Church-Turing thesis itself from the physical Church-Turing thesis, since I suspect our argument is partly just us running those together.
