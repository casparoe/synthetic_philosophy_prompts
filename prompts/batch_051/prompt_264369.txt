In my senior seminar on literature and philosophy we're reading Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas," and I want the next session to read it against David Pearce's hedonistic imperative rather than the usual utilitarian arithmetic. Pearce's program holds that suffering is a contingent, evolved feature of the nervous system, not a precondition of meaning or moral seriousness, and that the right long-term project is to use neurotechnology and genetic engineering to phase out the biological substrates of involuntary suffering — replacing pain and anxiety with functionally informative gradients of well-being, not flat contentment — while leaving choice and sensitivity intact. I'd like a critical reading of the story through that lens, with particular attention to the cellar, the children's initiation into the secret, and the walkers' departure. Does Omelas dramatize the position's deepest obstacle — that a flourishing society can be structured so that it seems to require someone's misery, so that the problem is not one citizen's suffering but the suffering the structure itself demands — or does it instead exhibit the very assumption Pearce says we should abandon, a kind of conservation of suffering in which joy must be paid for in pain? Where does the story genuinely resist the hedonistic-imperative reading, and where would an honest critic have to say so rather than force it? And do the ones who walk away model the right response to the child, or do they model the abolitionist's failure to treat the city as the thing that must be re-engineered, so that no one has to occupy the cellar at all? End with what the story adds to the philosophical debate that the program, as a set of arguments, doesn't.
