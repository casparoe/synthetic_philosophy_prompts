Was arguing with a friend on Discord about counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail, and we ended up constructing a hybrid I don't remember seeing written up anywhere, and now I'm half-convinced it dissolves the usual dispute, but I keep going back and forth. In the standard counterfactual mugging, Omega flips a fair coin; on tails it asks you for $100, and on heads it pays you $10,000 if it predicted you would have paid on tails. Now suppose Omega instead flips two fair coins, A and B, and the whole transaction is gated by the XOR of the two results. You are told the rule in advance: if the coins come up the same—both heads or both tails—nothing happens, no request, no payout. If they come up different, then for whichever coin came up tails Omega asks you for $100, and for the coin that came up heads Omega pays you $10,000 if it predicted you would pay in exactly that situation, meaning the situation where the coins came up different and the tails side asked. So the actual request and the actual reward both happen in the same world, just on different coins.

Here's the part that's bugging me. In the ordinary counterfactual mugging, when you're actually asked to pay, the heads branch is purely counterfactual: it didn't happen, so paying feels like it's only buying you membership in a class of agents Omega would have rewarded in a world you don't get to experience. But in this XOR-gated version, when Omega walks in and asks for $100, you know the coins are different, which means the other coin is heads. So there is an actual, already-run branch of this same world where Omega is or is not handing you $10,000, conditional on a prediction about what you'll do now. That makes it feel much closer to Newcomb's problem, or to an all-upside version of counterfactual mugging, but I'm not sure the causal structure is actually any different, because Omega's prediction was still fixed before the coins were flipped. What I'd like worked through: what do causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, and updateless reasoning each say you should do when actually asked to pay in this XOR-gated setup? Do they now agree, or does the old disagreement survive? If they disagree, what exactly is doing the work—the fact that the other branch is actual, the fact that you can infer it exists when you get the request, or something else? And if you were allowed to precommit before the coins were flipped, would the precommitment strategy come apart from the in-the-moment strategy in this version, and does that tell us anything about whether the standard puzzle's mixed-upside feature was the load-bearing weirdness all along?
