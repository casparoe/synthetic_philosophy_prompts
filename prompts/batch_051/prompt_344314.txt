I find myself defending an instrumentalist view of novelty: variety and adventure matter because they deliver more and richer pleasant states, stave off hedonic adaptation, and give a life shape, but once two lives are equal in those respects, the more repetitive of them is not thereby any worse. Yet I also have a firm intuition that a life consisting of a single satisfying day repeated indefinitely, with no loss of enjoyment, would still be worse than an otherwise equal life that contains genuine novelty, and that the difference is not exhausted by what the novelty does to the person's later pleasures or memories. I want a negotiated verdict, not a bullet-biting contest. Give me the strongest case for treating that intuition as debunkable, the strongest case for treating novelty as a non-instrumental component of well-being, and say which should give way if either must; if there is a reading on which both survive, say exactly what distinction does the work.
