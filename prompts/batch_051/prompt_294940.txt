I'm a teaching assistant marking midterm essays for an undergraduate course in nineteenth-century social and political philosophy. The course has spent several weeks on Marx, with assigned selections from the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844 and Capital, Volume I. I set the essay question below, and a student submitted the answer that follows. I would like a careful second opinion on how to grade it, since I want to ensure that my marks reflect the specific claims in the answer rather than an overall impression.

Question: Marx distinguishes between the worker's exploitation under capitalism and the worker's alienation. Drawing on the 1844 manuscripts and the first volume of Capital, explain each concept, and then assess whether, for Marx, exploitation is sufficient for alienation. Be precise about the mechanisms by which each condition arises.

Student's answer: Marx's concept of exploitation is developed in Capital. The worker sells his labor power to the capitalist for a wage corresponding to its value, which is the cost of reproducing the worker's subsistence. But labor power is a peculiar commodity: it can produce more value than it costs. During the working day, the worker creates value beyond the value of his labor power, and this surplus value is appropriated by the capitalist without equivalent. Exploitation, for Marx, consists in this extraction of surplus value; the rate of exploitation is the ratio of surplus value to the value paid for labor power, i.e., surplus labor time over necessary labor time.

Alienation, by contrast, is worked out chiefly in the 1844 manuscripts. There Marx treats alienated labor as having four dimensions: the worker is alienated from the product of his labor, which confronts him as an alien object; from the activity of labor itself, which is coerced rather than free self-activity; from his species-being, that is, his capacity for free conscious production; and from other human beings. Under capitalism, labor belongs not to the worker but to the capitalist, and the worker's own activity and product stand over against him as alien powers.

On the relation between the two, exploitation is sufficient for alienation. The mechanism is the wage. Because the worker is paid less than the value he produces, he lacks the means to appropriate the product of his own labor and cannot engage in self-directed productive activity; his capacities are exercised for the capitalist's purposes and under the capitalist's control. The gap between what the worker creates and what he receives back explains all four dimensions of alienation: the product becomes alien because it is produced for another, the activity becomes alien because it is not self-directed, and estrangement from species-being and from others follows from the same deprivation. If wages were raised substantially, this gap would narrow, and alienation would correspondingly diminish.
