A line of thought I keep meeting in discussions of mental health and well-being runs roughly as follows. Negative emotions such as sadness and grief are like physical pain: unpleasant, but informative and protective. Just as a body that felt no pain would be in constant danger—unable to learn from injury or to avoid what harms it—a mind that felt no sadness or grief would be in constant danger of drifting into attachments and situations that are bad for it, or of failing to register losses that matter. The analogy is then used to support the conclusion that a life without negative emotions would not be better, even if it felt more pleasant at every moment, and that projects of eliminating or routinely numbing sadness and grief are therefore self-defeating.

I want an audit of how far this analogy holds. Begin by setting out the intended mapping explicitly: what in the emotional case is supposed to correspond to the pain receptor, the noxious stimulus, the tissue damage, the felt unpleasantness, the protective reflex, the healing process, and the body's overall homeostasis. Then identify the points where the mapping strains or breaks. In particular, address these questions. Physical pain is usually a reliable signal of current or imminent bodily harm, and its intensity roughly tracks the seriousness of the harm; do sadness and grief track anything analogous, and is their intensity proportional to the harm in the same way? Physical pain typically ends when the stimulus is removed or the tissue heals; grief and sadness often lack that neat terminus, so what does the analogy imply about how long they ought to last? A body without pain is impaired in fairly specific, specifiable ways; state what the corresponding claim about a mind without sadness or grief would have to be, and test whether the analogy supports that claim or merely assumes it. Consider also cases in which the pain analogy appears to pull in the opposite direction: pain can become chronic and pathological, and we do not normally think that chronic pain is thereby justified or that a life containing it is better for that reason; under what conditions does the analogy push towards treating sadness or grief as something to extinguish rather than to preserve? Throughout, distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are merely superficial.

Finally, say whether the conclusion—that a life without negative emotions would not be better—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether it needs an independent argument about the value of sadness and grief themselves rather than borrowing its force from the pain analogy. If there is a better analogy that does the work more honestly, propose it and say where it, too, would break.
