I want to slow down the debate between ensemble-average and time-average approaches to risky choice and get the specification written first: what, exactly, must an adequate theory of choice over repeated multiplicative gambles be able to do? The purpose is prescriptive guidance for a single agent deciding how much of their wealth to stake in gambles whose payoffs multiply wealth — not fair pricing, and not a criterion for evaluating populations. Write the desiderata, not a survey.

Set up the problem around this case. A fair coin doubles your wealth on heads and reduces it by 60% on tails; the player stakes the entire bankroll each toss. The expected multiplier per toss is 1.2, so the arithmetic mean across a large ensemble of parallel players grows without bound. But the time-average growth factor for a single player repeating the bet is the geometric mean, sqrt(2 × 0.4) ≈ 0.894, which is below one; as the number of tosses grows, a single player's wealth converges to zero with probability one. The general point: for a multiplicative process, long-run growth along one path is governed by the expectation of the logarithm of the growth factor, while the ensemble mean is governed by the logarithm of the expectation, and the concavity of the logarithm is what lets the two come apart.

Now give me the list of requirements an adequate theory must meet, with the case or consideration behind each, and mark each as non-negotiable or as a commitment of one camp. Check and refine at least these candidates, and add anything I have missed.

- It must not collapse the time average into the ensemble average, and it must not let expected wealth per period be the criterion when the two disagree.
- It must get the one-shot/repeated distinction right: a gamble can be acceptable once and ruinous when repeated at full bankroll, and the theory has to say what repetition changes.
- It must respect the absorbing boundary: wealth cannot go negative, and a theory that licenses strategies whose probability of ruin tends to one over a long horizon needs to explain why that is acceptable or else reject them.
- It must be consistent with the mathematics of multiplicative processes: the long-run growth rate is the expected log growth factor; the concavity of the logarithm creates the gap to the ensemble mean; and, under standard conditions, the stake that maximizes expected log wealth is the growth-optimal stake, the Kelly rule. The theory has to locate these facts correctly rather than treat log utility as one arbitrary utility function among others.
- It must say which standard of success it is using for an individual over time — almost surely, in expectation, or something else — and justify that choice rather than switching between them.
- It must preserve the distinction between what happens to an average over parallel worlds and what happens along one world, and say which one individual decision theory is answerable to.
- It must say what happens at finite horizons and with non-stationary or correlated payoffs, where the infinite-horizon growth-rate result may be only an idealization.
