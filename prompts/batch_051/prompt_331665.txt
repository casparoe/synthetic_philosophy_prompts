I am a PhD student in ancient philosophy. In the modern revival of Stoicism, a recurring image is used to reconcile Epictetus's dichotomy of control with wholehearted pursuit of goals. The Stoic agent is an archer: their task is to draw, aim, and loose the arrow as skillfully as possible; whether the arrow strikes the target depends on the wind and other externals; so the archer does everything within their power to hit while treating the actual strike as a preferred indifferent rather than as the source of the action's value. The analogy is invoked to support a fairly strong outcome-independence thesis: once one has aimed well, hitting or missing cannot itself touch one's flourishing.

I would like you to audit the analogy, not merely explain it. Give the intended correspondence explicitly (bow, aim, release, arrow-flight, wind, target). Finally, say whether the outcome-independence thesis survives once these disanalogies are on the table, and whether some other image, or a modified archer, would carry it better.
