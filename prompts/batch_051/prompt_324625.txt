(1) In a metric space, the epsilon-delta definition of continuity is the one we start with: a function f is continuous if, for every point x and every epsilon greater than zero, there is a delta greater than zero such that f maps the delta-ball around x into the epsilon-ball around f(x).

(2) The open-set definition says f is continuous if the preimage of every open set is open.

(3) In a metric space, the open sets are exactly the unions of epsilon-balls. So the open-set definition just says: the preimage of any union of epsilon-balls in the codomain is a union of epsilon-balls in the domain.

(4) But that condition is equivalent to the epsilon-delta definition; it's the same requirement, just rephrased in terms of preimages.

(5) So on metric spaces the open-set definition doesn't add anything over epsilon-delta.

(6) But on a general topological space there are no epsilon-balls, so there's no epsilon-delta definition to generalize from. That means calling the open-set condition 'the right generalization' of continuity is really just a stipulation—we've defined continuity to preserve whatever structure we arbitrarily put into the topology.

(7) So the open-set definition is either redundant (on metric spaces) or arbitrary (on general topological spaces). Either way, it's not the 'right' notion of continuity.
