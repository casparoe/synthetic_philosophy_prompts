I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I'm planning a long essay on an argument that has been running, in shifting vocabularies, for over two thousand years: the dispute between the Mohist doctrine of impartial care and the Confucian doctrine of graded love. I want the essay to be a diagnosis of the debate's structure rather than a verdict for one side, and I'd like you to help me work that diagnosis out.

Here is how I understand the two positions. The Mohists argued that partiality — caring more for one's own parents, family, and state than for others' — is the engine of conflict, because people favor their own and thereby neglect or harm the rest. Their remedy was jian'ai, usually rendered impartial or universal care: we should care for others' kin and states as we care for our own, and they held that this is both psychologically attainable and, in the long run, mutually beneficial. The Confucian answer, associated especially with Mencius, was that care properly begins in the family and is cultivated through graded relationships, extending outward from there; a duty of equal care to all would, on this view, be either psychologically impossible, destructive of the special relationships that make moral development possible, or both. Mencius's famous charge against Mozi is, on the usual paraphrase, that impartial care amounts to denying the special place of one's father.

The same fault line reappears in contemporary moral philosophy as the standoff between impartialists and partialists. The familiar moves have been made many times: partialists appeal to the apparent requirement to save one's own child before a stranger's; impartialists reply that such partiality is a pragmatic concession or a useful heuristic rather than a deep normative truth; partialists respond that this is smuggling in a view of agency from nowhere; and so on. Contemporary moral psychology has added data about how favoritism works without, it seems, settling the normative question. No one moves.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of why this debate has stalled, and what kind of disagreement it really is. In particular: is it a substantive disagreement about one question, a verbal disagreement about the word "care" or "love," or two groups answering different questions under a single heading? Do the sides differ over descriptive-psychological facts (whether impartial care is sustainable, whether graded love in fact produces better outcomes), over normative standards for what would make a distribution of care correct, over starting intuitions neither side can argue for, or over the concept of care itself?

I have two hypotheses I'd like you to stress-test, not simply confirm. The first is that the two sides are answering different questions: the Mohist/impartialist is asking what a good world looks like from nowhere, while the Confucian/partialist is asking what a good human life looks like from inside a network of relationships. The second is that the dispute is kept alive by an unnoticed slide among three senses of care: care as an occurrent feeling, care as a standing policy of attention and action, and care as a claim on limited resources.
