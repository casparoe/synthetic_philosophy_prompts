I am a novelist working on a manuscript in which a psychologist studies several people whose temperaments do not behave the way traits are ordinarily supposed to, and I have hit a conceptual tangle that I suspect professional philosophers have already sorted out. Let me describe the three configurations that keep recurring in my notes, as concretely as I can.

First, a woman with a genuine temper, the sort of person of whom we would ordinarily say that she would explode under real provocation; yet whenever such a provocation actually occurs, the provocation itself sets off a wave of self-consciousness that snuffs the anger out before it can cohere. The anger fails to occur at the very moment it should, and not because she suppresses it; the disposition seems to be extinguished by its own trigger. Second, a man whose temper is intact and remains intact through a confrontation, but a medication already in his blood blocks the physiological route by which the provocation would ordinarily express itself. The anger is there; it never shows. Third, a child with no trace of courage in ordinary life, who would flee ordinary threats, but when genuine danger arrives the danger itself acts on her as a stimulant and produces a single convincing episode of bravery that disappears the moment the danger recedes.

I am not asking only whether these configurations have names. I want to know what the distinctions commit one to, and whether the three cases really differ in kind. In the first case, is the trait correctly ascribed to the woman at all, when it cannot survive the circumstances of its own exercise? In the second, does it matter that the blocking factor is inside the man's body rather than outside it, and does the trait remain fully present while blocked? In the third, can any trait be credited to the child, or is the brave act produced by something other than courage? I am less interested in a bare label than in the carving: where the line falls between a trait that is absent, a trait that is present but prevented from showing itself, and a trait that seems to be created by the very test meant to reveal it.

You are a professional philosopher specializing in dispositions, powers, and the metaphysics of character traits. Please begin by brainstorming the relevant distinctions and the clearest, simplest examples you would use to draw them. Then work through my three cases one by one, taking care at each step: say what the best-fitting concept is, where the case resists the concept, and whether any of the clean distinctions drawn for simple objects fray when they are applied to persons.
