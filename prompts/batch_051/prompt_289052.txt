Question: In animal communication, what problem are costly displays supposed to solve, and what does the handicap principle actually claim? Use a male bird's ornament to explain when a costly signal should be expected to stay honest.

Student answer: When senders and receivers have the same interests, signals can be honest for free, because there's no benefit to lying. But in mate choice, a low-quality male can benefit by mimicking a high-quality male, so if signals are cheap they can get exaggerated until receivers stop trusting them. The handicap principle, proposed by Zahavi, says that signals can remain honest if they are expensive to produce. A big ornament is reliable because growing and maintaining it costs energy and attracts predators, so it acts as a kind of waste that only a high-quality male can afford. As long as a display is costly enough, then, it can't be faked; the honesty comes from the cost itself. That's also why females should prefer ornaments that get as big and expensive as possible: the more costly the signal, the more reliable it is. One caveat is that the cost has to be real; if a display were only symbolically costly, the handicap logic wouldn't work.
