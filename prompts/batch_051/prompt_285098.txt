I'm a PhD student in political theory, and after a departmental seminar on rational choice models I listened to two colleagues argue about the paradox of voting — specifically, about whether a single citizen's decision to vote can be called rational given how unlikely any one vote is to decide an election. I took notes and can reconstruct the exchange fairly accurately, but I genuinely could not tell who had the better of it, and I'd like a careful, point-by-point adjudication rather than a general impression.

The first said the paradox exposes a real tension in the standard view that voting is a rational civic act: an individual's vote matters to the outcome only if that vote is pivotal, the chance of that in a large election is vanishingly small, so the expected instrumental value of voting is effectively zero and is outweighed by the cost of getting to the polls — yet millions still vote. The second replied that this assumes only private benefits count. If the difference between the candidates' policies would affect millions of people, then a voter who cares even modestly about the welfare of others holds a kind of lottery ticket with a tiny chance of producing an enormous public good; a small probability times an enormous benefit can exceed the cost, so voting can be instrumentally rational after all. The first answered that this "altruistic pivot" story, whatever its arithmetic, misdescribes actual voters: most people have not estimated a small probability times a large aggregate benefit, and if you told them their chance of being decisive were literally zero they would still trudge to the polls; this suggests their motivation is not the chance of affecting the outcome at all. The second said that is still compatible with rationality once one admits expressive value: a vote can function like applause or a slogan, worth doing even when it does not change the result, so the paradox rests on the false assumption that all value must run through the outcome. The first countered that if any action can be made "rational" by postulating a taste for the act itself, then the concept of rationality has stopped doing any explanatory or normative work, and the paradox has been defined away rather than solved.

I would like you to go through the exchange move by move, rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating; then say who is right about which specific point and what, if anything, both parties are missing. As you do so, please separate what is reasonably settled among people who work on this topic from what remains genuinely contested, and avoid presenting a contested interpretation as if it were the consensus.
