I help run a political-theory workshop for undergrads — about sixteen people, one three-hour evening slot, mixed backgrounds but no one with military experience. The session is on the ethics of conscription and armed neutrality, and I want the group to run into the problem themselves instead of being lectured at. Design a structured role-play where they act as a defense-review commission in a small, wealthy, neutral-ish country — I'm imagining something between Switzerland's militia system, Sweden's total-defence doctrine, and Finland's post-Cold War conscription, but I won't name those cases in the room until the debrief. They have to choose among three packages: a professional volunteer force (politically easy and liberty-respecting, but maybe not credible for territorial defense and skewed by class); full conscription with a civilian-service alternative (egalitarian and credible, but coercive and expensive); and a decentralized total-defense model that treats the whole society as a deterrent (reserves, shelters, cyber resilience — armed neutrality in the strong sense, but possibly free-riding on neighbors and fragile in a crisis). Give me the roles (maybe a career officer, a libertarian, a pacifist, a finance minister, an aging reservist), a budget and manpower table, the voting rule, a timeline for the three hours, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the ideas underneath: liberal neutrality, civic-republican equality, deterrence versus provocation, and what neutrality actually commits a country to. I want them to come away feeling why a liberal democracy might nonetheless choose conscription, and why neutrality is a policy rather than just a legal status.

Also tell me what predictably goes wrong — I've piloted a shorter version and it kept sliding into a seperate debate about whether the draft is slavery, and everyone treated neutrality as pacifism until someone brought up Ukraine. Be honest about what the role-play can't reproduce: actual fear, actual risk of death, multi-decade institutional commitments, and the path-dependence of the real Swiss, Swedish, and Finnish systems, which I'll have to supply myself in the debrief.
