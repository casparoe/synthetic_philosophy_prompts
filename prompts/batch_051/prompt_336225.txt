I am a novelist, and for the past year I have been serving as the lay member of a small statutory panel that reviews claims against the state and advises the minister on whether sovereign immunity should be maintained, qualified, or waived. A case now before us has unsettled me, and I have been asked to prepare the panel's decision memorandum. Two winters ago the state river authority, acting under its emergency flood powers, opened a relief channel to spare a larger town from an approaching storm surge. The diverted water inundated a downstream settlement of some four hundred households. A subsequent technical inquiry found that the authority relied on a hydraulic model that its own engineers knew was unreliable at precisely the discharges the storm produced, and that the decision passed through so many offices—modeling, operations, legal, executive—that no single official could be shown to have been culpably negligent. The residents no longer seek compensation alone; they are seeking a formal finding that the authority, and through it the state, wronged them. Counsel for the state has invoked sovereign immunity, arguing that a state as such cannot be a wrongdoer, that only natural persons can act wrongly, and that the natural persons involved were each doing what the institution legitimately asked of them under conditions of urgency and incomplete information. As a novelist, I keep looking for the character to blame, and I have come to suspect that this search for a single guilty hand is itself what the old doctrine trains us to do.

My colleagues have asked me to draft the memorandum we will circulate before the panel votes next Thursday. It must arrive at a clear recommendation: either affirm the immunity and decline to recommend a finding of wrongdoing, or advise the minister to waive it for this class of case and accept a formal finding of state wrongdoing, with an apology and a compensation scheme as consequences. The panel is genuinely divided, and I want my own leanings tested rather than assumed. I am inclined to think a state can do wrong in a way that is not simply the summed wrongs of its officials—through the ends it sets, the reasons it institutionalizes, and the way it disperses knowledge so that no one ever holds the whole decision. But I am not sure whether that conviction survives the strongest version of the other side. Please write the decision memo itself: it should give the panel a practical recommendation with reasons, engage fairly with the strongest argument for the position it rejects, and make clear whether what is really at stake is a procedural question about who may be sued and how, or the substantive question whether a corporate entity can be the subject of wrongdoing at all.
