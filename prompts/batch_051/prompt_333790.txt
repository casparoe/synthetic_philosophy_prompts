Question 3 (20 points). In Lewis Carroll's dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (Mind, 1895), A is "Things that are equal to the same are equal to each other," B is "The two sides of this Triangle are things that are equal to the same," C is "If A and B are true, Z must be true," and Z is "The two sides of this Triangle are equal to each other." Consider this passage:

"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"

"You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."

"Very good. I'm quite willing to grant it, as soon as you've written it down. We will call it

(D) If A and B and C are true, then Z must be true.

Have you entered that in your note-book?"

"I have!" Achilles joyfully exclaimed, as he ran the pencil into its sheath. "And at last we've got to the end of this ideal race-course! Now that you accept A and B and C and D, of course you accept Z."

"Do I?" said the Tortoise innocently. "Let's make that quite clear. I accept A and B and C and D. Suppose I still refused to accept Z?"

Explain why the Tortoise's maneuver can be repeated indefinitely. Then answer this: does the regress show that Z is not a logical consequence of A and B, or that Achilles has failed to give the Tortoise a reason to accept Z? Finally, contrast this regress with an epistemic regress of justification for a belief, and state which of the two, if either, is vicious in a way that threatens the project it serves.

Student's answer:

Each time Achilles adds a conditional such as C or D, the Tortoise treats it as a further premise of the argument rather than as a rule licensing the move to Z. The next step then requires saying that if all the premises accepted so far are true, then Z must be true. The Tortoise can refuse to take that step, it becomes yet another premise, and so on without end.

The regress does not show that Z fails to follow logically from A and B. Z follows from A and B in the standard sense, since there is no valuation on which A and B are true and Z false; the trouble is that the Tortoise will not let the inference be made. So the passage does not reveal a gap in the validity of the original argument.

Nevertheless, I think the regress is vicious in an epistemic sense. It shows that no finite set of accepted truths can rationally compel any agent to accept a conclusion, because the rule that connects premises to conclusion can always be withheld and turned into a further premise. This is exactly the same structure as the epistemic regress of justification for a belief: each new conditional D, E, and so on is what justifies the previous step, and because the chain never ends, no step in the chain is ever justified. Thus the Tortoise shows that reasoning itself cannot be justified without circularity or dogmatism.
