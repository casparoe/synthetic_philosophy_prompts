I recently had a long argument with a friend about whether it does any real work to describe a pathogen, a climate shift, or a volcanic eruption as a historical actor, and I came away unable to say exactly where his central analogy failed. As he presented it, a human actor in history has three features: standing dispositions accumulated from prior circumstances, a trigger that activates them, and an action that forecloses some futures and opens others for everyone downstream. A volcano, he argued, has all three: an internal state built up over time, a triggering event, and an eruption that violently changes what remains possible for the human societies around it. A pathogen has a genome, a reservoir or host population, and transmission dynamics; a drought has slowly accumulating atmospheric or oceanic conditions, a threshold, and a cascade of crop failures. He granted the obvious disanalogies—no beliefs, no desires, no deliberation, no intentions, no responsibility—but insisted that these are exactly the disanalogies that do not matter, because his claim was about explanation rather than blame. For explanatory purposes, he concluded, the difference between a king ordering an invasion and a drought collapsing a state is one of degree, not kind: both are nodes that, once triggered, redistribute what is possible, and both can occupy the same slot in a narrative. He saw this as the only honest way to avoid a history that treats human choice as the sole motor of events; I suspected it quietly licenses environmental determinism, but I could not make the objection precise without simply insisting that humans are different.

I would like you to audit this analogy. Start with the explicit correspondence table: what in the nonhuman case is supposed to play the role of disposition, trigger, action, and consequence, and which terms from the human side—intention, deliberation, choice among perceived alternatives, responsiveness to reasons, answerability—have no counterpart at all. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for his explanatory conclusion from those that do not. In particular, does anything in the human-actor image smuggle in a unity, narrativity, or personhood that climate and disease lack, and does that smuggled content do the real work of making a volcano seem like an actor rather than a standing condition? Would a better analogy be something like a parameter, a constraint, a selective pressure, or a shock rather than an actor? Finally, say whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it.
