I’m a university student currently taking a philosophy of law course, and we’ve been discussing desuetude and the rule of law. In Justice Frankfurter’s opinion in *Poe v. Ullman*, 367 U.S. 497 (1961), discussing Connecticut’s long-unenforced contraception ban, he wrote: "Deeply embedded traditional ways of carrying out state policy ..." – or not carrying it out – "are often tougher and truer law than the dead words of the written text."

The passage has stayed with me because it suggests something like a legal realism about nonenforcement. I’ve been turning over a concrete proposal that would try to make that observation into a rule. Suppose a state adopted a standing provision that any criminal statute becomes void automatically when three conditions have been met continuously for, say, twenty-five years: the conduct it proscribes is *malum prohibitum* only; violation has been open and pervasive; and there has been a conspicuous policy of nonenforcement by the officials charged with enforcing it. Walk through how this rule would actually play out. Who bears the burden of proving the three conditions, and in what kind of proceeding would they be litigated? How would the rule change the strategic situation of a prosecutor who wants to revive an old statute for a single high-profile prosecution, or of a legislature facing the choice between re-enacting the statute and letting it die? Most of all, test whether this mechanism captures what Frankfurter calls “tougher and truer law,” or whether turning his observation into a formal test with conditions and a timetable would miss exactly the point he was making.
