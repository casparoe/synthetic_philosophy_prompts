My PhD is on the ethics of risk imposition, and I keep ending up in arguments with a friend in the economics department who thinks the consent requirement is the whole problem. His proposal: any project that would impose a nontrivial risk on identifiable people, say an added annual fatality risk of one in a million or more to anyone inside a mapped exposure zone, doesn't need those people's explicit permission in advance. Instead, every adult resident of the city lists a price on a public registry, in dollars per microdeath per year, for accepting such risk. A polluter, trucking company, nightclub owner, whoever, can go ahead as soon as they pay every exposed person their listed price for that year; the payment is recorded as legally equivalent to consent, and if the harm later materializes the operator is shielded from suit unless they misrepresented the risk or failed to keep their exposure model updated. Prices get relisted each year, and residents can change them whenever they want.

I want you to treat this as a mechanism rather than a moral position and walk me through how it actually behaves. What determines the prices people will list, and how much of that is real information about risk tolerance versus strategic hold-ups, lowballing by renters who won't be around long, people who misjudge the risk entirely, or people who list absurd prices as a veto rather than a valuation? How do firms respond: shaping projects to fall just below the threshold, drawing the exposure map as narrowly as they can, shifting risk onto children or passersby or future residents who have no registry entry, timing their emissions awkwardly? I especially want to know where the gaming shows up first and who ends up holding the risk.

Then tell me whether the failure modes are patchable. For instance, could you fix the hold-up problem with a default price, fix the information problem with mandatory risk literacy briefings or an ombudsman who audits the exposure models, fix the missing-persons problem by having trustees for the unlisted? Or do the patches turn it into a different mechanism altogether, one that just smuggles a regulator back in? Also say what happens once intermediaries enter, brokers buying up registry slots or employees being quietly expected to list prices as a condition of work. End with your honest read on whether it selects for less risk, better distributed risk, or merely for better paperwork. No headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, just continuous prose like an email.
