I run a reading group on nineteenth-century liberal thought, and one member has been pressing a particular reading of Mill's defense of individuality. On his account, Mill's central image is that a free society functions as a laboratory in which different people's lives are so many experiments in living: each unconventional choice is a trial of a hypothesis about how a human being might flourish, and the community is the audience that observes the results. The conclusion he extracts is that society ought to tolerate and even protect nonconforming lives, not from any liking for eccentrics but for roughly the reason we fund experimental science: without such trials we could never discover whether the received ways of organizing a life, a family, or a vocation are better than the alternatives. He pushes the comparison further, arguing that conformity is a kind of data suppression, and that objecting to a person running a risky experiment in living is like objecting to a chemist running a risky experiment in a fume hood.

I find the comparison appealing but suspect it is doing illicit work. A life is not deliberately designed as an experiment: the person living it is usually not testing a hypothesis, no one records the outcome, there is no control condition, and the cost of a failed trial falls entirely on the person who ran it rather than on a research budget. I would like an honest audit of the analogy. Specifically: lay out what maps onto what between the two domains; separate the disanalogies that are merely cosmetic from those that undermine the particular conclusion the analogy is meant to license; and say whether that conclusion — that a society has an epistemic interest in protecting self-regarding nonconformity — survives once the disanalogies are in view. If the laboratory picture cannot carry the weight, say what a better comparison would have to look like and whether there is one.
