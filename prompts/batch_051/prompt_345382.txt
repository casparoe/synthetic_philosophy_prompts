Two people in my AI-safety reading group got stuck on iterated amplification and distillation last week and none of us could referee it. The general picture, as I understand it: you have a human plus an AI work through some task the human can't do alone, with the human approving each sub-step as they go; then you distill that behavior into a new model; then you repeat, using the distilled model as the assistant in the next round. The claim is that if each local step is aligned and each distillation is faithful, the whole procedure stays aligned while getting more competent.

Leo said the competence side unravels first. Each distillation gives you an approximation of the amplified policy, and the small per-step errors don't stay small, because you plug that imperfect model in as the assistant on the next round and it's mistakes shape the options the human sees. So you're training each generation on subtly degraded behavior, and after enough rounds the bottom model is much worse than the thing you thought you were copying.

Mara said that's a complaint about sloppy engineering, not about the idea. You can measure the mismatch after each distillation and keep training until the student matches the teacher to whatever tolerance you want. Drift isn't an in-principle failure of decomposition, because local verification lets you correct it as you go.

Leo said local verification is exactly what he doubts. For lots of tasks the property you care about is a property of the whole tree of work, not of the leaves. You can sign off on twenty separate sub-answers and still be endorsing a plan whose overall effect you'd reject if you could see it all at once. So 'the human checks every step' doesn't get you 'the human endorses the whole'; it gets you a stack of approvals whose sum they might hate.

Mara replied that then you decompose the checking too: have the model summarize the intended overall effect of the plan, present the human with contrasts, and let them judge that. Leo said that just pushes the problem up a level, because the summary is itself a piece of local work, and a misaligned assistant can write a summary that makes a bad plan look fine. So you're assuming the thing you're trying to get. They went around on that point for an hour and then asked the rest of us to settle it. I honestly couldn't call it. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what, and what they're both missing?
