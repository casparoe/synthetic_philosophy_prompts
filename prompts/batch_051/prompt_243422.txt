As a journalist covering the science of evaluation and incentive design, I have found myself defending a strong reading of Goodhart's law. In "Problems of Monetary Management: The U.K. Experience" (1975), Charles Goodhart wrote: "Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes." I treat that sentence as the core of a general claim: when a measurable proxy is made the basis of a high-stakes control decision, the useful correlation between proxy and underlying target should be expected to degrade, not because the target changes but because agents adapt to the proxy.

I want a map of what would count against this position. Which observations would be decisive defeats for the strong reading, and which merely awkward? What would a committed defender of the strong Goodhart claim concede as genuine evidence against it, as opposed to explaining away? Are there stable conditions under which collapse is unlikely enough that the strong reading should be qualified? And has the view been insulated into unfalsifiability by treating every apparent counterexample as a different causal channel rather than as a failure of the generalization? I am less interested in a catalogue of cases than in knowing where the position actually risks being wrong. After the assessment, include a table with these columns: Challenge, Severity (decisive/merely awkward), Defender's honest concession, and Whether it qualifies or refutes the law.
