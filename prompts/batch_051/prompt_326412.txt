I keep going in circles on the moral luck debate, and I want someone to diagnose the argument itself rather than pick a side. The standard case is the two drunk drivers: one runs the red light and kills a kid, the other runs the same light under the same conditions and nothing happens. Almost everyone agrees the first gets treated much worse, legally and morally, even though the only thing that differed was whether a child happened to be in the crosswalk. Since Thomas Nagel and Bernard Williams put this on the table in the mid-seventies, the same moves keep getting made. The control-principle side says moral assessment can only attach to what was in the agent's control, so resultant luck should not change how blameworthy the two drivers are, full stop. The opposite side says that if you take the control principle seriously it ramifies: constitutive luck, circumstantial luck, causal luck—all of it is outside your control, so you'd end up unable to blame anyone for almost anything, and our actual practices of praise and blame are not like that. Then there are the deflationary people who say resultant luck only looks like a problem because we have better evidence about the first driver, or because the outcome changes what he is culpable for rather than how culpable he is. And then there are the revisionists and skeptics who say the problem generalizes and we should either go error-theoretic about moral responsibility or rebuild blame as a forward-looking practice.

My working theory is that the debate is stuck because the two sides mean different things by 'responsibility'—roughly, a desert property of an agent's will at the moment of choice versus a social status tied to the reactive attitudes and to the community's need to coordinate blame. That would make a lot of the back-and-forth a disguised case of two questions sharing one word. But maybe that's too tidy. Maybe the real disagreement is about which intuitions count as data: one side treats the symmetry between the two drivers as a fixed point that any theory must preserve, the other treats the asymmetry in our judgments as a known bias in how humans process outcomes, and there's empirical work that could bear on that. Or maybe there's a hidden formal question about whether degrees of blameworthiness can coherently track objective chance instead of the actual outcome.

So, diagnose the stall. Is this a verbal dispute, a substantive disagreement, two literatures answering different questions, or an ill-posed question with several harder questions packed inside it? And be specific about what would count as progress given the diagnosis—a distinction, an empirical result, a formal model, a change of question—or argue that no progress should be expected and why. I don't want a survey of the positions; I want the structure of the disagreement.

One more request: keep it honest about what's settled and what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. If you're giving your reading of Nagel or Williams, flag it as a reading; don't present a contested interpretive claim as if it were the consensus.
