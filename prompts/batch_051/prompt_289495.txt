I am a high school student writing my final paper for a comparative government and policy course, and I want to go beyond the familiar talking point that Portugal "decriminalized all drugs and it worked." My paper asks whether Portugal's 2001 reform is best understood as decriminalization rather than legalization, and whether the reform and its aftermath support the harm-reduction framework that motivated it or instead expose that framework's limits.

Here is the episode as I understand it. In July 2001, Portugal began applying a legal framework under which personal use, possession, and acquisition of small amounts of all drugs — roughly a ten-day supply — stopped being treated as criminal matters and instead became administrative cases referred to Commissions for the Dissuasion of Drug Addiction, while the production and large-scale trafficking of drugs remained criminal. The reform was part of a broader public-health strategy that also expanded treatment and harm-reduction services such as needle exchange and methadone, and political support for the change grew out of an HIV epidemic concentrated among people who inject drugs. Since the reform, drug-related deaths and HIV transmission among people who inject drugs have fallen markedly, and overall use did not rise sharply, though the causal role of the law itself is contested because the expansion of services happened at the same time.

I would like you to analyze this episode through the lens of the framework that motivates decriminalization: that drug use is best understood as a health and social problem rather than a moral failing, that the criminal law is a poorly targeted tool for deterring use, that the appropriate levers for users are treatment and social support rather than punishment, while the supply side should remain criminalized. I want a disciplined mapping: who or what the framework identifies as the relevant actors and levers; which features of the Portuguese case it explains, including the administrative commissions and their referral-to-treatment logic; and which features it misdescribes or quietly ignores, such as the fact that possession remained a prohibited administrative offense with real sanctions, and the difficulty of separating the effect of the law from the effect of simultaneously expanded services. Then say what the episode shows in the other direction: does Portugal confirm the framework, show that it is incomplete, or reveal that the decriminalization-versus-legalization distinction does less work than policy debates assume? Finally, because I need to know how much confidence to place in the verdict, tell me what would change your mind about the assessment and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
