I work as a software engineer, so I tend to think about individual actions in terms of their marginal effect on a system and to test intuitions against edge cases. That habit makes one ordinary asymmetry in how people talk about voting hard for me to understand.

Non-voters are widely treated as having failed a duty: they are told that if they did not vote they have no standing to complain, and abstention is spoken of as a failure of civic responsibility. But someone who casts a vote casually — because a candidate's manner appeals to them, because one advertisement happened to stick, or out of party habit with little idea of what the candidate would actually do — is usually described as exercising a right and doing their civic part, and is very rarely criticized as having failed anyone. By the marginal-effect standard I apply to systems, this seems backwards. The abstainer simply leaves the decision to others, whereas the arbitrary or misinformed vote enters the aggregate on equal terms with considered votes and can cancel one of them out; in the relevant sense it contributes noise to a decision that binds everyone. Why should contributing nothing be judged more harshly than contributing noise?

I want to know whether anything genuinely justifies this asymmetry, or whether it is just an inconsistency we live with. Please construct the strongest principle you can that would generate both judgments, test it against adjacent cases such as jury service, a shareholder or board vote, or the selection of a surgeon for a hospital, and then render a clear verdict: should the asymmetry be kept, reversed, or abandoned?
