Ok, here’s a case that’s been bugging me in my epistemology seminar. The standard criteria I give for epistemic akrasia are roughly: an agent believes P, while also believing that her own evidence doesn’t support P—or, more weakly, that she should suspend judgment on P—and the higher-order belief is not formed from some separate, privileged source but from the same body of evidence. So akrasia is like believing against what you take your evidence to say.

Now consider a well-calibrated forecaster who reads the literature on overconfidence among experts. She forms a first-order belief that tomorrow’s rain probability is 80%. She also forms a higher-order belief that, for forecasts of this type, her initial probabilities are systematically overconfident and should be regressed toward base rates. She accepts this as a general rule about her own reasoning. But then, when she acts on the forecast, she keeps using 80% and doesn't adjust. Is she epistemically akratic? She doesn't believe 'my evidence doesn't support 80%' exactly; she believes something more like 'my first-order probability is probably too high.' Does that count? What if the higher-order evidence is itself just a generic statistical fact about people like her, not evidence about the weather at all?

Go criteron by criterion. I want to know whether the case qualifies, whether condition (2) needs to be weakened from 'I should suspend' to 'my first-order attitude is likely miscalibrated', and whether that would make epistemic akrasia too easy or too hard. Does the case show that the standard account is really blending two different things—believing against your evidence and believing against your self-assessment?
