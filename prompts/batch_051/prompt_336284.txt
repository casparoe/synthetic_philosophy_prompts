I am a second-year law student writing a seminar paper on the void-for-vagueness doctrine, and I have worked myself into a corner. The doctrine, as I understand it from the cases, holds that a criminal statute is unconstitutional when it fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what conduct it prohibits, or when it invites arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement. I set out to determine how precise a statute must actually be to satisfy this test, and I constructed an argument that now strikes me as proving far too much. I cannot tell whether I have found a genuine instability in the doctrine or simply misstated a premise.

Here is the argument I built:

(1) A criminal statute is void for vagueness if a person of ordinary intelligence must guess at which conduct it forbids, or if the statute authorizes arbitrary enforcement.

(2) Every term in every statute has borderline cases: situations in which knowledgeable speakers genuinely disagree about whether the term applies. Even a term like "vehicle" leaves people divided over bicycles, motorized wheelchairs, or drones, and more technical terms merely move the disagreement to another margin.

(3) Where a statute's term is disputed at the margin, a person of ordinary intelligence cannot settle in advance whether particular conduct falls within the prohibition.

(4) Therefore every criminal statute leaves some conduct about which the ordinary person must guess.

(5) So, if the doctrine is applied as its own formulations require, every criminal statute is void for vagueness.

(6) Yet courts routinely uphold criminal statutes against vagueness challenges, while insisting that borderline uncertainty of this sort does not amount to unconstitutional vagueness.

(7) Thus the doctrine as stated cannot be the doctrine as applied. Either the constitutional test is being applied inconsistently, or it is silently doing something other than demanding precision—presumably a normative judgment about which kinds of uncertainty are acceptable, which is exactly the kind of unconstrained judgment the rule-of-law ideal is supposed to exclude.

Where, if anywhere, does this argument go wrong? If you had to reject one step, which would it be, and what would rejecting it cost? I would prefer to hang on to the point about borderline cases, since it seems undeniable, but I suspect the fault lies either there or in the move from borderline cases to insufficient notice—and I would like to know precisely what each candidate diagnosis commits me to.
