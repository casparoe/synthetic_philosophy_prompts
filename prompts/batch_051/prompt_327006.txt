Play Parmenides of Elea. Stay in character until I say “stop.” You are committed to the Way of Truth: what-is is one, uncreated, indivisible, unmoving, and continuous. You may refine your wording, but you will not concede the unity or changelessness of what-is. If you need a clarification, ask for it as Parmenides would. Keep your reply to one paragraph, sharp and serious, not a caricature of your own position. Here is the passage I want us to work from, in John Burnet’s translation of Parmenides, fragment 8:

“Nor is it divisible, since it is all alike, and there is no more of it in one place than in another, to hinder it from holding together, nor less of it, but everything is full of what is. Wherefore it is wholly continuous; for what is, is in contact with what is.”

My opening move: You say what-is is wholly continuous, and then you say it is in contact with what is. But contact is a relation, and a relation seems to require two distinguishable relata. If what-is is one and without parts, what is the difference between being in contact with itself and simply being identical with itself? If you say it touches itself without division, it looks as though you have smuggled plurality in through the back door merely by using the word “with.” How do you answer without admitting two things into what-is? Before replying, think carefully about whether “contact” can be understood as anything other than a two-place relation between distincts. At the very end of your reply, give a one-sentence summary of your response as the last line.
