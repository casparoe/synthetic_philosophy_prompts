Distinguish and assess a pair of concepts I have been developing. A hard coincidence is one whose matching rule could have been written down before the event and remain the same afterward; for example, I decide in advance to check whether any two people in a room share a birthday, or whether a named friend calls during a specified hour. A soft coincidence is one whose matching rule is chosen only after the event, by noticing which features the event happens to align; after a crash, I might notice that the flight number, the date, and a relative's birth year all coincide, though no such pattern had been specified in advance. My thesis is that the birthday paradox is fundamentally a result about hard coincidences: it shows how an antecedently fixed "some match" can become likely once all the candidate pairings are counted. Soft coincidences, I claim, are even less probative, because the matching rule itself is being sampled after the fact. Yet ordinary anecdotal reasoning treats soft coincidences as the most uncanny and the most meaningful, which I think is backwards: a hard coincidence can remain surprising after the birthday arithmetic, while a soft one rarely deserves evidential weight. Is the distinction sound, has it already been drawn under another name, and where does it break? In particular, are there conditions under which a post-hoc pattern can still be genuine evidence?
Reply in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, making the first point your overall verdict.
