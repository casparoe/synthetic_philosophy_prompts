Set the existing theories aside for a moment and write the standard they should be judged against: the desiderata for an adequate account of the distinction between health and disease. Present each requirement with the case or consideration that forces it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp (naturalist or normativist). Where two requirements pull against each other—for instance, a demand that disease-predicates be grounded in biological facts and a demand that classification not pathologize socially disfavored groups—say so explicitly, and say whether you think the pair can be jointly satisfied.

Work through at least the following cases, though you may add your own:

- hypertension, elevated LDL cholesterol, and early cancers found by screening: future harm with no present felt suffering;
- situs inversus (complete mirror-image reversal of the organs with normal function) and balanced chromosomal translocations (a rearrangement that usually causes no symptoms): statistically abnormal, with no dysfunction and no felt harm;
- fibromyalgia and chronic fatigue syndrome, where there is reported suffering but no settled biological marker;
- dental caries and myopia (nearsightedness) in populations where the majority has the condition;
- presbyopia (age-related loss of near focus), menopause, and other age-related changes: statistically normal in a life-course sense, yet often brought under medical management;
- idiopathic short stature (short stature with no identified pathology) treated with growth hormone;
- ADHD and autism, where the line between neurotype and disorder is contested;
- the historical record of conditions classified as diseases on plainly moral or political grounds and later declassified.

For each, state what kind of test it is: whether the theory can separate disease from statistical rarity, from future risk, from socially disvalued difference, or from an ordinary phase of the life course.

Then specify the distinctions the theory must be able to draw and what it loses if it collapses them: at minimum, disease versus felt illness versus the social role of the sick; a disease-type versus a particular patient's having it; disability versus disease; and deviation from a population mean versus failure of a biological function versus disvalue to the person.

Next, list the results from biology and medicine the theory may not contradict, and the difficulty each creates. At least include these: many traits are maintained by selection without having a single privileged function, and some are by-products or vestiges, so a simple selected-for test of dysfunction will misclassify; norms of reaction mean one phenotype can be adaptive in one environment and pathological in another; most diagnostic thresholds are drawn on a continuous variable, so the placement of the line is partly conventional; and a pathophysiological process can run silently for years before it causes symptoms.

Finally, score the three main families of theories—biostatistical naturalism, normativism, and hybrid dysfunction-plus-harm accounts—briefly against your own list, and identify the single requirement that, in your view, excludes the most candidates. Keep the scorecard short; the desiderata are the deliverable.
