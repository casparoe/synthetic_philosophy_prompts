I keep noticing a thing in my interviews and I don't have a name for it. People who are not remotely religious, and who know they'll be dead before any payoff, still talk about their work as mattering only because of how it lands in a future they'll never see. A cancer biologist in her late sixties told me the point of her current project is a treatment that might help people thirty years down the line. That part I get; it's just altruism. But the stranger part comes when I push. Suppose you learned that exactly one month after your own death, some perfectly painless event would end the human species. You never experience it. Nobody suffers. It's not a horror story, it just stops. Would your current projects still be worth finishing? Nearly everyone says no, or says the projects would lose most of their point. The half-written book becomes pointless to finish. The savings for the grandchildren become pointless. Even the cancer research suddenly seems to have the bottom ripped out of it.

And it isn't that they're upset about the people who'd be gone, because I've stipulated those people don't suffer, and in any case the thing happens after the person I'm talking to is already dead. It's also not that they want to be remembered, because most of them say the projects in question won't make them famous. Its more like their sense that anything they do now has value depends on a background assumption that there will be more human life after theirs. Is there a name for this? I don't just want a label, though. I want to know how to carve it. How is it different from caring about your legacy, or from ordinary concern for future generations, or from the kind of mortality-denial stuff psychologists talk about? What exactly is doing the work here: the value of the activity itself, or just the motivation to do it, or something else? And where are the edges — are there things people keep caring about even when the human future is removed, and what does that tell you? I'd rather get the boundaries right then have a name.
