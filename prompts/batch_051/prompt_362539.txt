Some philosophers, most prominently Daniel Dennett, have proposed that the self is best understood as a center of narrative gravity. The analogy is with the center of gravity of a physical object: a center of gravity is not a material part of the object, yet it is not exactly nothing either; it is an abstract point whose behavior is determined by the arrangement of mass, and it can be genuine even if it cannot be located independently of that arrangement.

Please explain whether the self, on this view, should be classified as a fiction in the way a character in a novel is, or as an abstract object more like a center of gravity, and whether this disanalogy matters for moral responsibility. In particular, I would like to understand the difference between saying that the self is a useful posit for interpreting a person's behavior and saying that the self is a real causal entity. Does the view that the self is a center of narrative gravity commit one to the further claim that selves cannot make decisions, or can a center of narrative gravity nevertheless be an agent in a deflationary sense?
