I teach a final-year undergraduate course called Philosophy of Artificial Minds, and I am setting the final exam. The unit is on AI welfare, and the students have met the basic reinforcement learning setup — agent, environment, reward signal, policy, training loop — without any expectation of formal RL mathematics. Please draft one exam question, to be answered in 75 minutes, on whether a reinforcement learning (RL) agent can suffer in virtue of its reward signal.

The question should be built around a concrete invented case, such as a warehouse robot shaped by a reward of −1 for every collision, which has now learned to avoid collisions. It should ask whether this robot suffers when the negative reward fires, and what exactly one would be claiming in saying yes.

The question must be unanswerable by reciting the standard other-minds worry or the hard problem of consciousness. Its crux should be the difference between two theses about reward and valence:

- the identity thesis: for some trained systems, the reward signal itself has a felt quality — it is not merely a learning signal but a state that feels bad or good;
- the evidential thesis: the reward signal is a scalar learning signal that merely correlates with, or causes, some distinct valenced state elsewhere in the system, and that distinct state is the real candidate for suffering.

The question should force students to engage with at least one of two hard sub-problems. The first is policy–valence decoupling: a system can show punishment-avoiding or reward-seeking behavior with no felt valence, and a system could in principle undergo valenced states without any corresponding change in policy. The second is the scalar-structure problem: a single scalar reward signal, or the reward prediction error it drives, is graded, gets consumed in a weight update, and has no obvious duration, object, or felt location, so it is not clear that it has the structure of pain or pleasure rather than that of a mere error signal.

The question should be fully answerable from the case and the definitions given in the question itself; it should not require or invite named readings or citations.

Please provide:

1. The exam question, in one paragraph, including the concrete case.

2. A model answer of about 600 words that takes a definite position and defends it against the strongest objection.

3. A marking scheme for 20 marks built around five must-hit points. For each point, give the marks it is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each band must contain.

After the marking scheme, append a single valid JSON object with the following schema:

- course_level: string, the level of the course
- question_text: string, the full exam question
- question_in_one_sentence: string
- core_tension: string, one sentence stating the specific tension the question tests
- thesis_distinction: string, one sentence distinguishing the identity and evidential theses
- must_hit_points: array of objects, each with point (string, the required move), marks (number), and typical_failure (string, the one-line note)
- model_answer_summary: string, two to three sentences summarizing the model answer's line of argument
- rubric_bands: array of objects, each with band (string, such as A, B, C) and criteria (string, what that band must contain)

The JSON should be valid and contain no prose before or after it.
