I'm arguing with a coauthor about whether one-at-a-time interventions can settle when two systems have the same mechanism, and I want a case built to order that makes my point (or kills it, if it can't be done). Construct two small systems—classifier networks, logic circuits, a human procedure, whatever's cleanest—that have the same input-output behavior and are indistinguishable under every single-unit deletion or clamp, so a standard ablation-based audit would call them the same mechanism. But rig the systems so they're actually organized into different circuits: some joint intervention on two or three units, or a structural trace of which subgraphs carry information, reveals they're not the same mechanism. Constraints: no relabelings of the same underlying circuit, no random truth tables, no exotic anything. After the scenario, do the check: state the systems and the shared behavior, give the joint intervention that pulls them apart, and say which feature of the case makes one-at-a-time ablation blind to it. End with whether we should ditch single-unit ablation as evidence for mechanism identity or keep it plus a cheap extra test, and what that test is.
