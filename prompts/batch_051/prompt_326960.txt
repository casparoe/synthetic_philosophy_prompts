In preparing a lecture on behavioral criteria for mindedness, I keep tripping over an asymmetry in ordinary practice that I suspect cannot be defended, and I want to know whether it can. When a colleague pauses, qualifies a claim, corrects herself, and then reformulates, we treat all of that as evidence of thought; we do not even feel the need to deliberate about it. When a machine could produce the same performance, we call it simulation, a designed output, or bare computation, and we continue to ask whether there is any thought "really" behind it. The same behavior seems to count differently depending on what the agent is made of and where it came from. My question is whether anything justifies that differential treatment, or whether it is a prejudice about substrates that a consistent behavioral criterion should force us out of.

The question is sharpened for me by a passage early in Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," in which he defends the conditions of the imitation game. He writes:

"The new problem has the advantage of drawing a fairly sharp line between the physical and the intellectual capacities of a man. No engineer or chemist claims to be able to produce a material which is indistinguishable from the human skin. It is possible that at some time this might be done, but even supposing this invention available we should feel there was little point in trying to make a 'thinking machine' more human by dressing it up in such artificial flesh. The form in which we have set the problem reflects this fact in the condition which prevents the interrogator from seeing or touching the other competitors, or hearing their voices."

Turing is drawing a line between the physical and the intellectual capacities, and in doing so he refuses to let the interrogator's knowledge of the body do epistemic work. But our ordinary practice does not just let fabric do epistemic work; it lets it do work in one direction only—easing the test for flesh, stiffening it for silicon. I want one of two things from you, not a survey of positions. Either defend the asymmetry: give a principled reason that a stretch of behavior is strong evidence of thought when produced by a human but weak or no evidence when produced by a machine. Or conclude that the asymmetry should be dropped, and say what our ascriptions should look like after it is dropped. If you defend it, your defense must survive a machine that reproduces the deliberative performance above token for token, and it must also say what we should say about a human whose responses are a fixed rehearsal rather than occurrent thinking. I am less interested in which side you take than in whether the asymmetry can be made to rest on anything sturdier than the bare fact of ancestry.
