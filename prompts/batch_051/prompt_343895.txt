I have just begun a seat in the corporate practice at a London firm, and a passing remark from my supervising solicitor has been troubling me more than it probably should. Our due-diligence workflow now runs the review of thousands of supplier contracts through a large language model over a weekend; the client is billed on the understanding that the contracts have been reviewed, and the partner describes the tool's output, without any visible discomfort, as the system having "understood" the material, a human associate only spot-checking the anomalies. I had always assumed, rather vaguely, that such systems manipulate symbols by their shape rather than by their meaning, but the practice around them talks as though the meaning were already in the machine.

In his Scholarpedia entry "Symbol Grounding Problem" (2007), Stevan Harnad sets out the difficulty in a passage I cannot shake: 'So the meaning of a word on a page is "ungrounded." Nor would looking it up in a dictionary help: If I tried to look up the meaning of a word I did not understand in a (unilingual) dictionary of a language I did not already understand, I would just cycle endlessly from one meaningless definition to another. My search for meaning would be ungrounded.' That seems to imply that a system which only trades symbols against other symbols never breaks out of the circle of definitions, and so can never itself be the site of meaning—which would cut against the entire arrangement at my firm. What view of meaning and understanding does a practice like this presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory required to treat those outputs as "read", "understood", and "reviewed" for billing and advice; distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with (for example, whether the spot-checking human could be doing the grounding); and say whether that theory escapes Harnad's regress or simply abandons his requirement. Then tell me what would have to change in the practice—liability, billing, the descriptions given to clients and to a court, the role of the human reviewer—if the passage were taken as a constraint instead, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that more than one theory of meaning could license it.
