After my partner died last year, I began noticing something I still lack adequate language for, and I now see a milder version of it everywhere. I carry an internal version of him that does not behave like a memory: it talks back, it surprises me, it has his timing. At a dinner, when a mutual friend misremembered a story, I knew with almost bodily certainty what he would have objected to and exactly how he would have waved it off — not by reasoning toward it, the way one knows what one's own hand is about to do. I once caught myself saying something to our daughter that I would never have thought to say, in his cadence, and only recognized him in it afterward. It happens with the living, too: after a long visit, a friend's intonation lingers in my inner voice for days, and it can feel as though she is doing part of my thinking rather than merely being recalled. My question is whether philosophy of mind has a precise account of one self continuing to operate, in part, inside another. Is this simply memory plus imagination, with grief in the first case, or is there a stronger and more interesting claim in play — that a self can be spread across more than one head and can outlast the body in which it grew? I am not after a bare label. I want to know how to carve the phenomenon: what the internal version of a person is, what it is not, and whether any line can be drawn between my reconstruction of him and him.
