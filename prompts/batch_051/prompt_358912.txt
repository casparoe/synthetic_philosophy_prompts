I got into an argument about the old torture-versus-dust-specks problem, and in reconstructing the side that says the specks are worse, I ended up with a version that now seems to prove far too much. I would like to know where it goes wrong, if it does—or, if it does not, which premise is least costly to abandon and what exactly that costs.

Let me define the two options precisely. Option A: one person undergoes fifty years of the worst continuous physical pain. Option B: some number N of distinct people each receive a single dust speck—a brief and mild but genuinely unpleasant irritation to the eye. In the familiar presentation N is 3↑↑↑3, and I will keep that form: N is finite but indescribably large. Denote the badness of one speck by ε and the badness of the torture by T. Now the argument:

(1) A single dust speck is a genuine harm, so ε is some small but positive quantity.
(2) Fifty years of torture, however terrible, is a finite harm, so T is some finite quantity rather than an infinite one.
(3) The badness of a set of harms is additive across persons and across instances: when an option involves many separate harms, the badness of the whole is the sum of the badnesses of the parts, with no discount for the number of people harmed and no point at which additional small harms simply stop counting; and one option is worse than another when its total badness is greater.
(4) Since ε is positive and T is finite, arithmetic alone gives some finite n such that n·ε exceeds T; so n dust specks are worse than one torture.
(5) The reasoning from (1) to (4) never used any feature special to dust specks or to torture. Therefore, for any two harms H and L such that H is worse than L and L is a genuine harm, some sufficiently large finite number of Ls is worse than a single H.
(6) From (5) it follows that no harm is lexically worse than every pile of lesser harms: enough stubbed toes would be worse than one year of torture for a child, and enough hangnails would be worse than a single murder. That conclusion strikes me as morally absurd, and I cannot accept it.

So my question is: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it force on me? I would strongly prefer to keep (1)—the claim that a single speck matters at all—and I would also prefer to keep (2). I am most inclined to attack (3), but I suspect that denying (3) commits me to a threshold or a discontinuity somewhere up the staircase of n, and I have no idea how to state that threshold without arbitrariness. What, exactly, is the price of each option?
