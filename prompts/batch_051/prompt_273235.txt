I recently sat in on an argument between two friends about whether Anselm of Canterbury successfully reconciles God's compassion with the doctrine that God cannot suffer. The passage they kept returning to is from Anselm's Proslogion, chapter 8, in the English translation they were using:

BUT how art thou compassionate, and, at the same time, passionless? For, if thou art passionless, thou dost not feel sympathy; and if thou dost not feel sympathy, thy heart is not wretched from sympathy for the wretched; but this it is to be compassionate. But if thou art not compassionate, whence cometh so great consolation to the wretched? How, then, art thou compassionate and not compassionate, O Lord, unless because thou art compassionate in terms of our experience, and not compassionate in terms of thy being. Truly, thou art so in terms of our experience, but thou art not so in terms of thine own. For, when thou beholdest us in our wretchedness, we experience the effect of compassion, but thou dost not experience the feeling. Therefore, thou art both compassionate, because thou dost save the wretched, and spare those who sin against thee; and not compassionate because thou art affected by no sympathy for wretchedness.

Their exchange went roughly as follows.

Maya said the passage shows the problem is only apparent. Anselm is saying that God's compassion is real, but not in the mode of human feeling. When Anselm writes that God is compassionate in terms of our experience and not in terms of his being, he means the same divine beneficence is experienced by us as compassion, while in God it is not felt as pain. So God really saves the wretched, and that is what compassion is at bottom. Thus God can be compassionate without suffering; nothing important is lost.

Daniel replied that Anselm is saving the word by changing the subject. If compassion includes being moved by another's distress, then a God who is affected by no sympathy for wretchedness lacks the thing we mean by compassion, not just a felt component. To say God is compassionate only from our side is like saying a heater is compassionate because we experience warmth from it. The word has been drained of its interpersonal meaning.

Maya answered that Daniel is treating the emotional tremor as the essence of compassion, but in many cases the most compassionate person is the one who acts effectively while not falling apart. The doctrine of divine impassibility denies that God is acted upon involuntarily; it does not make him indifferent. What Anselm preserves is that the comfort the wretched receive comes from God's own active goodness, not from a divine emotional reaction.

Daniel said that still leaves an asymmetry. If God's own inner life has no counterpart to what we call compassion, then "God is compassionate" is true only because of an effect on us, not because of what God is. But Anselm explicitly denies that God is compassionate in terms of his being. So the next move, Daniel insisted, is to admit that at least one central strand of compassion cannot be applied to God. That may be acceptable if we revise our concept of divine love, but it is a bigger concession than Anselm acknowledges.

Maya responded that Daniel is assuming God's inner life must be a private feeling. But for Anselm, God's being is identical with his act; there is no hidden affective state behind the act. The reason God does not experience compassion as an affect is that his goodness is not a reaction to need but its source.

Daniel then said that this is exactly the problem. The word "compassion" becomes so identified with bare beneficence that it no longer marks the difference between love as helping and love as entering into someone's pain. If the doctrine of impassibility requires collapsing that distinction, then it has cost us something real.

They did not reach a conclusion.

I would like you to go through their exchange and say, for each specific point, who is right or partly right, and whether the dispute is ultimately about textual interpretation of Anselm, about the concept of compassion, or about a deeper metaphysical claim about divine agency. Also, what, if anything, are both missing? Please say how confident you are in each part of your adjudication, and identify the point you are least sure of.
