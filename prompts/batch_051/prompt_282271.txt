For a year now, the theology reading group I belong to has been stuck in the same argument about the atonement, and I have stopped wanting any side to win: I want to understand the shape of the disagreement and why it will not move. One faction holds penal substitution: on the cross Christ voluntarily bears the punishment human sin deserved, so that God can justly acquit sinners because the penalty has already been paid by a substitute. A second faction holds the satisfaction view: sin is primarily an offense against God's honor, an infinite debt sinners cannot repay, and Christ's obedient self-offering offers a satisfaction that restores God's honor and so grounds forgiveness. A third faction holds the moral influence view: the cross is God's decisive demonstration of love, aimed at softening and reorienting human hearts so that sinners come to trust God and repent out of love rather than fear. The odd thing is that everyone accepts the same textual accounts of the crucifixion and resurrection, everyone agrees that sin is a real problem the cross addresses, and everyone can state the others' positions accurately—yet no one has moved in a year.

What kind of disagreement is this? It may be a hidden disagreement about what the primary obstacle to reconciliation is: guilt that must be punished, an injury to God's honor that must be repaired, or a hardened human heart that must be won. It may be a disagreement about which root metaphor is carrying the real argument—law court, debt of honor, or exemplar. It may be a more basic disagreement about whether the cross changes something in God, in the sinner, or in the relation between them, or whether thinking in terms of change at all is the mistake. Or it may turn on whether desert, guilt, and punishment can intelligibly be transferred to an innocent person.

Please commit to the diagnosis that best explains this particular impasse, defend it against the other plausible diagnoses, and say what would count as progress for our group if you are right: a distinction, a reframing, an ordering or division of labor among the metaphors, or a reason to stop expecting the disagreement to resolve.
