Earlier this year I left a software job for a philosophy department, and a recent seminar on the epistemology of logic has been nagging at me ever since. We worked through Carroll's exchange between Achilles and the Tortoise over a simple modus ponens inference. The Tortoise accepts the premise, and accepts the conditional, but declines to take the step to the conclusion. Achilles then writes down, as an additional premise, the principle that if the premise and the conditional are both true then the conclusion follows. The Tortoise accepts this new premise too and still does not move, so Achilles must add yet another premise, and the process repeats without end. The moral we drew was that a rule of inference cannot do its work if it is treated merely as one more proposition to accept; at some point, accepting propositions has to be supplemented by actually applying a rule.

What struck me afterward is how closely this resembles an incident I used to encounter when I maintained a business-rules engine. We stored rules as records in a database—for example, a record stating that if an order total exceeds five hundred, then the order is to be flagged for review—and now and then a rule would be present, correct, and completely inert, because nothing in the system was actually applying it. The junior fix was always to add another stored rule: if a rule's condition is met, then apply that rule. But that new rule was itself just another inert record unless some evaluator already treated it as an instruction. The real fix was never an additional row in the rules table; it was a scheduler and an evaluation loop that fired the rules—and the scheduler itself was not stored as another rule in that same table. This looks to me like the point about the Tortoise: no amount of added data makes a system take a step, because taking a step is not itself another piece of data.

I am trying to decide whether this resemblance is deep or merely a loose analogy. On one side, both cases seem to turn on a type distinction between propositions or records on the one hand, and acts or executions on the other, and in both cases the regress begins when someone tries to use an item of the first type to do the work of the second. On the other side, the software case is purely causal—the machine simply lacked a mechanism—whereas the Tortoise is usually read as raising a normative question about what a reasoner ought to do, and a machine's evaluator applies rules blindly rather than being entitled to apply them. Please adjudicate this carefully: is the parallel structurally real, or does it come apart once the normative and causal cases are separated? If it is real, what exactly would have to be true of both sides for a result from one domain to transfer to the other? And can you identify something the parallel seems to predict that turns out false in one of the two settings?
