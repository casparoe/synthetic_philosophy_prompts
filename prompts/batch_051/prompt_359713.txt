I am working on a conceptual framework for a book on emergency government, and I want a working definition of the term "legitimate" as applied to emergency measures in a liberal constitutional order — a definition that keeps legitimacy distinct from mere legality but does not simply collapse into a general moral judgment about the policy's merits. I am not asking for citations or a survey of the literature; I want the conceptual work.

The six cases the definition must handle are these:

A. A three-week stay-at-home order issued under a pre-existing public-health statute, with a fixed expiration date, a published epidemiological justification, and judicial review available on ordinary grounds. Compliance is high, and the order is allowed to lapse as scheduled.

B. A presidential decree, issued during a declared emergency but without any statutory basis, that postpones upcoming elections indefinitely and bars the courts from reviewing the postponement.

C. A border closure announced under a public-health derogation from a human-rights treaty; the closure nonetheless turns away persons seeking refugee protection at the frontier. The government calls the measure temporary and medically necessary, and domestic opinion strongly approves of it.

D. An order closing schools and nonessential businesses, issued by the minister of health under a statute that does not clearly authorize such an order. Before a court rules on a challenge, the legislature passes a law approving the order with retroactive effect.

E. A curfew imposed under a standing emergency statute that allows the executive to renew the state of emergency every sixty days without a parliamentary vote and with only deferential judicial review. The curfew has been renewed seven times; compliance is high, and polls show that a majority accept it as necessary.

F. A ban on indoor religious services while large retail spaces remain open at reduced capacity, imposed under a statute that expressly allows such distinctions on public-health grounds. The measure is lawful and upheld by the courts, but many religious communities regard it as illegitimate, and some openly defy it.

For calibration, my own views are these. I am confident that A is legitimate and that B is not; in both cases the legal verdict and the legitimacy verdict point the same way, so they do not separate the two notions. I have no settled view about C and D, where legality and other considerations seem to pull apart. I lean toward calling E legitimate despite the democratic deficit, and I lean toward calling F legal but not fully legitimate, though I cannot state the standard behind either leaning.

I suspect the ordinary term does more than one job: at least a legal-institutional job (authorized by the governing rules), a normative job (justified, worthy of compliance), and perhaps a sociological job (accepted enough that voluntary compliance is stable and enforcement does not become mere coercion). Propose a definition or, if you conclude the term is ambiguous, several definitions; run them through cases A through F; say which of your verdicts is least secure; and either revise the definitions or defend the surprising verdicts.

After the prose, append a JSON object with the following fields:
- "term": the term being defined, in your own phrasing.
- "definitions": an array of objects, each with "label" (a short name for the notion, e.g., "institutional legitimacy") and "text" (a one-sentence definition).
- "case_verdicts": an array with one object per case, each having "case_id" ("A" through "F"), "verdict" (one of "legitimate", "illegitimate", "mixed", or "no determinate answer"), "definition_used" (the label that yields the verdict), and "confidence" ("high", "medium", or "low").
- "least_secure_verdict": the case_id of the verdict you would be least willing to defend.
- "cases_where_definitions_disagree": one or two sentences naming which cases the different definitions classify differently.
- "revision": any change you made to a definition after running the cases, or null.
