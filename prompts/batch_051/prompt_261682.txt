Grade the following student response to an exam question in a course on aggregation and distributive ethics.

Question: Suppose you must choose between (A) an astronomically large but finite number of people each suffering a brief, barely noticeable pain (analogous to a dust speck in the eye) and (B) one person suffering decades of severe pain. Some hold that (A) is the right choice no matter how large the number; others hold that for a sufficiently large number, (B) becomes the right choice. Reconstruct the strongest argument for each side, then say which you find more compelling and why.

Student answer: The strongest case for (A) is that harms are experienced by separate individuals. No one in (A) has an experience anywhere near as bad as the single person's experience in (B). Each speck victim, considered one by one, has a weaker complaint than the torture victim, so no individual in (A) is wronged as much as the individual in (B) would be wronged. Since no individual is wronged as much under (A), (A) is the right choice. The strongest case for (B) rests on the additivity of harms: each speck is a genuine, if tiny, disvalue, and disvalues sum across people. For any nonzero amount of harm per speck, there is a finite number of people large enough that the summed harm of the specks exceeds the harm of the torture, so choosing (B) minimizes total harm. But this additive argument works only if harms across different people may be combined as if they belonged to a single subject of experience. That is precisely what is wrong with it: persons are separate loci of value, and harms do not merge across lives. I therefore find the argument for (A) more convincing.

Grade the answer, and provide the point-by-point comments that justify the grade: identify which claims are correct, where the answer makes an invalid inference or begs the question, and what the student would need to revise or add to receive a higher grade.
