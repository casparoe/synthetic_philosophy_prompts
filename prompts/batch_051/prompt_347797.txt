Write a dialogue between two equally capable interpreters of C.S. Peirce who disagree about how much work the following passage from “The Fixation of Belief” can do in ruling out the method of tenacity:

“The object of reasoning is to find out, from the consideration of what we already know, something else which we do not know.”

The first interpreter argues that the passage, read in context, commits Peirce to the claim that reasoning is answerable to something beyond the inquirer’s present state of mind, and that this is exactly why the method of tenacity fails as a method of fixing belief: tenacity aims not at finding out something new but at preserving a belief one already has, and so it cannot count as reasoning in the relevant sense. The second interpreter replies that the passage states only what reasoning does when one reasons, not what makes reasoning good or which methods of settling opinion are legitimate; on this view, Peirce’s later argument against tenacity must import a further commitment—that the discomfort of doubt should be resolved by inquiry rather than by whatever restores calm fastest—and that commitment is a preference, not something the passage supplies.

Have the two develop their positions through real back-and-forth. In particular, make them address whether Peirce can distinguish the self-correcting character of the scientific method from the a priori method without presupposing the very standard of reasoning the passage is being used to establish. End by locating where their disagreement becomes a disagreement about what fallibilism requires.
