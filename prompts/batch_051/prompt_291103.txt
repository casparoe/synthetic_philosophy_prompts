For a workshop on intergenerational justice and climate policy, I have written down the position I currently hold and would like a consistency audit before presenting it. My commitments are these.

(1) The time at which a person lives is not a morally relevant feature of her. A person living two centuries from now has the same moral claim on us as a person living now, and no defensible ethical framework includes a positive rate of pure time preference.

(2) In the practical evaluation of climate policy, however, I hold that it is right to discount future benefits at a positive rate. The future is uncertain: future persons may not exist, their numbers are unknown, and the effect of present resources on their well-being cannot be predicted with confidence. These facts, I think, justify discounting.

(3) I take the non-identity problem seriously but deny that it dissolves our obligations to future generations. A policy that leaves the people who eventually exist with serious, avoidable disadvantages can be wrong even when, because the policy helps determine who those people are, there is no particular person who would have been better off under the alternative.

(4) Yet I also hold that the correct account of intergenerational ethics should remain person-affecting in spirit. I do not believe there is an impersonal value in the total sum of well-being as such; what ultimately matters is what our choices do for persons, one by one.

(5) I hold that the risk of human extinction, or of the permanent collapse of civilization, is not subject to ordinary expected-value trade-offs. Even where a cost-benefit calculation could be made permissive by multiplying a very small probability by a very large loss, I think there is a deontological constraint against policies that non-negligibly risk foreclosing the existence of all future persons.

(6) When comparing futures with different population sizes, I am drawn to the view that, other things equal, a future with more persons whose lives are worth living is morally better than a future with fewer such persons. Within a generation, however, I do not demand strict equality of well-being; I demand only that every person has enough for a decent life.

Go through these commitments in pairs and tell me which are in genuine tension, which merely appear to be in tension, and which are independent. Rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, specify what would have to give and what the least costly revision would be. Where a pair can be reconciled only by drawing a distinction I have not drawn, state that distinction explicitly rather than treating the pair as either consistent or inconsistent as it stands.
