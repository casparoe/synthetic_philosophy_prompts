I've come to accept the ergodicity-economics view: for a single agent facing choices that are repeated over time, the correct objective is the time-average growth rate, not the expected value over an ensemble of parallel worlds, and the gap between the two explains why expected-value reasoning can be systematically ruinous. Tell me what would count against this. Which commitments does the view actually need — that the relevant dynamics are multiplicative rather than additive, that the horizon is long enough for the time average to dominate, that ruin is a real absorbing boundary, that the agent cannot fully diversify or rebalance — and which of these, if false or shown to be beside the point in realistic settings, would be fatal rather than merely awkward? I would find a formal result showing that time-average and expected-utility prescriptions coincide under realistic constraints especially persuasive, but give me the full map: defeaters ranked from decisive to minor, concessions the view's own defenders would make, and an honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether that matters.
