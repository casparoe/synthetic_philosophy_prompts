I'm a distributed-systems engineer; my working vocabulary is leader election, quorums, epochs, failover, split-brain, liveness and safety properties, configuration changes, and Byzantine failures. I have no formal background in constitutional law. I've been trying to think about constitutional interregna: the situations in which the rules specifying who holds office, or how office-holders are replaced, themselves run out, come into conflict, or presuppose the very authority they are meant to generate. For example, a constitution may specify a line of succession for the president, vice president, and speaker, but not for a scenario in which all of them are dead or incapacitated at once, or in which the body that must certify the successor has itself been dissolved. I'd like you to restate this debate in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Translate the two main positions: one that treats such gaps as a genuine break in the legal order, after which a new order is founded by an extra-legal act that later comes to be treated as lawful; and another that treats the old order as continuing, with the difficulty being only epistemological or procedural, a matter of discovering or constructing the answer from the existing rules. Where do the two positions land once expressed in terms of protocol states, quorums, and failover?

Then give me the residue report. Which aspects map cleanly, which ones you had to distort, and which parts of the constitutional debate have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Be explicit about where the translation breaks, and don't smuggle legal authority in under the label "specification" or "consensus" unless you mark it as a fudge. Finally, tell me whether what does not survive suggests that the legal theorists are tracking something real my vocabulary cannot say, or that their problem is partly an artefact of not having an explicit model of state and agreement.
