A colleague of mine calls this the cleanest asymmetry in the criminal law, and I've never had a fully satifying answer to it. Suppose I learn, in a way that creates no duty of confidentiality, that a neighbor is having an affair. It would be legal for me to tell their spouse — it's true, so it isn't defamation. It would also be legal for me to walk over and ask the neighbor for five thousand dollars, no strings attached. Weird, shameless, but not a crime. But the moment I say "give me five thousand dollars or I tell your spouse," that's blackmail. Two acts that are each lawful on their own become a crime when I join them with an "or else." In most of morality and law, permissions combine: if you may walk in the park and you may hum, you may hum in the park. So why doesn't that hold here?

I want you to find the strongest justification for this asymmetry, and then try to break it. Watch out for the easy answers. "It's coercive" can't be the whole story, because plenty of legal transactions have exactly that conditional form — a landlord may say "pay rent or I'll evict you," a baker may say "pay for the bread or you dont get it." "He's profiting from someone's secret" can't be the whole story either, since journalists and publishers do that all the time. So tell me what the real difference is, and whether the law has it right. If it doesn't, say which way it should go: should blackmail be legal, or should the component acts be restricted too?
