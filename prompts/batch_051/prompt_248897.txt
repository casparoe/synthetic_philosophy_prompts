I'm taking a class on political philosophy and we've been going over Milgram and Arendt—how ordinary people end up doing terrible things less because they're evil and more because they defer to whoever is standing at the front of the room, or whoever is one rung up in the organization. In seminar someone proposed a rule for armed forces that I haven't been able to stop thinking about. The proposal: a soldier who believes an order is unlawful or immoral may refuse it, but the refusal is only protected if a second soldier of equal or lower rank, who also recieved the order or directly witnessed it, refuses too within a fixed window—say one hour—and co-signs the objection. A soldier who refuses alone forfeits all protection and faces the standard penalties; if two refuse together, the order is automatically suspended and goes to an independent review board. My friend's pitch was that this lets genuine moral resistance happen while filtering out cranks and cowards, because if the order really is wrong, finding one other person who sees it shouldn't be all that hard.

I'm skeptical, but I also can't quite articulate what goes wrong. Acting as an expert on organizational ethics and the psychology of authority, walk me through how this rule would actually play out in a real military unit. Who does it protect and who does it leave exposed? What does it incentivize among ordinary soldiers, NCOs, and officers—and how does it change the way orders get given, not just the way they get refused? Where would it be gamed first, and can the obvious failure modes be fixed without gutting the mechanism? Start by brainstorming the relevant actors and their incentives before you settle on any verdict, and mostly treat this as a question about mechanisms rather than about whether the rule is, in the abstract, a good idea.
