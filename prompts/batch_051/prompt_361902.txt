I am working on the conditions under which a person's earlier self has legitimate authority to bind a later, decisionally incapacitated self, and I have been using five rough conditions. A directive carries that authority only if the earlier self issued it while competent, adequately informed, and free of coercion; it expressed settled values rather than a passing inclination; the situation now before the clinicians is the very situation the directive specified; enough continuity obtains between the earlier and later self that honoring the instruction does not amount to one person's being ruled by another; and honoring it does not subject the later self to suffering the earlier self could not reasonably have anticipated and weighed. I would like to test these conditions against a case that seems to fall between them.

The case is this. At forty-five, a woman watches her mother die slowly with advanced Alzheimer's disease and drafts an advance directive. Its main instruction says that if she develops dementia severe enough that she cannot recognize her children or follow and remember a five-minute conversation, she refuses all life-prolonging measures beyond comfort care, including antibiotics and artificial nutrition and hydration. But she also adds a second clause, prompted by the memory that her mother took visible pleasure in being fed and in small social rituals. The clause says that if, once she is in exactly that condition, she consistently accepts ordinary food and fluids offered to her with evident enjoyment and shows no sign of persistent distress, then she wants that conduct to be treated as her present settled preference, sufficient to override the refusal in the main instruction where low-burden life-prolonging treatment such as antibiotics is concerned. Thirty years later she develops Alzheimer's disease and reaches the described condition. She cannot recognize her daughters, cannot follow a short conversation, and shows no capacity for the kind of reasoning a contemporaneous refusal would require. At the same time, she regularly smiles, reaches for food, cooperates with being fed, and sometimes says she is comfortable. When she develops pneumonia that oral antibiotics would likely clear with little discomfort, the care team divides. Her daughter says the main instruction must be honored, because it was written competently, is exactly applicable, and the later self lacks the capacity to revoke it. A physician replies that the second clause shows the earlier self had already transferred authority to the later self's plainly expressed contentment, and that refusing antibiotics now would betray both selves. A third colleague objects that an incapacitated person cannot revoke a directive, and that the second clause is merely a loophole the earlier self left because she could not bring herself to accept the full consequence of her own main instruction.

I do not need you to tell me what the law would say, but rather whether this counts as a legitimate exercise of the earlier self's authority over the later self in the matter of the antibiotics. Please go through each of the five conditions, note where they leave the verdict underdetermined, and say whether the case shows that one or more of the conditions need to be revised.
