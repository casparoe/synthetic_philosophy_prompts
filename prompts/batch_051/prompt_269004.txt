I recently retired from a career in geochemistry and have been spending the last two years working through political philosophy, mostly alone, which has meant I have had too few chances to get my convictions stress-tested. Next month I am giving a short talk to a university philosophy reading group, and I want to arrive ready for a hard question period. My talk defends a deflationary reading of Robert Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument. I do not read it, as it is usually read, as a self-contained argument for self-ownership or for the injustice of redistributive taxation. I read it as establishing a narrower structural point: any distributive principle that evaluates states of affairs by whether they exhibit a preferred pattern (equality, sufficiency, desert, need) will be undone by ordinary voluntary transfers among individuals, so the proponent of a patterned principle must either forbid those transfers after the fact or concede that the pattern can be realized only by constant interference with ordinary exchange. My further claim is that this result does not push toward Nozick's historical entitlement theory; it pushes toward principles that govern transfers themselves rather than end-state patterns, for example, principles requiring that transfers occur against fair background institutions or under conditions free of domination and necessity. On this reading, the Wilt Chamberlain argument disallows a certain style of egalitarian principle but leaves untouched any principle that constrains the rules of transfer rather than the resulting pattern.

The audience is a group of about fifteen academic philosophers, most of them working in the post-Rawlsian egalitarian tradition, including two who work on luck egalitarianism and one historian of political thought with little patience for what she describes as libertarian apologetics. There are no committed libertarians in the room. They are likely to grant that the Chamberlain example shows something about patterns, but to be suspicious of the word voluntary and of any argument that claims an egalitarian lesson can be extracted from Nozick. They care about what distributive principles can be institutionalized, and about the difference between a pattern and the background conditions that make transfers genuinely free.

Please prepare a Q&A prep sheet for this talk: the objections I will actually get from this audience rather than the standard libertarian-versus-socialist set pieces, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased roughly as a member of this group would put it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front and which point I must hold. Also tell me the question I should be most afraid of, the one that would expose the weakest joint in my argument. Finally, for your own part, say where your reasoning is least certain: what consideration or counterexample would most likely change your verdict about how the talk should go, or about the deflationary reading itself.
