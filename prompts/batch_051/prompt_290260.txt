I have spent the past year trying to work through the philosophical literature on the units of selection, and I keep arriving at an argument whose conclusion strikes me as both plausible and suspicious. I would like a precise diagnosis rather than a survey of positions. I have reconstructed the argument as follows.

(1) The Price equation decomposes the change in the population mean of a trait into a covariance term, which measures how strongly the trait covaries with reproductive success, and a transmission-bias term, which measures how faithfully the trait is passed on; the equation is valid no matter which units one chooses, whether genes, individual organisms, or groups.
(2) If the equation is applied to a single evolutionary episode at each of those three levels, the decompositions are different, but each sums to the same total change in the trait's mean, because all three decompositions describe the same population and the same events.
(3) Hence gene-level, organism-level, and group-level descriptions of that episode are empirically equivalent: they entail exactly the same facts about how the trait's mean changes.
(4) If two accounts of a process are empirically equivalent and differ only in how they allocate causal emphasis, then the disagreement between them is verbal rather than factual.
(5) Therefore the dispute between gene selectionists and group selectionists is, at bottom, a disagreement about notation rather than about the world.

Is this argument valid? If it is not, at which step does it first fail? Please be specific about the reading on which the offending step is to be understood. I have a suspicion that the move in (3) from a mathematical identity to empirical equivalence, or else the unstated interpretation of empirical equivalence in (4), is doing more work than it should, but I cannot state the difficulty cleanly.
