Consider the following asymmetry in our practical judgments. When a scarce, life-saving resource must be allocated, we think it is obviously right to give it to the patient who will benefit most, even if that patient is already better off than the others; a hospital that gave a scarce organ to the patient least likely to survive would strike us as wasting the organ. At the level of policy, we likewise judge a health program to be better when it produces more healthy years of life per dollar, and we criticize donors who ignore this test. Yet when a philosopher asks us to imagine a being whose capacity to convert resources into well-being vastly exceeds everyone else's—Nozick's utility monster—we firmly reject the conclusion that all resources should be transferred to that being, and many philosophers treat this rejection as a decisive objection to any unqualified maximizing utilitarianism.

So we are confident that the principle of producing the most benefit is the right one in the ordinary cases, and equally confident that it is the wrong one in the monster case. This is not a mere difference in scale, since the two cases have the same logical structure: in both, we favor the recipient who yields the most well-being from the next unit of resource. Your task is to determine whether anything principled justifies the asymmetry, or whether one side of it must be given up.
