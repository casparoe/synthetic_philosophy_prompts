Feyerabend died in 1994, a good while before psychology's replication crisis and the whole open science apparatus (pre-registration, registered reports, data sharing, badges, all that) became a real movement, and I'm curous what he'd have made of it. On the one hand, preregistration looks like exactly the kind of thing he tended to mock: a formal rule set down in advance about how you're allowed to collect and analyze data, with the implication that doing it any other way is epistemically suspect. He spent a lot of time arguing that method-talk is usually a rhetoric of legitimation, and that science advances precisely when people break the accepted rules. On the other hand, the open science folks mostly aren't defending a single monolithic scientific method; they treat preregistration as a narrow fix for a specific, well-documented failure mode (p-hacking, HARKing, file-drawer effects), not as a philosophy of how discovery works. You could even read Feyerabend as having predicted the replication crisis in a loose way: if methodology is mostly post-hoc storytelling and scientists mostly do whatever works, then what gets published will track incentives and fads more than any shared rational procedure. So what's the best Feyerabend-style verdict? Would he have said preregistration is a harmless bureaucratic patch for a narrow problem, a new form of authority in science that props up a false picture of how science actually works, or something weirder? Give me your best reconstruction of what he'd say, and where that reconstruction would be wrong about what preregistration actually does.
