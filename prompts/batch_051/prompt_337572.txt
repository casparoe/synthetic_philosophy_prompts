I've been retired from evolutionary biology for a few years and have been spending my reading time on political philosophy, mostly the reparations literature. Something's been nagging me that I'd like a professional philosopher to stress-test. In biology we've long distinguished proximate from ultimate explanations: a proximate explanation says how a trait works and develops in the present organism, while an ultimate explanation says why the trait exists at all — the historical, selective, or contingent causes that produced it over evolutionary time. I keep seeing the same two-register structure in debates about reparations. On one side you have forward-looking justifications, which start from the present: there is a current pattern of inequality, disadvantage, or need, and it ought to be remedied because of what it is now. On the other side you have backward-looking justifications, which start from history: the present state must be repaired because of the wrongful process that produced it — slavery, land theft, broken treaties. That looks to me like proximate and ultimate explanation wearing a normative costume.

I cant decide whether this is a deep structural identity or just me pattern-matching from my old feild. If it is deep, does anything actually transfer? For instance, biologists treat proximate and ultimate explanations as complementary, not rivals — they answer different questions, and giving one doesn't undercut the other. Would that mean the forward- versus backward-looking fight in reparations is partly a confusion, and a complete account needs both? Or does the analogy break exactly where normativity enters, since an ultimate explanation in biology carries no ought, while the whole point of the backward-looking story is that the wrongful origin generates a present obligation? And one more thing that worries me: in biology, plenty of traits have no tidy adaptive history (drift, path-dependence, frozen accidents), so the question why a trait exists can't always be answered with a satisfying story. Does the analogy predict that not every present inequality traces back to a discrete historical wrong, so that forward-looking justifications have to do independent work — and is that a feature of the parallel or a place where it snaps?

You're a professional philosopher working on corrective justice and explanation. Think carefully before you answer: brainstorm the mapping, check where the two structures match and where they come apart, and then give me an honest verdict on whether it's deep or superficial. I'd rather be told precisely where the analogy fails than have its cleverness flattered.
