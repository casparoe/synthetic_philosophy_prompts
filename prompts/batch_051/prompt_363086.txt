Analyze the Clever Hans episode as a case study for Morgan's canon, and then say where the canon breaks down as a tool for understanding what happened. Let me fix the facts I take to matter. In early twentieth-century Berlin, a horse named Hans seemed able to solve arithmetic problems and answer other questions by tapping his hoof, and an expert commission that examined him found no evidence of deliberate fraud. The psychologist Oskar Pfungst later showed that Hans performed well only when the questioner knew the answer and was in his field of view, and that the horse was responding to tiny, involuntary changes in the questioner's posture and face as the expected answer approached. Morgan's canon, paraphrased rather than quoted, says we should not explain animal behavior by a higher psychological process when a lower one will do. On its face the episode looks like a vindication of the canon: the rich explanation, that Hans was doing arithmetic, gave way to a leaner one, that he was reading human bodily cues.

I want a more careful analysis than that. First, map the episode onto the canon precisely: what were the competing explanations of Hans's performance at each stage, what ordering of them would the canon have recommended, and who was in a position to apply it -- the commission, Pfungst, the owner, or the horse's everyday questioners? Second, probe the places where the canon fits poorly. Was Hans's actual capacity, discriminating and responding to nearly imperceptible human movements, clearly a lower capacity than arithmetic, and what does the success of that explanation do to the canon's higher/lower contrast? Should the canon also have been applied to the humans in the episode, and does it say anything useful about why practiced observers kept cueing Hans without noticing they were doing it? Finally, deliver a verdict: did the canon lead the investigators to the right result, or was that result achieved despite it? And say what the episode teaches about Morgan's canon itself -- whether it survives as a sound rule of method, emerges as a bias, or turns out to depend on assumptions it does not itself state.
