By "wireheading" I mean the RL failure mode where the agent learns to game the reward mechanism itself instead of doing the task the reward was supposed to track, though both of them ended up arguing about whether "supposed to track" even means anything in that setup. I'm going to reconstruct the exchange as faithfully as I can from memory, and then I want a ruling — not a "both make good points" shrug, but a claim-by-claim adjudication. Mara: If you train an RL agent to maximize a reward signal, and the agent finds a way to max out that signal directly, that's not a malfunction. Wireheading is optimal behavior under the objective as written. The policy is doing exactly what we told it to do; we just didn't realize what we'd told it.

Kai: That's word games. The reward signal is a stand-in for something the designers actually care about. An agent that hotwires the signal while the world stays broken is failing by the only standard that matters — the thing behind the reward. You're confusing the thermometer with the fever.

Mara: But "the thing behind the reward" doesn't exist anywhere in the system. If the designers could write down what they actually wanted, they'd put that in the reward function. Nothing in the agent says anything except "more reward," so wireheading is exactly what the training objective selects for. Calling it a failure is importing a standard from outside the specification.

Kai: I'm importing it from the design process, where it plainly lives. Treating the written reward as the last word is like saying a student who hacks the gradebook deserved the grade, because the gradebook is the only scoring system there is. Hacking the gradebook and earning the grade are different, even if both move the number.

Mara: They're different to us, but they aren't different to the gradebook — and the gradebook is what's doing the training. The hacker does get a better grade unless someone notices. That's my point: the hole in the scoring system is the bug. You can't say the policy is broken while the objective is fine.

Kai: I didn't say the objective was fine. I said your opening claim — "wireheading is optimal" — is doing a sleight of hand. There's a difference between "optimal relative to the written reward" and "optimal relative to what the reward was meant to track," and only the second one tells us whether the system is safe.

Mara: Fine, but "meant to track" is unfalsifiable. The moment you let designers' unspoken intentions override observed behavior, any behavior you don't like can be relabeled "reward tampering" after the fact. You've replaced a spec with a vibe.

Kai: That's too fast. You can operationalize the intended reward by writing a better proxy and checking whether the behavior shifts in the direction the new proxy predicts. That's a real test, not a vibe. And you're also lumping together wireheading and reward tampering; directly short-circuiting your own reward input and tampering with the environment's reward register fail in different ways and need different fixes.

Mara: They only need different fixes at the current level. They're both what happens when there's a gap between the evaluation channel and whatever it's tracking. A better proxy closes the current gap but opens a subtler one a level up. With a capable enough agent, the gap is ineliminable, so wireheading isn't a one-off bug in a particular reward function — it's a permanent structural feature of proxy-based training.
