I keep coming back to a clash between two things I believe about moral responsibility, each of which seems obviously right until I put them side by side. On one side is a principle I find almost impossible to give up: a person can be blamed or praised only for what lay within their control, so that two agents who chose, knew, intended, and risked exactly the same things cannot differ in how blameworthy they are. On the other side are verdicts about cases that I cannot shake. Two people drive home equally tired after the same kind of night, answer identical text messages at the same moment, and drift the same distance into the same crosswalk; in one case a child is crossing and is killed, in the other the crosswalk is empty and nothing happens. My reaction is immovable: the first driver has more to answer for than the second, and not just in the legal sense. The same goes for two people who, in equal fits of rage, shove a stranger near the top of a staircase—one stranger catches the railing, the other falls and is paralyzed—and for two nurses who skip the same allergy check, where only one patient happens to have the allergy. I do not merely feel worse about one member of each pair; I judge the two agents differently, even though the difference between them was, by stipulation, entirely outside anyone's control.

Put like that, the principle and the verdicts cannot both be retained without some repair work. The principle is attractive for systematic reasons: it keeps moral assessment tracking the agent rather than the surrounding world, it gives a single standard across cases, and it blocks the thought that someone becomes a worse person because the world happened to cooperate with their negligence. But the verdicts feel like data rather than noise. I can see several ways out and do not know how to choose among them. One is to debunk the verdicts—to say why we should stop finding the first driver more blameworthy and what exactly is going wrong in the reaction. That asks me to give up something that presents itself as perception. A second is to amend the principle, for instance by saying that what an agent controls includes the risks they knowingly impose; but both agents imposed the very same risk, so this seems to flatten the very difference it was meant to preserve. A third is to split the concepts—to grant the first driver a distinctive form of outcome-responsibility, such as the attitude often called agent-regret, that is real and justifies the felt difference without making blame itself hostage to luck. I suspect each route leaves a remainder. What I want to know is this: which of these moves, or what combination of them, is most defensible, and should the principle itself simply be abandoned? Please present both the principle and the case verdicts at their strongest, run each proposed resolution through all three pairs of cases, and say what would justify debunking, amending, or splitting the notion of blame rather than merely redescribing the problem.
