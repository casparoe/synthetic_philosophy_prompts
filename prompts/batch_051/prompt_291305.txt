I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in democratic theory: sixteen students, one eighty-minute session. Design a structured role-play that makes the participants feel the boundary problem from the inside — the discovery that the question who should have a say in a binding decision cannot be settled by appealing to the people, since identifying the people is precisely the question at issue. The scenario should be a founding moment: three municipalities of unequal size are being merged into a single district and must adopt, before any substantive vote, the inclusion rule for a binding referendum on a shared water-management plan whose costs and benefits cross the old boundaries unevenly. Build in the complications that force the problem into the open: commuters who work and pay local taxes but reside elsewhere; downstream residents outside the district who bear the plan's environmental effects; children and future residents who will live with the long-run consequences; and a minority of the smallest municipality that knows it will be outvoted under any residency-based rule. Participants should draw role cards with private interests that cut across the policy question, so that no position on the water plan lines up neatly with a position on who should vote on it. The rules should require them to adopt an inclusion rule under a tight deadline, then immediately subject that rule to the same scrutiny: once an inclusion rule is proposed, any participant may ask who decided that the rule's authors were entitled to decide. The activity must surface, through what participants do rather than through any upfront lecture, the contrast between the all-affected principle, which over-includes and threatens to extend the franchise to everyone causally downstream of the decision, and the all-subjected principle, which seems to presuppose a criterion of subjection and a prior demos. Provide role cards or position briefs, a timed agenda for the eighty minutes, the rules of deliberation and of the final vote, and a debrief that maps the specific moments in the play onto the formal structure of the boundary problem. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — especially the urge to fall back on existing administrative boundaries, on some imagined neutral framer, or on simple majority of whoever is already in the room — and how to handle each failure without naming the lesson for them. End with an honest note on the feature of the boundary problem that the role-play cannot reproduce and that I will need to supply myself.
