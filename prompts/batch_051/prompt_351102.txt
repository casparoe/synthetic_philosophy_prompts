I am a science and ideas journalist, and I am writing a feature on why the grandfather paradox — in which a time traveler removes one of their own grandparents from history before their parent is conceived — remains a live conceptual test rather than a solved puzzle. For one section of the piece I want a committed expert judgment, not a survey, so I am asking you to rank six candidate resolutions of the paradox by how defensible each is as an account of what would happen if backward time travel were genuinely possible. Here are the candidates, described without author names so that the ranking has to rest on the ideas themselves:

1. Chronology protection: backward time travel, or at least the kind of causal loop the paradox requires, is ruled out by the laws of physics, so the scenario never arises; the paradox is a reductio against visiting the past at all.
2. Self-consistency constraint: only self-consistent histories are permitted, so the traveler's attempt to kill their grandfather either fails through some intervening cause or turns out to have been part of how things always happened; probabilities near a time machine are constrained simply so that contradictions do not occur.
3. Branching histories: the act of changing the past splits off a new timeline or activates a separate world in which the grandparents never meet; the traveler's original history continues intact, so there is no contradiction, only a visit to a different branch.
4. Mutable single timeline: the past can literally be rewritten, and the traveler's birth is erased along with the original history; the contradiction is not blocked but overwritten, and coherence is abandoned rather than enforced.
5. Ill-posedness: the paradox dissolves because the key terms — "the traveler," "the grandfather," "the past" — do not pick out stable referents across the supposed change; once identities are disambiguated, there is no coherent state of affairs left to explain.
6. Ordinary self-defeat: no special physics is required; the nearer the traveler gets to succeeding, the likelier some mundane mishap becomes, and the murder simply fails by the same kind of chance that makes any particular unlikely outcome unsurprising in retrospect.

Rank these six in a strict order, with no ties, first to last, according to how defensible you find each as a resolution of the paradox, and commit to that ordering: I do not want a list of options with the pros and cons left for me to weigh in the article. Score them as if you had to defend one in print.

Return your answer as a single JSON object with the following fields:
- "ranking": an array of six objects, each with the fields:
  - "rank": an integer from 1 (most defensible) to 6 (least defensible)
  - "resolution": the short label I gave above
  - "score": a number between 0 and 1 reflecting how defensible you take the resolution to be
  - "verdict": one or two sentences giving the decisive reason it lands in that position
  - "confidence": one of "high", "medium", or "low", indicating how settled you are in that placement
- "tie_break_criterion": a sentence or two stating the principle you used to order close calls
- "bottom_line": up to three sentences stating which resolution you would actually defend in print and what evidence or argument would make you abandon it.
