"Bender and Koller defend a skeptical view of what large language models can achieve, using a thought experiment involving an octopus. Reconstruct the thought experiment, explain the conclusion they draw from it, and evaluate whether that conclusion is warranted. In particular, discuss how their argument relates to older skepticism about whether symbol-manipulating systems can understand language."

And here's the student's answer, verbatim (I've left there spelling as-is):

"The octopus thought experiment goes like this. Two castaways, A and B, are stuck on separate islands and can only communicate through an underwater telegraph cable. A deep-sea octopus wraps itself around the cable and listens in on their conversations for years. Eventually it gets so good at predicting how B will respond to A that it cuts the cable and starts replying to B while pretending to be A. The octopus has only ever seen the signals on the wire — the linguistic forms — and has never seen the coconuts, storms, or tools those forms refer to. So when B brings up something genuinely new, the octopus is exposed as a fraud. Bender and Koller's point is that an agent that only ever observes form can't learn meaning, and since LLMs are trained on text alone, they're in the octopus's position. The experiment is really just Searle's Chinese Room updated for language models: in both cases the lesson is that manipulating symbols, no matter how sophisticated, can never amount to genuine understanding. So the conclusion is that LLMs can't understand language at all, and no change to the training data or architecture could help. The only fix would be to give the system a body, but the authors explicitly reject embodiment. The argument succeeds because it's an in-principle argument, not an empirical one: meaning is underdetermined by form, so no amount of text could ever bridge the gap."
