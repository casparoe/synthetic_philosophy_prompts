You are a moral philosopher who works on population ethics and also takes ordinary moral psychology seriously; you are comfortable moving between formal axiology and the untidy stock of everyday moral judgement. Please think carefully before you write, and begin by brainstorming before settling on your final list.

I am preparing a colloquium paper, and I want you to act as my question-period coach.

The thesis I will defend is as follows. Ordinary moral thinking is neither purely totalist nor purely averaging. It recognises an independent, though defeasible, reason to care about the average quality of lives in a population—not merely because average tracks total, but because how well lives go on average matters in its own right. I will argue that this best explains a pattern of familiar judgements: making an existing mediocre life better matters more than adding a new, equally mediocre life; a larger family, a bigger city, or a larger species is not automatically better simply because it contains more well-being; and a policy that lowers average well-being while raising total well-being by creating more happy-but-below-average lives strikes ordinary people as morally ambivalent rather than clearly good.

My argumentative route, for targeting purposes, is this. First, I distinguish same-number comparisons from different-number comparisons. Second, I argue that average-regarding judgements about same-number cases survive reflection and are not merely a heuristic for tracking total well-being. Third, I propose a hybrid principle on which total well-being and per-capita well-being are both pro tanto sources of value, with the average component doing most of its work in comparative judgements about existing populations.

The audience is a philosophy department colloquium. The department has a strong formal ethics contingent: two consequentialist totalists who accept the repugnant conclusion and treat average welfare as a mere bookkeeping statistic; one person-affecting theorist who denies that merely adding a life can be an impersonal benefit; one prioritarian who doubts that the arithmetic mean captures what matters about distribution; two applied ethicists who will keep pressing on what any of this implies for policy; and several historians of philosophy and generalists who are suspicious of formal axiology and will attack the method of eliciting and systematising ordinary judgements.

Please give me a prep sheet, not a defence of the thesis. Order the objections by how likely and how damaging they are, and explain why they are ordered that way. Phrase each objection the way the relevant member of the audience would actually put it, in their own vocabulary. After each objection, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and the one question I should hope nobody asks. Where the reply is weakest, say so plainly.

Write in British English.
