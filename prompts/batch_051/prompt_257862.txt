I keep bumping into a pattern in arguments about sweatshop labor and I can't tell whether it has a name or whether I'm slicing the concept in a way nobody else does. Two examples.

A clothing factory in a poor country. Everyone in the argument accepts the facts: the pay is awful by my standards, the hours are long, the safety record is spotty. But they also accept that the workers who take these jobs are better off then they would be under their next-best option, which might be subsistence farming or riskier informal work. People line up for the jobs; nobody is being forced at gunpoint. Yet one side calls it exploitation, and the other side says that can't be right, because the worker gained relative to their alternative — how can a voluntary, improvement-for-both-sides exchange be exploitation?

A landlord rents to someone facing eviction with nowhere else to go. The rent is steep, the unit is mediocre, but it really is better than a shelter or sleeping rough. The tenant signs willingly. Most people I describe this to feel the landlord is doing something shabby, but they can't point to a harm in the ordinary sense: the tenant's situation improved relative to the fallback.

So the pattern is: a voluntary deal where both sides end up better off than their fallback options can still strike us as wrong, and the wrongness isn't captured by 'harm' or 'coercion' in any straightforward way. I'm not asking whether these deals are actually wrong — I know that's the live argument. What I want to know is whether the concept has a name: the idea that exploitation can occur even when nobody is made worse off relative to their alternative, because the terms or the background options are themselves the problem. Or is this just ordinary unfairness in a tight spot, and calling it exploitation is a category mistake?

If there is a name, I don't just want the label. I want the carving too: what separates this from a rough but non-exploitative negotiation, whether it requires the exploiter to intentionally profit off the other person's constrained options, and whether 'better off' has to mean better off in absolute terms or just better than a terrible baseline. If there's no settled name, what would you call it, and where would you draw the line?
