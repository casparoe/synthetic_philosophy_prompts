I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so I'm going to mangle some terms; bear with me. This is for a book about a woman who has spent decades in the same job, same town, same routine, and I want a thought experiment I can dramatize in a scene.

The two positions I've been turning over are these. The alphabet view: a finite mind has a finite stock of experiential atoms—sensory qualities, emotional tones, bodily feelings, that sort of thing—plus rules for combining them. An experience counts as new just in case it's a combination of those atoms that hasn't occured before. So the total space of possible experiences is finite, though for any real person it's so huge that an ordinary life almost never gets near the bottom.

The sense-making view: novelty isn't about whether the atoms have been recombined. An experience is new only if it shifts or complicates the person's ongoing interpretation of their own life. On this view, someone can live through countless new atom-combinations that all amount to the same day, and a single repeated combination can count as new because it means something different now.

Here's what I want built. A case—one paragraph, realistic, no immortality, no sci-fi, no perfect memory—where the alphabet view is forced to say the subject is still having genuinely new experiences, while the sense-making view is forced to say their experience has been exhausted for years. That's the reverse of the way you'd expect the two views to lean, and I want the case itself to make them lean that way, not just stipulate it.

After the scenario, walk through what each view says about it and why. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed, and how a defender of the sense-making view would attack the case to stop it from counting—and what the alphabet theorist should say back.

I don't care which view is true; I care that the case has exactly that shape and could be set in an ordinary life.
