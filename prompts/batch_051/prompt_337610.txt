Consider the claim that democratic backsliding picks out a real process — an incumbent systematically loosening the institutional and informal constraints on its own power — and is not merely a partisan description of elected governments one dislikes. Using Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States as familiar illustrations rather than as the objects of any ready-made verdict, design the strongest available empirical test, or small battery of indicators, that would help tell these two readings apart. Be concrete about which changes you would measure, over what time horizon, and through which kinds of evidence; then state what pattern of results would support the claim that backsliding is real and what pattern would support the view that the term is mostly a partisan label. After that, say explicitly what a positive result would and would not settle: in particular, whether the baseline of democratic practice against which backsliding is measured can itself be specified without begging contested questions, whether the indicators can be weighted in a politically neutral way, and whether the final judgment inevitably retains a normative component. End by taking a position on whether the concept is more than a partisan label, and if so, which part of it is established empirically and which part remains a normative or contestable judgment.
