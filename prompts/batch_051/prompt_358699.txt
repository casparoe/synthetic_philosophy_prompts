Act as a professional philosopher who specializes in death and value theory. I want a specification, not a manifesto. What would a theory of death's badness actually have to get right? I keep seeing people argue about whether Epicurus or Lucretius is right by pointing at one case—the postponed flight, the condemned prisoner, posthumous reputation, a stillborn infant—but I want the full requirements list first. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it. In particular, a decent theory has to handle Lucretius's mirror argument from De Rerum Natura: if we don't think the eternity before our birth was bad for us, is that enough to show the eternity after our death isn't bad either, or does a theory need to explain the asymmetry? It also has to seperate being dead from dying, say whether death can harm a person at all rather than just harm the survivors, and respect the Epicurean idea that a harm must be experienced by someone at some time. Then tell me which requirements are negotiable and which are not, and which single requirement knocks out the most popular theories. A table is fine. Think carefully, and don't just list criteria; give the motivating case for each.
