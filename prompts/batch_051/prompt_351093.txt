A while back I was taught a four-part test for deciding when a legal system is right to let the passage of time bar prosecution. The test goes: (1) the evidence has decayed to the point where a fair trial is doubtful; (2) the deterrent benefit of punishing this late is negligible; (3) the offender is no longer the person who did it in any sense that matters, because they've reformed or simply aged out; (4) victims and the community have a real interest in finality, in the state not reaching back forever. The lecturer insisted these weren't just practical conveniences — they were the moral content of time, the reason time itself should matter to law.

Now consider a case a friend of mine who works in a cold-case unit raised. A man killed someone in a bar fight when he was 23, left town the next day, and then lived a completely ordinary and law-abiding life for 38 years: marriage, kids, a steady job, no arrests, nothing. A DNA review just tied him to the killing and he's been charged at 61. The jurisdiction has no statute of limitations for homicide, so the full machinery of prosecution kicks in. He doesn't dispute the facts; he says only that he isnt that person anymore.

Go through the four-part test and say whether this case qualifies as one where time should have barred prosecution even though the law doesn't. Where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, say so. And address the deeper question the case seems designed to raise: does any of the four criteria capture something about time itself, or do they all reduce to non-temporal facts about evidence, deterrence, personality change, and closure — time passing doing no moral work on its own? Is the no-limitation rule for homicide getting the moral relevance of time wrong here, or is this exactly the kind of case where time shouldn't matter? Does the case show the four-part test needs revising, or that the test was fine and the real pressure is somewhere else?
