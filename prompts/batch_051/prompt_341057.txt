I've been trying to get clear on the problem of old evidence and I keep going in circles. I have no formal philosophy background, so I'd like the structure evaluated rather than a loose description of the issue. Here is the argument as I currently understand it.

1. Let K be my background knowledge at the moment I first take a hypothesis H seriously, and let E be some evidence I already accepted long before H was on the table.
2. Since E is already part of what I know, K entails E, so P(E | K) = 1.
3. A standard Bayesian measure of confirmation says that E confirms H relative to K just in case P(H | K ∧ E) > P(H | K).
4. But if P(E | K) = 1, then P(H | K ∧ E) = P(H | K), because conditioning on a proposition already holding with probability 1 cannot shift other probabilities.
5. Therefore old evidence can never confirm a new hypothesis; any impression that it does is a mistake or a story we tell afterward.
6. Science seems to contradict this. The anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion was known well before general relativity was formulated, yet it is routinely treated as strong confirmation of general relativity.

Each step feels individually plausible, but step 5 feels false. Is the argument valid as written? If not, say exactly which step fails and what the corrected step should say. If it is valid but the conclusion is misleading, explain what the formalization leaves out and how old evidence should be modeled so that it can genuinely confirm a new hypothesis.
