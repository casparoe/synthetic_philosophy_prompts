I spent my career on insect population dynamics and only got into philosophy after retiring, so the insect welfare debate is where my old and new interests overlap. I'm trying to piece together the adversarial-collaboration document two camps should have produced after argueing for a year. One is an expected-value expansionist: she says even if you're only 2% confident that a larval Drosophila's nociceptive rolling is conscious pain, and there are on the order of 10^18 insects alive at any time, the expected suffering is too big to shrug off. The other is a robustness skeptic: he says the probability is too mushy to license that arithmetic, and multiplying a vague credence by a huge head count is basically a Pascal's mugging. Draft the document they'd both sign: (1) a numbered list of non-trivial claims both accept about insect nociception, avoidance learning, and where the evidence gives out; (2) a table of the remaining disagreements worded so each side accepts the phrasing of their own view; (3) two or three cruxes where settling one would move at least one side. I suspect one crux is whether nociceptive withdrawal and injury-grooming are evidence of a unified pain state rather than a reflex, and another is whether probability of sentience is even a well-defined quantity you can multiply by head count. For each crux give a concrete observation or experiment both sides agree bears on it, and record what each predicts in advance. They do both accept, for instance, that Drosophila larvae show nociceptive withdrawal and that some insects groom injured limbs; the fight is over what that shows. Dont resolve the cruxes.
