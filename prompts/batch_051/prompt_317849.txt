I've been going back and forth with a flatmate about whether a machine could literally feel pain, and the conversation keeps ending at the same place. Her view is that what makes a mental state the state it is isn't what it's made of but what it does: a pain is a state caused by tissue damage and causing withdrawal, wincing, self-report and so on, so a silicon system with exactly those causes and effects would be in pain, not just simulating it. A more sceptical friend says that's a definition dressed up as an empirical claim, since all we can ever observe is behaviour and mechanism, never whether some non-biological system is actually feeling anything. I'm not sure there's nothing to test. Someone floated a gradual-replacement experiment: swap out neurons in a mouse's pain pathway one at a time for artifical units that match each neuron's input-output behaviour, and see whether the mouse still behaves as if it's in pain as the replacement spreads. But I can't get clear on what outcome would settle what.

Could you design the best test you can of the claim that a non-biological system with the same functional organisation as a pain-state really is in pain? Make it concrete: what would you measure or manipulate, which pattern of results would count for the claim and which against, and then say honestly what even a clean positive result would still leave open — whether that residue is just a limitation of the test or a hole in the concept of 'really being in pain' itself. And please write the whole answer in British English.
