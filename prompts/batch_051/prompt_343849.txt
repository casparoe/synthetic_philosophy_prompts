I'm in a metaphysics of causation course right now, and causation by omission is the bit I keep getting stuck on. I'm strongly attracted to the counterfactual account: c caused e just in case, had c not occurred, e would not have occurred. It's simple, it unifies a huge range of cases, and it gives a clean answer to why anything counts as a cause. But there's a case I can't shake. Suppose I go away for a weekend, my flatmate forgets to water my plants, and the plants die. Her not watering them caused the death — I'd say that without hesitation. Yet by the same counterfactual test, some stranger on the other side of the world who has never heard of me also caused my plants to die, because if that stranger had watered them, they wouldn't have died. And so did everyone else who didn't water them. That strikes me as flatly false, not just misleading — I don't think the stranger caused my plants to die at all. So I've got a theory I don't want to give up and an intuition I don't want to disown, and I'm not interested in someone just picking a side. I want a map of the ways out. Under what conditions should I drop the intuition and accept that all those omissions really are causes, treating my resistance to the stranger case as driven by salience or blame or legal convention rather than by the metaphysics? Under what conditions should I amend or abandon the theory instead — for instance by restricting causes to positive events, or by adding a normality or default condition so that only omissions that depart from what we'd expect count as causes? Is there a stable third route on which both survive, maybe by seperating causal relevance from causal selection, or the metaphysics of causes from our practice of picking out 'the' cause in explanation and law? And whichever way you lean, be explicit about the price. If omissions aren't genuinely causes, what happens to negligent omissions, or to the claim that a parent's failure to vaccinate caused their child to get measles? If all those counterfactual omissions are causes after all, do we just have to live with the idea that almost everything almost everyone didn't do caused almost everything that happened? I want an honest verdict on which costs are worse, not a compromise that hides what we're giving up.
