A friend and I have been going back and forth about digital minds and moral patienthood, and somewhere in that argument he produced a chain of reasoning that I now think has to be wrong, because the conclusion is absurd, but I can't find the step where it breaks. I'm honestly at the point where I'm considering just accepting the absurdity.

(1) Whether a system has moral status depends on its mind, not on what it's made of. If something running on silicon has the same functional organization as a human brain, it has the same moral status as a human. (If you deny this, you're stuck saying "carbon chauvanism" and digital minds never matter no matter how good the emulation gets, which I really don't want to say.)

(2) A physical system implements a functional organization whenever there's a mapping from that system's physical states to the organization's formal states such that the system's actual transitions are carried to transitions the organization allows.

(3) For almost any physical system you point at — a rock, a wall, a puddle — there is *some* mapping from its microphysical states over a short chunk of time onto the state space of a waking human brain, provided you allow the usual tricks: hugely disjunctive states, states the system occupies under tons of microscopically different conditions that don't matter to us, and transitions defined to match whatever the thing was doing anyway.

(4) So by (2) and (3), the rock in my garden implements a human mind during those intervals. And by (1), the rock has full moral status during those intervals.

(5) But a rock doesn't have a mind in any sense that matters, and no sane moral theory owes it the kind of consideration we owe a person, even in brief bursts.

(6) So at least one of (1), (2), or (3) has to go. But (1) is the whole reason anyone thinks digital minds matter; and rejecting (2) or (3) means I need a non-ad-hoc account of "real" implementation, and every one I've tried — internal states have to do causal work, later states have to depend counterfactually on earlier ones, the mapping has to be simple or natural — either comes out circular or ends up excluding big lookup-table uploads or brains that are briefly not doing much.

Where does this actually go wrong? Which premise or step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically, if the fix is a restriction on what counts as implementation, tell me what restriction and whether it quietly gives up on any class of digital minds we'd otherwise want to count; if the fix is denying (1), tell me how digital minds can still matter at all.
