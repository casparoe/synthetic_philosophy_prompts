A reading group I'm in is working through Hofstadter, and the same argument keeps coming up. One regular defends it with an analogy: a person's self is a program, the brain is just the hardware it happens to run on, and when someone knows you well enough a lower-resolution copy of that program has been installed on their hardware too. On this picture, after you die the program is still running in maybe six other people, so saying you live on in others isnt just a comforting phrase; it's closer to a technical claim about where your self is located. The analogy is doing real work for him: he uses it to license the conclusion that the self is genuinely distributed across brains, and that treating the skull as the boundary of you is a kind of hardware chauvinism.

I want an audit of how far this analogy actually holds, not a list of every way it's imperfect. Give me the correspondence table first: what maps to the program, the original hardware, the copied hardware, the fidelity of the copy, the running process, and the original machine's death. Then say where the mapping breaks, and sort the disanalogies into the ones that matter for the conclusion and the ones that are merely cosmetic. In particular, I want to know which features of software the analogy quietly imports into the picture of a self without earning them—things like identical copies, no first-person point of view, no agency, or the copy freezing at the moment it's made. Then take a definite position: once the broken mappings are on the table, does the conclusion that an I is distributed across brains survive, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument? If you think a better analogy would do less damage, propose it and say what it gets right that the software one gets wrong.
