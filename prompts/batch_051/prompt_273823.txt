I've recently taken over a methods workshop on causal inference for graduate students in philosophy and public health—fourteen people, seventy-five minutes—and I'm planning a session on why the algorithms they already use should be trusted. Most of them can fit Bayesian networks or run causal discovery software, but few can say why the sparsest graph that reproduces the observed independence relations deserves belief. I want a structured activity, not a lecture, in which they act as the model-selection procedure themselves: from a small joint distribution over three or four variables, they propose graphs and are scored for fit and simplicity, and at some stage meet a constructed case where the best-scoring graph is wrong because two pathways in the true graph cancel out exactly, leaving an independence the true graph does not entail. The debrief should map their choices to faithfulness and minimality, say what will predictably go wrong the first time it runs and how to handle it, and be explicit about which aspect of the view no classroom game can reproduce. Please provide rules, materials, a timeline, and a point-based payoff scheme that uses no real money; the terms "faithfulness" and "minimality" should not be named until the debrief.
