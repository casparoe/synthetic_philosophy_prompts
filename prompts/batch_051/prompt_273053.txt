You are a professional philosopher specializing in analytic philosophy of religion, especially the problem of evil. I am a retired forest ecologist who has recently begun reading philosophy seriously, and I want you to build a diagnostic instrument for a reading group of scientifically literate nonspecialists—people who will answer concrete cases honestly but have no patience for jargon.

Please begin by brainstorming the logical space of possible responses to the problem of evil before drafting anything. The final deliverable should be a "which position on the problem of evil do you actually hold?" sorting quiz with nine questions and a scoring key. Its purpose is to sort readers by what they say about concrete cases, not by which label they like; accordingly, no question may mention any named position, and every question should be answerable by someone who has never heard of the standard theodicies.

Use the following five benchmark cases, at least one question each, and add four further cases of your own design, chosen specifically to separate positions that these five leave entangled. At least four of the nine questions should concern natural evil, understood as suffering whose proximate cause is not the free choice of a moral agent.

1. A fawn dies slowly and alone in a lightning-caused forest fire; no human is present and the death goes unobserved.
2. A young child dies slowly of a painful genetic disease before developing the capacity for moral agency.
3. A competent adult freely commits a brutal assault on an innocent person; the assailant is never caught, and no good visibly comes of the act.
4. An entire species of cognitively complex animals is wiped out suddenly by an asteroid impact, with no surviving members.
5. A person endures a severe injury and years of painful recovery, and later reports that the experience was the occasion of deep moral and spiritual development.

For each question, present the case and offer three or four answer options that express genuinely different responses: for example, treating the case as evidence against God, demanding a reason tied to this very event, accepting a general lawful or developmental system as sufficient, saying we are in no position to know, or saying that explanation is the wrong response entirely. Do not use these as fixed boilerplate; write each question and its options around the specific pressure of that case.

The scoring key should sort answer patterns into at least the following seven leaves, though you may add an eighth or ninth if the answer space demands it: the problem succeeds (naturalist/atheist); skeptical theism; free-will defense; soul-making theodicy; lawlike-world or natural-order theodicy; universalist/redemptive theodicy; anti-theodicy. Throughout the scoring key, preserve the distinction between a defense—which shows only that God's existence is compatible with some evil—and a theodicy—which offers a positive, putatively true justification—and mark any leaf that is a defense rather than a theodicy.

The angle of this quiz is the following, and it is not optional. Build it around three diagnostic distinctions; define them briefly in a preamble to the quiz and make sure both the questions and the scoring key respect them.

(a) Token-level versus pattern-level justification: whether God must have a reason for permitting this very event involving this very sufferer, or whether it is enough that the event falls under a general pattern or system—lawlike regularities, free creaturely development, soul-making conditions—whose overall value justifies it.

(b) Graspable versus in-principle-ungraspable reasons: whether a justifying reason, if it exists, must be one that finite creatures could in principle recognize as such, or whether God may have a genuine reason that is cognitively unavailable to us.

(c) Victim-relative versus world-relative defeat: whether an evil is adequately outweighed only if the good comes to the victim in a way the victim could recognize as redeeming or compensating the suffering, or whether it may be outweighed impersonally by goods that are not the victim's.

After the scoring key, include a short diagnostic appendix. Go through the answer-pattern space and mark every combination of answers that scores to no standard position, saying for each whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because nobody has explored it. Also flag any two leaves the quiz collapses into the same answer pattern, and say what additional case or question would separate them. For each leaf in the scoring key, add one sentence on the position's most awkward commitment, so a reader can see what they would be signing up for.

Take the time to reason through the design before writing the final draft, but do not show the scratchwork. The output should contain only the preamble, the nine questions with their answer options, the scoring key, and the diagnostic appendix.
