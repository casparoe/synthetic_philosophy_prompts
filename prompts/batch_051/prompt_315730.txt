So my friend and I keep having this fight, and last night it got long enough that we decided to turn it into something productive. He's basically a per-calorie death-count person: he's convinced that if you're going to eat meat, chicken is clearly worse than beef, since a single steer yields a huge number of calories whereas a broiler is small, so a thousand calories of chicken means killing roughly an order of magnitude more individual animals, and at some point the sheer count has to dominate. I'm more of a whole-production-system person, and I think he has it backwards: beef looks good only if you refuse to count land use, the feed crops grown for cattle, the wild animals displaced or killed when that land is converted or worked, and the fact that a cow is a longer-lived animal with a much richer mental life, so each cow's death and suffering plausibly weighs a lot more than each chicken's. We each trot out the same gotcha against the other: he says my land-use logic would make grains look worse than meat once you count field mice and insects in row crops; I say his pure-count logic would make a thousand crickets morally swamp one cow, which is exactly the kind of result you should distrust. We're both honestly unsure about the wild-animal-suffering side of this, but we've agreed its time to write down what we actually think and where the real cruxes are.

Write the joint statement the two of us should be able to both sign, the way an adversarial collaboration would report it. Don't adjudicate between us and don't add an introduction or conclusion; the document itself is the deliverable. Write it in first-person plural, as "we." Use exactly these four titled sections, in order:

1. Agreed claims (about 150 words): a numbered list of claims both of us accept, including the empirical uncertainty both sides can live with. If a claim is actually contested, it belongs in the next section.
2. Genuine disagreements, stated neutrally (about 200 words): each disagreement as a single proposition we can both agree captures the disagreement, followed by one sentence for each of us saying what our own view is.
3. Cruxes (about 250 words): the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of us; for each, say whose mind would change in what direction, and why.
4. What would change our minds (about 200 words): for each crux, a concrete number, scenario, or argument that both of us agree would bear on it, and what each side predicts in advance. Make the predictions exact enough that we can later say someone was wrong.
