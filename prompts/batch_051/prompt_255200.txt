Me and a colleague keep having the same fight about vagueness and I'd love an external audit of whether it's even an empirical fight. She's an epistemicist: for predicates like 'heap' there's a sharp cutoff in number of grains, we just can't know where it is because knowledge needs a margin for error. I lean supervaluationist: I think 'heap' is true on all admissible ways of sharpening the predicate, false on all of them for the clear non-heaps, and neither true nor false in the borderline region, so there's no boundary fact to be ignorant of. What frustrates me is that the two views make the same obvious predictions: speakers are good on clear cases, inconsistent near the boundary, and both can keep classical logic (even 'there is a cutoff somewhere' comes out true on both, though for different reasons). So I half think no observation could every choose between them. She disagrees and says behavioral data could, e.g. binary forced choices on adjacent pairs under time pressure, with incentives for self-consistency — maybe the pattern of errors, confidence, and within-subject stability differs between a hidden sharp line and an indeterminate zone. So design the strongest feasible test (or battery) to pull the two views apart. Spell out what result pattern should push toward epistemicism, what toward supervaluationism, and then say exactly what even a clean result would fail to settle — which part of the disagreement is stubbornly about hidden semantic facts rather than anything measurable.
