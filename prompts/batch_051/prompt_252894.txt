I spent my career as an organic chemist, and I was used to classifications that are either right or wrong in a rather plain sense: a molecule either has a stereocenter or it does not, a functional group either survives a reaction or is destroyed. Since retiring into philosophy, I have repeatedly encountered a view of mathematics that sounds temptingly similar, though it concerns objects no instrument can inspect. The view is that mathematical structures have their own seams, that a correct definition is one which has managed to trace a seam rather than slice across it, and that the abstract concepts mathematicians study were therefore discovered rather than invented. In discussions of why the modern group concept replaced the older permutation-theoretic algebra, or why open sets came to supply the standard axioms of topology instead of metric, neighborhood, or convergence formulations, I have heard it maintained that groups and open sets are the right notions in just the way that a jointing is the right cut: the mathematician is compared to a butcher who separates an animal along natural divisions instead of hacking through bone. The conclusion this image is used to underwrite is that there is a fact of the matter, independent of our interests and our history, about which definition is correct. I would like you to audit how far this analogy actually holds, the way an experimentalist audits a proposed mechanism. Give the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the butcher, the knife, the carcass, the joints? — then separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative, and finally tell me whether the discovered-rather-than-stipulated claim survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If it survives only partly, say what a better analogy would be.

While auditing, please work through at least one of the two cited cases in real detail — groups, say, or open sets — and address a hunch of mine. In a carcass, the joints are there before the butcher arrives, and butchers with different aims would find the same ones; in mathematics, by contrast, what counts as a natural seam seems to shift with the chosen notion of sameness. A topologist who regards homeomorphic spaces as the same will find different seams than one who regards only homotopy type as invariant, and the open set formulation is itself intertranslatable with closure-operator and neighborhood formulations, which makes the idea of a single pre-existing joint look suspicious. I am not asking you to refute the analogy; I am asking how much of its argumentative work is smuggling in a picture of mathematics as a pre-carved landscape, and whether the conclusion it was used to support is actually being carried by that smuggled picture.
