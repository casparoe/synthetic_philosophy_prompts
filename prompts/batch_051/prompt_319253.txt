I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether the universe is, in principle, predictable. He started from the old clockwork picture — the one Laplace's demon is supposed to illustrate: if the world is deterministic, and you somehow had the complete state of the universe at a single time plus all the laws, then a sufficiently powerful calculator could compute the state at any later time. So the future would be fixed and knowable in advance, and human ignorance would just be a practical limit on memory and speed.

Then, because he works with cellular automata and can't stop talking about Wolfram, he brought in computational irreducibility. His gloss was: for many deterministic systems there's no shortcut. The only way to find out what a system will do is to run the system itself, so even an ideal predictor with arbitrarily large but still finite resources couldn't get ahead of the thing it's simulating. He concluded that this breaks the demon's promise — the future isn't computable in advance from state-plus-laws — and that determinism therefore doesn't get you explainability.

I don't know whether that kills the old argument or just quietly changes the demon into an actually infinite being doing infinite computation in zero time, which feels like cheating. Which premise of the clockwork picture is computational irreducibility actually attacking? If the objection lands, is there a weaker claim that survives — something like: the future is still fixed, but the only complete 'explanation' of it might be the whole things own history rather than any compressed rule we could state ahead of time? And should this change what I count as a good explanation, or is explanation a different game than prediction to begin with? Tell me what a careful defender of the original argument should concede and what they should dig in on.
