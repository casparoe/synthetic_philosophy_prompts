I’ve been turning over an argument that keeps surfacing in discussions with some LessWrong-adjacent friends about whether language models are fundamentally confined to recombining their training data, or whether they could in principle make the kind of discontinuous jump a working mathematician makes when she hits on a new proof strategy. One of them keeps defending the following skeptical argument, and I keep feeling there must be a mistake in it, but I cannot locate the step precisely. It runs as follows:

(1) At inference time, a language model samples from a distribution determined by two things: its weights and the prompt. The weights are fixed before any particular inference, and the prompt is whatever the user supplies.
(2) The weights are produced by a training run: a finite corpus, a fixed architecture, an optimizer, and — idealizing slightly — a deterministic procedure with a fixed random seed.
(3) Therefore, across all possible prompts, the model’s output distribution is a function of the training corpus plus the architecture and the training algorithm.
(4) Since World → Corpus → Model → Output is, under those idealizations, a Markov chain, the data-processing inequality applies: the output cannot carry more information about the world than the corpus does. So whatever the model says must, in the only sense that matters, already be contained in the corpus.
(5) But a genuine intuitive leap is precisely an output not contained in the creator’s prior corpus: a new conjecture that turns out to be true, a proof idea, an experimental design. Such an output is by definition outside the training distribution.
(6) Therefore no language model can make a genuine intuitive leap; anything that looks like one is either a memorized or trivially recombined fragment of the training data, or blind sampling that merely lands on the truth by accident.

I half-believe this argument, but I am suspicious of step (4): the data-processing inequality says something about mutual information between the output and a source, and I am not sure it licenses the inference that no output can be intelligibly novel relative to the corpus — a string no competent reader would call “already in” the training data. But I cannot make that suspicion precise. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and under what interpretation of the step and the conclusion? In particular, if the flaw is in step (4), give the strongest reading of the data-processing inequality on which that step is true, and say whether the argument still goes through on that reading. If the argument is valid, then state the strongest sense of “novelty” that remains available to such systems, and whether that residual sense is enough to count as an intuitive leap.
