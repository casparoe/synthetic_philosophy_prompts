I am a high school junior who will be presenting a paper at my school's philosophy club next month, followed by an open question period of about twenty minutes. My thesis is that the category of the supererogatory does not mark a real boundary between what morality requires and what it merely recommends; rather, it marks an enforcement boundary. On my view, any act we describe as supererogatory is an act impartial morality already endorses, but which we refrain from demanding as a duty because the cost or risk to the agent would make the demand unfair or too harsh. The act itself is not beyond duty; we simply decline to hold nonperformers responsible.

My argument in outline is this. First, in easy rescue cases the act is treated as required, and the reason is that the harm is serious, the cost to the agent is small, and we blame those who refuse. Second, when the cost or risk becomes extreme, the strength of the moral reasons does not change; what changes is that we stop blaming the refuser, because demanding heroism of everyone would make moral life too harsh. Third, therefore the line between the required and the supererogatory tracks nothing intrinsic to the act or to the reasons for performing it; it tracks our judgment about how much sacrifice we can demand. I expect the hardest objections to land on the second premise, and on the suspicion that I have not preserved supererogation but abolished it under a friendlier name.

Here is the audience I will face. The faculty advisor is a Kantian: she treats duty as binding regardless of whether we can enforce it, and she is suspicious of any account that lets considerations like cost or compassion determine what is owed. A religious studies teacher will also attend, and he holds that there is a real difference between acts that are commanded and acts that are merely held up as ideals or counsels, because the moral life needs space for saints and for extraordinary devotion. The strongest student member is an act utilitarian, who will say that if impartial morality really endorses the heroic act, then failing to perform it is simply wrong and my enforcement limit is a form of leniency. Another senior member is a virtue ethicist, who will object that I am still working with acts and duties instead of character and practical wisdom. The rest of the club has no settled theory but responds strongly to particular cases, especially the contrast between an easy rescue and the bystander who runs into a burning building.

Write me a Q&A preparation sheet. I want the objections I will actually receive from this audience, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do. Phrase each objection the way the relevant person would actually raise it. For each one, tell me what I should grant, what I should resist, give me a short reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and note the part of the reply that would be most exposed under follow-up. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why it is the dangerous one.
