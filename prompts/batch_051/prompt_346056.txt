I've been arguing with two other students in my high school's philosophy of probability group, and we have reached an impasse that I would like help resolving. One of them maintains that to call an outcome possible just is to say it has some non-zero probability, so possibility and positive probability march more or less in step. The other counters that a dart thrown at a continuous interval will land at exactly one point, yet the probability of each exact point is zero, so possibility can come apart from positive probability. The first replies that zero is still a probability assignment, so this does not yet show that possibility can outrun probability altogether. The argument has now sharpened into the following claim: there are no possibilities without probabilities — for every well-specified possible outcome, a precise probability, at least zero, could in principle be given. The student defending the contrary position has described a lottery that is supposed to be fair over all the integers: each integer can win, and no integer is favored over any other, but no uniform probability distribution over the integers sums to 1 under the usual rules.

I want your own credence in the claim that there are no possibilities without probabilities, not a survey of positions. Please decompose your number into the distinct sub-questions it depends on — for example, how much weight you place on the difference between metaphysical, physical, and epistemic possibility; whether the fairness of the lottery is a constraint that must be respected or an optional extra; whether probabilities must satisfy countable additivity; and whether a failure to construct a model is evidence against the possibility of the thing itself. Give a rough probability for each component and show how you combine them. Finally, tell me which component your answer is most sensitive to, and what argument or evidence would most change your mind.
