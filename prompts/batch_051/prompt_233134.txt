I've been fighting with the inefficacy objection — the line that says your individual contribution to a big collective harm won't make enough difference to matter, so you might as well not bother. I need a pair of cases, both ordinary and realistic, where an individual is deciding whether to pay a small personal cost to avoid adding one unit to a large collective harm (one car trip's emissions, one farm's runoff, one vote for a harmful policy). Same number of other potential contributors, same stakes, same cost, and the agent's beliefs about how many other people will contribute — the whole distribution — exactly the same in both. The only thing that's allowed to differ is the structure of how units add up to harm.

Case A should be one where the inefficacy objection goes through: on expected-utility grounds, the person's unit has essentially zero expected effect, so paying the cost buys nothing. Case B should be one where the objection fails: the expected effect of the very same kind of unit is clearly large enough to justify the cost, even though the probablity this particular unit is the one that tips things over the edge is tiny.

To keep it honest, make both cases threshold-structured (a tipping point the total gets pushed past), so the contrast can't come from the cheap move of making one case a continuous dose-response and the other a threshold. After you lay out the cases, run the expected-value calculation for each and show exactly why the numbers come out the way they do. Then say which feature of the aggregation is doing all the work, what happens to the two verdicts if you slide the threshold, and how a defender of the inefficacy objection as a general argument would attack Case B — and what your reply to that attack is.

This is for a class handout on collective action, so the cases can't just stipulate the verdicts; they have to generate them from the structure.
