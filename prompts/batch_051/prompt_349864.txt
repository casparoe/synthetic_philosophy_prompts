I am a novelist, and the vocabulary I think in is the vocabulary of character craft: flat versus round characters, want versus need, agency, flaw, arc, narrative sympathy, and the assumption that a character's choices only carry weight when genuine alternatives are live. I recently encountered a debate in moral philosophy over whether a morally perfect person, someone who devotes the whole of life to maximizing moral virtue at the expense of aesthetic, intellectual, and personal passions, is an ideal we should want to approximate, or is instead a strangely barren and unappealing figure whose life no one would choose. Restate each side of that debate in my craft vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report: which of the philosophers' moves translate cleanly into claims about why paragons make unsatisfying protagonists, which ones translate only if their meaning is quietly changed, and which have no counterpart in the craft of character at all. Finally, assess the residue. Is my working novelist's assumption that flawless virtue is narratively inert a reason to think the philosophers are tracking a real truth about human lives, or is it a professional deformation that biases me against the very possibility of an admirable, perfectly good character?
