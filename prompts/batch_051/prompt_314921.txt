I am a philosophy professor who works on the value of states of mind, and I have been collecting my own judgments about pain in the hope of extracting a theory from them. I would like you to work out what principle, if any, captures my judgments, and to tell me frankly where I would have to give something up.

Consider the following cases.

First, the hot pan. When I touch a hot pan, I feel a sharp, localised, searing sensation, and the pain itself seems bad in a way that is not exhausted by the fact that it signals tissue damage or makes me withdraw. If the same damage occurred without that awful feeling, something important would be missing. Verdict: the felt pain is non-instrumentally bad.

Second, pain asymbolia. A patient with pain asymbolia can describe a pinprick or a hand held in icy water as sharp or burning, and can grade its intensity, but she reports that it does not bother her, does not wince or withdraw, and sometimes will not move away from a damaging stimulus even while accurately saying that it hurts. My verdict: her experience is genuinely pain, but it is not bad in the way my burn is bad. The ingredient that makes pain bad seems to be missing in her case.

Third, the same asymbolic patient some hours later. Because she does not guard an injured limb or avoid damaging heat, she sustains wounds a normal person would have prevented. My verdict: this is a serious bad, but the badness attaches to the injuries and to a lost protective system, not to the felt sensation itself.

Fourth, the masochist. A person in a consensual sexual setting seeks out and reports positively enjoying the very sensations he describes as burning, stinging pain. My verdict: the pain is not bad for him in that context, and may even be good. I notice, though, that this differs from the asymbolia case: there the condition of being bothered is absent, whereas here the caring is present but its polarity is reversed.

Fifth, the wounded soldier. A soldier, immediately after being wounded, reports that he can feel the wound but is not bothered by it — he says the adrenaline has taken the edge off — and he keeps working as if nothing is wrong. An hour later he reports that the same pain was horrible all along. My verdict: I want to say the pain was bad for him even during the hour in which he was not bothered by it.

Sixth, the runner's endorphin high. A mildly injured runner notices that the ache in her leg is present but reports it as not unpleasant, almost agreeable, and not in the least aversive. My verdict: the ache is not bad for her while it is in that state, though I worry that this verdict is too generous.

The pair that unsettles me is the second and the fifth. The asymbolic patient and the wounded soldier both report pain that does not currently bother them, yet I say the first pain is not bad and the second is bad all along. Is there a difference in kind that justifies that asymmetry, or is one of the verdicts simply mistaken?

What I want is a principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that draws the line correctly across all six cases. For each candidate, please tell me which of my judgments it forces me to revise, whether any more complex principle saves all six at an acceptable cost, and what the principle predicts for cases I have not listed: for example, a person with chronic low-intensity pain that she has learned to ignore, or an intense nausea or itch that is awful but has no clear pain-like sensory quality. Is the principle fundamentally about the felt unpleasantness of pain, about what the pain motivates the person to do, about what the person wills or values, about bodily damage, or about something else?
