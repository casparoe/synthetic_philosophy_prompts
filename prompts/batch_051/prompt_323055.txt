Teaching a film-and-theology session next term on Scorsese's The Last Temptation of Christ (the Kazantzakis adaptation), and I want the discussion to be about divine impassibility, not just was-the-film-blasphemous stuff. Background for you: classical theism says God is impassible — he can't undergo suffering, change, or be acted on — and yet Christians also say Christ is God and that Christ suffered and was tempted. The usual two-natures fix is that in the incarnation the human nature does the suffering and the divine nature stays untouched, and its okay to say God suffered in the flesh, in his humanity, so long as you don't say the divine nature itself suffered. A lot of modern theology runs the other way: a God who can't suffer can't really love, so the cross has to be God himself in pain. The film leans way into Jesus's terror, doubt, nausea, not wanting the job, and of course the last-temptation sequence on the cross, where he gets to come down, marry Mary Magdalene, live an ordinary life, grow old, then realize the trick and drag himself back to die. So read the film through the impassibility debate for me. Go scene by scene on the moments doing the most theological work — maybe the early ones where he's building crosses for the Romans, the desert temptation, the final vision — and say for each whether the suffering and temptation are being located in the human nature only, where the two-natures doctrine can absorb it, or whether the film is making the divine person, or God outright, the one who gets broken and tempted. I especially want to know about the last temptation: if the whole meaning of the crucifixion in the film depends on Jesus being genuinely, internally torn about coming down, does that put the struggle inside God in a way classical impassibility has to reject? Or can a careful impassibilist watch that sequence without flinching? And end by saying what the film adds to the debate over impassibility that the abstract arguments don't — does Kazantzakis dramatize a real passibilist or two-natures position that prose has trouble stating, or does the sheer humanness of his Jesus quietly concede that only the humanity suffers and God stays offscreen? Take both the film and the theology seriously, and don't force a resolution if the film really does pull in both directions.
