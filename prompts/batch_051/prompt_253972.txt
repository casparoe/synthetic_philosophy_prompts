You teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of mathematics, so I want you to think in terms of the actual technical fault lines and not the textbook one-liners. I keep seeing logicism, formalism, and intuitionism presented as three rival answers to the question 'what are mathematical objects?' — logicism says mathematics reduces to logic, formalism says it's the manipulation of meaningless symbols, intuitionism says it's a matter of mental constructions. But that framing makes them sound like they're arguing over the same question when I suspect they often aren't, and it also gets Hilbert and Brouwer wrong. What I want is a careful explanation, pitched at an advanced undergrad who's seen some logic, of what the three schools genuinely disagree about — the points where the disagreement is substantive and would force different mathematical practice, not just different metaphysics. Start by brainstorming how each school would line up on each of the following, then use that to build your final explanation: the law of excluded middle, impredicative definitions (where an object is defined by reference to a totality that already includes it), the completed infinite (treating, say, the natural numbers as a finished totality), the status and importance of consistency proofs, and what counts as a proof at all. And I'd like your honest take at the end: is there a single core disagreement driving the rest, or is 'the three schools' a misleading package for several partly independent controversies? Dont give me the standard history; give me the conceptual geography.
