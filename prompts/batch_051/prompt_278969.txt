I'm a second-year taking a course on Plato's later political thought, and I'm getting in a tangle with a passage in Laws Book 1. It's the bit where the Athenian is defending drinking parties as an institution, and our lecturer says it's a really important argument about how you can train people's emotions, but I can't follow how it hangs together. The passage we were given is from the Bury translation, Laws 649d–649e:

"And are not these the conditions in which we are of the character described,—anger, lust, insolence, ignorance, covetousness, and extravagance; and these also,—wealth, beauty, strength, and everything which intoxicates a man with pleasure and turns his head? And for the purpose, first, of providing a cheap and comparatively harmless test of these conditions, and, secondly, of affording practice in them, what more suitable pleasure can we mention than wine, with its playful testing—provided that it is employed at all carefully? For consider: in the case of a man whose disposition is morose and savage (whence spring numberless iniquities), is it not more dangerous to test him by entering into money transactions with him, at one's own personal risk, than by associating with him with the help of Dionysus and his festive insight?"

But immediately before this he's been saying that courage is practised amidst fears, so the opposite quality ought to be cultivated in the opposite kind of conditions, which seems to be pleasurable ones that make us confident and shameless.

The bits I'm stuck on are: what exactly is the "test" supposed to be doing—is it exposing a person's hidden character, or giving them practice in resisting something, or are those meant to be the same thing? What is the "opposite quality" to courage here? Is it moderation, self-control, or something more like not being shameless? And how does the comparison with the money transactions and sexual temptation actually work as an argument? It feels like he's saying wine is the safest way to test people, but then I don't see how that turns into a defence of drinking as a civic practice rather than just a harm-reduction point. I also cant tell whether this is meant as a literal policy proposal for the city or as an exercise in seeing the soul from outside, so to speak. Can someone walk me through the actual steps of reasoning in this passage, including anything you think a first reading would miss?
