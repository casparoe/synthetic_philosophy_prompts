I've been writing up lecture notes for a course on the philosophy of mathematics and keep going back and forth on a dispute I would like settled in a form I could use with students. The standard philosophical objection to the first computer-assisted proofs runs roughly as follows. A proof, unlike a computation or an experiment, must be surveyable: a competent mathematician can in principle check the reasoning step by step and come to see, as a whole, why the theorem holds. The four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture, the objection says, fail that test, because the exhaustive case analyses are too huge for any human to survey; accepting them is therefore not proof but a kind of trust in testimony, or in an opaque machine.

That objection was aimed at a certain picture: a bespoke computer search whose output a mathematician cannot audit. Now consider a different development: the routine use of interactive proof assistants, such as Lean or Coq. Here the machine does not replace judgement with an unauditable search. The argument is decomposed into discrete steps, each mechanically checked against a small, independently inspected kernel of formal rules; the formalised statement of the theorem is explicit, and the proof object could in principle be checked again by a different checker. In practice the formal proof objects are far too large for any human to survey, so surveyability in the old sense is gone even though trust in an opaque search is also gone.

Does the surveyability objection survive this shift? Which premise of the old argument is doing the real work, and is there a repaired version that preserves what felt right about it — or does it force the conclusion that 'proof' has become a partly social or partly computational category? Lead with your verdict in the first sentence, then explain which premise the new development bears on. Keep the whole response under 450 words.
