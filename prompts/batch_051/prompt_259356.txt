Im teaching a unit on collective action and want to open with the old fable of the ant and the grasshopper, because everyone knows the moral: the ant works all summer storing grain, the grasshopper sings, winter comes, the grasshopper asks for a share, and the ant turns him away. The easy reading is that the grasshopper is a free rider—he wants the payoff of the ants labor without contributing. But I've been poking around the public goods side of things and Im not sure that's right. Free riding, in the strict sense, is about consuming a nonexcludable good that others are supplying while not kicking in: group projects, public radio, union wages. The ants granary is private and excludable, and the grasshopper isnt inside any cooperative scheme with her; he's just an improvident neighbor asking for charity. So maybe "social parasite" or "moocher" is closer, but those also carry implications the fable may not support—a parasite lives off a host's ongoing effort in a way that harms the host, while the ant is fine until the knocking starts. And I read that in the La Fontaine retelling its a cicada and an ant, which makes me wonder whether the fable just assumes that only storing grain counts as contributing, when the grasshopper was arguably providing something—music, company, whatever—that nobody asked him to provide. What I want is a careful reading of the fable through the free riding / social parasitism distinction that is willing to say the categories don't fit cleanly, plus a clear account of what exactly, if anything, the grasshopper does wrong and whether the ant's refusal is defensible or just harsh. And tell me how confident you are in the reading overall, and which point you're least sure of.
