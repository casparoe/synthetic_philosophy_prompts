My reading group has spent three weeks on Anselm's argument in the Proslogion, and we are stuck on what to make of Gaunilo's island. One member treats it as a decisive parody: run Anselm's inference with the subject swapped out, so that instead of a being so great that none greater could be thought, we reason about an island so excellent that no greater island could be conceived. The very same moves then appear to prove that such an island exists in reality, which is absurd; and since the moves license an absurdity when applied to the island, the member concludes that Anselm's moves cannot license his conclusion when applied to God either. Another member replies that the island is not a faithful analogy but a botched one: islands are bounded, material, contingent particulars, so there is no coherent maximum of island-greatness — for any candidate island a greater one can always be described by adding another inlet or acre — whereas Anselm's argument is meant to apply only to a being whose perfections admit of intrinsic maxima and whose nonexistence cannot be conceived. Existence in reality, on this reply, enters the relevant ordering of greatness in a special way for such a being and not for islands.

I would like you to write the audit of this analogy that our group should have produced. Set out Anselm's argument step by step, then give the island argument in parallel form, with an explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of God, of existing in the understanding, of the relevant sense of 'greater,' of existing in reality, and of the conclusion — and include anything else you think is doing real work. Then go through each correspondence and say where the island genuinely replicates Anselm's premises and inferential moves, and where it does not. For each disanalogy, say whether it matters for the purpose the island is being put to, namely showing that Anselm's form of reasoning proves too much, or whether it is a diversion from that purpose. In particular, address the objection that arose in our discussion: when the defender of Anselm says the comparison fails because the argument only works for a uniquely great, necessary being, is that an independent reason for thinking islands and God differ in the relevant way, or just a restatement of the conclusion the island was meant to challenge? Finish by saying whether Gaunilo's overgeneration charge survives once the genuine disanalogies are conceded, and, if the island breaks at a load-bearing point, propose a better parody that captures what remains of the worry while avoiding the island's specific defects.
