I follow a standing rule when I read popular accounts of scientific history. Whenever a discovery, theorem, or phenomenon bears a person's name—Halley's comet, Pell's equation, and similar cases—I assume the named individual was probably not its first discoverer, and I discount whatever part of the account turns on their having discovered it. I adopted this rule because eponymy has such a poor track record: the comet named for Halley was observed and recorded for centuries before him, and the equation named for Pell was solved earlier by others. A friend says I have overlearned Stigler's law—the law that no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer—and that I am letting a statistical tendency about labels bias my judgment of particular attribution claims. He grants that many eponyms are misattributed but insists that some are earned, and that my policy is a heuristic dressed up as skepticism. My difficulty is that I am not a historian of science and will not investigate most cases; I need a defensible default for how much weight to give named-discoverer stories in science writing. Is my habit defensible? Where does it break down, and can it be restated so that it keeps its benefits without dismissing accurate attributions out of hand?
