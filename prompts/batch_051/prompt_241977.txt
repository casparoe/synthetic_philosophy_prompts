I'm teaching an introductory philosophy module on Descartes' Meditations, and I want to close the unit with a piece of verse that forces students to see how little the cogito actually establishes. Please write a fourteen-line sonnet in which the first eight lines set up the skeptical backdrop: doubts about the senses, dreams, the possibility of an evil deceiver, the whole edifice of knowledge collapsing, and then the one point that seems to survive. The volta, at line nine, should shift from relief at having found something certain to the realization that this something is very thin: a bare thinking activity, at the moment of thinking, with no guarantee of a continuing substance, body, or even a fully unified self. The closing couplet should say exactly what the cogito proves and what it leaves open.

Accuracy matters to me: avoid the common slide from something-is-thinking to I-am-an-immaterial-soul. I would also like the poem's form to do some philosophical work, so use enjambment where the argument itself strains against the line, and make the turn genuinely function as the moment the meditation's overconfidence collapses. The result should be precise enough that a Descartes specialist would not wince, and good enough as a poem that it works for students who have never read him.
