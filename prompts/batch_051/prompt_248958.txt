I have a rule about how much weight to give introspective reports concerning the temporal structure of conscious experience. When someone — including me — reports, after the fact, that an experience occurred at a particular point in an observed sequence (say, that a color change was consciously seen before the second flash appeared, or that a felt intention preceded a movement by two hundred milliseconds), I treat the report as a reconstruction of the experience rather than as evidence about the experience itself, unless the report was made concurrently with the stimulus or is corroborated by an independent measure such as reaction time or forced-choice accuracy. I adopted the rule after learning about the color phi phenomenon: if a red dot flashes and then a green dot flashes at a nearby location, observers often report a single dot moving from the first location to the second and turning green midway along the path — which means the reported experience places the color change before the green dot was ever presented. The standard way of framing what happens in such cases is as a choice between an Orwellian revision, in which the experience occurs first and memory or report is edited afterwards, and a Stalinesque revision, in which the editing is completed before the experience reaches consciousness; some writers add that the choice may be illusory because it presupposes a definite sequence of conscious events that is there to be either faithfully reported or edited. My question is not about which of those options is correct; it is about my rule. The rule seems to protect me from taking confabulated timing judgments at face value, but I worry that it is a bias wearing the clothes of a principle — it gives me a standing license to discount first-person evidence that is awkward for my views, and a blanket discount of delayed reports may be no more defensible than a blanket trust of them. Is the rule defensible as stated? Under what conditions, and for which aspects of experience (order, duration, simultaneity, the content of what was seen), does a delayed introspective report remain reliable, and what would a defensible version of the rule say? How could I tell, from the inside, whether I am applying it evenhandedly rather than using it to protect a prior view about consciousness?
