I'm trying to get clear about where I draw the line between legitimate historical reconstruction and pseudohistory, because my gut verdicts on particular cases keep pulling in different directions. On one side, I'm confident: Plato's Atlantis, understood as a literal lost continent with an advanced Bronze Age civilization that sank in a single night, is pseudohistory rather than a hypothesis awaiting vindication. So are ancient-astronaut claims according to which the pyramids or the Nazca Lines could only have been made with extraterrestrial help; Fomenko's New Chronology, on which most of ancient and medieval history was fabricated by later scholars; and the older colonial claim that Great Zimbabwe must have been built by Phoenicians or the Queen of Sheba rather than by the local African state that the archaeology plainly indicates. I would put a lost advanced ice-age civilization, of the sort popularized in recent bestsellers and supposedly destroyed in a cataclysm with only myths surviving, in the same bucket, even though it borrows the vocabulary of archaeology.

On the other side, I don't think it was pseudohistory when Schliemann and others took Homer seriously enough to go looking for Troy, and I accept the resulting picture of a Late Bronze Age Anatolian city destroyed under circumstances that give the Iliad a historical kernel, provided nobody insists on taking the gods or the wooden horse literally. I think the Norse sagas' account of a landfall in North America is a legitimate case of a tradition long dismissed as legend before archaeologists corroborated it with a site in Newfoundland. I think the standard modern view of Herodotus' women warriors is itself too tidy: excavated Scythian graves with women buried alongside weapons, and with injuries consistent with mounted combat, suggest the old stories had a real kernel, so writing them off in advance as legend would have been a mistake. And I do not think a modest historical-kernel hypothesis about the Exodus, in which a small group of Levantine slaves left Egypt without plagues or a mass migration, is pseudohistory; I think it is merely an underdetermined historical hypothesis, possibly false but methodologically respectable.

I would like to know whether a single principle captures these verdicts, or nearly all of them. The pair that bothers me is Atlantis against Troy, Vinland, and the Scythian women: all four start as old stories that learned opinion long dismissed, yet I count only Atlantis as pseudohistory, and I want the principle to explain why the difference isn't merely that the other three happened later to be corroborated. If the best principle forces me to revise one of my verdicts, say which and why. Please state the principle precisely, say which case motivates each clause, apply it to cases I haven't listed—say, the claim that the Olmec colossal heads depict African visitors, and the claim that a real King Arthur lies behind the medieval legend—and name the verdicts it cannot accommodate. Also tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
