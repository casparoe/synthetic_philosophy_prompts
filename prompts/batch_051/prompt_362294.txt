I retired from a career in the natural sciences and have only recently begun reading philosophy. I want help reconstructing the view of knowledge presupposed by a familiar design choice. Online retailers rank products by sales volume and tag items as bestsellers; restaurant sites sort by bookings and five-star ratings; diners join the queue outside a crowded restaurant while an equally rated one next door stays empty. The usual justification is epistemic: such signals let a person without first-hand knowledge pool the private information of many earlier choosers. Yet these same mechanisms are standard cases of information cascades, in which later choosers rationally set aside their own impressions and copy earlier choices, so the aggregate signal can come to rest on very little independent information and amplify an early accident. What theory of knowledge—or of when other people's choices count as evidence—does ranking by aggregate popularity presuppose? Does it strictly require that each observed choice transmits genuine private information, or is it compatible with many choices being unwarranted imitation? And if we replaced that epistemology with one on which only independently grounded choices carry evidential weight, what would these systems have to change? Please answer in American English.
