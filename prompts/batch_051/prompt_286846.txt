I am a novelist, not a statistician, and for the past several months I have been researching a book about a mid-career psychologist whose most-cited finding fails to replicate. In the course of that research I read my way into a methodological literature I was not trained for, and I keep running into the same stalled argument. One camp holds that the replication crisis is at bottom a failure of the dominant tool: null hypothesis significance testing rewards threshold-crossing rather than belief-updating, treats the data as speaking for themselves once significance is reached, and invites researchers to use p < 0.05 as a certificate of truth; the cure is to reason as Bayesians do, with priors, posteriors, and credible intervals. The other camp replies that the mathematics is not the culprit; the culprit is the social system built around it: p-hacking, HARKing, file drawers, grinding until significance, journals that will not publish null results. Switching to Bayes, they say, merely relocates the researcher's degrees of freedom into the choice of prior and leaves the perverse incentives untouched.

More than a decade of replications, preregistration, multi-lab projects, and public retractions has not ended the argument; each side reads the same replication rates as vindication. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey or a verdict. My working hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement any single replication project can settle, because the two camps differ about what a statistical method is for: a machine for controlling long-run error rates, or a model of what a rational mind should do with evidence, and therefore about what would count as a solution. But I can also see it as a hidden dispute over the base rate of true effects, which would make it an empirical question trapped inside a prior that neither side will grant the other; or as a value disagreement about whose failure matters more, the individual researcher's epistemic hygiene or the community's incentive structure.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal or substantive; do the two sides differ about facts, about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Is there a clean way to state the question on which they divide? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal result, a conceptual distinction, a different question, an institutional experiment, or is the sensible conclusion that no progress should be expected here, and if so, why?
