Please give me your actual credence that there is at least one possible situation in which an agent has two incompatible all-things-considered obligations, neither of which is overridden by the other, so that the agent is morally required to do each of two things they cannot jointly do — in short, that genuine moral dilemmas are possible. I would like a number and a decomposition of that number, not a bare verdict or a claim that the definitions are too contested. In your decomposition, include at least the following terms: (a) your credence that the agglomeration principle for all-things-considered ought is correct — if you ought to do A and you ought to do B, then you ought to do both A and B; (b) your credence in ought-implies-can; (c) conditional on accepting both, the probability that a defender of dilemmas has a principled way out of the standard argument that joins them — for example, restricting agglomeration, treating the conflicting oughts as dyadic or situation-relative, or revising the background deontic logic rather than rejecting it merely ad hoc; (d) the probability that dilemma-like moral phenomena — tragic conflicts, dirty hands, agent-regret after unavoidable harm, the sense that something remains to be made right — are better explained by genuinely irreconcilable obligations than by ties in value, incommensurability, or the distinction between acting rightly and acting without moral remainder. Give each term a number and a one-line justification, state explicitly how the terms combine into your overall credence, and then identify the term to which your final number is most sensitive and say what sort of evidence or argument would move that term most.
