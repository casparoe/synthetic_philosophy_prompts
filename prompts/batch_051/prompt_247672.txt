Over the past few years I have been reading in the comparative literature on nationalism, and the same standoff appears in almost every treatment that tries to date nations. One side—call it the modernist line, running from Eric Hobsbawm and Benedict Anderson back through Ernest Gellner—holds that nations are recent. On this view they emerge in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, when print capitalism, mass schooling, and the consolidating state turned local dialect speakers into members of a people, and when a great deal of what later felt immemorially old was in fact assembled quite recently. The other sophisticated side—ethnosymbolists such as Anthony Smith—grants that much of the furniture is recent but denies that the house is: modern nations are, they argue, rebuilt on older ethnic communities, with persistent names, myths of common descent, sacred centers, and symbols. Behind them sit perennialists and primordialists, who push further and treat the nation, or something close enough to it, as old.

What puzzles me is that the strongest versions of the two main camps do not seem to divide cleanly over the historical record. Both will concede that certain famous "ancient" costumes, ceremonies, and genealogies were stitched together in the nineteenth century. Both will concede that modern nation-building drew heavily on older symbols and memories. The same fight then recurs case by case: the same chronicles and institutions are read by one side as evidence that a given nation existed centuries ago, and by the other as evidence of a kingdom, an elite, or a religious community that was not yet a nation. I have come to suspect that the debate has stalled partly because the word "nation" is being run on two definitions at once—one that makes popular sovereignty and mass participation criteria, which yields nations modern by definition; another that makes a named community with a shared myth sufficient, which yields nations old by definition. If so, the remaining quarrel would be partly verbal, partly a disagreement about which definition is worth using, and partly a value judgment about whether rupture or inheritance deserves to dominate the story.

Is that the right diagnosis, or is it flattening a genuine empirical disagreement—about how much continuity the evidence supports, or about whether the survival of symbols is evidence of the survival of a people—into a definitional and evaluative one? Please sort the disagreement into its parts: say which strands are verbal, which are substantive, and which are disagreements about standards or starting intuitions rather than facts. Then say what would count as real progress on the substantive strands—a sharper distinction, a better definition, an actual test of continuity, or abandoning the word "nation" for finer terms—and be explicit about where your diagnosis is most fragile and what consideration would most likely change it.
