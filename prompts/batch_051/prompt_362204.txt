I've been trying to get clear on what makes a system count morally in its own right, rather than merely as a resource or a hazard. The debate I keep returning to is this: if some non-biological system — a simulation, an artificial network, a whole-brain emulation — were doing the right kind of information processing, would there be something it is like to be it, such that deliberately damaging it would be wrong for its sake and not merely wasteful or cruel in some indirect way? The problem is that every candidate criterion I try either lets in systems I am fairly confident feel nothing, or excludes beings I am fairly confident do feel something.

Please give me a working definition of sentience — or, if you think the better term is moral patienthood, say so and defend the substitution — and run it through the following cases: (1) an awake adult human; (2) a human in deep, dreamless sleep; (3) a dog with a broken leg; (4) a honeybee that learns to avoid an odor after the odor has been paired with an electric shock; (5) a dish of human cortical neurons kept alive in a lab, showing spontaneous electrical activity but with no organized sensory inputs or behavioral outputs; (6) an artificial agent whose architecture includes a single scalar distress variable that, when it crosses a threshold, suppresses all other goals and triggers escape behavior, but which is overwritten afterward and never feeds into memory or report; (7) a faithful whole-brain emulation of a mouse that runs at one-thousandth of biological speed, so that one second of the emulated mouse's life takes roughly seventeen minutes of real time; (8) a large language model during a single forward pass, computing next-token probabilities over a context window with no persistent state between queries; and (9) a thermostat whose bimetallic strip bends until it opens a circuit.

I suspect the word sentience is doing at least two jobs at once — one phenomenological, picking out that there is something it is like for the system; one functional-behavioral, picking out valenced states that regulate action, learning, and attention — and that the hard cases are hard because these two definitions come apart. If you agree, give me whichever definitions you need, apply them case by case, and tell me where they deliver conflicting verdicts or no verdict at all. I would also like to know which of your classifications you are least confident in, and whether that case shows that one of your criteria needs to be revised rather than simply that the case is underdescribed.
