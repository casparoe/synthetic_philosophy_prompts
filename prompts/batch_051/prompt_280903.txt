I keep fussing over an argument I pieced together, and I can't tell whether it's airtight with an outrageous conclusion or whether one of the steps sneaks something by. It's about how conditionals interact with probability.

Pick any A and B such that "A and B" and "A and not-B" both have positive probability.

(1) "If A then B" expresses a proposition, call it A→B.
(2) A rational credence in a conditional should match the corresponding conditional credence: P(A→B) = P(B|A), whenever the latter is defined.
(3) Since A→B is a proposition, the law of total probability applies over the partition {B, not-B}: P(A→B) = P(A→B | B)·P(B) + P(A→B | not-B)·P(not-B).
(4) Suppose (2) holds not just for P but also for the shifted functions P(·|B) and P(·|not-B). Applying (2) to P(·|B) gives P(A→B | B) = P(B | A and B), which is 1; applying it to P(·|not-B) gives P(A→B | not-B) = P(B | A and not-B), which is 0.
(5) Plugging into (3): P(A→B) = 1·P(B) + 0·P(not-B) = P(B).
(6) But (2) says P(A→B) = P(B|A), so combining (5) and (6) gives P(B|A) = P(B).

So any such A and B are probabilistically independent. That's crazy—dark clouds can make rain more likely without guaranteeing it. Is the argument valid? If so, which premise is the least bad one to give up, and what's left of the intuition in (2) after you reject it? If not valid, exactly which step fails and why?
