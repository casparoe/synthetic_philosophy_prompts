Suppose that around the year 2040 a one-time medical procedure becomes available that permanently removes a person's capacity for suffering. A person who has had it can still register injury, illness, and danger as information, but none of it feels bad: there is no physical pain, fear, grief, shame, dread, or boredom in the usual felt sense. Desire, attention, energy, attachment, and the capacity for enjoyment remain intact, and the change passes to any children the person has afterward. The procedure is safe, cheap, and free at the point of use. It appears first in a handful of wealthy countries and spreads within a decade to every country that can manufacture it; after about twenty-five years, roughly four out of five people have had it, or have had it chosen for them at birth, while a substantial minority refuse it for themselves and their children.

I want you to reason through what follows two or three generations after this becomes common. I am less interested in a narrative than in the mechanisms. Which functions that suffering currently performs — in learning from mistakes, staying motivated, caring for children, seeking medical help, obeying the law, being deterred by threats, making art, and taking morality seriously — would still be performed some other way, and which would simply be lost? If pain no longer hurts, does the threat of violence deter? If grief no longer hurts, do close relationships and long-term obligations change? Would people become careless with their bodies, or would neutral damage-signals be enough to keep them safe? Would felt suffering reappear in a new form, such as frustrated preferences, unsatisfied wants, or a sense of a flat, meaningless life? Finally, what would be the social position of the minority who can still be hurt in a world where most people cannot?

Please be explicit about your confidence at each step: which predictions you regard as robust, which are guesses, and what would have to be true about human nature for each uncertain prediction to go the other way.
