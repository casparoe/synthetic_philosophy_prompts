I work as a software engineer, and for the past few months my team has been building a service that mediates two-party negotiations over a fixed resource — customers and vendors splitting a cost saving, or two internal teams splitting a compute budget. I have no formal philosophy background, but the design work forced me to read about axiomatic bargaining solutions, and I have come to hold a definite view: the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is the right default rule for two-party, fixed-resource compromises. In my own words, the rule works as follows. For each side there is a disagreement payoff (what they get if no agreement is reached) and an ideal payoff (the most they could get in any feasible agreement); the solution picks the Pareto-efficient payoff vector at which each side realizes the same fraction of its own maximal possible gain above disagreement. That is, with disagreement payoffs d_i and ideal payoffs a_i, choose the maximal feasible u satisfying (u_1 − d_1)/(a_1 − d_1) = (u_2 − d_2)/(a_2 − d_2). I am drawn to it because it is Pareto optimal, symmetric, invariant to separate rescaling of the two sides' utility scales, and individually monotonic: if the feasible set expands in a way that raises one side's best possible outcome while leaving the other side's best possible outcome unchanged, the latter side should not be made worse off. I also regard its rejection of independence of irrelevant alternatives as a feature, not a bug, because it seems to me that what counts as a fair split should be allowed to depend on what was on the table.

What I want from you is not another summary of the solution and not a reading list; I want a map of what would count against my view that this is the right default rule for the setting I described. Please treat my position as a risky, falsifiable choice among rival compromise rules rather than as a label, and separate the considerations that should genuinely move me from the ones that are merely aesthetic or question-begging. Specifically: which axiom violations, constructed bargaining problems, monotonicity failures, misreporting incentives, or problems with eliciting the utilities in the first place should lead me to abandon or restrict the rule? Which objections would a committed defender of the solution concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss on the grounds that they simply amount to the intuition behind independence of irrelevant alternatives? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say whether my position, as stated, has been insulated by the phrase the right default rule for this setting — and, if it has, whether that kind of domain-qualification is a genuine problem for a view of this sort or just an honest specification of where the reasons run out.
