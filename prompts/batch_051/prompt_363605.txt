I am a PhD student in decision theory, and I have spent the past several months in a reading group on expected-value fanaticism that has fallen into exactly the same rut every week. By fanaticism I mean the following pattern. If we take expected-utility maximization in its unrestricted form, and if utilities are unbounded while probabilities are never exactly zero, then for any ordinary course of action with a modest expected value, there is some astronomically improbable but astronomically valuable alternative—sinking one's resources into a one-in-10^50 chance of preventing a quantity of suffering that dwarfs the ordinary scale by many orders of magnitude—such that a naive expected-value calculation recommends the moonshot over the ordinary action. One camp in the group treats this as a reductio of unrestricted expected-value reasoning and wants to amend the theory: bound the utility function, impose stochastic dominance constraints, discount sufficiently small probabilities, or replace the real-valued utility scale with something less permissive. But no one can agree on which patch is principled rather than gerrymandered. A second camp insists that the conclusion is not a bug: if you genuinely accept the axioms and genuinely assign the enormous value, then the fanatical choice just is what maximal expected value demands, and the reluctance to bite that bullet is ordinary risk-aversion or a failure of numerical imagination. A third camp suspects the first two are using the word rational in two different senses—one for coherence constraints on preferences, the other for substantive guidance about what to do in a world of open-ended, poorly measurable possibilities.

I want a diagnosis of the structure of this stalled debate, not a survey of it. Please act as a philosopher of decision theory with a strong interest in philosophical methodology, someone used to diagnosing impasses of this kind. Begin by brainstorming several possible diagnoses before committing to one. My own working hypothesis is that the disagreement is only superficially about expected-utility theory; underneath it, the two main camps disagree about what a normative decision theory is supposed to be for—characterizing coherence-avoidance axioms, versus giving usable advice under radical uncertainty—and they also disagree about which data the theory must respect: the reductio camp treats our stable intuitive revulsion toward the moonshot as evidence against the unrestricted theory, while the bullet-biting camp treats that revulsion as an untrustworthy heuristic, not a datum. But I can see competing diagnoses: a hidden formal question about whether any acceptable modification of expected-utility theory is unique or well-motivated; a verbal dispute about terms like choiceworthiness; or a bedrock clash of intuitions that neither side can argue for. Give me your diagnosis with reasons, locate where the disagreement actually bottoms out, and say honestly what would count as progress given that diagnosis—a formal result (and of what kind), a conceptual distinction, a change of question, or an argument that the impasse is here to stay and why.
