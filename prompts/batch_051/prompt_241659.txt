I've been chewing on Dennett's four-creature tower (Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian) and I think there's a missing rung between Skinnerian and Popperian. As I understand the ladder: Darwinian creatures have behaviors fixed by their genes and only the lineage learns; Skinnerian creatures try things in the world and keep what gets rewarded; Popperian creatures let hypotheses die in their head instead of in the world; Gregorian creatures add tools like language and culture so they can pre-test other people's guesses. I want to wedge in what I've been calling 'affective simulators.' The rough idea is that before you get full-blown Popperian simulation—a rich inner model where you run an action and get a detailed predicted outcome—there's a level where a creature can try out a few actions internally but only gets back a valence signal: a felt good/bad, a gut lean, one option just feels right. My dog hesitating between jumping the fence and going around isn't, I suspect, running a little movie of both outcomes; he's getting an affective push or pull for each candidate. That's still internal selection, with hypotheses dying in the animal instead of out in the world, but the representational payload is valence rather than a simulated world.

I know this could just be Popperian lite, or a difference of degree rather than kind. But I have a hunch it's actually a separate computational stage, and that lumping it in with Popperian creatures hides how much of animal cognition is built out of felt evaluations rather than rich prediction. So: is this a real distinction? Does it already exist under another name in Dennett or in people who think about model-free vs model-based learning? Give me the strongest objection to treating these as a distinct level, a concrete case my category would likely misclassify, and a verdict on whether the objection is fatal or just uncomfortable.
