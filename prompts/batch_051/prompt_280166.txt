I’m taking a course on intertemporal social choice, and we have been reading Frank Ramsey’s 1928 paper, “A Mathematical Theory of Saving.” Early in the paper Ramsey argues that pure time preference — discounting a future enjoyment simply because it is later in time — is ethically indefensible and reflects a weakness of the imagination rather than any genuine reason. He is careful to distinguish this from discounting due to uncertainty, mortality, or changes in expected marginal utility. What interests me is how far this distinction can be extended.

Suppose Ramsey had lived to see two later developments: first, the empirical evidence that real people discount in quasi-hyperbolic ways, weighting the very near future much less than the medium-run future; second, the modern debate over the appropriate social discount rate for long-horizon problems such as climate change. Would Ramsey have classified quasi-hyperbolic discounting as just another manifestation of the same imaginative weakness, or might he have conceded that it is a different phenomenon — perhaps a response to uncertainty about whether later selves or future persons will actually experience the goods in question? And would his rejection of pure time preference commit him to using something like the Ramsey formula with a zero or near-zero utility discount rate in climate economics, or did his argument leave room for a positive social discount rate on other grounds? I’m less interested in the yes-or-no answer than in reconstructing the argument he would have given from his own commitments.
