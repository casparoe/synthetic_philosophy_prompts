I'm in an ethics course on promising and contracts, and I've found myself pretty sold on the expectation view of why promises bind: roughly, breaking a promise is wrong because you deliberately lead the promisee to form an expectation about what you'll do, and then you wrong them by disappointing that expectation. Not because promises are only intelligible inside a social practice (the practice view), and not because I've handed the promisee some kind of authority or right over my future action (the transfer view). I want to stress-test this before I committ to it in my paper.

So: treat the expectation view as the position to attack. What would have to be true for it to be false? I'd like a real map of its exposure, not just the usual "people don't always rely on promises" thing. The answer should have these sections, in this order:

- Commitments I may not have noticed (~150 words): what the view actually has to say—the hidden load-bearing bits, e.g. about how expectations get formed, whose expectations count, and what happens when expectations and reliance come apart.
- Defeaters, ranked (~450 words): from fatal to merely awkward. For each, say what would have to be the case, and whether it's a case the view can't describe, a consequence it's stuck with, or a result that collapses it into a rival. Include at least one that is a moral-psychological or empirical claim about real people, not just a thought experiment.
- What defenders concede vs. dismiss (~200 words): which of these would an expectation-view person themselves count as genuinely counting against the view (even if just as a cost), and which would they dismiss as misreading the view or as problems for everyone?
- Is it insulated? (~150 words): has the view, with all its qualifications, become impossible to refute? And if so, is that a feature or a bug?
- My personal bottom line (~100 words): which one or two defeaters you think I should actually take most seriously given that I already lean this way, and why.
