I have come to hold the position John Stuart Mill sets out in Chapter III of On Liberty, "Of Individuality, as One of the Elements of Well-Being," and I want a map of what would count against it. The passage I have in mind is this:

"As it is useful that while mankind are imperfect there should be different opinions, so is it that there should be different experiments of living; that free scope should be given to varieties of character, short of injury to others; and that the worth of different modes of life should be proved practically, when any one thinks fit to try them."

I accept this as two claims joined together. The epistemic claim: the worth of different modes of life is best discovered by having people actually live them, so that a society that permits many experiments of living learns more quickly which modes of life are worth pursuing. The political claim: the state and social opinion should protect varieties of character and plans of life up to the point of injury to others. I want you to tell me what would count against this position. Treat the epistemic claim as carrying real predictions—for instance, about whether a society with freer scope for experiments corrects its errors about how to live rather than compounding them, and about whether self-chosen plans of life fare better than inherited or conformist ones. Walk through the observations, cases, and arguments that should move a committed Millian: which of them would be decisive, which merely awkward, and which a defender of the position would themselves concede as counting against it rather than dismiss. Then give me an honest assessment of how much the view actually risks. In particular, say whether the "short of injury to others" clause and the move from "should be proved practically" to "is best proved practically" have insulated it against serious refutation—and if they have, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind, or an acceptable feature of a political principle.
