As a PhD student in political philosophy working on the normative assessment of free riding, I find that the literature returns repeatedly to a descriptive diagnosis while leaving the underlying moral question unsettled. In A Treatise of Human Nature, Book 3, Part 2, Section 7 (“Of the origin of government”), David Hume writes:

“Two neighbours may agree to drain a meadow, which they possess in common; because it is easy for them to know each others mind; and each must perceive, that the immediate consequence of his failing in his part, is, the abandoning the whole project. But it is very difficult, and indeed impossible, that a thousand persons should agree in any such action; it being difficult for them to concert so complicated a design, and still more difficult for them to execute it; while each seeks a pretext to free himself of the trouble and expence, and would lay the whole burden on others.”

I read Hume as isolating a difference between the small-scale case, where one person’s defection is visible and decisive, and the large-scale case, where no individual contribution matters observably and each person can rationalize leaving the burden to others. My concern is not the psychological or game-theoretic explanation but the moral question it opens: What exactly is wrong with free riding in the mass case, and how much of that wrong depends on the causal structure Hume identifies?
