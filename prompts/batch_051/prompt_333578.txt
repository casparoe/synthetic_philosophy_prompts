I'm a PhD student working at the intersection of decision theory and the philosophy of action, and I keep going back and forth on a puzzle of the sort usually called Parfit's hitchhiker. The scenario is this. You are stranded in a desert, and a driver stops and offers to take you to safety, but only if you promise to pay him a substantial sum once you reach home. He is an excellent judge of character and will not accept insincere agreement; he will drive on unless you genuinely intend, at the moment of promising, to pay. You also know that once you are home he will have no way to find you or force payment. Suppose you do form the intention, are rescued, and arrive home. At that point, causal decision theory—or more loosely, the principle that a rational act is the one with the best expected causal consequences—seems to say that the question has already been settled: the ride is a sunk benefit, paying now would cost you money and buy you nothing, so the rational thing to do is not pay. I find this principle very attractive for its simplicity, its generality, and its clean treatment of bygones. Yet I also have a firm intuition that a person who gets himself rescued on the strength of a sincere intention and then refuses to pay is not merely unkind or dishonest; he is practically irrational in some important sense, even though at the payment moment every identifiable consequence of paying is bad. The puzzle sharpens further: if I know in advance that my later self will follow the causal-consequence principle, I will be unable to form the genuine intention the driver requires, and I will be left in the desert.

I want to know which of these should yield. Should the intuition be debunked as a heuristic shaped by repeated games and social enforcement, one that misfires in a genuinely one-shot case? Should the theory be amended or abandoned, for instance by allowing precommitment, by preferring dispositions over act-by-act evaluation, or by distinguishing act-rationality from some broader standard of rational character? Or is there a reading on which the two are compatible—for example, by treating the sincere intention itself as the choice that mattered, or by re-describing the later payment decision as already bound by the earlier commitment? Please give me the strongest version of each side and state what considerations would justify each resolution, rather than just picking one.
