I draft benefit-eligibility rules for a city agency. Our rules have always been written in the old syllogistic forms — all X are Y, no X are Y, some X are Y — because that is all the staff who apply them can reliably follow. But a few times a year the lawyers hand me a sentence that will not fit the mold, like: every applicant must be assessed by a clinician who is not employed by the organization paying for the assessment. I have read enough on my own to know the rough shape of Frege's break with syllogistic logic: subject–predicate form cannot handle relations or sentences with more than one quantifier, and variables plus quantifiers were introduced to fix that. What I need is a reusable procedure for my team, none of whom have formal logic training. For each incoming rule it should say, in order: which features signal that the syllogistic form would distort the rule; how to test for those features in ordinary language; when the old form is safe; how to restate the rule so it covers exactly the people it is supposed to; and where a human judgment call cannot be eliminated. The stakes are high: a misdrafted rule either pays people who should not be paid or cuts off people who should. Run the procedure on three rules, making one of them a rule we have relied on for years that the procedure reveals was quietly over- or under-including: (1) all applicants with a dependent child are priority applicants; (2) every applicant must be evaluated by a clinician who is not employed by the organization paying for the evaluation; (3) no caseworker may approve an application they did not personally review. Then tell me what would change your mind about the procedure — a specific kind of sentence or counterexample that would make you tighten or drop a step — and where the procedure is most likely to go wrong.
