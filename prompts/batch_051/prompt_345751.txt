Write a sonnet about the Dutch book argument. Centre it on a bettor who would pay sixty cents for a ticket that returns a dollar if some proposition is true, and sixty cents for a ticket that returns a dollar if that proposition is false. Let the two quatrains of the octave treat the two bets separately, from his point of view, each appearing reasonable on its own. Put the volta at the moment he sees the two tickets together: the bookmaker has sold him both, exactly one will pay out no matter what, and the two prices together surpass that single payout by twenty cents. Use the sestet, and especially the closing couplet, to draw the general lesson that coherence is a property of the whole set of prices at once, not of any one belief taken in isolation. Aim to make the arithmetic sharp enough that a decision theorist would recognise the Dutch book theorem in it, while keeping the verse strong enough that a reader who has never met the argument can feel the trap closing.
