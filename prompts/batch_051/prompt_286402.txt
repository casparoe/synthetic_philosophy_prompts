I'm a PhD student in legal theory and I've been working on the corporate-person analogy for AI. I keep seeing people say that because we already give personhood to corporations—which are obviously not human—there's no conceptual problem giving it to an AI. I have a distinction I've been using to sort these proposals, and I'd like it torn apart.

I've been calling them lens persons and sink persons. A lens person is an entity that bundles a bunch of diffuse human action (shareholders, managers, employees) into one solvent, identifiable target that victims and regulators can actually grab hold of. A sink person is the opposite: an entity inserted between an injury and the humans behind it, where the entity has nothing to answer with—no serious assets, no memory, no one under a duty to show up—so liability runs into it and stops. On my view, corporate personhood works when and because the corporation is mostly a lens, and the standard analogy to AI quietly borrows that lens credibility. A lot of AI personhood proposals look to me like sinks: make the delivery robot or the general model a person, and the operator, the trainer, and the deployer each end up one legal hop futher away.

So my actual claim is procedural rather than metaphysical. We should stop asking whether an AI "is" a legal person and instead ask, for each incident in the proposed bundle, whether it adds lens or sink. Specifically: a grant of personhood to a non-human is justified only if it doesn't reduce expected recoverability and doesn't sever the chain of imputation back to an identifiable human decision-maker. If both fail, we shouldn't call it personhood at all, even if it technically is in some bundle-of-incidents sense.

I know limited liability already makes most corporations partly sinks, and I know this is bumping up against the responsibility-gap literature. That's fine; I'm not claiming corporations are pure lenses. What I want from you: (1) is the lens/sink distinction doing work beyond "personhood is good when plaintiffs win and bad when they don't"? (2) has this exact point already been made under another name, since it feels too obvious not to have been? (3) give me the three strongest counterexamples—the deliberate sink corporation we nonetheless keep, the penniless human person, whatever—and say whether any of them kills the distinction rather than just forcing a patch.
