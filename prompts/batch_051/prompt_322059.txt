Okay so I'm in this seminar on the philosophy of privacy and we're reading Warren and Brandeis's 1890 Harvard Law Review article, "The Right to Privacy." There's one sentence early on that I keep coming back to:

"The intensity and complexity of life, attendant upon advancing civilization, have rendered necessary some retreat from the world, and man, under the refining influence of culture, has become more sensitive to publicity, so that solitude and privacy have become more essential to the individual; but modern enterprise and invention have, through invasions upon his privacy, subjected him to mental pain and distress, far greater than could be inflicted by mere bodily injury."

I got into an argument with my roomate about whether the article is just about gossip columns and photographers, so it has nothing to say about modern tech. But the case I actually want to ask about is the thing his startup is building: exam-proctoring software that runs entirely on a student's laptop. His company's whole pitch is that it can't violate privacy because nothing about you is made public.

What I'm trying to figure out is whether the principle in that sentence reaches a case like this one. If you read the words literally, does "invasions upon his privacy" require publication or disclosure? If you read it by their larger purpose — protecting people from the mental distress that modern enterprise and invention cause — does it apply? What would Warren and Brandeis have said if you could actually explain webcam proctoring to them? And what would a reasonable reader today understand the passage to mean?
