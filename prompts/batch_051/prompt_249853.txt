I'm a PhD student in philosophy of law, and there's a puzzle I keep circling back to. Blackmail is a crime in basically every legal system: if I threaten to reveal your affair unless you pay me, that's criminal. But the component acts seem legal on there own. Revealing true, legally obtained information is generally not a crime, and asking someone for money is generally not a crime. Stick them together and suddenly its a serious offense. What theory of the wrong in blackmail does that practice presuppose? I have candidate answers in mind — privacy or control over information, coercion, exploitation of vulnerability, the idea that the blackmailer uses the victim's secret as a means — but I'm not sure the practice actually commits to any one of them, and some of those candidates would seem to make gossip or hard-nosed negotiation come out wrong too. Reconstruct the implicit theory of the wrong, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and say what would have to change in the law under each rival account. Also, please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested theory as the consensus.
