I am preparing a session of a reading group on the closing, essayistic part of Tolstoy's War and Peace, where the novel steps out of its story to defend a long argument about history, free will, and why historians go wrong. I would like you to act as a philosopher of social science who works on the dispute between methodological individualism and methodological holism, and to read that section through that lens.

For background, I take methodological individualism to be the view that social and historical facts are ultimately to be explained by facts about individual persons — their choices, beliefs, and interactions — while methodological holism allows that there are social facts, forces, or regularities that are not reducible to facts about individuals. I am less interested in definitions than in where Tolstoy falls.

Here is the puzzle I want the session to press on. Tolstoy denies that Napoleon's orders explain the movements of his army, and treats the 1812 invasion of Russia as emerging from countless small, mostly uncoordinated private decisions rather than from any commander's plan. At other moments he writes as though the mood of an army or a crowd is itself a real causal thing, and as though the mass of individual wills adds up to a historical process with its own regularities, with free will shrinking as one moves from the individual to the aggregate. Yet the novel's actual storytelling works almost entirely through close attention to individual minds — Pierre's vacillation, Prince Andrei's pride, Natasha's impulses — and its battle scenes are famous for presenting combat as confusion that no one steers rather than as a tidy social force.

Please think carefully about this. Before settling on an interpretation, brainstorm several candidate classifications of Tolstoy's position: a methodological individualist with an unusual theory of aggregation; an emergentist or holist despite himself; a determinist who takes free will to be an illusion; or a text genuinely torn between these. Weigh the options against each other before choosing, or before concluding that the inconsistency is itself the position.

Then give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Each point should stand on its own, and together the five should form a coherent philosophical reading of what the novel shows that a straightforward statement of the individualism–holism debate might miss.
