Here's an argument from a reading group that I can't defuse, and I want a real diagnosis of it.

(1) If equality is about resources, the only point of that must be that resources let people do and be the things they have reason to value. (2) But the same bundle of resources lets different people do and be very different things—a disabled person, someone with heavy care duties, someone with chronic health costs—so equal resources don't equalize the thing that made (1) matter. (3) So the real metric should be what people are actually able to do and be: their capabilities. (4) But once we're equalizing capabilities, we need a reason not to go one step further and equalize actual doings and beings (functionings): the capability to join the party and actually joining it look like equally live candidates for the unit of equality. (5) The only reason I can see available is a liberal scruple about not imposing a view of the good life, and that scruple isn't grounded in equality itself. (6) So equality, taken strictly, should equalize functionings—how well people's lives actually go. (7) The natural measure of how well a life actually goes is welfare. (8) But if the metric is welfare, we have to compensate expensive tastes: the person who needs fine wine and opera to reach the same welfare as me gets more resources. (9) That's absurd, and avoiding it was the whole original reason to choose resources. So we seem bounced between a metric that ignores what matters and one that compensates expensive tastes.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I'd definately rather keep the denial of (8) than give it up, but that just means I need to know which earlier step I'm paying to reject. Tell me which step you reject, why it looked fine, and what it costs.
