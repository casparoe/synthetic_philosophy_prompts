I am an undergraduate taking a course on the history and philosophy of the exact sciences, and we are currently reading Newton's Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy in the 1729 English translation. I have been turning over Definition III, and I find it harder than the modern summary of Newton's first law prepared me for. The part I am stuck on reads:

"This force is ever proportional to the body whose force it is; and differs nothing from the inactivity of the Mass, but in our manner of conceiving it. A body, from the inactivity of matter, is not without difficulty put out of its state of rest or motion. Upon which account, this Vis insita may, by a most significant name, be called Vis inertiæ or Force of Inactivity. But a body exerts this force only, when another force, impress'd upon it, endeavours to change its condition; and the exercise of this force may be considered both as resistance and impulse; it is resistance in so far as the body, for maintaining its present state withstands the force impressed; it is impulse in so far as the body, by not easily giving way to the impress'd force of another, endeavours to change the state of that other. Resistance is usually ascrib'd to bodies at rest, and impulse to those in motion: But motion and rest, as commonly conceived, are only relatively distinguished; nor are those bodies always truly at rest, which commonly are taken to be so."

My difficulty is this. In the physics I know, inertia is not a force; it is a law or tendency of bodies to continue in their state of rest or uniform straight-line motion. Yet Newton here speaks of a vis insita and calls it 'Vis inertiæ or Force of Inactivity,' says it 'differs nothing from the inactivity of the Mass, but in our manner of conceiving it,' and then says a body 'exerts' this force only when an impressed force tries to change its condition. Worse, he divides the exercise of this force into resistance and impulse, and the impulse clause seems to say that a body, merely by not easily giving way to an impressed force, 'endeavours to change the state' of the other body. That reads to me as if Newton is turning persistence-in-motion into an active causal power, which my modern textbook would reject.

Could you help me understand what Newton is actually committed to in this passage, without flattening it into later textbook physics? Specifically: (1) how does Newton's use of 'force' here map onto our distinction between forces, laws, and dispositions, and where does the mapping break down; (2) is the claim about impulse asserting a physical action, or is 'impulse' doing different conceptual work in this text than I assume; and (3) what should a careful reader retain from this definition as physics, and what should be attributed to an older framework for thinking about matter and motion? I would prefer an analysis that stays close to the quoted sentences, because I am trying to learn how to read this kind of text, not just get the modern equivalent.
