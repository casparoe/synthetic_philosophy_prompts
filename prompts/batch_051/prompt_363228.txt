Consider the standard practice behind modern semantic search, retrieval-augmented generation, and "related text" recommendations. Words, sentences, and documents are mapped to vectors in a high-dimensional space, and two texts are treated as semantically similar to the degree that their vectors point in the same direction — cosine similarity. The system never consults a definition or a grammar; it measures closeness in a learned geometric space. I want you to reconstruct the implicit semantics of this design: what must meaning be like for closeness in embedding space to count as similarity of content, rather than as a merely convenient statistical artifact? Then interrogate one specific weakness. Cosine similarity is symmetric by construction, and the setup treats semantic structure as a geometry of shared features. But similarity judgments in people are often asymmetric — most people are far more comfortable saying that a son resembles his father than that a father resembles his son — and semantic similarity may carry the same asymmetry, with more prominent or prototypical items serving as the reference point. Does the technology therefore quietly presuppose a symmetrized, feature-based, gradient account of meaning? And what would a retrieval system have to look like if meaning-space could not be forced into such a geometry? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined. If I have framed this badly — if you think a retrieval engine presupposes no theory of meaning at all, or that the symmetry complaint is a red herring — say so directly and set the question up the way it should have been asked, rather than politely playing along.
