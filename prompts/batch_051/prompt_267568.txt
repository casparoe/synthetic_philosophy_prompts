You are a professional poet with a genuine command of analytic philosophy of language, able to distinguish Donald Davidson's deflationary account of metaphor from the conceptual metaphor theory of George Lakoff and Mark Johnson without name-dropping. I am a novelist, and I need a sonnet for a scene in which a philosopher tries to explain to non-philosophers what actually separates those two views.

Think carefully before you write. Begin with a short prose sketch—two or three sentences—of the two claims the poem must carry, then draft the poem itself.

Write a fourteen-line, metrical, rhymed sonnet with a clear volta after the eighth line and a closing couplet. The octave should build Davidson's picture: the words keep their literal meanings, and whatever a metaphor does—making us notice a likeness, see one thing as another—is an effect of its use, not an additional proposition it silently says. Let the volta be the conceptual-metaphor theorist's objection: metaphor is not an isolated turn of phrase but a systematic conceptual mapping, argument-as-war or time-as-money, something we think, infer, and plan with rather than merely decorate with. The sestet should stage the two positions passing each other, and the couplet should say exactly where they divide: not over whether metaphor does work, but over whether that work belongs to meaning and thought or to something closer to cause and effect.

A specialist in the debate should recognize both positions and find the poem accurate; a reader who has never encountered it should find it a good poem rather than a lecture.
