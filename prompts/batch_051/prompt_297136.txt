I'm teaching a philosophy and literature unit on moral responsibility, and I want to pair Crime and Punishment with P. F. Strawson's essay Freedom and Resentment, the one about the reactive attitudes. I'm trying to avoid the standard essay about whether Raskolnikov has free will, and instead get a reading that actually uses Strawson's machinery: the participant stance versus the objective stance, the reactive attitudes like resentment, indignation, guilt, gratitude, and forgiveness, excuses versus exemptions, and Strawson's claim that determinism is beside the point because we can't just give up the participant stance on theoretical grounds.

The puzzle I keep coming back to is this. Raskolnikov seems to be trying to exempt himself from the participant stance before he commits the murder. His article divides humanity into the ordinary and the extraordinary, and it claims the extraordinary have a right to step over the line. But after the murders, the novel throws him right back into exactly the attitudes he thought didn't apply to him: shame, guilt, paranoia, the unbearable feeling of being seen. That looks like Strawson's point that you can't opt out of the web of reactive attitudes by an act of theory. But there's Svidrigailov, who really does seem to live outside those attitudes, and the novel treats him as nihilistic and finally suicidal, not as a refutation of Strawson. And Porfiry is the oddest case: he doesn't get resentful or indignant; he waits and plays with Raskolnikov almost tenderly. Is that the objective stance being used to maneuver someone back into responsibility, or is it a way of respecting Raskolnikov as a participant? And Sonya's response is the closest thing to forgiveness, but is it forgiveness in Strawson's sense—the appropriate relaxation of resentment—or is it something more like pity or religious love?

So I'd like a reading that goes through the major movements of the novel (describing what happens, not quoting) and says, for each: which Strawsonian stance or attitude is in play, and what the novel shows, complicates, or gets wrong about Strawson's picture. Take both the novel and Strawson seriously: don't turn Dostoevsky into an illustration of a philosophy paper, and be honest about where the novel resists, with the epilogue and Sonya as the obvious candidate, since the redemption there can look more religious and psychological than interpersonal. I'd also like a clear answer to whether the novel vindicates Strawson, complicates his picture, or resists it, and whether it adds anything to the philosophical debate that the essay by itself can't.
