For a long article about how the brain builds the feeling of "the present," I interviewed a vision researcher who laid out an argument he called the case against seeing the present. I have not been able to locate the load-bearing error in it, and I want a diagnosis before I write it up. His argument runs as follows. (1) In the color-phi phenomenon, a red dot is flashed at one location and, shortly afterward, a green dot is flashed at a nearby location; subjects report seeing a single dot move across the gap and change from red to green around the midpoint of its path, before the green flash actually occurs. (2) On a single, unanticipated presentation, no information about the green flash is available to the visual system until that flash arrives, so no perceptual process running at the time of the midpoint could have produced the element "green at the midpoint." (3) That element must therefore be inserted into the conscious episode after the fact: either a completed percept is later edited (the Orwellian route), or the whole episode is delayed and staged so the color change is present from the first moment of consciousness (the Stalinesque route). (4) Introspectively, the two are indistinguishable; the experience presents itself as a seamless, real-time transition. (5) If a retrospective edit can be introspectively undetectable in a case where we know from the physics that one must have occurred, then we can never rule out that any ordinary visual experience—watching a ball cross a room, following a speaker's lips—is also a delayed, edited reconstruction. (6) We therefore have no reason to believe any visual experience is of the present moment rather than a retrospective construction. (7) But then the difference between perceiving something as it happens and merely remembering or confabulating it seems to dissolve, and the phrase "seeing the present" becomes empty. Which numbered step should be rejected, and why did it look sound? If you think the argument is instead valid, which premise is the least costly to surrender, and what is left of ordinary talk about perceiving the present?
