ok so ive been going down a rabbit hole on this thing called sphexishness. the example is something like: theres a wasp (a sphex) that brings a paralyzed cricket back to its burrow, drops it at the entrance, goes inside to inspect the tunnel, comes back out, and then drags the cricket in. but if you nudge the cricket a couple inches away while the wasp is inside, it comes out, drags the cricket back to the doorstep, and runs the whole inspect-the-tunnel routine again instead of just taking it in. you can do this over and over and it never improvizes. the point people draw is that it looks like the wasp is being careful and smart, but really its just locked into a fixed loop.

and now im pretty convinced humans are a fancier version of the same thing. like when i think ive made a free choice, maybe im just running a way more complicated stack of fixed routines with better sensors and memmory. so the position is: free will is basically an illusion, or at least theres nothing about human flexibility that deserves to be called free will.

what would count against this? i dont mean the cheap stuff like "but i feel free" or "we can deliberate." i want an honest map of what would have to be true for the sphexish view of humans to be wrong. which observations, abilities, or arguments should actually move someone who holds it? rank them from decisive to just awkward, and say which ones a defender of the view would themselves admit count against it vs which ones theyd dismiss as just question-begging. also tell me what the view predicts about us that a free-will view wouldnt — like what neuroscience or AI would have to show. and does the position end up defining itself so broadly that nothing could falsify it, and if so is that actually a problem for a view like this? personally i think the strongest thing would be finding something humans do that couldnt in principle be captured as some finite routine, but i honestly dont know what that would look like or if "finite routine" is even the right line to draw.
