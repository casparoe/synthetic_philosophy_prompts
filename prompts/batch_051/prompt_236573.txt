Ok so I keep coming back to a reading of Midnight in Paris through Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, the one where he distinguishes monumental, antiquarian, and critical history. I think there's something real here, but I dont want the usual move where the film is just an illustration of the trichotomy. I want someone to do the honest version: where does the film actually dramatize those three stances, and where does it pull against the categories.

The obvious mapping: Gil's whole thing is antiquarian, he worships the texture of 1920s Paris and treats the present as thin by comparison, and his midnight time travel is a fantasy of living inside monumental history, where the greats are his peers and his draft gets validated by Gertrude Stein and Hemingway. Then Adriana's Belle Époque move, and then the 1890s people looking further back, show the infinite regress of nostalgia. The film seems to take that as evidence that every age flees its present, and Gil's ending is him giving up the time travel and, I guess, choosing the present and his own writing. My prior is that this is actually closer to Nietzsche's remedy, the capacity to forget and live unhistorically, than to critical history. Critical history is supposed to put the past on trial and break its authority, but Gil doesn't really judge the 1920s, he just stops using it as a hiding place. So maybe the film refuses the three-way schema in an interesting way rather than instantiating it.
