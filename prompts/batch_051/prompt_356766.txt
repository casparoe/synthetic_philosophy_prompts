i'm an ML engineer, and a friend roped me into a philosophy reading group. Last month we read Sellars's Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind, and the essay has been stuck in my head because it reminded me of a debate at work. Sellars's target, as I understand it, is something like the idea that there are basic sensory givens — sense data or raw feels — that are known directly and that justify everything else without needing any prior concepts. His objection is that this gets things backwards: a bare sensation cant justify anything because it isn't the kind of thing that can stand in inferential or justificatory relations. It only becomes epistemically usable once it's already been caught up in concepts, language, and commitments. Fine.

The parallel: at work we're building a model that takes raw audio and tries to learn representations for speech. We have a running argument about whether the raw waveform is a kind of given for the model — the neutral, uninterpreted input that the model then conceptualizes. Some people think the model starts from pure data and builds upward. Others, including me half-convinced, think that's a myth too: the raw waveform only functions as an input relative to the architecture, the loss function, the pretraining task, and the data curation. Those choices already encode a huge amount of conceptual structure. The waveform is not given to the model in any epistemically interesting sense; it is given by us under a description.

I can't tell if this is a real structural parallel or if I'm pattern-matching. Sellars is talking about epistemic justification for a human knower. A neural network doesn't know things in his sense; it doesn't have beliefs, offer reasons, or enter the space of reasons at all. So maybe the analogy breaks exactly where his argument gets its force. But then again, if the target is just the idea that there can be a pre-conceptual, unmixed given that grounds a system's categories, maybe the ML case is a good concrete example. Is this deep or superficial? And if it is real, does anything from Sellars predict something about why pretraining on raw signals often fails to produce the kind of concepts we expect?
