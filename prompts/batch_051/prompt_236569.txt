I'm a software engineer, and the vocabulary I actually reason in is version control: commits, branches, forks, merges, upstream, tags, and force-push. I want you to restate the debate about whether a traditional story — a myth, a folktale type such as Cinderella, a ballad — has a canonical version, using only that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Lay out the main positions one by one: roughly, (a) a single original lies behind all the variants and later versions are corruptions of it, (b) the variants simply are the story, with no privileged original, and (c) a "standard" version exists only because some institution or edition was later forced into the role of main. For each position, say what translates cleanly, what has to be bent to fit the version-control metaphor, and what cannot be expressed in git terms at all. Then give a residue report: tell me whether the untranslatable part is a limitation of my vocabulary or a sign that the very idea of a canonical version smuggles in assumptions from print and code that oral tradition never made. Do not pretend the metaphor is exact — flag every point where git's assumptions about linear ancestry, content identity, and change are doing illegitimate work, and treat the residue as the interesting part.
