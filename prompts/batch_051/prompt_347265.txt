I am a philosophy professor working on formal epistemology and practical rationality, and I have gradually realized that my own considered verdicts about the norms of wagering no longer fit the view I have taught for years. The view is roughly geometric rationality: for decisions whose payoffs multiply wealth, a rational agent should maximize expected log wealth—equivalently, maximize the geometric mean of multiplicative returns—because this is what maximizes long-run growth and avoids ruin; in a standard even-money bet with a known edge, this yields the Kelly fraction. I would like you to help me extract from my concrete judgments the principle that actually governs them, stated precisely, even if that means abandoning the Kelly criterion as a fully general norm.

Here are the verdicts I want the principle to fit. (1) A coin known to be biased 60/40 toward heads will be flipped once a day for 1,000 days; each morning I may stake any fraction of my current bankroll, which compounds, on an even-money bet on heads. I am convinced the uniquely rational policy is to stake 20% of the current bankroll each day: zero leaves value on the table, and going all-in is reckless. (2) The very same 60/40 even-money coin is offered exactly once, I may again stake any fraction, but the bankroll is my entire retirement savings and no future income is possible. Here my verdict is that I should stake well under 20%—perhaps around 5%—and that 20% is clearly too much. (3) A single all-or-nothing wager on a fair coin: heads increases my total net worth by 75%, tails decreases it by 50%, and I must accept or decline the wager exactly as stated. I would decline, despite its positive expected monetary value of $0.125 per dollar of net worth. (4) Faced with the St. Petersburg game, I would pay only a modest finite sum—something in the neighborhood of $20—and I feel no rational pressure at all from its infinite arithmetic expectation.

Please tell me what principle, or small family of principles, captures this pattern, and be explicit about which feature of each case is doing the work. If the answer is not simply to maximize expected log wealth, explain exactly how to repair the view: for instance, does Kelly/geometric reasoning govern only repeated multiplicative sequences, with one-shot decisions answered by a different norm—and if so, what is that norm, and why does it not also govern the repeated case? Then test the repaired principle on two cases I have not settled: (a) the 75-up/50-down all-or-nothing wager from (3) offered independently every morning for 300 days, with no fractional option; (b) the 60/40 even-money fractional coin from (2), except that the bankroll is $100 of play money ring-fenced from my actual wealth. Finally, say which of my four verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to reverse, and state the strongest case a committed full-Kelly geometric rationalist could make against your diagnosis.
