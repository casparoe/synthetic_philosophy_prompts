Here is an argument I have reconstructed from Hobbes's remarks about geometry, and I cannot tell whether it proves more than it should. (1) We can have demonstrative knowledge only of what we ourselves have made, because making something gives us knowledge of its causes. (2) Geometrical figures are things we make: their generation is laid down by our own definitions and constructions, and the theorems about them unfold from what we have so laid down. (3) So geometry is a demonstrative science. (4) The commonwealth is likewise something we make, through the covenant by which a multitude authorizes a sovereign. (5) Therefore the science of the commonwealth can be demonstrative in just the way geometry is. I keep wanting to reject (5), but I cannot locate a break in the reasoning. Is the argument valid? Is each premise true? If the trouble is an equivocation on "make" or "cause," say on which reading the argument is valid and whether its premises are true on that reading; if a specific step fails, identify it precisely.
