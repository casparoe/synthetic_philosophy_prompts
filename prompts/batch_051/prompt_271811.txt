I am a high school student, and in my philosophy club we have been discussing the problem of vagueness. I understand the basic Sorites argument: if a person who is 180 centimeters tall is tall, then a person who is 179.9 centimeters tall is also tall, and by repeating this kind of step one seems forced to conclude that a very short person is tall. I have read that two important responses to this puzzle are epistemicism and supervaluationism, but I am having trouble seeing what actually separates them beyond the slogans that epistemicists believe vague predicates have sharp but unknowable boundaries while supervaluationists believe there are many admissible ways of making a vague predicate precise.

Could you please explain, in some detail, what each theory says about the tolerance premise, meaning the conditional that leads from one case to the next in the Sorites argument? In particular, what does each theory accept, reject, or classify as true, false, or neither? Could you then explain how each theory would handle a genuinely borderline case, such as a person whose height is right around the threshold for counting as tall? Finally, to test whether I have actually understood the distinction, could you apply both theories to a case I have not seen discussed before: a school policy that bans winter hats indoors, and a student who is wearing a thin knitted headband that covers the ears. Do the two theories yield different verdicts about whether the headband counts as a winter hat, or do they arrive at the same verdict for different reasons? I am not looking for a policy ruling; I want to see the philosophical positions in action.
