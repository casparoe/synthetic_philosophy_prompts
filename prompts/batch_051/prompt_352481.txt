Draft the joint statement that would result from a good-faith adversarial collaboration between a committed aggregationist and a committed Taurek-style anti-aggregationist about rescue cases. For the purposes of the statement, a rescue case is one in which an agent has a scarce resource — the standard illustration is a single dose of a life-saving drug — and can use it to save exactly one person, David, or exactly five different people, where no one on either side is distinguished by relationship to the agent, urgency, age, expected years of life, responsibility for the situation, or prognosis. The aggregationist holds that the numbers may count in such cases: the fact that five distinct individuals would survive can itself be a ground for choosing to save the five. The anti-aggregationist holds that the numbers do not count in that direct way: each of the six has an equally strong claim to the agent's aid, and the fact that five such claims fall on one side does not by itself give the agent a stronger reason to favor that side.

The statement should have three parts.

First, a numbered list of agreed claims. These should be substantive rather than merely terminological. They should include, for example, what each side grants about the strength and character of an individual's claim to be saved; whether and how tradeoffs within a single life differ structurally from tradeoffs across lives; what the two can jointly say about fairness when not everyone can be saved; whether they agree that the agent may, may not, or must flip a coin; and what they both affirm about how numbers figure in the agent's deliberation as opposed to in the justification of the choice.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should isolate one remaining point of dispute. The row must state the issue in neutral wording, and then give the aggregationist's own formulation of what they affirm and the anti-aggregationist's own formulation of what they affirm, each written so that the relevant side would recognize it as exactly their view. Neither column should reduce the other side to a strawman.

Third, a list of cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one of them would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a concrete case or argument that both sides would agree bears on it, and record in advance each side's prediction or what each side would say about it. At least one crux should concern a distinction the two camps may have been using differently — for example, pairwise balancing of one person's claim against another's claim versus comparing the total number of lives at stake; ex ante justification, which evaluates the decision by the prospects it gives each person beforehand, versus ex post justification, which evaluates it by the outcome that results; or treating persons as sites of value that can be summed versus treating them as sources of claims that must be answered one by one. If you conclude that part of the dispute has been verbal rather than substantive, identify it and give the disambiguated terms both could now accept.

The document should read as one that both individuals could sign, should not include footnotes, citations, or references to the secondary literature, and should make the residual disagreement exact rather than softening it.
