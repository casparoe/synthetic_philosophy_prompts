I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of history, sixteen students, one 80-minute session. I want an activity that makes students feel, before the theory, that archives are not neutral warehouses of the past: who gets recorded, what gets preserved, and how later inquirers can ask questions are all outcomes of contingent exercises of power, and the resulting silences in the sources are themselves evidence of those exercises.

Design a simulation in which a single event is documented, culled, and then reconstructed under constraints, so that students discover the silences for themselves rather than being told about them. The activity should run with only index cards, pens, and a one-page handout; no phones or outside research. Please give me:

- a concrete scenario (I have in mind a port town around 1750, with a dockworkers' wage dispute, a smallpox outbreak, a disputed inheritance, and a shipwreck, but feel free to modify this)
- the roles and rules, especially what each role is permitted to record and what incentives lead each role not to record certain things
- a culling round that represents archival selection and loss without any single villain and without reducing it to mere accident
- a reconstruction round in which later students must answer a factual question they were not told in advance, using only the records that survived
- a timeline for the full 80 minutes, with the theory deliberately held back until the debrief
- a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto concepts such as provenance, original order, appraisal, the power to name and classify, the economics of preservation, and the idea that a silence in the sources is produced rather than simply given

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, and be frank about which feature of archival power a classroom simulation cannot reproduce and the facilitator must therefore supply afterwards.
