I'm a PhD student in philosophy of art, writing a chapter on why originals matter, and I've been collecting my own snap judgments about cases because I can't tell if they're coherent. I'm hoping you can reverse-engineer whatever principle is making these calls, or tell me which ones I have to give up.

By 'value' I don't mean market price exactly — I mean whatever it is that makes you care about the object. Here's what I've got. (1) A modest 17th-century Dutch still life I once loved turns out to be a 20th-century forgery. The canvas hasn't changed, but my sense of its worth drops off a cliff. (2) I'm offered a molecule-for-molecule replica of a Vermeer, not passed off as real, visually indistinguishable from the original even under a microscope. I'd love to see it in a show, but it doesn't pull at me the way the original would, and I wouldn't pay anywhere near the same for it. (3) A posthumous bronze cast of a Rodin, authorized by the estate and pulled from Rodin's original plaster, is for sale, and I find I don't mind at all that Rodin never touched the bronze; I'd happily own it. (4) My grandfather's watch, which he said he wore during his military service, turns out to be a mall-bought replica from the 1990s. I'm quietly gutted, even though the object itself is unchaged. (5) When a song file gets copied bit-for-bit, I truly cannot bring myself to see the copy as any worse or less valuable than the original file. (6) But a high-resolution facsimile of Beethoven's handwritten score leaves me cold compared to standing in front of the actual manuscript.

The pattern I can't reconcile is this: in (2) and (6) an exact physical copy is worth less, but in (5) an exact digital copy is worth just the same. And in (3) a later cast made through the artist's mold feels fine, while a later painting made by a forger's hand feels worthless, even though neither object was made by the artist. My working hypothesis is that value must track some kind of causal link back to the act of creation — but that seems to die on the digital case, and maybe on (4). Is there a single principle that gets all six, or nearly all? What does the best principle say about a case I keep flip-flopping on: a note-for-note perfect, fully acknowledged re-performance of a famous 1959 jazz solo — is it the same work, and does it carry the same value? And which of my six verdicts, if any, does the principle force me to abandon?
