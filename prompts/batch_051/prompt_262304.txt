Six months ago I was diagnosed with a cardiac condition that substantially raises my risk of stroke, and I have spent much of the time since trying to decide how to live with the numbers. My cardiologist quotes a 4% annual risk, because that is the rate for everyone with my diagnosis. The surgeon quotes 11%, because she restricts to patients my age with my blood pressure, ejection fraction, and family history. Both figures are, in some sense, accurate; they cannot both be the probability I should act on when I decide whether to have the procedure. I have come to think that what looks like a medical disagreement is really the reference class problem: when we assign a probability to a single case, we must first place the case in a class of similar cases, and the choice of class does the real work.

I would like a working definition of the correct reference class for a single-case probability: when a statement like "your risk is 6%" is made, what makes one class rather than another the class the claim is properly relative to? I am less interested in a survey of the literature than in a definition I can actually apply.

Please run your proposed definition against the following cases, say explicitly where it gives the wrong answer, gives no answer, or forces you to defend a surprising verdict, and revise the definition or defend those verdicts as you go. The cases are:

1. A fair coin is about to be flipped once. The probability of heads is 1/2 relative to the class of fair coin flips; it is unknown relative to the class containing only this exact flip. Which class is the correct one, and why does the problem not simply dissolve by saying the case belongs to both?
2. My own stroke risk, described above: the broad class of everyone with the diagnosis versus the narrower class matching my age, sex, blood pressure, and family history. I want a definition that distinguishes genuine narrowing from cherry-picking.
3. A 41-year-old man with no prior convictions is arrested for burglary, and a judge wants the probability that he will reoffend before trial. Is the correct rate the one for all people charged with burglary, for people with his age and clean record, or for people matching his full history? How narrow is too narrow for a class to support a rate?
4. The probability that the current President will win re-election. The class of all incumbent Presidents seeking re-election is small; the class of incumbents with precisely this approval rating, this economy, and this opponent is empty. What is the correct reference class for an event that is in an important sense unique?
5. A woman has won a major lottery twice in three years and is investigated for fraud. The prosecution says the probability of her winning twice is astronomically small, relative to her specific ticket purchases. The defense says the relevant probability is that someone, somewhere, among all lottery players over decades, would win twice, and that event is not surprising at all. Which framing is correct, and does the correct answer depend on what question is being asked?
6. A 30-year-old in good health wants the probability that he will live to 90. The class of all humans ever born includes the enormous early-childhood mortality of past centuries; the class of healthy 30-year-old men in wealthy countries now is plainly more relevant; the class of people with his exact genome, habits, and life history has one member and provides no rate at all. What rule governs how much of his past and present should enter the class?

I am inclined toward the view that the correct reference class is fixed by the decision the probability is meant to serve, not by the world alone, so that there may be no reference-class-free answer to "what is my risk, really?" But I suspect that view will struggle with cases (1) and (5), and I would welcome a definition that either supports it or gives me something better.
