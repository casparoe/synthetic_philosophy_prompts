You are a political philosopher who works on property, consent, and the moral foundations of taxation. I want a committed answer, not a survey of positions. Think through the harder cases before you settle on a definition, and begin by brainstorming two or three candidate notions of theft and the moral cutoff each would imply.

The passage I want you to pin your analysis to is from John Locke, Second Treatise of Government, Chapter XI, "Of the Extent of the Legislative Power." I do not have the exact wording in front of me, so I will paraphrase rather than quote. In substance, Locke says that the supreme power cannot take from any man any part of his property without his own consent, because the preservation of property is the end of government. The question should turn on this passage, not on the better-known labor-mixing passage in Chapter V.

Assume for the sake of argument that the passage means at least this: political authority may not legitimately expropriate property without the owner's consent. On that assumption, propose a working definition of "theft" that meets three desiderata. First, it classifies a mugger taking a wallet as theft. Second, it classifies a person taking an unowned stick from the woods as not theft. Third, it gives a definite answer in each of the contested cases below.

Then run your definition against each case, say which verdict follows, and identify the two cases on which your definition is least secure and why:

- an income tax enacted by a majority in a fair election
- a fine for driving through a red light
- a court order to pay child support
- conscription of soldiers in wartime
- a high inheritance tax on estates above a threshold
- a fee charged to enter a public park
- a carbon tax on emissions
- a user fee for a public water system, where the alternative is that no clean water is provided to those who do not pay
- a democratically enacted plan that bulldozes a home under eminent domain and pays the owner compensation at above market rate

Finally, answer this directly: does the Lockean passage, interpreted honestly, make all taxation theft, some taxation theft, or no taxation theft on your definition? Which of those three answers does your argument actually force you to accept, and why might that answer surprise someone who begins by saying "taxation is theft"?
