A medium-sized city decides to govern itself in a way it calls “Deweyan.” The rule is: no ordinance is ever passed permanently. Every law starts as an 18-month trial. Before the council votes, the department that would administer the law has to write down three outcome measures and, for each one, a threshold they’ll count as “the law worked” (e.g. complaints down more than X%, on-time payments up more than Y%, whatever). A citizens’ assembly of 40 randomly selected residents then has to approve those measures and thresholds, and can amend them, after hearing arguments from the law’s supporters and opponents. At the end of the 18 months, if the law hits its thresholds it converts automatically into a permanent law (well, until someone passes a new trial to repeal it); if it misses any threshold it expires with nothing replacing it, no vote. The assembly dissolves and a new one is drawn for the next round.

The city’s pitch is Dewey-ish: policies are fallible experiments, and the public isn’t just there to pick winners but to help decide what would count as failure. How does this setup actually behave? What should we expect from departments writing their own report cards, from assemblies that have to choose metrics for a law they’ll never see again, and from a council whose legacy depends on wether things pass or expire? Are there failure modes where the city ends up with only conservative, easily gameable laws, or where good laws die because their actual point didn’t match the three metrics? Could the system be bent, without gutting the experimentalist idea, so it really treats policies as hypotheses rather than as branding?
