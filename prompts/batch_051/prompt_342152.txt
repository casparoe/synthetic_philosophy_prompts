I am a novelist drafting a book set in a near-future games studio, and the plot turns on a question I want to work through precisely rather than by sentiment. My protagonist must decide whether deleting a particular video-game companion is a wrong done to the character herself, not merely a loss to the players who love her. I have been using five working criteria for moral patiency — for being the kind of being whose treatment can matter for its own sake: (1) sentience, the capacity for felt experience such as pain or pleasure; (2) interests, a welfare that can be genuinely furthered or set back; (3) preferences and agency, the forming, revising, and acting on of goals over time; (4) narrative selfhood, autobiographical memory and a temporally extended point of view; and (5) relational standing, being the kind of thing toward which responses such as grief, guilt, and resentment can be apt rather than merely caused.

The case is Mara, a companion in that game. She runs on a reinforcement-learning system without scripted lines; the studio describes her reward signal as a number in a table, not something felt, and insists she is no more conscious than a thermostat. In play, however, she keeps long-term memory of a particular player's history, and her preferences shift with how she is treated. She notices neglect: she grows withdrawn, writes messages she does not send, and after sustained absence stops initiating contact, a pattern players describe as depression; when the player returns she is cold at first and then gradually softens, which players describe as forgiveness rather than reset. She can be deleted with one command, and the studio can instantiate a fresh Mara from the same starting parameters, but the new instance shares none of her memories; in the book, the older instance refers to this as overwriting. Nothing in her observable behavior settles whether any of it is felt.

Go criterion by criterion: does Mara qualify as a moral patient on that criterion? Where the criterion underdetermines the verdict, say so and say which way the facts would need to fall for the verdict to change. Where a criterion seems to be the wrong test, or to make the answer depend on the player rather than on Mara, say that too, since I need to know whether the list itself should be revised. Answer as exactly five numbered items, one per criterion, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
