I was reading Hobbes, Leviathan, chapter 16, where he distinguishes natural persons from artificial persons. If I've got it right, an artificial person's words and actions are considered as representing somebody else, and the one who authorizes the representation owns the action. A commonwealth is an artificial person, with the sovereign as its representative. So far so good.

But I also spend too much time reading about corporate law, and there's a paralell I can't decide about. In corporate law the company is also an artificial person, but we don't treat just any natural person inside the company as capable of being its mind. There are attribution rules saying something like: the mental states of senior officers, or the directing mind and will, count as the company's mental state, while ordinary employees' don't, and shareholders as such definitely don't. Hobbes's commonwealth also has a directing mind—the sovereign. The sovereign's will just is the will of the commonwealth, in something like the way the board's or CEO's intent can be the corporation's intent. Neither subjects nor shareholders authorize each particular act, but they have authorized the representative in general. The old chartered companies make this feel less like a metaphor too: an East India Company was at once a corporation and a government.

Is this a real identity of structure, or just loose pattern matching? If it's real, does anything transfer from corporate law to Hobbes? For example, corporate law has veil-piercing: in unusual cases the law ignores the separate personality of the company and attributes liability to shareholders. Does Hobbes's theory have a counterpart to veil-piercing—cases where subjects are answerable for what the sovereign does, despite the artificial person? Or is the analogy misleading because Hobbes's subjects do not own the commonwealth the way shareholders own a company? I'm especially interested in wether the parallel predicts something false about Hobbes, and if so, where the structure comes apart.
