Over the weekend my cousin Dana and my friend Chris nearly derailed a cookout arguing about wether animal suffering before the Fall can be squared with evolution and the doctrine of a good creation. Dana is in a systematic theology program and Chris is an ecologist, so neither was going to let the other get away with hand-waving. I got drafted as the referee and honestly kept flipping. Here's roughly how it went.

Dana said, "I'm not denying the fossil record. But look at what it shows: hundreds of millions of years of predation, parasitism, disease, starvation, all before any human being existed. If that's the world God originally called very good, then the word good has stopped doing any work."

Chris said, "That was never a claim about zero animal pain. In Genesis, good is about a functioning, ordered cosmos, not a moral grade for every organism's experience. And Paul's line about death coming through sin is about the human condition before God, not about cell death or a lion's lunch."

Dana said, "That's genre-shifting on demand. You read the text literally when it fits your theology and switch to it's poetic the moment biology gets uncomfortable. If death through sin only means human spiritual alienation, then creation groaning in Romans 8 is just a metaphor, the fall wasn't cosmic, and the last enemy to be destroyed is nothing but my own mortality."

Chris said, "No, I'd say the fall is cosmic in a different way: human sin has structural consequences like cruelty, injustice, meaninglessness. It doesn't follow that bacteria became pathogenic in 4000 BC. Look at an anteater's tongue or a cheetah's spine. Those are adaptations. Either they're part of the good order, or God added them to creation after a garden incident as a punishment. Pick one."

Dana said, "I pick the first, but then the good order runs on the agony of sentient creatures. If I found a watch that only worked by grinding up kittens, I wouldn't call the watchmaker good just because the watch keeps accurate time. You've rescued the doctrine of creation by making predation into something God is fine with."

Chris said, "I'm not saying I'm fine with it. I'm saying the alternative, where every carnivore's dentition and every parasite's life cycle appears after the fall as a punishment, doesn't fit the biology and doesn't make God look better. And maybe a biosphere that can produce creatures capable of knowing and loving God couldn't get there without competition and death. Then good would mean fit for the whole story, not pain-free on day one."

Dana said, "And now good means whatever God does, so the doctrine is unfalsifiable. But if creation is in bondage to decay, as Romans says, it's bondage God put it in. The only honest options I can see are that the fall reaches backwards and forwards in ways we don't understand, or that there are two deaths, one animal and one spiritual, and Paul is talking about the second. Yours is just relabeling."

Chris said, "I never promised it would be comfortable. But two deaths isn't an explanation unless you can say what the first death was for, and a fall that reaches backwards just moves the mystery into the past without solving it. I'm not the one relabeling here."

I cant adjudicate this myself, because every time I think one of them has won, the other lands a real hit. Can you take the exchange apart and tell me who is right about which specific points, where they're talking past each other, and which reconciliations each of them could honestly accept?
