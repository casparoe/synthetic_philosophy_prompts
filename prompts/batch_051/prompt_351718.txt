Here's a scenario I keep throwing at my decision theory students, and I'd like you to actually work through the mechanics instead of just repeating the usual slogan. Somebody builds an automated prediction market—call it the Box—for a three-way race between Abel, Baker, and Chen. Abel and Baker are both from the Left Party. The Box posts prices and is programmed to buy or sell any contract at the posted price, with no position limits and no updating between the opening and the close. But its pricing function is badly non-additive: it posts 0.6 on Abel, 0.6 on Baker, and 0.8 on Left Party Wins. The owner has seen the numbers and refuses to touch them; he thinks Dutch book arguments are overrated and says a bookie with incoherent prices can always just decline the trades. Except the Box, as built, can't decline.

Predict, with visible reasoning, what happens during the six hours the market is open, and then across the next fifty markets if the owner keeps the design. I want you to seperate what follows mechanically, given the prices and the algorithm, from what depends on who shows up, how much capital they have, reputation, or regulation. Does the Box get drained? Does its pricing become any more informative about the election through the mere fact that people trade against it? Does the owner come around, or does he find some ad hoc way to keep the thing running? Then give me your diagnosis: does being Dutch-bookable show that the Box's prices are irrational, or just that they're unprofitable as a market-making strategy? Since its the distinction I actually care about, be explicit about which predictions are robust, which are guesses, and what would change your mind about the diagnosis. American English, please.
