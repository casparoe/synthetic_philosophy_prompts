Consider the following asymmetry in ordinary moral judgment. A person walking beside a shallow pond sees a small child drowning; saving the child would require wading in, ruining an expensive pair of shoes and missing a meeting, but would involve no danger. Most people would say that walking on is a serious moral failure, and if the person instead sat on the bank and checked their phone they would be regarded with something close to horror. Now consider a person who receives an appeal from a reputable charity showing that a donation roughly equal to the cost of those shoes is expected to save a child's life; the person deletes the email and spends the money on dinner. Most people judge that person to have done nothing wrong, or at worst to have missed an opportunity for optional generosity. In both cases a child lives or dies depending on whether an agent bears a modest cost, but the blame we assign differs enormously. Is that difference justified by anything morally relevant, or only by what is psychologically salient? Identify the strongest candidate principle that could justify the asymmetry—physical proximity, directness of the causal chain, certainty of outcome, identifiability of the victim, uniqueness of the rescuer, the need for a functioning norm of rescue, and so on—and test each against harder variants: a child drowning just behind a fence, a child visibly drowning on a live video feed, a drowning child in the presence of a crowd, a donation that is only probabilistically linked to saving a life, a rescue that could be performed by mailing a check to a local service. Then give a clear verdict: should the ordinary asymmetry be kept, revised, or given up? If you would keep a version of it, say exactly which feature does the moral work and why the variants do not collapse the distinction. Limit your answer to 900 words.
