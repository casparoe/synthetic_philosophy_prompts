You are an expert in decision theory and bounded rationality, with a particular interest in how satisficing translates into real institutional procedure. I am an emergency department attending and chair of our hospital's diagnostic safety committee. Our junior physicians vary wildly in when they stop investigating: some order every conceivably informative test, others commit too early and miss things. We want a reusable procedure—not advice about one patient—for deciding when to stop testing and act on a working diagnosis, when to keep searching, and when to escalate to a senior attending. It should be grounded in satisficing: an explicit aspiration level for how sure we need to be, sequential acquisition of information, and stopping when the aspiration is met rather than when uncertainty is zero. It should be a card a tired intern could run in under ten minutes. The constraints: decisions happen many times per shift under fatigue and queue pressure; tests cost time and create iatrogenic risk; a plea of thoroughness is not a defense if the workup was irrational given what was known at each step; and discharges and admissions require a senior sign-off. Before you write the checklist, briefly brainstorm the two or three places where satisficing is hardest to translate to the bedside.

Then write the procedure as an ordered checklist. For each question, state what counts as a sufficient answer and what the next step is. Say explicitly how the aspiration level should be adjusted when the department gets busier or when the base rate of serious disease changes, and flag every step that is a genuine judgment call rather than a computation. Then run the procedure on these three cases: (1) a 28-year-old with sharp pleuritic chest pain, a normal ECG, and one negative troponin; (2) an 80-year-old with vague weakness and several mildly abnormal labs that do not cohere into any single diagnosis; (3) a 45-year-old smoker whose pain and exam findings already meet the aspiration level for costochondritis after five minutes, even though a rare but dangerous aortic dissection has not been formally excluded. For at least one case the procedure should give an answer we will not like—identify which one and explain why the procedure still demands that answer. End by saying what this procedure cannot do: the cases it will systematically get wrong and the residual judgment it cannot eliminate.
