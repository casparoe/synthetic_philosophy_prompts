A provision in my country's communications offences legislation reads:

"Section 9(1). A person commits an offence under this section if the person publishes material by means of an electronic communications network, the material is accessible to a person located in this State, and communicating that material directly to that person in this State would be an offence under any other enactment. Section 9(4). It is immaterial that the publication occurred outside this State or that the material was first stored outside this State."

The drafters evidently had a conventional arrangement in mind: an operator places content on a server abroad, and users in my country retrieve it from that server. My question is whether the provision reaches a case that arrangement did not anticipate.

A journalist in another country publishes an investigative article on a decentralised microblogging protocol. The protocol has no central server and no single operator: anyone may run a relay that stores posts and serves them to whoever requests them, and readers deliberately retrieve posts to their own devices through whatever relays they choose. No relay through which the article reached the reader is located in my country. The article is lawful where the journalist lives and where the relays are situated, but it would amount to criminal defamation under my country's law, because it accuses one of our ministers of corruption and our law does not admit truth as a defence for that kind of imputation. A reader in my country requests and reads the article through a relay in a third country. The journalist did not direct the article at readers here and had no particular reason to think one would seek it out.

I am not looking for practical legal advice. What I want to understand is who, if anyone, publishes the article within the meaning of section 9: the journalist, who released it into a network from which any person could pull it; the relay operator, whose server cached the bytes and handed them to the reader; or no one at all, because the reader's own act assembled the material in this State. I would be interested in what the plain words, the purpose of the legislation, what the drafters would likely have said if asked, and what a reasonable user of the network would take the provision to mean each yield — and, behind that, what the case suggests about whether one country's speech law can govern a network deliberately built so that no one stands where the drafters assumed a publisher would stand.
