I was in a study group last week and we went down a rabbit hole about regress arguments—the distinction between the harmless infinite ones and the ones where the whole thing falls apart unless the chain bottoms out. A friend ran this argument on us and I still can't find the exit. It seems to prove there are no relational facts, which is obviously wrong, but each step sounded fine at the time:

(1) If a stands in a relation to b—the cup is on the desk, the email came before the phone call, whatever—something has to do the relating, to tie a, b, and the relation together; otherwise the relation would just sit there doing nothing.
(2) Whatever ties them is itself a further relation, and so it too must be tied to a, b, and the original relation by yet another relation.
(3) So you get a third relation tying the second one to the first and to a and b, then a fourth, and so on forever.
(4) But a fact never actually obtains if it depends on an endless chain of further facts, each of which only does its job after the next one does its.
(5) So nothing ever has any relational property: being on, being to the left of, being heavier than—none of it ever actually happens.
(6) But that's nuts. The cup is sitting on my desk right now.

I'd really like to keep (1) and (6), since I don't see how a relation could genuinely relate without something binding it to its relata, and I don't see how to deny what's in front of me. But then (2) or (4) has to go, and I can't tell which loss is worse. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? And does this case show anything general about how to tell a vicious regress from a benign one?
