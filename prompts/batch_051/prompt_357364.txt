I help run a small tenants' organizing group in a city with a severe housing shortage. We have perhaps forty active members, little funding, and almost no legal support. We are campaigning for a local ordinance that would cap rent increases and require landlords to show cause before evicting a tenant. A more experienced organizer from a neighboring city has told me that we are organizing as though we had power we do not actually have: the mayor and the landlord lobby have both signaled that they will kill the ordinance, and by investing scarce morale and energy in an almost certain defeat, we risk exhausting our members and collapsing the group. He advises us to drop the ordinance and fight instead for something modest and winnable, such as stricter enforcement of housing-code violations, precisely because we cannot afford a grand failure. I see the wisdom in this, yet I also think something important would be lost if we shrank our aims to only what we already know we can win. How should I think about this? In particular, is there something epistemically, morally, or prudentially wrong with letting political hope outrun the evidence about what is achievable? Can a hope be unrealistic and nevertheless be the right hope to hold—and if so, what distinguishes that from mere wishful thinking or self-deception? I am less interested in a practical verdict about this specific campaign than in a principled account of how to assess hopes that are, on the evidence, very unlikely to be realized.
