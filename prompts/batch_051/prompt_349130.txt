You are an expert decision theorist and behavioral economist, hired as a consultant for a project that is trying to separate genuine violations of expected utility theory from artifacts of how choices are framed. Please think through the following carefully and in explicit steps. Before you answer, lay out the lotteries and write down their expected utilities; do not skip the algebra, and then interpret the result conceptually.

Consider the classic Allais choice problem, with payoffs measured in millions of dollars. In Problem 1, a decision-maker chooses between:

Option A: $1M for sure.
Option B: a 0.89 chance of $1M, a 0.10 chance of $5M, and a 0.01 chance of $0.

In Problem 2, the decision-maker chooses between:

Option C: a 0.11 chance of $1M and a 0.89 chance of $0.
Option D: a 0.10 chance of $5M and a 0.90 chance of $0.

The classic finding is that many people choose A over B in Problem 1 and D over C in Problem 2. That pattern conflicts with the independence axiom of expected utility theory — the axiom that says mixing the same common lottery into two options should not reverse the preference between them. Under expected utility theory, the two problems embed the same underlying comparison, so the pattern cannot be given a coherent expected-utility representation.

Now consider the following variation. Keep everything above fixed except that in Problem 2's option D, the $5M payoff is replaced by a parameter V > 0. Problem 1's option B still uses $5M.

Assume an expected-utility maximizer with a strictly increasing utility function u over money, normalized so that u($0) = 0.

1. Write down the two expected-utility inequalities that would need to hold for the Allais pattern (A over B in Problem 1, and D over C in Problem 2) to be consistent with expected utility theory, and rearrange them into a single chain of inequalities.

2. Use that chain to explain exactly why the pattern is inconsistent with expected utility theory when V = $5M. Then specify, in terms of u, the range of V for which the pattern becomes consistent with expected utility theory, and state the condition u must satisfy for such a V to exist at all.

3. Now take the specific utility function u(x) = x^(1/20), with x measured in millions of dollars. Verify that an expected-utility maximizer with this function prefers A over B in Problem 1, and compute the cutoff value of V (in millions of dollars, to two decimal places) above which the same person also prefers D over C in the modified Problem 2.

4. Interpret the result. What does the fact that raising V in Problem 2 is enough to make the Allais pattern expected-utility-consistent reveal about what is doing the work in the original paradox? Is the original pattern best understood as a structural violation of the independence axiom, as a certainty premium, or as a local preference reversal produced by evaluating the two problems in isolation from one another? Finally, if you ran the modified Problem 2 with V just above your threshold, what pattern would you expect from people who genuinely obey expected utility theory, as opposed to people whose choices in the original problem tracked the classic Allais pattern?
