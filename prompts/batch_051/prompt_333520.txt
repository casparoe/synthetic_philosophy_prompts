Yesterday evening I argued for hours with a friend about whether the death of a language is a loss in itself, and I came away unable to justify an asymmetry in our shared reactions. We both held that when the last fluent speaker of an endangered language dies, something of value disappears from the world — even where the community shifted voluntarily across generations, no individual was made worse off, and the grammar and lexicon were thoroughly documented. Yet neither of us would have counted the emergence of a new language as the addition of the same kind of value: a new sign language crystallizing among deaf children, a creole taking shape in a migrant port city, or a carefully designed auxiliary language would each increase linguistic diversity by one system, but we would not have greeted the gain with anything like the grief we attached to the loss. If the underlying value is linguistic diversity itself, gains and losses should be symmetric; if they are not, then it is probably not diversity that was doing the work, and we owe an account of what exactly was lost when the last speaker died. I would like your strongest defense of a principle that can justify this asymmetry, tested honestly against the thought that we are simply attached to antiquity and dramatic endings, and a clear verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
