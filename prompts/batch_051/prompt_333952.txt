Me and a friend got into it last night about existence in mathematics, and I walked away with an itch I can't scratch. He's fine with proofs that go like this: you have an infinite collection of nonempty boxes, and since each box is nonempty you just pick one item out of each — no rule for how, no algorithm, just pick — and then you assemble all the picked items and use them to prove something exists. I keep saying that knowing each box has at least one item in it isn't the same as being able to actually carry out the picking, and his comeback is always that each box is nonempty, so picking is just allowed, the items are already there, you're not making them. I can't tell if I'm being parnoid or if he's cheating. So: is there a name for that specific move, the infinitely-many-arbitrary-choices proof of existence? And is there a name for the broader position I seem to be sliding into, that to prove something exists in math you have to be able, even in principle, to exhibit or build it, not just infer that it has to be there? What I actually want is boundaries, not just a label: why does almost everyone accept the move for finitely many boxes, what exactly breaks in the infinite case, and do people who reject the move reject the whole style of proof or only some uses of it. My friend also pulled a trick that bugs me: he said the infinite-choice move is fine for proving things about infinite objects, but then five minutes later he used it to prove something about finite objects too, and I can't put my finger on why that felt like smuggling it in through the back door.
