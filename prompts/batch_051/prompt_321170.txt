I'm a PhD student in moral psychology, and I've been rereading the situationist critique of virtue ethics—the one that says virtue ethics needs robust, cross-situationally consistent character traits, but mid-century social psychology (Milgram, the Good Samaritan seminary students, the dime-in-the-phone-booth study, the Stanford prison experiment) shows that small situational features swamp individual differences in moral behavior, so the traits either don't exist or can't do the work virtue ethics asks of them. Here's the thing that keeps bugging me: the empirical base of the critique has aged badly. We've now had a decade-plus replication crisis in social psychology, and a lot of the exact studies the situationists lean on were small-N, selected, or haven't held up; SPE in particular has basically collapsed as evidence. I'm not saying every one of them failed, but suppose we accept that the strongest version of the situationist's empirical claim—that trivial, incidental features of a situation reliably produce big changes in apparently moral behavior—is no longer supported in its original form.

Give me a committed answer, not a survey. Does the situationist critique of virtue ethics survive this? Which premise is actually taking the hit: the empirical one, or the conceptual move from 'situations matter' to 'there are no robust traits'? Is there a repaired version that's weaker but still does real damage to virtue ethics as a theory, or does the virtue ethicist just get it's empirical footing back? If you think the critique is dead, say what the debate should be about now. American English, please.
