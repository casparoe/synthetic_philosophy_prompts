my lab meeting keeps coming back to the idea that perception is basically controlled hallucination. the story is that the brain is a prediction machine: it generates top-down guesses about the scene in front of you, and incoming sense data only matters because it produces prediction errors that correct the guesses. normal seeing is supposed to be the same underlying process as hallucinating, except that in normal seeing the errors keep anchoring the guesses to the world, whereas in a hallucination or a dream the brain dials down the weight on sensory error and just runs its own predictions full blast. someone in the group uses this to argue that we're never in direct contact with the world at all — what you experience is a construction, and the world only ever pushes back through error signals, like a spreadsheet flagging a bad guess.

i want an audit of how far the controlled hallucination picture actually holds. first give me the correspondence table: in the source case (a genuine hallucination) and the target case (ordinary waking perception), what maps onto what among top-down prediction, prediction error, sensory input, the gain or precision assigned to errors, and whatever does the reality-checking. then the places it breaks, separated into breaks that matter for the conclusion and breaks that are just cosmetic. i can already think of a few suspects but i dont know if they're real: hallucinations aren't obviously generated by the same fine-grained mechanisms as normal perception; sensory input doesn't just correct an already-formed guess, it constrains the space of possible guesses from the start; and the brain also sets the expected precision of its errors based on what it has learned, so the 'control' is doing more than turning a dial. tell me if those are actual disanalogies or my confusion. then say what the analogy quietly imports: does calling it controlled hallucination make perception sound like it's 90% fabrication and the world is basically unknowable, when maybe the machinery is just ordinary causal contact with some extra inference on top? and finally: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that we're never in direct contact with the world survive, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? if the analogy is bad, give me a better one.
