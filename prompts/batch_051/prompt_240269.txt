I've been tinkering with a short essay for a philosophy blog and could really use comments, especially on whether the core argument is doing genuine work or just re-skinning an old pragmatist point in Rortyan terms. The basic idea is this: people often say a mental state (or a model's internal state) represents the world when it lets the organism (or system) successfully cope with that world. Rorty's antirepresentationalism wants us to drop the whole mirror-of-nature picture and treat beliefs and vocabularies as tools, not as attempted pictures of reality. I want to push that one step further and apply it to machine learning: when someone calls a neural network's hidden vector a "representation" of a cat, they're making exactly the category mistake Rorty warned about—they're treating a useful dynamical shortcut as if it were a little portrait of catness. The working title is "The Cat Is Not in the Vector," and the claim is that what matters isn't whether the vector is about cats, but how it lets the rest of the network keep doing things fluently.

Does that step from "pictures don't explain successful coping" to "we shouldn't call these vectors representational at all" actually hold up? Don't just lay out the options; give me a committed verdict on whether the antirepresentationalist line is the right one or whether its overreaching, and say where the strongest objection would come from.
