I'm on my school's student council and we're rewriting the 'student rights' section of the handbook. Right now it reads like a list of things the administration has decided to let us do, and a bunch of us want to open it with a Student Bill of Rights that says students have certain rights by nature — free expression, privacy, that kind of thing — and the school doesnt grant them, it just has to respect them. Our principal is open to the idea but she asked a question none of us had a good answer for: if these rights don't come from the handbook or the school board or the law, where do they come from, and why should the school be bound by them? The teacher who advises us brought up Jeremy Bentham, who apparently thought natural rights talk is basically nonsense — that a right only exists once a legal system creates and enforces it, and saying you have a right no law gave you is just a dramatic way of saying you really want the thing. That landed harder than I expected because I actually like the natural rights language, but now I'm not sure if it's doing any real work or if it's just decoration.

I have to write a short memo for the council to read before our vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action: put the natural-rights preamble in as written, scrap it and justify the rights some other way, or keep it with changes. I want the memo to take Bentham's objection seriously instead of just calling him a killjoy, and to give the strongest version of the natural-rights side a fair run — if the memo ends up recommending against it, it should be for real reasons, not just because Bentham sounded clever. The council wont read anything that sounds like a philosophy paper, so keep it practical and give us an actual bottom line.
