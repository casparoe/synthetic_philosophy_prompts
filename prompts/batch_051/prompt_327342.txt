I keep going back and forth on the concept of human dignity. The deflationary view attracts me because it promises tidiness: on this view, when a bioethics document or a human rights charter appeals to dignity, the word is doing no independent work. Either it just means respecting the person's autonomous choices, or it is a solemn-sounding intensifier that could be deleted without loss. Every time I try to think of a case where dignity changes the answer, I find I can restate the relevant consideration in terms of autonomy or harm or respect, and the mystery evaporates. I like how economical that is.

But there is one intuition I cannot make evaporate. Picture an art performance in which a woman with full capacity, under no pressure, having taken legal advice, agrees for a large fee to spend an hour on all fours while the audience uses her back as a footstool. She is not deceived about what will happen, she is free to leave at any time, and afterwards she says she found it strange but not distressing and would do it again. If autonomy is the whole story, I do not see what is left to object to. And yet I am quite sure something is wrong with making a person into furniture, even briefly, even with consent. It feels degrading in a way that is not just a worry about her welfare, since by stipulation there is not much of one.

So I am torn between a theory I find compelling and an intuition I do not want to give up. I am not asking you to tell me who wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the deflationary view to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a third reading where dignity names a seperate wrong in the footstool case but adds nothing in the bioethics cases. Tell me what each option costs. Oh, and please keep the response free of headings, bullet points, bold text and tables; continuous prose only, like you are writing me an email.
