A colleague and I have been working through Sleeping Beauty cases, and we seem to have built an argument that proves too much. The conclusion it pushes toward is that Beauty's question has no well-defined answer at all, or worse, that conditionalization breaks down for self-locating evidence generally. That cannot be right, because ordinary agents do learn things like what day it is. I want to know where the argument goes wrong — or, if it does not, which premise is least costly to reject.

Set up the familiar case. Beauty will be put to sleep Sunday night. A fair coin is tossed. If it lands Heads, she will be woken once, on Monday. If it lands Tails, she will be woken twice, on Monday and Tuesday, with a drug erasing any memory of an earlier waking, and with the Tuesday waking subjectively indistinguishable from the Monday one. Beauty knows the entire protocol on Sunday. When she wakes during the experiment, what should her credence be that the coin landed Heads?

(1) On Sunday, before she is put to sleep, Beauty's credence in Heads is 1/2, and she is certain that she will be woken at least once no matter how the coin lands.

(2) Beauty updates by conditionalization: whatever her total evidence teaches her, she should move to her old credence conditional on the proposition that states completely and correctly what she has learned.

(3) Candidate A: her total evidence is the uncentered proposition that the protocol produces at least one waking. Since she was already certain of this on Sunday regardless of the coin, conditionalizing on it leaves her credence in Heads at 1/2.

(4) Candidate B: her total evidence is the centered proposition that this present waking — the one she is now in — is one of the three candidate awakenings the protocol defines: Monday-Heads, Monday-Tails, Tuesday-Tails. These three centered possibilities look symmetric; nothing in her evidence distinguishes them. By the Principle of Indifference, each deserves equal credence. Since exactly one of the three is a Heads-waking, conditionalizing gives a credence of 1/3 in Heads.

(5) But Candidate A and Candidate B are not two different discoveries Beauty might make. They are competing specifications of the very same waking experience. A complete theory of rational updating cannot allow one and the same total evidence to be specified in two ways that mandate different credences.

(6) So either exactly one of the two specifications is correct, or conditionalization does not apply to self-locating evidence in the way the argument assumes, or Beauty has no well-defined credence in Heads at all.

(7) If Candidate A is correct, then the centered possibilities in Candidate B are not the right objects of credence, and we owe an account of how anyone ever learns a self-locating truth such as that today is Monday. If Candidate B is correct, then we need an account of centered conditionalization, and we must explain why Beauty's Sunday certainty that she would wake at least once does not already exhaust her relevant evidence. If neither is fully correct, then Beauty apparently has no determinate credence in Heads, which seems to conflict with the fact that she would have coherent betting dispositions about the coin if she were offered wagers.

(8) The argument therefore seems to force a choice among three unappealing options: restrict conditionalization to uncentered evidence, extend it to centered evidence and accept the resulting treatments of self-location, or deny that Beauty's credence in Heads is well-defined.

I would most like to keep conditionalization and some form of the Principle of Indifference; I am least attached to the claim that Beauty's credence in Heads is well-defined. But I cannot see how that exit avoids infecting everyday self-locating beliefs. Which step should I reject, why did that step look compulsory, and what does rejecting it cost the halfer and thirder positions in this debate?
