I keep noticing a structural parallel between two results that are rarely discussed together. One is Good's theorem on the value of information: for an expected-utility maximizer, costless information can never reduce ex ante expected utility, because the agent is always free to ignore the signal. The other is the elementary decision-theoretic fact that enlarging a choice set can never make an agent worse off, because the agent need not select any of the new options. Both seem to be licensed by the same "you can simply ignore the new thing" argument, and both are said to fail in commitment and strategic settings: information can hurt an agent who cannot abstain from acting on it, while new options can hurt an agent by changing how others respond to them or by introducing temptations. I would like to know whether this parallel is deep or superficial. Are the two results instances of a single underlying theorem, and what exactly is the common structure? Where do their assumptions come apart, and would a counterexample to one transfer to the other? In particular, is Good's theorem just the fact that a signal expands the space of contingent strategies available to the agent, or does information contribute something beyond adding options?
