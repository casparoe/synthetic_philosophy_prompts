Suppose that John Stuart Mill, who died in 1873, could be brought up to date on a century and a half of media technology—including present-day generative models able to fabricate convincing footage of a person saying or doing something that never happened—and that he were then invited to advise a parliamentary committee on whether and how to regulate synthetic media.

In On Liberty Mill defends a near-absolute presumption in favour of free expression, grounded in the harm principle and in the view that open contest gives truth a systematic advantage over falsehood, since falsehood tends to collapse under scrutiny. A recent line of argument maintains that deepfakes erode the epistemic backstop: the default reliability of audio-visual recordings as evidence of what was actually said or done. Once fabricated footage can counterfeit that baseline, the very evidence by which public scrutiny corrects misrepresentation is itself suspect, and Mill's picture of inquiry may no longer hold.

Reconstruct, from his own principles, the position Mill would most plausibly take on deepfake regulation. Would he treat synthetic media as protected speech? Would the harm principle permit prohibiting non-consensual deepfake pornography, fabricated statements by public officials, or deepfakes of private individuals where no physical harm ensues? Where would he draw the line between permissible counterspeech and impermissible prior restraint? Be explicit about where his commitments pull in different directions, and separate what follows from his stated views from what you are inferring on his behalf.
