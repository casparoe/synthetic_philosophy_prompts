I'm writing a paper that touches on the relationship between perception and theory, and I recently read a chapter in a popular cognitive science book that made a strong impression on me. The chapter is about the cognitive penetration of perception, and its message is that perception is far more theory-laden than older accounts assumed. As the author presents it, the central question is whether what we believe, want, or expect can alter perceptual experience itself, rather than merely altering how we interpret or report what we perceive. The author says the evidence that it can is now overwhelming, offering examples such as a radiologist whose training makes abnormalities visually pop out where a novice sees only noise, a hiker expecting snakes who sees a coiled rope as a snake, and experimental subjects who are told a face is angry and then literally see the face as angrier. The opposing position, that perceptual processing is cognitively impenetrable, is described mainly as a residual commitment to modularity and to the myth of a passive, innocent eye. The author claims this impenetrability view has been empirically refuted by such cases, and that its defenders preserve the view only by insisting that the expectation merely directs attention or affects later judgment, a move the author calls an unfalsifiable dodge. The chapter then draws what it presents as the important philosophical consequence: because cognition infiltrates perception itself, perceptual experience cannot serve as an independent check on theoretical or ideological commitments, so appeals to observation in science, politics, and everyday life are ultimately circular.

The broad picture feels right to me in outline, since I am inclined to think experience is more theory-laden than the innocent-eye picture suggests, but I also know popular books sometimes make live controversies sound settled. I would like to know how much of this I can safely repeat in my paper. Specifically, is this a fair summary of the state of the debate? Which parts are accurate, which parts conflate two things the relevant literature keeps separate, where does the summary overstate what the evidence actually shows, and what would a careful defender of the impenetrability of perception say in response? Most importantly, does the final epistemological conclusion about observational circularity actually follow from the accurate parts of the summary?
