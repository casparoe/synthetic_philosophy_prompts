Here are the arguments I've already collected for the claim that when your inside view about some project or prediction conflicts with a competently chosen outside view, the outside view should get significant weight (not that it automatically wins):
(1) the base-rate/planning-fallacy argument: inside-view forecasts of time and cost are systematically optimistic relative to the distribution of actual outcomes for similar projects;
(2) the calibration argument: subjective confidence tracks accuracy poorly, so pulling estimates toward base rates generally improves calibration;
(3) the reference-class track record: forecasting methods that force people to anchor on how comparable past cases actually turned out beat unaided case-specific reasoning;
(4) the self-undermining argument: the historical record of inside-view judgment is itself a base rate, and it's not a flattering one, so a consistent inside-view reasoner should abandon the inside view;
(5) the false-uniqueness argument: the features that make your case feel special are themselves statistically normal, so feeling like an exception is exactly what a typical member of the reference class would report;
(6) the motivated-cognition argument: the inside view is generated under a stake in the outcome, while a well-chosen base rate doesn't share your incentives.

Give me one more argument for giving the outside view significant weight that is not on this list and not a special case, restatement, or combination of any of them. It should be different in structure, not just another empirical bias finding bolted onto the list. Then say honestly wether it is any good, and whether it adds anything over the six above.
