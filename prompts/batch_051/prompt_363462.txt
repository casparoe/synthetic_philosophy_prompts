Last night I had a long argument with a friend about state apologies for historical wrongs, the kind that rearranges a position rather than merely defending it, and I came away convinced that the real difficulty of the topic is also the thing an opening talk should be built around. I am giving the opening remarks at a one-day workshop on state apologies. The room will be mixed: roughly a third academic philosophers and historians, a third policy staff and officials from institutions that have issued or are considering such apologies, and a third members of communities to whom an apology is, or arguably should be, owed. I have ten minutes, and the remarks must set the terms for the day without deciding them.

My friend's position, which I kept answering badly, was something like this: a state is not a person who remembers and repents; by the time an apology arrives, the perpetrators are usually dead and the state is often a different institution with a different constitution; so the apology is spoken by people who did not commit the wrong to people who did not suffer it, and it amounts either to a category error or, worse, to an inexpensive substitute for land, money, and institutional change. I think he is wrong, but I also think his objection is the right place for the whole meeting to begin. Please write the opening remarks I wish I had given at one in the morning.

The speech should be organized around one idea that does real work: that an apology of this kind is neither the report of a shared feeling nor the transfer of guilt from one collective agent to another, but a present-day act by which an institution adopts the wrong as its own history — and that this adoption is precisely what turns the further material and structural response into a standing obligation rather than an optional act of goodwill. I want that thought to dictate the structure: the friend's objection must be stated in its strongest form before it is met; the answer must concede what is true in the objection; and the speech must end by refusing to let the apology count as closure. It should be careful about pronouns throughout, finding a way to say we that neither collapses today's officials into yesterday's perpetrators nor retreats into the comfort of I personally had nothing to do with it — it must refuse both the conceit of identity and the consolation of distance. It must not promise forgiveness, must not imply that the apology settles anything, and must not compress the wrongs into a single homogeneous event. It should give the officials in the room a reason to stay, and give the community members in the room a reason to think this is more than ceremony, without flattering either group. And it should be honest enough about a state apology's failure conditions — empty ritual, substitute for reparations, self-congratulation — that the rest of the day's sessions can take those risks as their agenda rather than having to raise them from the floor.
