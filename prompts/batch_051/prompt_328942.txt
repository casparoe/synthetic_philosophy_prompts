Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about the sunk cost fallacy, and I keep going back and forth on it. I'll lay out her argument below. She's not dumb, and all the steps sounded right when she said them, but I've got a nagging feeling she's pulling something on me — or maybe I just don't like where the argument lands. Her case: you pay $40 for a ticket to a new experimental play. Ten minutes in, you're miserable and you're sure the next two hours are going to be just as miserable. I said the standard thing: the $40 is sunk, you should leave, and staying just because you paid is the sunk cost fallacy. She said the standard line is too quick, and gave this argument:

(1) A rational choice is one that maximizes expected utility over the consequences that the choice can affect.

(2) If you stay, then at the end of the night the world contains this fact: the $40 bought you an evening at the theater.

(3) If you leave, then at the end of the night the world contains this different fact: the $40 bought you ten minutes of misery and a grudge.

(4) The difference between those two worlds is a future consequence of the stay/leave choice.

(5) You care a lot about not having thrown away $40.

(6) So staying has a future benefit that leaving lacks.

(7) Therefore, staying because you paid doesn't have to be a fallacy — the past payment gives you, through this mechanism, a perfectly good future-directed reason to stay.

I'm especialy stuck on step (2): is "the $40 bought you an evening at the theater" the kind of thing that can be a future consequence in the decision-theoretic sense, or is she sneaking the past in by redescribing it as a future state? Please think carefully, give me a couple of candidate diagnoses before settling on the strongest one, and then tell me whether her conclusion should be accepted, rejected, or narrowed. If the argument is valid, say exactly what she's right about and what, if anything, it shows about the standard sunk-cost advice. If it fails, name the step (or the transition between steps) where it goes wrong, and rewrite that step in a way that would make the argument valid, if that's possible.
