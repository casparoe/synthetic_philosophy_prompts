A friend and I have spent the better part of a year collecting short descriptions of political violence and recording whether each should be classed as terrorism. She is not a lawyer or a scholar; she just has considered, fairly consistent judgments. I have her verdicts on six cases, and I want to know what she would say about a seventh—and, more than that, which rule she is implicitly running.

Her verdicts:

1. A network from an occupied territory detonates a car bomb in a crowded market to pressure the occupying army to leave. She calls this terrorism.
2. A state's air force bombs its enemy's ammunition depot during a declared war. She does not call this terrorism.
3. A rebel unit kills the opposing side's military commander at a checkpoint and does not announce the killing, attach a threat, or make any demand. She does not call this terrorism.
4. A militia abducts and kills several villagers from a rival community, with the stated aim of making the rest of that community leave the area. She calls this terrorism.
5. A state's intelligence service bombs the office of an exiled dissident radio station abroad, saying it intends to silence the station. She calls this terrorism.
6. In the middle of a riot, demonstrators set fire to an empty police patrol post; nobody is hurt, and no group claims the act or attaches a demand. She does not call this terrorism.

The new case I want to predict: a separatist group captures uniformed soldiers at a checkpoint and releases a video threatening to execute one of them each week unless the government withdraws its troops from the region. The direct targets are combatants, and the stated purpose is to force the government and the wider public to change course.

I can see at least two rules that fit all six of her old verdicts but split on this new case; I suspect one has to do with who is targeted and the other has to do with what the act is for. I want you to spell out the main candidate rules that fit her record, identify which of the six cases is the closest analog to the new one, and give a best-guess prediction of her verdict along with the reasoning for preferring it to the others. If her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so plainly: for each choice, state which principle she would be adopting by making it, and describe the further case that would most cleanly tell the rules apart. Treat her verdicts as expressions of a consistent underlying standard rather than dictionary definitions.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in your prediction and which single point you are least sure of.
