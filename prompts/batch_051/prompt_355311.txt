I keep returning to an argument I had with friends who play military shooters. Their defense of nearly any in-game violence is that it is just a game, and on reflection I find a strong version of that view compelling: a game is a system of rules and fictions, its characters and events are not real, and the moral status of an action inside a game derives only from its effects on actual people — desensitization, addiction, what the player does after the match, and the real production practices behind the game. On this view, shooting a civilian non-player character in a war game is no more wrong in itself than taking a knight in chess; the model is not a person. The theory has real virtues. It is simple, it gives a single test for whether a game is criticizable, and it avoids the special pleading and protectiveness that usually attach to arguments about offensive content. Yet I cannot get myself to endorse it all the way to the end. I am disturbed by the thought of a photorealistic, mass-market level in which the player, for entertainment, waterboards or executes a prisoner while he begs, set in a recognizable historical conflict and scored for technique. My intuition is that this is not on a moral par with chess, and that any theory saying otherwise has lost sight of the fact that the point of the representation is the representation: the fun would be the fun of doing that to a depiction of a person. I also suspect this matters for whether games can depict war seriously at all, since if fictional content is morally inert, seriousness is merely an aesthetic property, whereas if it is not inert, then using an atrocity as a playground may itself glorify or trivialize it.

Present the strongest version of each side and give me a negotiated verdict. Specifically: is my unease a moral perception tracking something the content-is-inert account leaves out, or is it an artifact of appearances that I should debunk and drop? If the theory should be amended or abandoned, what exactly is the amendment, and how does it avoid collapsing into the conclusion that no violent or transgressive fiction is permissible? If the two sides are compatible, give me the reading that reconciles them — for example, by separating what may be wrong in virtue of the fiction itself, what may be wrong in virtue of how the game frames and rewards the fiction, and what is merely ugly or in poor taste. I want to know what would justify each move, not just which side you pick.
