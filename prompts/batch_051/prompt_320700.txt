I find the decision-theoretic case for individual precommitment compelling: an agent who can foresee a future preference shift may now adopt a rule that better tracks his considered long-range aims, even at the cost of restricting that future self, and the constitutional version of the argument seems to scale the same logic up from persons to polities. At the same time, I have a firm intuition that the analogy runs out exactly where it does the most work: in the individual case the later self who resists the constraint is the same subject whose considered aims justified it, whereas a future majority bound by an earlier generation never shared in forming those aims, and the past act ends up deciding in advance whose considered judgment counts. Is that asymmetry morally weighty, or is it an artifact of an over-individualistic picture of collective agency that a good theory of the polity should dissolve? Present both sides at their strongest, then say which should yield — the unified theory or the intuition — and specify what would justify each resolution: debunking the intuition, limiting the theory (for instance to procedural rather than substantive entrenchment, or to rules the later majority could revisit), or showing the two are compatible.
