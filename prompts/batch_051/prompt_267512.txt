I have been reading Sidgwick’s *The Methods of Ethics* alongside accounts of his leadership of the Society for Psychical Research, and I keep coming back to a question about whether the two projects are philosophically continuous. In the later parts of *The Methods of Ethics*, Sidgwick argues that the method of ethical egoism and the method of utilitarianism both seem to be supported by rational intuition, yet they issue contradictory directives; he concludes that the conflict can be resolved only if we accept some further postulate about the moral order of the universe, for example a deity who coordinates duty and happiness or a future life in which vice and virtue receive their fitting returns. He then spent years as the first president of the Society for Psychical Research, investigating mediums, apparitions, and claimed evidence for survival after death. Some readers treat this as a sober and even admirable attempt to gather empirical evidence for a postulate his ethics needed; others treat it as an embarrassing loss of philosophical nerve, or as a case where his desires corrupted his standards of evidence.

I want an essay that takes a definite side on this controversy: was Sidgwick’s psychical research a reasonable extension of his ethical theory, or a failure of epistemic judgment that his theory cannot vindicate? Weigh the main arguments on both sides. In doing so, explain exactly what his ethical system required of him, what empirical result he hoped to obtain, what sort of evidence would have been needed, and how he in fact conducted the investigations insofar as that bears on the philosophical issue. Do not end on a fence-sitting note: reach and justify a verdict, even if it is a qualified one.
