I am a novelist working on a book set in a country emerging from state violence, and I have been asked to contribute an afterword on fiction and national memory. While preparing it, I returned to Friedrich Nietzsche's The Use and Abuse of History, the second of his Untimely Meditations. There is a passage in which he distinguishes three ways of relating to the past—the monumental, the antiquarian, and the critical—and argues that the past should be made to serve life, not allowed to accumulate until it crushes the living. A people that cannot forget, he suggests, cannot remain healthy. I find that argument pressing, because it cuts against much of what I otherwise believe about coming to terms with a violent past.

Here is the set of positions I currently hold:

1. Public acknowledgment of past crimes is normally necessary for a polity's moral health; the German working-through of the Nazi past is my paradigm.

2. A nation can nevertheless be too burdened by its past, and some forgetting or mythmaking can be life-serving in Nietzsche's sense.

3. The American South's Lost Cause is best understood as a coherent memory culture rather than merely a set of historical falsehoods; for that reason I find it more disturbing, not less.

4. Japan's comparatively restrained public reckoning with wartime atrocities was probably necessary for postwar democratic stability, and I would not call it a straightforward failure.

5. Turkey's official denialism about the Armenian genocide is indefensible, even if it may have served some political continuity in its own way.

6. Historical fiction may legitimately alter facts and do reparative work that trials, archives, and truth commissions cannot, because it addresses imaginative and emotional needs rather than evidentiary ones.

I want to know whether these commitments hang together. Audit the set: which pairs are in genuine tension, which are in motivational conflict, and which only appear to conflict until the terms are clarified? Order the tensions by severity, and say what would have to give in each case and what the revision would cost. I am not sure where the problem is, and I would rather not say which of these I am most tempted to surrender, because I want the diagnosis before I start negotiating with myself.
