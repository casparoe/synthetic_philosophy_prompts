A doctoral student in my seminar has reconstructed an argument he credits to a critic of meritocracy, and it has left the seminar unable to say exactly where it goes wrong. I want a diagnosis that engages the argument as written, not a survey of positions. Here is the argument.

(1) A person deserves an advantage, reward, or punishment only if she is ultimately responsible for the features in virtue of which she is held to deserve it.
(2) Every feature of a person is either an initial endowment — native capacities and the circumstances of birth and early formation — or an acquired trait produced by some process of action, training, or shaping.
(3) No one is ultimately responsible for her initial endowments, since they precede anything she could choose or do.
(4) A person is ultimately responsible for an acquired trait only if she was ultimately responsible for the actions through which she acquired it.
(5) But those actions flowed from earlier dispositions and capacities, and she is ultimately responsible for those only if she was responsible for their still earlier sources; the demand therefore launches a regress that never terminates in something for which she is responsible.
(6) Hence no one is ultimately responsible for any trait or capacity whatsoever.
(7) Therefore no one deserves anything in virtue of any trait, effort, or performance — and this holds for grades, praise, blame, prizes, offices, and punishments as fully as for competitive advantages.
(8) Consequently, statements like "she deserved the prize" and "he deserved the sentence" are never true, and our practices of grading, hiring, praising, and holding one another to account rest on a systematically false picture of human agency.

The same regress would reach every action, including the act of advancing or rejecting this very argument, so the conclusion appears to prove too much. The student is unwilling to accept (8) and hopes to keep something like the responsibility condition in (1); those two commitments seem to force him to reject something in (4) through (6), but he cannot name the step, and he does not know what the rejection costs. Tell me where the argument goes wrong, if it does, and what the repair costs. Answer in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion; together the five points should isolate the defeated step or premise, say why it looked acceptable, and state what giving it up costs and what survives.
