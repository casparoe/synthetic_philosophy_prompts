Having come to epistemology late, after a career in the natural sciences, I have been working through Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge, and I would like a thought experiment built to order, with the machinery made explicit. I am interested in whether sensitivity-based failures of epistemic closure are confined to the familiar skeptical-alternative shape, or whether they can arise in the ordinary logical operation of conjunction elimination.

The standard presentations of the tracking theory's clash with closure all run the same way. A subject knows an ordinary proposition but, on Nozick's account, fails to know an entailed consequence of it, because that consequence denies some deceptive alternative: a disguised animal, a fake barn, an envatted brain. I want a case in which closure fails in a more pedestrian direction: the subject knows a conjunction of P and Q, and yet does not know one of its conjuncts, Q, even though the subject is a competent reasoner who fully appreciates that the conjunction entails Q.

Construct a mundane scenario involving no brains in vats, no demons, no lotteries, no fake-barn towns, and no costumed or disguised objects; ordinary perceptual or practical life will do. The failure must be generated by the tracking theory's sensitivity condition, and not by the subject's irrationality, inattention, or ignorance of logic. State clearly what method or methods the subject uses, and do not simply stipulate the verdicts; they must fall out of the tracking conditions together with the details of the scenario.

After presenting the case, do the check in full. For the conjunction and again for the conjunct, state the proposition, the method, and the verdict on the four tracking conditions: truth, belief, sensitivity, and adherence. For sensitivity, make clear why, if the proposition were false, the subject would or would not still believe it by the method in question; for adherence, make clear why the subject tracks the proposition in nearby worlds where it remains true. Explain the relevant nearby worlds precisely enough that a reader can see why the conjunction is sensitive while the conjunct is not. Then identify the known entailment, the subject's knowledge of it, and how the two verdicts produce a closure violation. Finish by saying which feature of the scenario is doing the work, what changes if that feature is removed, how a defender of epistemic closure would attack the example, and how a tracking theorist should reply. The point of the exercise is to test whether Nozick's rejection of closure is forced on him only by skeptical alternatives or is built into the sensitivity condition itself.
