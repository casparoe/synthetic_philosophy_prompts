I spent forty years in a psychophysics lab before I retired, measuring heat-pain thresholds with thermal stimulators. One thing that gets in your bones after all that testing is how smooth the transition into pain is. You turn the stimulator up half a degree at a time; the volunteer can't reliably tell adjacent settings apart, but after enough half-degree steps she has gone from comfortable to unmistakably in pain. I've recently started reading epistemology, and I keep coming back to that old lab fact in the form of an argument that there are no luminous mental states. I want to know whether the argument actually works, or where the sleight-of-hand is. Here's my reconstruction.

Call a mental state C luminous just in case: whenever C obtains, the subject is in a position to know that C obtains. Let C be "being in pain." Take a series of cases s0 through s100, each a half degree hotter than the last, with s0 clearly pain and s100 clearly not pain, and with each adjacent pair subjectively indistiguishable to the subject.

(1) If being in pain is luminous, then for any case si, if the subject is in pain at si, she is in a position to know she is in pain at si.

(2) For any adjacent pair si, si+1: if the subject is in a position to know she is in pain at si, then she is in pain at si+1 as well. My thinking: a judgment counts as knowledge only if it has a little margin for error; it can't be true at si but false one step later in a case she can't distinguish from si, otherwise she would be right and genuinely know something while being one half-degree away from being wrong in the very same frame of mind.

(3) She is in pain at s0, so by (1) she is in a position to know she is in pain at s0. Then by (2) she is in pain at s1; by (1) again she is in a position to know at s1; by (2), pain at s2; and so on. So she is in pain at s100.

(4) But s100 is stipulated to be clearly not pain. Contradiction.

(5) Therefore being in pain is not luminous. And the same reasoning seems to go through for any condition that can come on gradually—feeling cold, looking tired, maybe even believing something confidently. So no non-trivial mental state is luminous.

I keep coming back to step 2; it sounds right in the spirit of what I knew in the lab about thresholds, but I can't make it precise without feeling like I'm cheating somewhere. So: is this argument valid? If it is, which premise should I reject, and what would an honest, corrected version of that premise say? If it isn't, exactly where does the logic slip? And what becomes of the claim that there are no luminous mental states?
