So, my brother spent most of last month in a minimally conscious state after a car accident, and I got a different answer from every neurologist about whether he could feel anything—whether the pain meds were for him or basically for us. That made me realize how much moral weight is sitting on a question I'd always treated as a weird phil mind puzzle: which things are capable of feeling at all, and how we'd ever know. Before I read further, I want the dessiderata. Like a spec sheet for an adequate theory of sentience (or moral patienthood—I'm not sure those come apart, and part of what I want to hear your view on is whether they do). Not citations, just the requirements themselves, with the case or consideration behind each. It needs to get the difference between responsiveness and sentience right: a spinal reflex and a thermostat both respond, and that can't be the bar. It needs to say something about dreamless sleep, general anesthesia, and the minimally conscious state without collapsing consciousness into reportability—my brother couldn't report anything, but whether he felt things was exactly the question. It should face the nonhuman cases honestly: where on the tree of life the line goes, or at least why a clean line is hard, and it shouldn't set the bar at language or abstract thought if it wants to be a theory of feeling rather than of sapience; that's part of why the question about sophisticated AI systems, and maybe organoids, matters. Cover the strange human cases too—blindsight, split brains, people who locate a pain but say it doesn't actually bother them—without just defining them away. And it has to be a theory of attribution, not just a definition: it should tell us how to act under uncertainty, since both over-attributing (say, to every simulation that mimics distress) and under-attributing (to my brother) have real moral costs. Then flag which of the requirements you think are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preferences, and especially whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable. My suspicion is you cant demand a theory that's independent of verbal report and also demand one that's easy to test, but convince me either way. Briefly score the main existing approaches—behaviorism-ish, functionalism, integrated information theory, whatever else—against the list; keep that part short, the criteria are what I'm after.
