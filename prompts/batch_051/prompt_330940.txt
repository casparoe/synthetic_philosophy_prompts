I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, mostly longform features. Lately I've noticed a pattern in my own drafts (and in a lot of the field): I reach for historical episodes to make the science feel like a story, and it always comes out the same shape — the past is full of lone geniuses who saw the truth early and got ignored, dogmatic gatekeepers held everything back, and the present is the obvious destination of all that struggle. I know the critique, "Whig history," writing the past as a progress report with us as the punchline, but knowing it hasn't made it easy to stop doing it on a deadline.

So I want a procedure, not a lecture. Build me a checklist I can run on a draft feature in about twenty minutes before I file, designed to catch this specific failure mode and force a fix. I file maybe two or three features a month, not all of them historical but enough that I'd use it constantly. Stakes: my editor trusts my framing, we don't have a historian on call, and once its in print the "they laughed at Galileo" energy is there forever. The procedure has to be concrete: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the checklist tells me to rewrite versus just add a caveat versus kill a framing altogether, and which steps are judgment calls I can't offload onto a checklist. Worked examples matter more than theory to me, so run it on three hypothetical drafts:

1. A short feature on Ignaz Semmelweis and the rejection of handwashing in mid-19th-century Vienna, written, let's be honest, as a lone visionary versus dogmatic establishment story.
2. A retrospective on Alfred Wegener and continental drift with the headline-level claim that he was "ahead of his time" and vindicated by plate tectonics.
3. A feature on the history of eugenics in genetics, framed as the field bravely correcting its own past and emerging as a moral success story. This is the one I'd really like to keep, so don't go easy on it.

Also tell me what the procedure can't do — where it will flag something as Whiggish that isn't, where it might let bad history through, and what a checklist can never replace about actually knowing the history. I'd rather have a tool that sometimes nags me than a theory I nod along with and forget.
