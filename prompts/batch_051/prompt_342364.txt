Here is a reconstruction of a line of thought attributed to Maimonides about the sacrificial cult. Assess the argument as follows: does it go through, and if not, where does it first fail, and what exactly is wrong at that step?

(1) On Maimonides' view, the commandments concerning animal sacrifice were not given because sacrifice is the intrinsically fitting way to worship God. They were a concession to the historical condition of the Israelites, who could not then conceive of worship without sacrifice; the law redirected an ingrained sacrificial habit away from idols and toward God.

(2) If a religious practice is commanded only as a concession to a defect, then the practice has religious value only for someone who still has that defect, and no religious value for someone who has outgrown it.

(3) The defect addressed by the sacrificial laws was the habit of taking physical offerings, and the destruction of animals, to be what honours God.

(4) Someone who fully grasps that God is incorporeal, lacks nothing, and is worshipped through the intellect has outgrown the defect described in (3).

(5) Therefore, for intellectually mature worshippers, the sacrificial laws have no religious value.

(6) Yet Maimonides also seems to hold that the sacrificial laws remain a permanent part of the Torah for the community, not merely a temporary remedy that the mature may set aside.

(7) Therefore, Maimonides' position implies that the Torah permanently contains a body of law without religious value for its most mature adherents.

Identify the first step, if any, at which the argument goes wrong, and state whether the error is a false premise, an invalid inference, or an equivocation tied to a specific term. If a step fails only on one reading of a key term, distinguish the readings. In your answer, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among interpreters of Maimonides, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
