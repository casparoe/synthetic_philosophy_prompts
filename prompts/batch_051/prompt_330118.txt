I'm a PhD student in phil of bio and I've been trying to get the handicap principle straight by checking my gut reactions against a pile of cases. I keep making judgments that don't obviously line up, and I'd like to know whether there's a single principle behind them or whether I should just drop some of the judgments.

Here are the verdicts.

1. Peacock's tail. Honest, and the mechanism is the cost: a tail like that is a standing burden, so only a male in genuinely good condition can grow it and still survive. Cost is doing the work.

2. The gazelle that spots a cheetah and does those stiff-legged bouncing leaps instead of running. I'm sure the display is honest — predators do seem to change who they chase based on it — but I don't think the cost of stotting is what makes it honest. It's honest because the bounce is a physical feat you can't do unless you're fast and full of energy; if every gazelle could pull it off equally, the signal would mean nothing. So that's a different mechanism from the peacock.

3. A poison frog's warning colors. Honest enough (the brightness tracks the toxicity), but the color isn't costly to produce in the relevant sense; it's linked to the poison. So cost clearly isn't what keeps this one honest.

4. Nestling birds begging loudly. I buy that the loudness is honest because it's risky — it attracts predators. A full nestling won't screech for one more worm if screeching might get it eaten, but a starving one will. Textbook cost story. Except the cost is only paid while you're actually begging, not a standing burden like the tail, and I'm not sure whether that distinction matters.

5. The guy who buys a Rolex to signal he's wealthy. I think it's a handicap when the price is a real sacrifice for him, and just a convention when it isn't; counterfeits ruin the signal precisely because they break the link between cost and trait. So cost counts, but only relative to the signaler's means.

6. Diamond engagement rings. I have much less symapthy for the idea that they're honest signals of commitment. A rich guy can pay the "two months salary" thing without feeling it and a poor guy can't, so the ring seems to track willingness and ability to spend, not fidelity. The signal that would actually track commitment seems like something closer to closing off exit options, not buying a thing. I don't know why I'm fine calling the Rolex a handicap but not the ring.

7. Costly rites of initiation — dietary restrictions, scarification, long boring classes before you join. I think these work as honest signals of commitment to the group, and the cost is what screens out shirkers. But here the quality being signaled (commitment) seems to be created by paying the cost, whereas the peacock's tail doesn't create his quality. That feels like a different beast, and it bugs me that "the handicap principle" is supposed to cover both.

8. A Fisherian runaway ornament: females get an arbitrary preference for a longer tail and it spreads even though the tail says nothing about male quality. I'm inclined to say such an ornament can be expensive without being informative at all. But then why doesn't that threaten what I said about the peacock? I want to say the peacock's tail is honest because its cost is condition-dependent in a way the runaway tail's isn't, but I can't make that precise.

I keep trying to make do with a hunch along these lines: a signal is kept honest by cost only when the cost is smaller for the signaler who actually has the quality being signaled. But that doesn't obviously get me stotting (where I said cost isn't the mechanism), and I can't tell whether it covers the Rolex and the ring.

So: is there one principle — or a small set of principles — about signal honesty that gives me all or most of these verdicts, and if not, which of my verdicts have to go? Please state the principle(s) precisely, say which case motivates each clause, tell me what the account predicts about a case I haven't listed (say, a male bird that defends a food-rich territory to attract females), and be honest about which of my eight judgments the account can't accommodate.
