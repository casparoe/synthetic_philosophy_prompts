I run a second-year undergraduate seminar in metaethics with about 15 students, meeting for 90 minutes. I'd like an activity for the first session of our unit on the is/ought gap and the naturalistic fallacy — one that gets students to feel the gap before I name it. The session should be a runnable structured game or role-play with this arc: students are given only descriptive materials (cards, reports, data, or role-specific briefs — your choice) and a contested practical question, and are asked to derive a recommendation. The materials and rules should be arranged so that, to reach any recommendation, each group must at some point rely on a premise that was not among the descriptive materials, and the moment they notice this should be engineered into the activity rather than saved for the debrief. The debrief should then let students sort the premises everyone actually used into descriptive and normative categories, locate the bridge premises, and see that each bridge could have been different while every descriptive fact stayed the same.

Please give me:

- the full setup: setting, group size, timing, materials, and the exact task instructions students hear;
- the rules or roles, and an explanation of how they force the discovery rather than letting a group skip straight to announcing the conclusion;
- a debrief flow that maps what happened in the game onto three things: (a) the inferential point that a set of ordinary factual premises does not by itself entail an ought-conclusion unless at least one normative bridge premise is added; (b) Moore's distinct naturalistic-fallacy point — the attempt to define good in purely descriptive terms — and the open question argument that exposes why such definitions fail; and (c) the way thick or mixed concepts such as cruel or efficient can make this boundary look softer than it is, because they arrive already carrying normative weight;
- what will predictably go wrong and how the facilitator should handle it. In particular, write the instructions so that a quick student cannot win by a Prior-style trick — for example, moving from the factual premise that the river is rising to the disjunction either the river is rising or we ought to evacuate and treating that disjunction as a recommendation. Say explicitly what is to count as a recommendation in the activity so this move is blocked without the facilitator having to stop the game and explain;
- one feature of the is/ought gap that the activity cannot reproduce mechanically and that the facilitator must supply in the debrief, with a sentence or two on how to supply it without lapsing into a lecture.

I do not want a reading list, a bibliography, or lecture notes; I want a runnable session plan. The activity should produce genuine disagreement about which bridge premise is correct, not just a demonstration that everyone needed one, and the debrief should treat that disagreement as the payoff rather than as a problem to be smoothed over.
