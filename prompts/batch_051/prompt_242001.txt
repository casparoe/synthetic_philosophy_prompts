I serve on the Committee on Named Honors of the Pelagic Society, a learned society in the earth sciences. The committee must recommend to the Board of Trustees what to do about the Liddell Medal, awarded each year for the best early-career paper in paleoceanography. The medal is named for Dr. Arthur Liddell, who has been credited for half a century with discovering what every textbook now calls the Liddell asymmetry: a systematic offset between oxygen-isotope records from the eastern and western tropical Pacific. Work in the Society's archive has now shown that a Polish marine geologist, Dr. Stanisław Baranowski, published a three-page note in 1931 describing the same pattern and mechanism three years before Liddell's definitive study; the idea also appears, in scattered form, in a nineteenth-century German handbook. Liddell's paper was broader and more conclusive, and our archivist believes he had read Baranowski's note but never cited it.

The Society's bylaws require that "a named honor shall be named solely for the person whose work first established, by publication, the discovery or advance for which the honor is given." Read strictly, that points toward renaming the medal for Baranowski. But our historian warns that this is Stigler's law at work — the observation, usually credited to the statistician Stephen Stigler, that "No scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer" (Stigler credited the observation itself to the sociologist Robert K. Merton, making the law an instance of itself) — and that the hunt for the true first discoverer may simply begin the same cycle again under a new name. The committee has therefore narrowed itself to three live options: leave the name and correct the citation that accompanies the medal; rename the medal for the earliest documented discoverer; or retire the eponym and adopt a descriptive name.

Draft the short memo I will submit to the Board. It should recommend one of these options, or a defensible variant, defend the recommendation, and give a fair treatment of the strongest consideration in favor of the option we reject. The Board will act only on a definite instruction, so the memo must close with a concrete bottom line rather than further study.
