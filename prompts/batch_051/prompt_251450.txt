I've been going down a game-theory rabbit hole latley — no formal training, just blog posts, a few YouTube lectures, and one very patient friend — and the centipede game has been eatting at me for weeks. You know the setup: two players take turns either grabbing a growing pot for themselves or passing it to the other person, and if both keep passing, the pot keeps growing so that at the very end both walk away with more, but at any given turn the player who grabs now gets more than if they let the other person grab next. Backward induction says player one should grab on move one, since the last player would grab at the last move, so the second-to-last should grab, and it unravels all the way back. But in actual experiments people pass for several rounds and the game only falls apart near the end. I've been collecting the standard explanations for this gap between what backward induction tells people to do and what they actually do. So far I have:

(1) bounded rationality — people can't run eight steps of backward induction in their heads, so they don't;
(2) social preferences — some subjects care about fairness or the other guy's payoff, so the dollar amounts written down aren't their real utilities;
(3) missing common knowledge of rationality — backward induction only works if everyone is rational, everyone knows everyone is rational, everyone knows everyone knows, and so on, and real players don't have that;
(4) reputation under incomplete information — if you assign even a small chance to the other player being the cooperative type, passing early can be rational because it keeps play alive and buys you a cooperative reputation;
(5) trivial stakes — in many experiments the amounts are so small that the cost of deviating from the 'rational' path is basically zero, so passing is cheap.

I'm looking for one more explanation that is genuinely different from those five — not a rewording, not a special case (like trying to pass off inequity aversion as something other than social preferences), and not some combination of the items above. Ideally it makes the backward-induction story wrong, or not even applicable, in a way none of the five does. Put on your game-theorist/experimental-economics hat and think carefully: first brainstorm two or three candidate explanations, throw away the ones that collapse into my list, then give me the strongest one and be honest about whether it's a real explanation or just an ad hoc patch.
