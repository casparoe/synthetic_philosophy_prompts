I am a final-year school pupil, and after a lesson on medical law I cannot get past what looks like a double standard in the authority we grant to a person's earlier self.

Consider an adult who writes an advance directive refusing a particular treatment, such as antibiotics for pneumonia, if she later develops advanced dementia. Years later, when she has dementia and no longer understands the document, she seems content and would probably accept the treatment if it were offered. In many legal systems her earlier refusal is still honoured: the woman of thirty years ago is allowed to bind the woman in the bed today.

Yet ordinary life grants our earlier selves nothing like that power. If on Sunday I resolve never to eat sugar again, and on Tuesday I change my mind, nobody treats Sunday's resolution as binding—not my family, not the law, and usually not even me. The Tuesday self is simply allowed to revise the Sunday self's values. So we enforce a previous self's decision in one kind of case while treating it as irrelevant in another.

What, if anything, justifies this asymmetry? I can see candidate distinctions: the later self with dementia lacks mental capacity whereas the Tuesday dieter has it; an advance directive is written and witnessed whereas a private resolution is not; one case concerns life and death and the other concerns sugar. I would like you to make the strongest case for a justifying principle, test it against harder nearby cases (for example, a witnessed, signed document in which a competent adult tries to forbid her future self from eating sugar, or from leaving a religion), and give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or abandoned.

Please write the answer in British English.
