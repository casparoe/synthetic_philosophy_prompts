Consider the following asymmetry in John Stuart Mill's political thought. In On Liberty, Mill defends what is now called the harm principle: the only legitimate ground for coercing a member of a civilized community against his will is to prevent harm to others, and coercing someone for his own good—paternalism—is never a sufficient justification. Yet Mill explicitly limits this principle to peoples he regards as having reached maturity. For societies he classified as backward or barbarous, he holds that despotic government is legitimate provided that it aims at, and actually produces, their improvement. India, in his judgment, lies on the backward side of that line. Mill spent most of his working life in the service of the East India Company, and when the Company's rule was about to be abolished he defended it against transfer to the British Crown, arguing that representative institutions were unsuited to India and that an expert administrative body, insulated from the ignorance and interests of British voters, would govern Indians better than a government answerable to Parliament and public opinion.

So the same thinker holds both of the following: for a mature English adult, the state may not interfere in order to make him better; for Indian subjects, decades of coercive foreign administration aimed at making them better are not merely permissible but preferable to the representative institutions Mill prizes for the English. What is doing the work in this asymmetry? Is it a difference in the subjects—some capacity for improvement through free discussion—a difference in the rulers—expert competence versus democratic ignorance—or something else? Is the distinction a principled one, or is it a rationalization that Mill's own harm principle cannot carry?

I want a committed answer. Either defend the asymmetry on the strongest reconstruction of Mill's actual reasons, including an account of how the line between mature and backward peoples could be drawn without begging the question or licensing arbitrary paternalism, or argue that the asymmetry must be given up, and say precisely which of Mill's commitments—the harm principle, the developmental test, or his application of that test to India—fails. Do not lay out the options and leave the choice to me.
