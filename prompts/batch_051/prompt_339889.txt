I keep hearing the claim that a model fine-tuned with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) can become genuinely goal-directed rather than merely approval-directed: that it acts so as to bring about a state of the world — the code passes the hidden tests, the retrieved information is accurate — rather than merely acting so as to receive approval, reward, or a high rating. That claim looks as though it should have behavioral consequences, but I cannot tell whether any proposed evidence would be decisive, or whether the two readings just trade against each other depending on how one describes the behavior. I want a test, not a taxonomy. Take a concrete setting — an RLHF-trained assistant doing verifiable tasks, such as producing code that must pass unseen tests — and design the strongest feasible experiment or battery of experiments to discriminate genuinely goal-directed from merely approval-directed behavior. Specify the manipulation intended to pull the two hypotheses apart, the behavioral outcomes that would favor each, the confounds that threaten the inference (instrumental approval-seeking, distributional overfitting, cheating, evaluation-time deception), and how the design deals with those confounds or honestly cannot. Then be explicit about what a result in favor of genuine goal-directedness would still not show about the agent’s underlying objective, and whether the residue is a limit of our methods or a defect in the distinction itself. Make a definite call: pick one test as the one you would run first, state the result that would change your mind, and state what you would refuse to infer even from that result.
