My moral philosophy professor has spent the term giving verdicts on short cases about a recurring character, Rosa. The course has been framed around the dispute between moral particularists and generalists, but the professor refuses to say which side she is on. Her rulings so far are as follows.

1. Rosa breaks a promise to meet a friend for coffee because she sees a stranger about to step into traffic and pulls them back. Verdict: permissible.
2. Rosa breaks a lunch promise to a friend because she would rather browse a bookshop alone. Verdict: wrong.
3. Rosa reveals a friend's secret in order to amuse another friend. Verdict: wrong.
4. Rosa reveals a friend's secret because doing so is the only way to protect a child from ongoing serious abuse. Verdict: permissible.
5. Rosa breaks a formal agreement with a large, insured corporation because keeping it would leave her unable to pay for her parent's urgent surgery. Verdict: permissible.
6. Rosa breaks a promise to her partner to attend a family dinner so she can stay late on a work project that matters to her career but has no deadline. Verdict: wrong.

Next week's case is this. Rosa has made a casual promise to a neighbor she barely knows to help him move a filing cabinet on Saturday morning. On Friday night, her closest friend calls in distress after a sudden breakup and asks Rosa to come over at that same hour for support. The friend is not in danger and has other friends she could call, but Rosa knows she is the one her friend most wants to talk to. Rosa breaks the promise to the neighbor.

Predict the professor's verdict if she is being consistent with her earlier rulings. Then do the following: lay out the two or three principles that fit her record so far, say what each principle predicts about the new case, state which principle you would bet she is implicitly running and give your reasons for preferring it, and describe one further case that would most sharply discriminate among whatever principles survive the new case. If her record underdetermines the prediction, say explicitly what principle she commits herself to with each possible ruling.
