You are a political philosopher and mechanism designer who works on the logic of institutions and self-referential rules, and you are comfortable moving between the abstract structure of a governance scheme and the concrete incentives of the people inside it. I want you to analyze the scheme below as a mechanism to be reverse-engineered, rather than to evaluate whether it is a good idea in the abstract.

A brief conceptual frame, so the vocabulary is shared. A tangled hierarchy forms when control or interpretation flows downward through the levels of a system but also loops back upward, so that a lower level helps produce or regulate the higher level that regulates it. An inviolate level is the stratum that must remain outside that loop—fixed, protected from amendment, or left uninterpreted—if the system is to settle on determinate objects or outcomes instead of circling forever. Think of Escher's image of two hands each drawing the other into existence: the two hands form the loop, and the page on which they are drawn, which neither hand is drawing, plays the role of the inviolate level.

Now the mechanism. A research foundation will award its annual fellowships through a self-appointing cycle. The foundation itself appoints the first cohort, and the cycle then runs as follows. Each year, anyone who wishes to be considered submits a proposal. The current fellows—the winners from the preceding year—write the scoring rubric and the aggregation formula for the year and publish both, along with the number of fellowships to be awarded, before submissions open. Those published rules are implemented in the ledger's ranking software and cannot be changed until the next cohort is seated. Members of the current cohort may not apply during their own year in office, but they may apply in later years like anyone else. Every applicant is also a scorer, assigned a fixed set of anonymized proposals to score on the published rubric, never including their own. Each submitted score enters an immutable, tamper-evident ledger before any aggregation is performed; the ranking is then produced automatically by applying the published formula to the stored scores, and no one may alter the computation once submission begins. The current fellows must select the next cohort in accordance with that ranking and by no other method: the top-ranked applicants become the next year's fellows. Those new fellows then draft the rubric and formula for the following year. One clause is declared inviolate: once a score is in the ledger it cannot be altered, deleted, replaced, or retroactively reinterpreted as a different number, and no future rubric, formula, or amendment may weaken that clause, retroactively reweight a submitted score, or redefine what counts as the raw score.

Walk me through how this system would actually behave.

Before giving your polished answer, brainstorm at least three distinct moves available to a self-interested actor in each role—applicant, current fellow, and ledger custodian—and note which move you judge most consequential.

Then organize your analysis around these questions:

1. Where does the tangled hierarchy concretely form in this scheme, and where does the inviolate level sit day to day, as opposed to where the drafters say it sits?
2. What does the scheme incentivize for applicants in the first few cycles and then in the long run: strategic scoring of rivals, proposal-writing aimed at the current rubric and formula, collusion, cultivating the current fellows, or something else?
3. Since the current fellows cannot touch raw scores but can write the rubric and the aggregation formula prospectively, what is the cheapest path by which a cohort makes the outcome what it wants while leaving the ledger untouched? In what sense has the inviolate level then been bypassed rather than violated?
4. What happens to the content of the research over successive cohorts, and where does power actually accumulate: among current fellows, new fellows, coalitions of applicants, or around the ledger itself?
5. Where does the system fail first, and can the failure modes be fixed without simply relocating the inviolate level to some other stratum that will then be attacked in the same way?

Do not settle for saying that the scheme can be gamed. Specify the mechanisms, the sequence in which they would appear, and the second-order effects each one produces.
