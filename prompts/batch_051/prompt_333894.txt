Last night my friend and I argued into the early hours about a case I want to resolve with an actual rule in front of us, because we kept talking past each other about what "death" could mean. The case is a delayed variant of the teletransporter from the personal-identity literature, and it goes like this.

Alice steps into the scanner of a destructive matter transmitter. At 10:52 the machine records the complete physical state of her body and then destroys the body. A fault delays transmission; at 1:17 the destination machine assembles a new body from matter on site precisely according to the recorded state. The resulting person has Alice's memories, mannerisms, plans, and relationships, and reports waking abruptly with her last memory being the interior of the scanner at 10:51. Between 10:52 and 1:17 there was no living Alice-body anywhere. My friend insisted that Alice died at 10:52 and that the reconstructed person is a distinct individual; I insisted that Alice never died, because what mattered about Alice's life—the continuation of her psychology—was interrupted but not ended, and the loss was not irreversible, since the stored record guaranteed her reconstruction.

Section 9(a) of the Civil Status Code provides: "A person is dead when the person has irreversibly lost the capacity for consciousness and for the integrated functioning of the organism as a whole." The provision was drafted with ordinary medical cases in mind—cardiac arrest, brain death, and the like—and says nothing about scanning or reconstruction.

Apply Section 9(a) to the case. I want the readings separated: what the words themselves yield, what the purpose of the provision yields, what its drafters would likely have said if asked about destructive scanning, and what a reasonable reader would take the provision to mean here. Then say which approach should control for a statute of this kind, and whether the disagreement exposes a prior question the statute cannot settle—namely whether "person" and "death" track the continuing organism or the continuing psychology, and whether the provision can even see the difference between those.
