I'm a TA for an upper-level epistemology seminar, and I'm grading a set of short-answer questions on higher-order evidence. The professor wants comments that are specific enough to justify the grade, so I'm trying to get a second read on one answer before I mark it up. The question I wrote was:

"Distinguish first-order evidence from higher-order evidence, giving an example of each. Then explain what epistemic akrasia is, and lay out one reason for thinking it is always irrational and one reason for thinking it can sometimes be rational. Finally, say which side you lean toward and briefly say why."

The student wrote:

"First-order evidence is evidence that bears directly on whether the relevant proposition is true; for example, the scratchy lesion on an X-ray bears directly on whether the patient has a broken rib. Higher-order evidence bears on whether I'm evaluating that first-order evidence correctly — say, being told that I've been awake for 30 hours and that sleep-deprived people systematically misread X-rays.

Epistemic akrasia is believing P while also believing that one's evidence does not support P (or that one's belief is irrational). For instance, I keep believing the patient has a broken rib even though I also believe my sleep-deprived reading of the X-ray is unreliable and that my overall evidence does not establish the fracture. Notice this is basically Moore's paradox: the akratic person is committed to both P and 'my evidence doesn't support P,' which are inconsistent. So there's a strong intuitive case that no rational person can be akratic.

On the other side, someone who thinks akrasia can be rational would say that my first-order evidence hasn't changed — the X-ray still looks the way it looks. The higher-order evidence is evidence about me, not about the patient's rib, so it isn't really evidence about P at all. On this view I should go straight with my first-order evidence and not let facts about my own fatigue count for or against the medical claim.

I lean toward this second view. Whenever I try to correct for my own unreliability, I have to use the same sleep-deprived reasoning I'm trying to second-guess, so I can't genuinely step outside my own point of view. If the first-order reasoning still feels compelling, that's the only standard I've got, so believing on its basis has to be rational — in fact, I'm stuck with it."

Can you give me a grade out of 10 and a point-by-point breakdown of what this answer gets right and where it goes wrong? I'm looking for the kind of comments I could actually write next to each part of the answer, not just a general impression.
