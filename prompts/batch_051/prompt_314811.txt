Before I read any more on whether we're actually in a simulation, I want the spec for what an argument for that conclusion has to deliver. I keep seeing people defend or attack it by pointing at one step—the technology premise, or the "we can't assume we're special" step—and I'd like the full requirements list first. What must an argument that we're probably in a simulation get right, and why is each a requirement: the move from "our descendants could run ancestor simulations" to "we are currently in one", the assumption that simulated minds would be conscious (or that simulation is substrate-independent), the fact that empirical evidence cant decide the question either way, and the anthropic/selection effect that says sims outnumber non-sims. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of a camp, and be clear about distinctions that can't be collapsed—a simulated ancestor vs. a simulated universe, or "there exist simulated minds" vs. "our world is one of them." Then flag any subset you think is jointly unsatisfiable. Like, can the argument both assign a high probability to us being in a simulation and insist no observation could ever confirm it? Thats the part I actually care about. Skip a full scorecard, but say which requirement the standard trilemma-style version handles worst.
