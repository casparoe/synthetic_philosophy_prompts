I've got a definitional problem I keep going in circles on. I want a criterion for when it's literally true to say an AI "believes" something, as opposed to just using belief-talk as shorthand for predicting its behavior. I know this is adjacent to the intentional stance stuff, but I'm aiming at something stronger than "it's useful to treat the system as if." I've been working with three criteria:

(1) The attributed content has to do real inferential work: the system has to be able to combine it with other things it knows and act on the consequences, not just parrot the sentence in contexts that look like its training data.
(2) The state has to be updateable: if the system gets good evidence against the content, it revises or drops it, and the revision sticks across contexts rather than being an in-the-moment concession.
(3) The best way to predict the system's behavior over a range of novel cases has to be to treat the content as a standing state of the system, not as a one-off output.

Now the borderline case. Imagine an assistant with a memory bank of facts about its user ("sister is allergic to peanuts," "user lives in Chicago"). It retrieves these at query time, uses them to answer questions ("can my sister eat this granola bar?"), draws spontaneous consequences from them, and when the user says "actually she outgrew it," it updates the bank and answers accordingly forever after. On my three criteria it seems to pass. But I still feel odd saying it literally believes the user lives in Chicago, since nothing in the system ever "has" these facts except as stored entries waiting to be looked up; there's no point of view that lives with them between queries.

So does the assistant literally believe those facts, or is this a case where my three criteria misclassify? If you think it doesn't believe them, is the real problem with the case or with the criteria—and what would a fourth criterion need to say, without just sneaking in "the system must be conscious," to capture what's missing?
