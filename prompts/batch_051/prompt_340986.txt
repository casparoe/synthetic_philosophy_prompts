Hey — I teach a 300-level applied ethics class, 16 students, and we have one 75-minute session for animal ethics. I want an in-class activity rather than a lecture, something that makes students feel how the clean "just eat less meat" position gets shaky once you include crop cultivation deaths (field mice, voles, insects), chicken vs beef as a choice of units (deaths per calorie vs intensity/duration of suffering), and wild animal suffering as a scope problem. The takeaway I'm hoping for is that the students' own choice of moral metric does most of the work before anyone argues about the facts.

Design a game around a university dining-services commitee that has to choose a default menu. Use four or five menu cards (e.g. factory-farmed beef, factory-farmed chicken, plant-based, pasture-raised beef, maybe a "wild lands" option) with made-up but plausible numbers — animals killed per 1,000 kcal, typical welfare conditions, estimated field deaths for the plant option, land use and climate notes. The numbers are for the exercise and should be labeled as illustrative, not real research.

The key mechanic I want: participants must agree on an accounting rule *before* they see the full field-death and wild-population data — e.g. deaths per meal, suffering-weighted harm, total net welfare, something like that — and then they have to apply that same rule to all the menus including the hidden field and wild numbers. This should produce the moment where someone realizes their pro-vegan rule commits them to worrying intensely about harvest deaths, or their pro-beef rule commits them to counting a lot of short miserable chicken lives. Role cards could help (lives-counter, suffering-intensifier, wild-lands auditor, budget-and-scaling person, climate person). Give the game a scoring scheme that doesn't need real money — points, rankings, vetoes, whatever.

Please give me:

1. Goal and setup — 100–150 words
2. Materials and role cards — 250–300 words, with the actual card text and the illustrative numbers
3. Timed 75-minute sequence — 350–400 words, including when the data reveal happens and how scoring works
4. Debrief — 250–300 words, mapping moments in the game to the actual philosophy (deaths vs suffering, chicken vs beef, crop deaths, wild animal suffering, leave-nature-alone)
5. What will go wrong the first time — 200 words, with fixes
6. What the activity can't reproduce — 100–150 words, be blunt about where you had to distort the real debate

Keep it runnable by one instructor with no outside materials beyond what you specify.
