hey—I'm a fourth-year undergrad in a Hellenistic ethics seminar and I present my term paper next week. The thesis I'm defending is that the Stoics' apatheia costs more than they let on, and there standard move—distinguishing the pathê, the full-blown passions the sage is supposed to be free of, from the eupatheiai, the good affective states the sage keeps—doesn't actually rescue the kind of attachment to particular people that makes love, grief, and care worth having. My argument in a slogan-y way: for the exact objects that matter to us, the same evaluative judgment that's meant to ground a eupatheia (joy at a child flourishing, say) also rationally supports the matching pathos when the child is threatened; the Stoic can only stop the pathos by demoting the child's welfare to a preferred indifferent, and that demotion is the cost I don't think they can shrug off. I also have a bit on propatheiai—the involuntary first movements not even the sage can prevent—where I argue that saying the sage flinches but does not assent is a nice line but underdescribes what actually caring about someone is like. I'm not defending an Aristotelian metriopatheia exactly, and I'm not saying the sage has flat affect; I'm saying the residual eupatheiai are too thin to be what Stoics themselves tend to say they want.

The audience is the seminar: fifteen people, mostly philosophy majors who came in liking Aristotle's moderation line and now think Stoicism is impressive but a bit creepy. Two students are actual Stoic practitioners who've read the primary sources and will defend the system the way it wants to be defended; they'll grant the propatheiai/eupatheiai machinery and use it against me. The professor is an early-Stoicism specialist, very precise about Chrysippus, assent, and the pathê/eupatheiai taxonomy; she'll care a lot about whether I've got the psychology right and whether I'm conflating technical apatheia with the modern "equanimity" self-help thing. I've also got a friend from a philosophy of mind class visiting who thinks emotions are just judgments, so she'll be sympathetic to the Stoics but might push me on what "just judgments" actually implies.
