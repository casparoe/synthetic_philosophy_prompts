I’ve been arguing with a friend about whether “liberal” means the same thing in American and European political talk, and I’d like your help turning the argument into something more precise. My loose sense is that the word hangs together around some idea of freedom, but two traditions seem to cash that out very differently: one as freedom from state interference in markets, the other as freedom from poverty and discrimination backed by an active state. I want a working definition of liberal — or, if the word turns out to do several jobs, several definitions that together cover it — tested against the following hard cases.

Here are the cases I want the definition to handle:

1. An American voter who calls herself a liberal and supports a single-payer health system, higher income taxes on the rich, and stronger antidiscrimination enforcement.
2. A German voter who calls himself a liberal and supports the Free Democratic Party’s platform of lower taxes, deregulation, and a leaner welfare state.
3. The Australian Liberal Party, which is the country’s main centre-right party.
4. A classical liberal in the tradition of Locke and Adam Smith, for whom the core commitments are private property, free trade, religious toleration, and limited government.
5. John Stuart Mill, whom American-style progressives and European-style free-marketeers both claim as one of their own.
6. The phrase “liberal democracy” as applied both to the United States and to the Scandinavian social democracies, together with the use of “illiberal democracy” for states that hold elections while weakening courts and the press.
7. The expressions “liberal arts” for a nonvocational education in the humanities and sciences, and “a liberal amount” in a recipe, meaning generous.

Here is where my own opinions are shaky: I’m fairly confident that cases 1 and 2 are both correctly called liberal in their respective settings, which is exactly why I suspect the word is doing two jobs; I’m confident the Australian Liberal Party’s name does not by itself make it liberal, though I can imagine arguments either way; and I have no settled view about whether “liberal arts” and “a liberal amount” are the same word or mere homonyms.

Please approach this as a philosopher of language with a good grasp of the history of political thought. Start by brainstorming two or three candidate definitions and say what each one gets wrong before committing. Then state your preferred definition, run it through all seven cases, and tell me which verdicts surprise you and whether you ended up revising anything.

After the main analysis, close with a JSON object with exactly this schema:

- working_definition: a one-sentence statement of your final definition of the political sense of the term.
- additional_senses: an array of strings naming any further senses you found, each with a one-line gloss.
- case_assessments: an array of objects, one per case, each with the fields case, verdict (one of: core_political_sense, secondary_political_sense, nonpolitical_sense, borderline, not_liberal), confidence (low, medium, high), and rationale (one sentence).
- surprises: an array of strings listing cases where your definition forced a verdict you did not initially expect.
- definition_revision: a string saying what, if anything, you changed after running the cases.
