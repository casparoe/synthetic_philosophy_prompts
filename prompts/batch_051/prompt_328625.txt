I am a novelist, and I have been reading Lucretius’s *De Rerum Natura* in William Ellery Leonard’s translation while working on a novel in which a character becomes convinced that the solid objects around her are, at bottom, not solid at all. I want to get this idea right rather than use it as a vague metaphor. The passage I keep coming back to is from Book I, where Lucretius argues that what looks solid is in fact mingled with void:

> Then too, however solid objects seem,
> They yet are formed of matter mixed with void:
> In rocks and caves the watery moisture seeps,
> And beady drops stand out like plenteous tears;
> And food finds way through every frame that lives;
> The trees increase and yield the season’s fruit
> Because their food throughout the whole is poured,
> Even from the deepest roots, through trunks and boughs;
> And voices pass the solid walls and fly
> Reverberant through shut doorways of a house;
> And stiffening frost seeps inward to our bones.
> Which but for voids for bodies to go through
> ’Tis clear could happen in nowise at all.
> Again, why see we among objects some
> Of heavier weight, but of no bulkier size?
> Indeed, if in a ball of wool there be
> As much of body as in lump of lead,
> The two should weigh alike, since body tends
> To load things downward, while the void abides,
> By contrary nature, the imponderable.
> Therefore, an object just as large but lighter
> Declares infallibly its more of void;
> Even as the heavier more of matter shows,
> And how much less of vacant room inside.
> That which we’re seeking with sagacious quest
> Exists, infallibly, commixed with things—
> The void, the invisible inane.

Lucretius seems to slide between two claims: that every ordinary object is mixed with void, and that the only truly solid things are the atoms themselves. I am not sure what single concept of solidity could make both of those claims true at once. I would like you to propose a working definition of “solidity”—understood here as whatever the ancient atomists were attributing to atoms and, in a qualified or derived sense, to composite bodies—that gets the following cases right: (a) a lump of lead; (b) a ball of wool of equal volume; (c) a sponge; (d) a stone wall through which a voice passes; (e) water seeping through granite; (f) a single Lucretian atom; (g) two magnets repelling one another before they touch; (h) a sealed glass bottle full of air; (i) a living human body through which food and drink pass; and (j) a diamond. I am confident that (a), (f), and (j) count as solid, and that (c) and (h) do not count as solid, but I have no settled view about the rest. Run your definition through all ten cases, tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts it forces.
