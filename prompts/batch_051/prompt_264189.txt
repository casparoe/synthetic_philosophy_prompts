My father had a serious stroke several months ago, and my siblings and I have spent the intervening weeks arguing about what each of us owes him. Some of us talk as if we are repaying a loan; others talk as if the only thing that matters is how close our relationship actually is now; still others appeal to gratitude, to the bare fact of being a son or daughter, or to the plain fact that he needs help and that is enough. I run a small discussion group for adult children caring for ageing or incapacitated parents, and I would like to commission a short sorting quiz from you. The quiz should help readers discover which view of filial duty they actually hold from their answers to concrete cases, rather than from the label that sounds best to them.

You are a professional philosopher specialising in moral philosophy and applied ethics, with experience turning philosophical distinctions into diagnostic instruments for non-academic audiences. Please think carefully and resist the temptation to produce a generic quiz built from a memorised list of positions.

Before you draft any questions, begin by mapping the logical space. Identify the dimensions along which everyday views of filial duty genuinely come apart — for example, and only as examples: whether the obligation arises from past sacrifices, from the present quality of the relationship, from the parental role itself, from gratitude for benefits received, or simply from the parent's need; whether the duty is conditional on how the parent treated the child; whether it can ever be fully discharged; and whether it requires warmth of feeling or merely outward conduct. Then work out which combinations of answers are coherent, which are unstable, and which appear merely unexplored. You may keep this mapping brief in your final answer, but include a short note on which distinctions you kept and why.

The final quiz should contain eight to ten questions. Each question should present a concrete, realistic case and offer two or three answer options. The options must be written in ordinary language and must not name or hint at the position they represent. No question should simply ask the reader which principle they endorse. At least the following cases, or close variations of them, must appear among the questions:

- A parent who made large financial and practical sacrifices for the child's education and stability, and later needs years of care.
- A parent who was cold, neglectful, or emotionally harmful in childhood, and now needs care in old age.
- A parent and an adult child who genuinely enjoy one another's company and are in weekly contact, set against a parent and child whose contact is dutiful and strained.
- A parent who refused offered help, behaved ungratefully, and made the child's efforts miserable, yet still needs help.
- A child who has already given care whose rough market value equals what the parent once spent on them.
- A parent who was absent for most of the child's life and reappears only when in need.
- A parent with advanced dementia who no longer recognises the child.
- A family in which one sibling has provided nearly all the care while another has contributed little but still claims an equal say.

After the questions, provide a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto the following positions, described in plain language:

- The repayment or debt view: what children owe tracks the value of what parents earlier gave or sacrificed.
- The friendship view associated with Jane English: obligations arise from the current relationship, mutual affection, and shared history rather than from past sacrifices, and they persist or lapse as that relationship does.
- The gratitude view: benefits received generate a distinct obligation of appreciative responsiveness, which is neither strict repayment nor simply a matter of current affection.
- The role-based view: being a son or daughter is itself a source of duties that do not depend on warmth or on the parent's past conduct.
- The deflationary view: the parent-child tie adds nothing beyond the ordinary duties anyone has toward a person in serious need.

Flag clearly any of these positions that the quiz cannot reliably separate, given the cases you use, and explain why. Also note any combinations of answers that the quiz can register but that no listed position occupies; for each, briefly diagnose whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. If a plausible care-ethics or mutual-dependence position turns out to collapse into one of the listed positions under your questions, say so rather than inventing an artificial leaf for it.

Finally, include a short preamble for the reader explaining how to take the quiz and how the scoring works, keeping it to a sentence or two per point.

The audience is thoughtful but non-academic adults facing real decisions about an ageing parent's care. Keep the language plain, and avoid technical terms such as normative, supererogatory, or prima facie unless you define them on first use. Please write the entire instrument, including the preamble, questions, scoring key, and commentary, in British English.
