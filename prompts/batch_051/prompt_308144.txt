I am a theoretical computer scientist whose native vocabulary is cryptography and computational complexity. When I ask whether something is random, I reach for distinguishers and adversaries, feasible computation, pseudorandom generators and one-way functions, short seeds stretching into long pseudorandom outputs, negligible advantage, and computational indistinguishability; I think in ensembles and asymptotic guarantees, not in single-string properties.

Restate, in that vocabulary, the debate over whether an individual finite binary sequence can be random, as faithfully as you can. Work through the main positions: (1) the von Mises-style view that randomness belongs to infinite collectives, sequences whose limiting frequencies are undisturbed by every admissible place-selection rule; (2) the Martin-Löf view that randomness is passing every effective sequential test, equivalently avoiding every effective measure-zero set; and (3) the Kolmogorov-complexity view that a finite string is random to the extent that its shortest effective description is about as long as the string itself. For each, tell me what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion and where the distortion enters, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the original disagreement survive this translation, and which do not? In particular: is randomness a property of an individual object or of the generating process, and is there any fact of the matter about the randomness of a particular finite string? Is what fails to translate a sign that my framework is missing something the debate was really about, or that the original debate was less clear than it looks? Be concrete about the cases that pull the frameworks apart: a long pseudorandom string stretched from a short seed; a Kolmogorov-incompressible string that no efficient test detects except by its long description; and a computable, highly compressible string, such as the first million bits of pi, that nonetheless passes ordinary statistical tests.
