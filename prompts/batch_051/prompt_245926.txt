I have recently been put in charge of designing a procedure for a small regulatory body that must grade the plausibility of contested factual claims and decide what can responsibly be asserted from them. My predecessor left a long memo arguing that this problem was settled long ago: any coherent scheme for assigning degrees of plausibility must be representable as an ordinary probability measure, so entertaining non-probabilistic alternatives is a waste of time. The argument, the one associated with Cox's theorem and pressed hard by Jaynes, runs roughly as follows. If degrees of plausibility fall on a single real-valued scale, if the plausibility of a negation is determined by the plausibility of what is negated, and if the plausibility of a conjunction is determined by the plausibilities of its conjuncts, then the familiar probability axioms follow uniquely up to an inessential rescaling. Sets or intervals of probabilities, non-additive degrees of belief, ranking procedures, and bare qualitative confidence levels are therefore not just nonstandard; they violate constraints so basic that any rational agent should already accept them. I find this deeply compelling: it offers a theorem where I otherwise face a messy design choice, it promises a definite answer to every dispute about how much credence a body of evidence supports, and it explains why every serious attempt to formalize uncertain reasoning seems to circle back to probability theory.

And yet I cannot shake a contrary conviction. A single number appears to collapse epistemically distinct states. Having seen a long run of balanced evidence for and against a claim, having received sharply conflicting testimony whose reliability I cannot begin to assess, and having no evidence at all about an entirely new process can all be represented by the same probability, but they feel like importantly different states of evidence. Forcing each into a crisp number seems less like coherence than like manufacturing precision I do not have. When I try to reason with the numbers, I notice that I want different behavior in the ignorance case than in the balanced-evidence case, and I do not think this is merely an emotional failure or a failure to understand my own uncertainty.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the Cox-style argument to be the thing that goes or to be narrowed, and whether there is a defensible reading on which both survive, for example one view governing ideal agents while the other tracks something about the evidence itself. Give me the strongest version of each position and a negotiated verdict.
