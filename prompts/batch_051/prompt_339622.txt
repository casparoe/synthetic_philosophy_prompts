The seminar I'm in has spent the term on complicity, and the running question has been whether someone can be complicit in a wrong they did not cause. The instructor has given firm rulings on a series of cases but has never stated the principle behind them. Her rulings: acting as a lookout for a burglary, complicit; lending one's car to a friend knowing it will be used to transport stolen goods, complicit; keeping the payroll for a smuggling operation while knowing exactly what it is, complicit; standing by silently while a stranger is mugged, with no involvement or encouragement, not complicit; buying a laptop one knows was stolen, months after the theft, not complicit in the theft itself though guilty of receiving stolen property; making ordinary suits for a known gangster without knowing of any particular crime, not complicit.

The case she has not yet ruled on is the unused getaway driver: someone agrees to drive the car for a planned robbery and waits at the agreed rendezvous, but the robbers flee on foot in the opposite direction and never call on him, so his waiting made no difference to how the robbery or the escape went. I'd like a prediction of how she'll rule, but the prediction matters to me mainly as evidence about her principle. Say which rules fit her record so far, what each predicts about the getaway driver, which rule you'd bet on and why, and what further case she should be asked about to settle which rule she is actually using. If her record genuinely underdetermines the verdict, say precisely what each possible ruling on the new case would reveal about her principle.
