Consider the claim that a peaceful society has not discovered one virtue of courage running through both military and civilian life; it has merely extended an honorific. On this view, the courage praised in a soldier who advances under fire is a different underlying disposition from the courage praised in a whistleblower who risks her career, a founder who risks his savings, or a patient who persists through exhausting treatment. The civilian cases, the skeptic says, involve tolerance for social, financial, and emotional risk, while the martial paradigm involves something else: the regulation of fear in the face of physical danger. Part of this disagreement looks semantic or evaluative, but part of it looks empirical. Treat the claim as a partly testable hypothesis about whether civilian and martial courage are one underlying disposition or a loose cluster of distinct capacities linked mainly by a shared word.

Design the best test you can of that hypothesis. Specify what you would measure, in whom, and under what conditions; say how you would operationalize the idea of a single underlying disposition without begging the question; and describe what pattern of results would count as evidence for unity and what pattern would count as evidence for fragmentation. Then state, as precisely as you can, what even a decisive result would and would not settle, including whether it could settle whether the civilian uses of courage are metaphorical or whether the civilian cases genuinely deserve the honorific. Conclude by saying how confident you are in the design as a whole and singling out the single point on which you are least confident.
