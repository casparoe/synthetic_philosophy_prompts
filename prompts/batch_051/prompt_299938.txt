Epistemic status: I've been tugging at Avicenna's flying man all week and I've convinced myself my reconstruction is cleaner then the usual presentations, only now I can't tell if the argument is any good.

Here's the setup as I'm using it. Imagine a person created all at once as a fully developed adult, floating blindfolded in a void, limbs spread so he isn't touching himself, no air moving on his skin, no sound — total sensory deprivation, but awake and thinking. Avicenna says this person will still affirm his own existence.

The argument:

(1) The flying man can affirm his own existence.

(2) The flying man cannot affirm that he has a body: not hands, face, heartbeat, any particular body part, or the body as a whole. There's no sensory route to any of that.

(3) If the self were a body or a body part, the flying man would be able to affirm that body (or part) as what he is.

(4) So from (1)–(3), the self is not a body or body part.

(5) Therefore the self is some non-bodily thing.

I keep thinking (3) is doing the smuggling: it quietly assumes the self is transparent to itself — that if I were my body, I'd have an infallible inner line on that fact. That's exactly the assumption the argument should be earning rather than spending. But then (1) bugs me too: a freshly created void-brain might not affirm existence at all, because there's no I-representation without some minimal embodied self-model. And (2) might overclaim: maybe proprioception alone lets him gesture at a body even with no touch, or maybe he can infer he's some finite physical-ish thing. So: is the argument valid? If not, which step is the actual weak point, and what would a steelman fix look like? If the argument can be saved by weakening (3) to something like 'the self presents itself as body-free,' give that version and say exactly what it buys you — because at that point it might not buy the metaphysics.
