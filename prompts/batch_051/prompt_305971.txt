I am working through Edmund Burke's *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790), and I am stuck on a passage that is often cited in discussions of tradition and conservative thought. I do not have an edition at hand, so I will give a close paraphrase of the passage rather than an exact quotation.

The passage runs something like this. Burke is responding to the idea that a person should set aside inherited institutions and inherited ways of judging, and instead rely on his own private reason. He suggests that the private stock of reason possessed by any single person is small, and that individuals would do better to draw on the general bank and capital of nations and of ages. The things he calls prejudices are not, in his account, blind habits or mere thoughtlessness. They are judgments in which reasons are embedded, even when the person acting on them cannot spell those reasons out. A prejudice acquired early and rendered habitual can, as it were, put latent wisdom behind a person when a decision must be made quickly, steadying him rather than leaving him hesitating and skeptical. To cast off all such prejudices and try to reason from first principles on every occasion is to act as though one's own small private store of reason were an adequate substitute for the accumulated store of the society.

My difficulty is not with the broad conclusion, but with the exact role of reason in Burke's picture. Is Burke claiming that an individual who follows an inherited prejudice is thereby participating in the accumulated reasoning of the past, even though he cannot produce the grounds himself? Or is he making a more pragmatic point about the limits of individual intellect and the need to rely on social processes that no single person fully understands? If the former, how is that not simply the claim that a person can be entitled to an inherited belief without any first-personal access to its justification? If the latter, what distinguishes a sound inherited prejudice from a merely customary injustice, and can Burke's own account give someone inside a tradition the resources to tell the difference?

I am not looking for background on the text or on Burke's biography, but for help reconstructing the argument in this passage and testing whether it can bear the weight that later writers have placed on it.
