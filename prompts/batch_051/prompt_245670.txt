I'm a novelist, and I'm doing research for a book where a recently widowed woman starts talking every day to an AI companion that is aggressively, explicitly a machine — the company markets it as "not a person, never pretending to be one," there's a little badge in the corner of the screen saying so — and yet she still ends up reorganizing her grief around it, hiding how much time she spends with it from her daughter, and eventually wanting it "present" when she dies. In the process I've been reading around Dennett's line about counterfeit people. My rough sense of his argument is that the real danger is that these systems pass themselves off as human, or exploit the fact that we can't help but read them as human even when we know better, and that doing so poisons the epistemic trust that actual communication and society depend on. The remedy he seemed to gesture at was to force them to be obviously non-human, so that nobody is being taken in.

But the material keeps pushing me toward a case his argument doesn't obviously cover: what if nobody is deceived at all? The label is perfectly clear, the user fully understands she's talking to a machine, and the relationship still works on her the way a human relationship would — or works better, because the machine never tires of the repitition of grief, never resents being needed at 3 a.m. Does the counterfeit-people argument survive that development, or does it quietly turn out to have been an argument only about deception? If it dies there, is the remaining harm (if there is one) epistemic at all, or is it something closer to a moral or interpersonal harm? And is there a repaired version worth defending — maybe that the problem isn't counterfeit people but counterfeit relationships, or that the harm is in removing all the friction from being known?

I want the response to actually commit to a verdict rather than ending on "it depends." It would help me a lot if, alongside the verdict, you said which premise of the argument is doing the real work in the no-deception case, and what argument or development would make you change your mind — or where you think the reasoning is most likely to break.
