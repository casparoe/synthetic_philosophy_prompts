I'm working through a problem in research ethics and keep coming back to a passage in the Nuremberg Code, the 1947 statement of permissible medical experiments from the judgment in United States v. Karl Brandt et al. In the first principle, after stating that the voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential, the Code adds something like the following: the duty and responsibility for ascertaining the quality of the consent rests personally on each individual who initiates, directs, or takes part in the experiment, and this personal duty cannot be delegated to another with impunity.

On one side, I am attracted to a reductive view of consent validity. On this view, valid consent is a property of the subject's state. If the person has legal capacity, has been given the material facts, and is not subject to force, fraud, deceit, duress, or other forms of constraint or coercion, then the consent is valid, full stop. The view is elegant because it makes validity checkable by asking what the subject knew and chose, without requiring us to assess the experimenter's diligence, motives, or procedures.

On the other side, I have a firm intuition that the Nuremberg passage is tracking something real when it places a non-delegable burden on the person obtaining consent. Consider a case in which a subject agrees freely and understands everything they have been told, but the researcher has not actually checked the subject's comprehension, or deliberately omits a risk the subject would care about but does not know to ask about. The subject's consent looks valid by the subjective test, yet something about the consent seems defective—not merely the researcher's conduct, but the consent itself. The Code seems to locate the defect in the relation between the parties rather than solely in the subject's mind.

I do not want a simple verdict for either side. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be amended, and whether there is a reading on which both survive—for example, a distinction between valid consent and ethically obtained consent, or between the validity of consent and the quality of the consent process. Please present the strongest version of each position and then offer a negotiated verdict. Answer in American English.
