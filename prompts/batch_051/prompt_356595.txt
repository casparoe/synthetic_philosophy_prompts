I've been rereading Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments and getting stuck on Part III, Chapter 1, where he lays out the self-examination mechanism. As I read him, Smith claims that when I judge my own conduct I split myself into two roles: the examined person and the examiner. The examiner's job is to enter into the point of view of an impartial spectator—not a real bystander, but an imagined one—and to feel how such a spectator would feel about my motives and choices. What seduces me about this is how much it explains. It explains why conscience speaks in a voice that feels third-personal, why our self-blame mirrors the blame we have seen others express, and why people raised in different moral cultures develop different inner critics. It gives a single mechanism for judging others and judging oneself, no special moral faculty required. But my own experience resists it. When I feel ashamed of something every actual spectator in my life would have praised, I dont experience the shame as my borrowing an imagined spectator's verdict. I experience it as a first-person correction of the spectators. I seem to know something about my motives—their sourcing, their timing, their exact quality—that no spectator, however impartial, could fully occupy. The intuition isn't that Smith left out some special moral sense. It's that self-assessment has a residue unlike spectator-taking, and that this residue is exactly what does the hardest moral work.

So the clash, as I'd want the response to frame it: Smith's theory says the impartial spectator, internalized, is all there is to normative self-judgment; my intuition says first-person access to my own mental states gives me a private authority over the verdict that no imagined spectator can override, not because I'm special but because the spectator is necessarily in the same position I am when I imagine him—one guess among others. Which yields? If I should keep Smith, give me an argument that the inner evaluator is itself an impartial spectator, properly described, or that the private-authority intuition is introspection misfiring. If I should keep the intuition, tell me exactly which Smithian premise has to go: the spectator mechanism itself, the claim that it's internalized from actual social judgments, or the claim that it's the ground of moral self-approbation. And is there a reading of impartial spectator broad enough that the private voice is just what the mechanism produces at a fixed point, so the two stop looking like rivals? Walk me through the costs of each option and dont just tell me Smith was right.
