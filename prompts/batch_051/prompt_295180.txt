I am preparing a graduate seminar on Leibniz's best-of-all-possible-worlds theodicy and have been trying to fix, for the first session, the exact set of objections that the literature treats as canonical. The list I have settled on is this: (i) the no-maximum objection, which says that a best among possible worlds need not be well-defined, since for any candidate world a better one appears possible and Leibniz offers no clear principle that halts the series; (ii) the divine-freedom objection, which says that if God is morally necessitated to choose the best, then the choice is not free in any sense strong enough to ground praise or responsibility; (iii) the author-of-sin objection, which says that willing a whole that includes particular evils entails willing those evils, however carefully Leibniz distinguishes permission from positive willing; (iv) the Euthyphro-style vacuity objection, which asks whether the standard of world-goodness is independent of God, in which case God is constrained by something external, or fixed by God's own nature, in which case the claim that God chooses the best threatens to become empty; (v) the empirical objection pressed by Voltaire, which takes the actual quantity and distribution of suffering to be simply incompatible with optimality; and (vi) the particularity objection, which grants for the sake of argument that the whole is optimal but notes that this still gives no answer to the person who asks why this evil befalls her, and appears to treat persons as replaceable parts of a globally best arrangement.

A colleague has argued that this list is effectively complete and that any further objection will turn out to be a rewording, a special case, or a combination of these six. I am not asking you to recover a seventh standard objection I have forgotten; I would like you to try to construct a genuinely new one. Please propose an objection to Leibniz's theodicy that is not on the list and that targets a different premise or inference than any of the six do. Then say which of the six it sits closest to, or whether it opens a new family of objections altogether, and give your honest assessment of whether it is a good objection or merely a clever one.
