I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and this semester I'm guest-teaching a unit on legal proof in an undergrad course. I've got a batch of short-answer questions to grade, and one answer has me flip-flopping on what's fair. The rubrick requires point-by-point justification for a grade, so could you do the grading for me? Here's the question and the student's answer.

Question: Courts consistently refuse to let civil plaintiffs win on "naked statistical evidence" alone — say, 80% of buses on the relevant route belonged to the defendant, or a bare majority of rodeo attendees didn't pay — even though those probabilities look like they clear the preponderance standard. Yet those same courts will accept a single eyewitness at the same (or lower) reported reliability. What exactly is the puzzle here? And is the standard counterfactual or "sensitivity" explanation enough to make sense of the law's resistance, including in criminal cases where the standard is proof beyond a reasonable doubt?

Student answer: "The puzzle is that the courts seem to care about something other than the size of the probability. In the blue bus case the statistic is about 80 percent, which is comfortably above the civil preponderance threshold, and in the gatecrasher case a bare majority still crosses it, but courts still won't find liability. Since an eyewitness who is right at the same rate is accepted, the law must be tracking the kind of evidence, not just the amount of probability. The standard explanation is that naked statistics are insensitive to whether this particular defendant actually did it: the blue bus figure would look the same whether or not this particular bus caused the crash. An eyewitness, by contrast, is counterfactually sensitive — if the defendant hadn't been there, the witness wouldn't have identified them. So statistical evidence can never establish that this particular defendant caused the harm; only individualized evidence does. The courts' resistance is therefore justified, and the criminal case is even easier to explain, because 80 percent or 51 percent never reaches the very high probability that proof beyond a reasonable doubt demands."

Give me a grade (A through F) and the specific comments that justify it: what the answer gets right, exactly where it goes wrong, and how much each mistake costs. I need this laid out point by point, not just as an overall impression.
