I'm writing a novel set in a near-future city and I want the central institution to behave like a real mechanism, not a clean allegory. The city's civil rights commission has adopted a kind ledger. Any resident can declare, update, or remove their membership in any of the city's protected social categories—race, gender, ethnicity, disability, religion—online, for free, as often as they like. For every official purpose the most recent entry is conclusive: discrimination hearings can't take evidence about how other people actually classified the parties, and employers, landlords, and police get a safe harbor for relying on the entry that was current at the time of an incident. The ledger is public and time-stamped, and a judge may look at someone's full entry history when assessing credibility, but not when determining what category the person belongs to. The city also uses the ledger for funding formulas, hiring goals, and hate-crime statistics, so the population counts in each category carry money and political weight.

What I want worked out, for the novel, is how this would actually play out in practice. Who would switch declarations and when—employees before suing, employers before firing, police before charging? How do people game the safe harbor, and how do others game the honesty-of-history rule back? What happens to the meanings of the categories themselves over time, once one's own declaration drives the official count and everyone can see the switches? Where does the whole thing mutate or collapse? And are there fixes—waiting periods, fees on updates, requiring attestation by others, freezing the ledger during a dispute, seperate ledgers for self-identity and third-party classification—that would stop the worst failure modes without gutting the point of making self-declaration authoritative?
