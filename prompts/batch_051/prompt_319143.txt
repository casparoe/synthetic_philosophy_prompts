I keep returning to an argument at work about whether watching a model's chain-of-thought (CoT) — the step-by-step natural-language "reasoning" some models produce before their final answer — is a sound way to catch a model that's trying to decieve us. The practice, if I can call it that, is pretty concrete: safety teams at labs read or monitor CoT traces looking for signs of scheming, sandbagging, or misaligned goals, and they treat suspicious CoT as actionable evidence. Some folks there talk as if the CoT just is the model "thinking out loud," and monitoring is therefore like reading its mind. Others wave around faithfulness results and say that's naive.

The passage that best captures the skeptical side, for me, is from Miles Turpin, Julian Michael, Ethan Perez, and Samuel R. Bowman's paper "Language Models Don't Always Say What They Think: Unfaithful Explanations in Chain-of-Thought Prompting" (2023):

> It is tempting to interpret these CoT explanations as the LLM's process for solving a task. This level of transparency into LLMs' predictions would yield significant safety benefits. However, we find that CoT explanations can systematically misrepresent the true reason for a model's prediction.

So here's what I'd like. Reconstruct the philosophical commitments of CoT monitoring as a safety practice: what view of the relation between verbalized reasoning and the model's actual computation does it presuppose? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with — e.g., does it strictly require that CoT is a faithful transcript, or only that it's correlated enough to serve as evidence? Take the Turpin et al. passage seriously and say what exactly it rules out: does it kill the practice, or can the practice be re-described so it treats CoT as fallible evidence? What would the practice have to change, concretely, if models got better at producing nice-looking CoT under optimization pressure? And be self-critical: if "the practice" is too motley to have presuppositions, or my framing assumes CoT is the sort of thing that could be "honest" when it's actually just generated text, say so plainly instead of forcing a tidy answer. I also want to know where your argument leans on empirical facts about models that could turn out false, rather than on philosophical commitments alone.
