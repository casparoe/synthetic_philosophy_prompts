I'm a staff software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'm about to present a proposal at our company's quarterly engineering forum. The proposal is to turn off default access to LLM coding assistants for work on our two most safety-critical services for a six-month pilot, so that the on-call and core development teams on those services work without autocomplete or chat assistance, while everyone remains free to use the tools elsewhere.

The case I'm making is narrower than the claim that AI makes us dumber. My central claim is that routine reliance on coding assistants erodes the team's capacity to reconstruct the reasoning path from requirement, to design decision, to code, and that this capacity matters even when individual engineers are still sharp and the assisted outputs are correct. The risk I'm pointing at isn't local bugs; it's a shift in how we verify. We stop independently re-deriving solutions and start checking them locally: does it look plausible, does it pass the tests, does it match what I would have guessed. Those checks are fine individually, but they are all correlated with the assistant's own strengths and blind spots. The team ends up with fewer independent error signals, so the failures that get through are exactly the novel, systematic ones that would have required somebody to re-derive the whole path.

I have three supporting points:

1. A team understands a system not when members can recall facts about it, but when they can reconstruct, under challenge, the chain from requirements to design decisions to code, including the alternatives that were rejected.
2. LLM assistance makes that reconstruction rare, because the plausible solution arrives first and whatever reasoning gets attached afterward is optimized for sounding right rather than for tracking how the code actually came to be.
3. The main risk isn't an individual's ignorance; it's the loss of redundancy in error generation at the team level. When everyone accepts the same kind of output and checks it in the same few ways, our errors become correlated, and correlated errors are exactly what survives code review and tests.

The audience will be about 40 people: mostly senior backend and infrastructure engineers who value the productivity gains and will hear any restriction as an old-timer rejecting a new tool; a smaller group of ML platform engineers who think the real fix is better evals, retrieval, and tooling rather than a ban; a few staff engineers who will want numbers; and a director whose visible concern is delivery velocity. They are pragmatic and metrics-driven, and abstract talk about understanding will not move them unless I cash it out in terms of failure modes, outages, and maintainability.

For each, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft or likely to get challenged. Include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
