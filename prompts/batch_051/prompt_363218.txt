Suppose a small foundation that mostly funds global health gets a new director who's just been converted to the self-indication assumption. She imposes a grant-ranking rule: a proposal gets scored by the expected number of conscious observer-moments it will add across all of time, multiplied by the probability that the intervention actually brings them into existence, and there is deliberately no separate term for making existing lives better. Her rationale is that if SIA is right, you should treat yourself as a random draw from the pool of all conscious observer-moments, so enlarging the pool is the main thing the evidence of your own existence supports. I don't want you to judge whether the view is correct: treat the rule as a mechanism. What does it incentivize grantees to actually do, as opposed to just how they frame their applications? How would a savvy grant-writer game the scoring, especially through the choice of reference class (humans? all animals? future digital minds?) and through counterfactual claims about how many additional minds their project creates? What happens to interventions that improve lives without adding new ones, or that shrink population while raising average wellbeing a lot? And then say whether the failure modes can be fixed by bolting on an average-wellbeing term, a capping rule, or some SSA-style conditioning, or whether each fix just drags the presumptuous philosopher problem back in through a side door.
