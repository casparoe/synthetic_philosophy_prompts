I'm a journalist working on a feature about how anti-discrimination law decides which traits get protected. The longer I report the piece, the more the existing system looks like an accretion of court doctrines and statute-by-statute line-drawing rather than a principled method. One legal scholar described to me a proposal—admittedly a thought experiment—that I would like to think through carefully before I write about it.

The proposal is to replace much of this ad hoc process with a Standing Commission on Protected Grounds, plus a sunset rule. Every currently protected ground, and every newly petitioned ground, would be scored on four 25-point criteria: how costly the trait is to change or conceal; the documentary record of historical subordination; evidence of present-day disparate treatment in standardized audits of employment, housing, credit, and similar domains; and the group's ability to protect itself through ordinary political processes. A ground would qualify for protection only if it scores at least 70 out of 100 and no single criterion falls below 10. All existing protections would sunset after ten years and have to be renewed under the same standard. A randomly selected citizens' panel of forty would hear the evidence over two weekends and would have to approve any new or renewed ground by simple majority. Any trade association, insurer, union, civil-rights organization, or similar body could submit evidence or challenge the commission's statistical methods, and the commission would have to publish its data and scoring model.

I want to treat this as a mechanism rather than as a moral or legal position. Walk me through how it would actually behave: what it would incentivize among advocacy groups petitioning for protection, among employers and insurers facing regulation, among the commissioners who assign scores, and among the citizens' panelists. How would the scoring itself be gamed, and which actor would game it first? What happens as a previously protected ground approaches its sunset review? Would the no-criterion-below-10 rule distort which groups even try? And what second-order effects would this have on what counts as a trait, a group, or a claim of disadvantage? I especially want to know where the design would fail first and whether that failure could be patched without unraveling the rest.
