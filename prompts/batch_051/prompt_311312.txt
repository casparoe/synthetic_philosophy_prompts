I have a friend, a retired palliative-care physician who now leads a small contemplative-practice group, whose incidental judgments about other people's emotional lives have been so consistent over the years that I have started treating them as a kind of common-law record and trying to extract the rule behind them. For example, she dismissed a colleague's three-in-the-morning anxiety about a tenure decision—the checking, the rehearsed replies, the sleeplessness—as a disturbance worth dissolving, because the agitation was doing no work and was fixed on something no amount of checking could settle. But she praised a teacher who, after being publicly humiliated in a department meeting, felt one brief surge of anger, noticed the single fair criticism buried in the insult, took it, and went home untroubled; that, she said, is what equanimity looks like. She has also insisted that a woman who, many years after losing an adult son, is occasionally overtaken by tears at the sight of his handwriting is not disturbed and should not be talked out of her grief; she says she would not want to be freed from it, because it is love's way of staying in relation to him. She defended a young physician who let a sharp, well-aimed anger at a hospital policy carry her through a year of meetings and letters, but she warned the same physician that nursing the same fury after the policy was changed would be making herself sick to no one's benefit. And she once said of two viewers of a distant disaster that the one who felt nothing was not enviable—something in him had gone dead—while the one immobilized for a week by the footage was not feeling too much but feeling badly; both, she thought, miss the mark.

The case I actually need to settle is a friend in my reading group who carries a quiet, old anger—grief mixed with indignation—about an atrocity whose last perpetrators were gone before she could ever address them. She can do nothing that would matter, she is not looking for an outlet, and the anger does not much interfere with her ordinary life. She says she does not want to let go of it, because staying angry is the only way she knows to take the victims seriously.

What would my physician friend predict?
