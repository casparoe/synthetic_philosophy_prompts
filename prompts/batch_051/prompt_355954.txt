I've been chewing on a distinction I made up a while ago and I want it pressure tested, because it either collapses into something boring or it's doing real semantic work and I honestly can't tell which.

I call it the tag/kind split for personal names. The claim is that a person's full name bundles two very different devices that we usually treat as the same kind of thing.

Given names are tags: they denote without describing. Their job is to pick out a person and nothing else; they aren't true or false of anyone, and they carry no correctness conditions above "this is the label by which the relevant people reidentify you." That's why Alex doesn't mean "defender of man" in any semantic sense, only etymologically. You can name a kid Tuesday, or rename yourself, and nobody thinks a fact about you got revised.

Family names are kind terms for social lineages. They don't primarily denote the person; they classify the person as a member of a socially recognized descent-kind. Smith doesn't name you; it predicates membership in the Smiths.

This would explain some asymmetries I keep noticing. Family names pluralize and genericize productively ("the Smiths," "a real Einstein," "she's no Rockefeller"), while given names only do that when they're being deliberately warped into classifiers, like "a Karen." A family name can be false of someone in a way a given name can't: someone passing as a Rockefeller can bear the name without being one, but it's odd to say someone "isn't really an Alex" without changing the subject. And across languages we translate given names (John/Jean/Juan) but mostly keep family names intact, as if the latter were labels for a kind whose history is doing the work.

I know given names carry gender and generation info — Bertha doesn't get assigned to newborns. My move is that this is regular knowledge about who gets which tag, not semantic content of the tag itself, the same way "gold is expensive" is knowledge about gold and not the meaning of "gold." But I keep going back and forth on whether that move is legitimate or just me immunizing the theory.

So: is the tag/kind split coherent? Is it already a known position — a Millian story for given names and something sortal for surnames? And where does it break? I'd like the strongest version of my view first, then the part that kills it if anything does, then a verdict on whether the asymmetry is semantic or just social.

Want the response in four sections, by name: (1) Steelman, 200 words; (2) Nearest existing view, 100 words, no citations; (3) The case against, 250 words — hit the hardest counterexamples, like Icelandic patronymics, legal name changes, adoption, mononyms, whatever you have; (4) Verdict, 100 words.
