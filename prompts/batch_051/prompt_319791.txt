I'm a philosophy professor, and over the last few years I've ended up, more times than I'd like, in arguments about whether the state should fund literary fiction. I've come to think the debate is stuck in a way that isn't just ordinary intransigence. The instrumentalists defend funding on the grounds that serious fiction is a sort of civic technology: it trains empathy, perspective-taking, moral imagination—habits a democracy needs. The autonomists cringe at this and insist that art's value is intrinsic, that its not a tool for anything, and that justifying it by its effects is a category mistake. The sceptics wave the patchy empirical record on whether reading actually improves theory of mind, and add that even if the intrinsic view were right, a liberal state can't fund souls; it funds schools, health, infrastructure.

Everyone knows each other's moves now, and nobody shifts. My suspicion is that this isn't one debate at all: the instrumentalists and sceptics are having an empirical and welfarist argument, the instrumentalists and autonomists are having a conceptual argument about what kind of value art has, and the autonomists and sceptics are having an argument about what can legitimately justify public spending in the first place. The whole thing only looks like a single debate because everyone uses the word 'value' and talks passed each other.

So diagnose the stalemate for me. Is it verbal, substantive, or a bundle of several disagreements under one heading? Which parts are hidden factual questions, which are conceptual, and which are bedrock normative commitments? And on the back of that diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress—a distinction, an experimental design, a change of question—or whether I should stop expecting the two sides to move at all.

One more thing: mark clearly what's settled and what's genuinely contested among people who work on this in aesthetics and public policy—don't dress a contested position up as the consensus. Hard limit: 650 words.
