Say a middle-income country's tax authority is in trouble: evasion is rampant, and the status quo mixes ethics workshops, a "pay your fair share" taxpayer-trust campaign, and auditors who use a lot of individual discretion. In 2030 a new government, advised by someone steeped in Han Feizi, scraps all of it for a pure two-handle system. Every auditor is scored each quarter on revenue assessed above target, minus a penalty for assessments later overturned on appeal. Bonuses, promotions, and firing follow mechanically from that one number. All talk of public service, integrity, or cultivating honest auditors disappears from training; the rules are the rules, and the only thing an auditor needs to know are what gets rewarded and what gets punished.

Walk me through how this plays out over the next fifteen years for: (a) revenue actually collected, (b) taxpayer trust and voluntary compliance, (c) auditor behavior, collusion, and corruption, and (d) the content of the tax code itself. Make the reasoning visible: which predictions you'd stand behind no matter how the details are set, and which ones flip depending on things like whether the quarterly quota is fixed in advance or ratcheted up, how hard it is for taxpayers to appeal, and how much auditors can coordinate with each other. And finish by saying what would have to be true about human motivation for the Han Feizi-style bet to beat the virtue-based approach it replaced.
