I keep going back and forth on the following argument and cannot localize the error; if there is no error, I have to accept a conclusion about legal systems that seems too strong.

(1) A legal fiction is a rule instructing courts to treat a proposition as true even though everyone understands that the proposition is not literally true: the corporation treated as a person, the child not yet born at the father's death treated as already born for purposes of inheritance.

(2) To treat a proposition as true while understanding that it is not literally true is to reason from a premise one believes to be false.

(3) A judge who reasons from a premise she believes to be false is not applying existing law to the facts; she is rationalizing a decision made on some other, unstated ground.

(4) Yet legal fictions are pervasive in mature legal systems, and the fields in which they do the most work—corporate law, inheritance—seem to be paradigm cases of applying existing rules to facts, producing stable and predictable rights.

(5) Therefore either those fields are not really cases of applying existing law to facts, or a legal fiction is not really a case of treating a nonliteral proposition as true.

Is the argument valid? If it is not, identify the first step that fails and give the corrected version of that step. If it is valid, say which disjunct in (5) should be accepted and explain why the conclusion is less catastrophic than it looks.
