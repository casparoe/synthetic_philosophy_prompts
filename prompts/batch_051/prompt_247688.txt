Clinical equipoise is a widely accepted condition on the ethics of randomized controlled trials: patients may be randomized between two treatments only while there is honest, unsettled disagreement among expert practitioners about which arm is superior. Yet actual trial practice often seems to depart from that condition. Trials routinely continue after the data begin to lean toward one arm, stopping only at a pre-specified statistical threshold rather than at the moment any investigator forms a personal leaning; and a clinician who would recommend treatment A in her own clinic may, in the trial, be expected to set that judgment aside and let randomization assign the patient to A or B. What view of the clinician's obligations, of the moral difference between care and research, and of what makes uncertainty genuinely clinical does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and say whether the theory is coherent. Then explain what trial design, informed consent, or monitoring would have to change if we replaced that presupposed view with the principle that a clinician should always give each patient the treatment that currently has the highest expected benefit given all available evidence.
