I write about science and ideas for outlets that expect a statistically literate audience, and I'm preparing a long feature on how readers of ancient texts decide when a recurring number is a message and when it is a coincidence. The background reading has me stalled. One family of interpreters treats repeated numbers in scriptural texts — seven, twelve, forty, and the like — as deliberate compositional or theological devices whose recurrence carries meaning. Another family treats the same observations as largely the product of pattern-hunting, the textual analog of seeing faces in clouds. I don't work in biblical studies, and both positions are stated in idioms I can't quite evaluate. What I have instead is a working journalist's fluency in statistical reasoning: null hypotheses and alternatives, base rates, multiple comparisons, false discovery rates, selection effects, priors on the intentions of an author or redactor, data-generating processes, model fit versus overfitting. Fluent, not expert — I can think in these terms, but I'd fail a graduate seminar in statistics.

What I'd like from you is a systematic translation, not a summary. Restate the deliberate-and-meaningful position and the pattern-hunting position as faithfully as you can in that statistical vocabulary, as if each scholar were forced to express their view as a claim about a data-generating process, a prior, and a test. Keep the translation honest: flag every place where you had to bend a statistical concept to make it carry the hermeneutic claim, and every place where a claim simply has no counterpart in my vocabulary.

Then give me the residue report. Of the material that did not survive translation, which parts failed because my statistical idiom is genuinely too narrow for what the interpreters are talking about, and which parts failed because the claim was never doing more work than the bare observation that a pattern is being noticed? I also want you to identify which node of the translation is doing the real work, and what evidence or argument would shift it. I'm not asking you to rule on the truth of any particular religious text — I'm asking what the two sides of this methodological dispute look like once they are made to speak in the language of inference.
