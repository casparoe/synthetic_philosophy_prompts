I'm a software engineer — never took a philosophy class in my life — but I've been reading up on Eliciting Latent Knowledge (ELK) and I wrote a short essay that I'd like someone with actual philosophical training to critique. I'm fairly sure there's a hole in it, but I can't pinpoint it, probably something I should of caught myself. Tell me where the argument goes wrong and be as specific as you can. Here's the draft:

ELK is mostly a calibration problem.

People talk about a model's latent knowledge as if it's locked away somewhere we can't reach. That seems backwards to me. If a model can fluently describe a situation — like what a security camera is showing — then its internal state must already encode that situation. Where else would the words come from? So the hard part is supposedly reading the state out, but the model already gives us a readout for free: the next-token probabilities. For any claim s, I can feed it a prompt like Statement: s. Is that true? Answer yes or no. and take the softmax probability assigned to the token "yes" as the model's degree of belief that s is true. Nothing exotic — this is just P(token | context), the same quantity the model computes at the output layer anyway.

The obvious objection is that the model can say something false even when it knows better. But that's just miscalibration. If the model is well calibrated, then when it assigns 0.8 to "yes," the claim should be true about 80% of the time. And calibration is a solved-ish engineering problem: temperature scaling, ensembling, prompting the model to think out loud before committing, that sort of thing. As calibration gets better, the gap between what the model says and what it knows shrinks to zero.

So I want to conclude ELK is not a deep problem at all. It's calibration engineering plus a bit of prompt design to get the model to commit to one token. The philosophical point that a language model predicts tokens rather than world states strikes me as a technicality — the token "yes" picks out exactly the state of affairs I care about, and its probability is a perfectly usable measurement of what the model thinks.

Am I just not seeing the hard part? Be harsh. In particular I want to know whether my step from "the network's internal state encodes the answer" to "the output probabilities are a faithful readout of that answer" is the load-bearing one. If that's the mistake, exactly why does it fail? And if it isn't, where's the real fork in the road?
