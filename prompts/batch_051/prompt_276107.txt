I'm on my university's animal care and use committee, and were stuck on a protocol that has to be voted on Thursday. It's from a neuroscience lab that wants to run about two hundred crayfish through a study where they injure parts of the tail and record from the nerve cord afterward, without any anesthesia or analgesia. Their justification is that decapod crustaceans are invertebrates with no cortex, so the escape responses are "just nociceptive reflexes, not pain." A few of us started pushing on whether that distinction can carry the weight they're putting on it, and now the committee is split. One faction wants to reject it outright as likely cruel. Another says this isn't even in our purview, since our charge covers vertebrates and the lab only came to us voluntarily; they also point out that we don't have reliable anesthetic doses for crayfish, so requiring anesthesia might kill more animals than it spares. A third faction wants to approve it with conditions like local analgesics and caps on the number of animals, but nobody can actually say the analgesics do anything for a crayfish.

Underneath this is the question of where consciousness actually starts in the animal kingdom and what a committee should do when it doesn't know. A rat can't tell us it's suffering either, but we don't make it prove it has a human-style self-model before we take its pain seriously — so why is a crayfish guilty until proven sentient? Then again, if the bar is just "has nociceptors and responds to damage," fruit fly larvae clear it too, and at some point "better safe than sorry" turns into a category error. I've ben going back and forth on this all week, and honestly it's gotten personal: my kid's class had a crayfish over spring break and it ended up at our house, and watching the damn thing rearrange its tank and flare its claws at me made me a lot less sure it's just a little biological robot.

Some members keep bringing up that the UK's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 lists decapod crustaceans and cephalopods alongside vertebrates as sentient, which makes our "invertebrates don't count" boilerplate look dated. But I also can't just import another country's line and call it a reason.

Please draft the short decision memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should come down on one side — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — and give the committee reasons we can defend. Whatever you recommend, make it concrete enough that I could turn it into a motion, and make sure the strongest argument for the losing side gets a fair run, because somebody is going to be angry either way.
