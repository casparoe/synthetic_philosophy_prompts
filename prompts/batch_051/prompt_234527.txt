I'm a minister at a midsize Episcopal church, and next month I have to preach on petitionary prayer. The trigger: in adult ed we read something on praying to an omniscient and immutable God, and the room split badly. One group said the whole practice is pointless or even superstitious—if God already knows what you need and isn't going to change, then asking can't do anything. Another group got defensive and basically landed on "it's a mystery, shut up." I'm supposed to give the sermon that holds the room back together, but I won't do it by papering over the problem.

The constraints are real. Twelve minutes, ordinary Sunday morning, and the congregation is a mix: old farmers, a few engineers and physicists, at least one retired philosophy professor who will catch every dodge, and a family whose teenage son is dying and who pray constantly. So the sermon can't turn into a philosophy lecture, and it can't quietly reduce prayer to therapy or positive thinking—that would be a pastoral and theological failure aimed straight at the family in the back. By my own lights, God is not a being who changes his mind because enough people asked, and I also believe petitionary prayer is a real practice, not just self-talk. I need a sermon that does justice to both commitments at once, without pretending the tension isn't there.

Please write the sermon. You can use one homey analogy—a parent and a child asking for something, say—but the analogy has to actually fit the metaphysics and not smuggle in a God who changes his mind at the last second, because the retired philosopher will stand up at coffee hour and explain exactly where it broke.

Then, after the sermon, give me a separate note (not to be read aloud) telling me honestly where the sermon leans on a contested move in the theology of prayer and where it stays on ground that serious Christians across traditions mostly share. Keep the note in the same plain language as the sermon—no bibliography, no jargon—and dont present a contested view, like whether petition can ever affect what God does, as if it were settled.
