A colleague passed me a copy of a popular philosophy compendium in which Peter Singer's article 'Famine, Affluence, and Morality' is condensed into the following gloss:

Singer starts from the uncontroversial claim that if you see a child drowning in a shallow pond, you ought to wade in and save the child, even though it will muddy your clothes, because the child's life is worth more than the cost of clean clothes. He then says that the same principle applies to the global poor: because you can prevent deaths by donating to famine relief at relatively little cost to yourself, you are morally required to give until the point where any further sacrifice would harm you as much as the harm you would prevent. On this view, buying anything beyond bare necessities is wrong while people are starving, and the distinction between duty and charity collapses entirely.

I am not sure whether this is a fair summary. I have since read Singer's original essay, and I found a passage, roughly a page into the section where he responds to objections, that seems directly relevant to the status of the drowning child example and to the role of physical distance. The passage, as I recall its content, runs along these lines: Singer denies that the principle he is defending is weakened by proximity or distance; he says it makes no moral difference whether the person one can help is a neighbour's child or a distant stranger whose name one will never know; and he then observes that the contemporary development of a 'global village', with modern communications and relief organisations, has changed our moral situation by expanding the class of people to whom we can render effective help far beyond those physically near us.

I suspect the compendium's claim about giving until further sacrifice harms you as much as the harm prevented is stronger than Singer's actual argument, but I cannot tell whether the rest is fair. Please audit the compendium's summary: which parts are accurate representations of Singer's position, which are overstatements or conflations, and which conclusions attributed to him would a careful defender of his argument reject? In particular, does the summary correctly handle what Singer does and does not claim about how much one must give and about the duty/charity distinction?
