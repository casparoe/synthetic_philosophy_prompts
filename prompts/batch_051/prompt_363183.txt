So I've ended up thinking about humility a lot this year, partly because my girlfriend's family are pretty devout and treat humility like the central virtue, and partly because my manager at work told me I keep underselling my contributions in reviews and its costing me actual money. A friend who did a philosophy degree sent me an argument he says shows intellectual humility cant really be a virtue — it either does nothing or it collapses into underconfidence. The conclusion feels like it proves too much, but I can't find the fault, and it's bugging me.

Here's the argument, with the steps numbered like he wrote them:

(1) Intellectual humility is, at least in part, the disposition to hold your beliefs less confidently than you would by default, out of awareness that you might be wrong.
(2) For any belief you have, you might be wrong — fallibility covers everything.
(3) Therefore, an intellectually humble person must hold every one of their beliefs less confidently than they would by default.
(4) But your default confidence, if it's set by your evidence, already matches what the evidence supports; so "less confidently than default" means less confidently than your evidence supports.
(5) So the humble person is systematically under-confident relative to their evidence, about everything.
(6) There's no non-arbitrary discount to stop at. Say you shave off 10% — but you might be wrong that 10% is the right amount, so humility pushes you to shave off more, and so on, so the only stable endpoint is confidence collapsing toward zero.
(7) A policy of holding all your beliefs below what the evidence supports, tending to nothing, isn't a virtue; it's a defect — servility or timidity.
(8) The only way to block the collapse is to say humility doesn't require lowering confidence at all, but then it's vacuous.
(9) So intellectual humility is either self-undermining or empty, and it can't be the virtue people say it is.

I'd really like to keep the idea that intellectual humility sometimes *is* a virtue, and (2) feels undeniably true to me, so something in between has to fail, but I can't tell which step is doing the real work or whether the whole chain is just valid and I'm stuck swallowing the conclusion.
