I am convening a working group to draft recommendations on the governance of griefbots and other postmortem agents — systems that reconstruct a deceased person as a chatbot, voice agent, or video avatar from the person's texts, recordings, and social media. Before I read the group's policy options, I want a specification of what an adequate ethical theory in this area must get right, whatever position it ultimately takes.

Write the desiderata for a theory of the ethics of simulating the dead, with the case, distinction, or empirical fact behind each requirement, and with each requirement marked as non-negotiable for any adequate theory or as a preference of one camp. Cover at least the following, and add any further requirements the standard would be incomplete without:

- the canonical and hard cases it must be able to adjudicate without ad hoc exceptions — for example, a parent quietly simulating a dead child; a company selling simulations of a dead celebrity; a simulation that invents recollections to fill gaps in its training data; a simulation of someone who would have found the practice abhorrent; a simulation that one survivor uses while others who knew the deceased find it distressing; and a user who comes to prefer the simulation to living relationships;
- the distinctions it may not collapse — for example, between modeling a person and continuing that person's life, between honoring a pre-mortem statement and attributing new words to the dead after the fact, between private use and commercial provision, and between value to the immediate user and effects on third parties and the public record;
- the empirical constraints it may not contradict — for example, that generated outputs can be fluent and confident while false, that digital traces are curated and partial, and that grief has no standard timeline, with reminders of the deceased being adaptive for some people and harmful for others depending on context;
- the interests it must be able to represent — at minimum, those of the deceased, the primary user, third parties who knew the deceased, and, for public figures, the integrity of public memory.

Then state which subsets of these desiderata, if any, cannot be jointly satisfied, and how deep each conflict is. Identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude candidate views. Finally, score the main families of positions briefly against the list — for instance, views centered on the wishes of the deceased, views centered on the welfare of the bereaved, and views centered on dignity or public memory. Keep the scoring to a short table; the desiderata and their motivating cases are the deliverable.
