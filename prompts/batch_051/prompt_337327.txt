I'm a PhD student in philosophy, working mostly on al-Ghazālī's occasionalism about causation, and I've been turning over whether his central argument is actually still live or whether the modern debate has just moved somewhere else.

Here's the setup as I understand it. Al-Ghazālī's argument in the fire-and-cotton case is that when fire touches cotton and the cotton burns, all we observe is a sequence: fire, then burning. We don't observe a necessary connection between the two, we don't perceive a power in the fire that forces the burning, we just get used to the order. So he concludes that the philosophers go beyond what experience licenses when they say fire causes the burning; strictly speaking God is the only efficient cause, and what look like natural causes are only occasions for God's habitual action. So far so good.

But here's the development that's nagging me. A lot of contemporary work on causation—interventionist or difference-making accounts, of the kind that define causation by whether you can wiggle one variable in a controlled way and watch the effect variable wiggle correspondingly—doesn't claim to find a necessary connection in nature either. It explicitly treats the search for hidden oomph or productive necessity as a wrong turn. Causation, on those views, is basically about stable, exploitable patterns of dependence, not about a metaphysical glue. If that's the going picture, then al-Ghazālī's whole argument seems to be aimed at a target almost nobody in that corner of the debate is defending. They could reply that, right, there's no glue; the invariant dependence is all they meant.

So my question is: does al-Ghazālī's argument survive this changed assumption, does it now support something else, or is it just defanged? I want you to work through the structure, not give me a survey. Specifically: which premise of his argument does the interventionist account bear on, and does it actually touch his conclusion, or only its target? Could he rerun the argument one level up—say, by asking what justifies treating our interventions on X as different in kind from God's habitual creation of the sequence? And, as a slightly uncomfortable final thing, if we do adopt the interventionist definition, would al-Ghazālī's own occasionalism count as a causal theory of nature (with God as the wiggler) rather then a denial of natural causation?
