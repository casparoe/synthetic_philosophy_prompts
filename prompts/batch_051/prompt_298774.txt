I am a novelist rather than a philosopher, and I make most of my writing decisions in the vocabulary of craft: narrative desire and the curiosity gap, suspense and withheld information, exposition, setup and payoff, closure, subplots, and the recurring editorial judgment that a scene or detail either earns its place or is merely decorative. I have no formal philosophy background, but I have found myself entangled in a philosophical question through a practical problem. I am drafting a novel about an aging translator who spends decades reconstructing a minor dead language that no one needs — no dramatic payoff, no practical use. My editor keeps telling me the research scenes do not advance anything, and I half agree; at the same time, cutting them offends something in me. I want to know whether my craft vocabulary is stacking the deck in favor of one side of an old philosophical argument.

The argument, as I understand it, concerns whether curiosity and knowledge pursued for its own sake have final, non-instrumental value, or only instrumental value. The sharp version is something like this: a person who knows something may have no practical advantage over a person who merely holds the same true belief, and yet many of us regard the knower as better off or more admirable than the lucky guesser. People who work on this also ask the parallel question about curiosity — whether it is a virtue in its own right or only a useful drive that tends to produce good outcomes. Please restate this cluster of positions, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary of the novelist's craft. For each position — instrumentalism about the value of knowledge and curiosity, final-value views, and any important middle positions — say what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression in craft terms at all. For example, does instrumental value map onto setup and payoff, and does final value map onto something like lingering, texture, or rereading?

Then give me the residue report. I especially want to know whether craft vocabulary is essentially committed to an instrumental picture of curiosity, because every planted element must pay off later or be cut, or whether there are craft concepts that express something like final value. And tell me whether the parts that do not survive the translation are a defect of my craft framework or a sign that the philosophers' distinction between final and instrumental value is less clear than it looks. Throughout, please separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. If my description of the value problem is itself a caricature, correct the description before translating it.
