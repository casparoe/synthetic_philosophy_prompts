I have been defending a view in a seminar on collective decision-making, and I would like help mapping out what, precisely, it stands or falls with. The view is a piece of Condorcetian optimism, not the jury theorem itself but the philosophical position people build on top of it: that, under the right conditions, the majority of a sufficiently large group is more likely to arrive at the correct answer than any individual member, and that this supplies a real though defeasible reason to prefer majority rule over rule by experts or by a single decider. I accept the mathematics of the theorem as far as it goes; what interests me is the broader commitment that majority rule has a kind of built-in epistemic authority.

My question is not the standard objection that real voters are uninformed. I want a fuller account of what would count against the position. Which observations, formal results, and concrete cases would undermine it, and which would merely complicate it? For example, I can see that violations of voter independence, correlated errors from a shared information environment, strategic voting, the restriction to binary choices, and the assumption that there is a determinate correct answer all put pressure on the view. But I am unsure which of these a committed Condorcetian would have to concede as a genuine defeater and which they could reasonably dismiss as a change of subject or as already built into the phrase "under the right conditions." I would also like to know what the position predicts that its rivals do not, and whether the retreat to "under the right conditions" is a legitimate qualification or a form of insulation that makes the view unfalsifiable. The deliverable I am after is a ranked map of its exposure: which defeaters are fatal, which are merely awkward, and whether a view can survive being this heavily qualified while still doing the political work its defenders want it to do.
