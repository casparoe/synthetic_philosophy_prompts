I organize an interdisciplinary workshop series for early-career researchers in economics, physics, and philosophy, and next month's session is on risk and time. I'll have about two hours with roughly twenty participants. Before any lecture or naming of concepts, I want them to inhabit the distinction between what happens to a typical individual over time and what happens to the aggregate. Design a runnable activity: I'm imagining a repeated multiplicative betting game in which every participant starts with the same bankroll and each round a fair coin determines whether their current bankroll is multiplied by 1.5 or by 0.6, with the whole bankroll bet each round. Give me exact rules, the number of rounds, what the facilitator records and posts on the board (group total, mean, median, percentiles, and a few visible individual trajectories), and how to keep the game moving in the time available. Also specify one or two variations to run after the first shock registers, such as allowing participants to bet only a fixed fraction of their bankroll each round, or allowing them to pool and rebalance. Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the game—the rising mean against the collapsing median, the few lucky trajectories carrying the average—to the distinction between ensemble averages and time averages, and that makes clear why a positive expected value each round does not guide any single participant in how they will actually fare over time. The debrief should say what predictably goes wrong when this game is run and how to handle it, and be frank about what the game cannot reproduce: continuous-time dynamics, genuinely independent repeated gambles, infinite horizons, and the fact that real agents can sometimes diversify or stop betting altogether. Participants should leave having felt the force of non-ergodicity before the word is given to them, and with a sense of why maximizing expected wealth can diverge from maximizing what a single agent can expect to experience over time.
