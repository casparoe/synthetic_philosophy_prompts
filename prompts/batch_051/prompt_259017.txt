I am a doctoral student in political theory, and in the course of preparing a chapter on constitutionalism I keep meeting the claim that constitutions are best understood as precommitment devices. A recent handbook chapter—by someone whose competence I have no independent reason to doubt—summarised the Ulysses-and-the-Sirens argument in a way I found suspiciously tidy. It ran roughly as follows: 'Elster's central claim is that a constitution is the political equivalent of Ulysses having himself bound to the mast. Democracies know they will at some point be swept away by panic or popular passion, so they lay down constitutional rules—supermajority thresholds, separated powers, entrenched rights—that tie their own hands. The point is that constitutional constraints are a solution to weakness of will: the people, like Ulysses, are bound by a decision taken in a cool hour, and that is what gives constitutional rules their authority over ordinary legislation. This has become the standard justification for constitutional constraints, and its upshot is that a democratic people is genuinely freer when it prevents itself from doing what it will later wish it had not done, just as Ulysses could hear the Sirens only because he surrendered the freedom to steer his ship.'

I have read enough of the underlying literature to suspect that the Ulysses story carries more weight in the argument than this paraphrase lets on, but I am not confident I can say exactly where the gloss goes wrong without importing my own sympathies. Please go through it and tell me which parts a careful defender of the precommitment account would accept, where they would wince, and what is overstated or conflated—and say whether the concluding claim about being 'freer' survives the corrections. Could you lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
