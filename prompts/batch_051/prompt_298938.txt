Construct a thought experiment in which a mid-sized corporation is morally blameworthy for a harmful outcome, while the two main rival positions about corporate blame both deny that the company is blameworthy. The domain is corporate moral agency — specifically, whether a company can be blameworthy when no natural person is.

Here are the specifications the case must satisfy.

First, realism. The scenario should take place in an ordinary commercial setting — a manufacturer, a logistics firm, a financial services company, or the like — with no deliberate wrongdoing, no gross recklessness, no one acting above the law, and no contrived legal device doing the philosophical work.

Second, no culpable individual. Every employee involved must act within the norms of their role and respond competently to the information actually available at their desk. No single person is to see the full causal picture. Each decision, viewed in isolation and with the knowledge its maker had, must be defensible or at least excusable.

Third, an ordinary corporate decision structure is present, but the harmful outcome must not be the object of any collective decision. The company should have a board, a management hierarchy, and standard compliance mechanisms, yet the harm must arise without that structure ever forming a collective intention to take the harmful course or to accept the risk that materializes. The harm should emerge from the organization's design — its role partitions, incentives, reporting lines, or rules for distributing information — rather than from any single decision that someone made badly. Do not stipulate the verdicts; they must be generated by the positions themselves.

Keep the scenario to one paragraph.

The positions to run through the case are these. (i) The individualist: only natural persons can be morally blameworthy, so talk of corporate blame is at best shorthand for blame distributed across individuals; if no individual is blameworthy, nothing is left for the company to be blamed for. (ii) The intentionalist group-agency view: a corporation is blameworthy only when it forms a group intention through its decision procedure and acts on it; without such an intention there is no corporate action of the relevant kind to evaluate. (iii) The structural view: an organization can be blameworthy for harms that are a predictable upshot of how it is arranged — its roles, incentives, reporting lines, and distribution of knowledge — even where no individual and no collective intention is at fault.

After presenting the scenario, demonstrate that it meets the specifications. Show the reasoning each of the three positions gives about the case, and identify the precise structural feature that does the work: what the structural view points to, why the two rival views are forced to deny blame, and what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed. Then give the strongest attack available to a defender of the intentionalist view — for example, that the board's standing decision to delegate amounts to a tacit collective intention, or that blame without any agent's intention is not really blame — and say whether the case survives it. Conclude by stating whether the case establishes that corporate blame can come apart from both individual blame and collective intention, or whether one of the rival views can absorb the case after all.
