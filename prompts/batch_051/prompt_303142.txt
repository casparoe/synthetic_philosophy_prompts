I'm a retired entomologist, so I'm comfortable with the ant-colony end of this but never really learned the microbiome side, and lately I've been reading into philosophy of biology. A popular science piece I read summarized the current state of things roughly like this. Biologists used to ask where one organism ends and another begins, but the microbiome revolution has settled the question: every animal is really a holobiont, a host plus its resident microbes, and natural selection works on the whole consortium rather than on the host genome alone, so the old picture of the individual organism is a fiction—a cow is, biologically speaking, a walking microbial ecosystem. The same logic extends to ant colonies: because the workers are sterile and only the colony as a whole reproduces, a colony is a single superorganism, and a worker ant is no more an individual than a liver cell is. People only resist these conclusions because they are sentimental about big mammals. My sense is that this is partly right and partly overreach—my old ant colleages used to say the superorganism idea is a useful heuristic, not a settled fact, and the cow line sounds like a punchline. But I don't know the holobiont literature well enough to say where the fair summary ends and the overstatement begins. Which parts would a careful biologist accept, which would they wince at, and does the conclusion about worker ants actually follow from the holobiont claim, or is that a seperate argument?
