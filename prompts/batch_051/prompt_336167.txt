So I got into an argument with a friend of mine who reads alot of philosophy, and he keeps insisting that self-deception isn't actually paradoxical. His go-to move is to compare the mind to an organization that hides information from itself: the PR department quietly buries a bad report before the CEO sees it, so the company as a whole "knows" the bad news (it's sitting in the files, a few employees saw it) and also doesn't know it (the person making the decisions never did). He says self-deception is just that — information-hiding between sub-systems — so it doesnt require a single self that both believes P and not-P, which is the thing people always say is impossible.

Being a backend engineer, the version I can't shake is: self-deception is like a service that deletes or rewrites its own error logs before the monitoring dashboard scrapes them. The dashboard says everything's green, the logs (if you knew where to look) are full of stack traces, and there's no single component that both knows and doesn't know — the log-filtering code has the data, the dashboard doesn't, and the system as a whole is quietly lying to itself. My friend uses exactly this kind of picture to conclude that self-deception is obviously possible, because we already accept that complex systems partition information and present a clean story back to their own control plane.

I'd like you to audit that analogy properly. Give me the correspondence table — what corresponds to the dashboard, the logs, the log-scrubbing filter, the engineer who configured the filter, the on-call responder, maybe even the "incident" itself. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which of the disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion he wants (that self-deception is possible and not paradoxical) versus which are just cosmetic. I'm especially interested in whether the analogy quietly imports a designer — somebody had to write the filtering logic — and what it would mean for the self-deception case if there's no equivalent of that. And say whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing all the work.
