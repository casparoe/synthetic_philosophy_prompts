I am a novelist, and I have noticed that when I weigh a character's inner life I find myself presupposing an arithmetic of experience: a stretch of consciousness counts, for me, as the vividness with which it is felt multiplied by how good or bad it feels, so that a brief, fully alert agony can outweigh a long spell of half-conscious unease. I want to know whether that arithmetic tracks something about the structure of value itself or only something about my own sensibility. Treat the following as a partially testable conceptual claim: the value of a stretch of experience is the product of its phenomenal intensity and its hedonic valence — not the sum of the two, and not a lexicographic ordering in which valence is lexically prior and intensity enters only as a tiebreaker when valence is equal. Design the strongest empirical test you can to distinguish the multiplicative, additive, and lexical accounts. Specify the subjects, the task, and the measurements; state which pattern of results would favor each account; and then say precisely what even a decisive result would fail to settle, including the point at which the operationalization of value parts company with the concept.
