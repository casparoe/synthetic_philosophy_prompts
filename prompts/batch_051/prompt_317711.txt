I'm a novelist, and last week at a dinner I watched two friends argue a point so persistently that I'm still turning it over. I want to render this argument accurately in the novel I'm drafting, and I'd also like to understand whose reasoning actually holds up.

The background: a few weeks before our dinner, an anonymous account posted an audio clip purporting to be a mayoral candidate accepting a cash bribe. It circulated widely; then forensic analysts concluded the clip was almost certainly synthetic — a deepfake. The correction traveled far less well than the original.

My two friends — Miriam, a criminal defense attorney, and Evan, a documentary radio producer — turned to what the real damage of such fakes is. I can reproduce the exchange nearly verbatim from notes I took that evening.

(1) Miriam said the central harm isn't that some people were fooled this time. It's that, once deepfakes are common, every inconvenient recording can be waved away as probably fake. Audio and video used to function as something like an epistemic backstop for public life — a record that didn't depend on anyone's say-so. Deepfakes remove that backstop.

(2) Evan replied that the backstop was never in the recording itself. It was in the practices around recordings: editors, chain of custody, corroboration. An anonymous leak was never self-authenticating, so the “backstop” she's describing was already a social and institutional achievement, not a property of the file.

(3) Miriam came back: institutions bottom out in recordings too. A court admits the tape; a newsroom publishes the audio; a congressional hearing plays the clip. If the pieces of evidence those institutions handle become optional, the institutions lose their floor, not just a convenience.

(4) Evan: courts don't treat a recording as self-authenticating — there are witnesses, experts, rules of evidence. The fix for deepfakes is more scaffolding: provenance records, forensic authentication, better editorial standards. The floor was never the file; it was the process around it.

(5) Miriam: process is too slow and too narrow. The fake got millions of views before any analysis; the correction reached a sliver. So the practical default becomes not “wait for authentication” but “believe whichever version matches your politics.” That's a different damage than deception — it's the corrosion of the very idea of a shared record.

(6) Evan: corrosion of trust in unverified clips is progress. People shouldn't have treated an anonymous audio drop as decisive evidence in the first place. What she's mourning as a loss of a backstop was largely credulity.

(7) Miriam: call it credulity if you like, but the same credulity is what gives a whistleblower's recording or a body-camera video its power. If every real recording can be dismissed as synthetic, the people best positioned to benefit are the powerful. That's not skepticism doing its work — it's a new shield for official deniability.

They then looked to me, and I admitted I couldn't referee. I can see that each is right about some local point without knowing what the overall verdict should be.

Please adjudicate the argument move by move. For each numbered move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and explain briefly. Then identify what, if anything, each side concedes too quickly or fails to account for, and give an overall assessment of who has the stronger case.
