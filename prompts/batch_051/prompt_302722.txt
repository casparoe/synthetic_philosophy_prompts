I chair a workplace safety review board at a regional chemical plant. We meet monthly after any serious injury or near miss to assign contributing causes and recommend corrective actions; our recommendations go to the plant manager, who has final sign-off. Last year we deadlocked over an incident. A technician was overcome by fumes while cleaning a tank. He had entered without first testing the air, contrary to written procedure. But the ventilation system was also running poorly that day, and the supervisor had canceled the morning safety briefing. Some members said the failure to test the air caused the injury; others said an omission cannot cause anything—only the ventilation failure or the technician's entry can. We need a consistent way to decide when an omission counts as a cause.

I have been reading on my own about causation, and I was struck by John Stuart Mill's discussion in A System of Logic. As I understand him, Mill treats the complete cause of an event as the whole set of antecedent conditions, positive and negative alike; a negative condition—the absence of something that would have prevented the event—is part of that complete cause. But he also observes that ordinary speech singles out one condition and calls it the cause, usually the one we can control or need to correct, and that this selection is practical rather than an analysis of everything that made the event happen. That distinction seems exactly right for our reviews, but I do not know how to make it usable.

Please turn this into a procedure my board can use at our monthly reviews. We have members with engineering, production, and safety backgrounds but no philosophy; we have about twenty minutes per case. The procedure should list the questions to ask in order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values or judgment rather than fact. Mark the steps that cannot be made fully mechanical. Then run the procedure on two cases: the tank-cleaning injury above, and a case where a shift team skipped a weekly inspection but the inspection checklist later turned out to contain an error, so that performing the inspection would not have caught the defect that led to the injury. I especially want you to say whether the procedure forces us to treat that second omission as a cause, because most of my board will not like the answer.
