A friend in my philosophy-of-law seminar laid out the following argument, and I have not been able to find the exit. (1) The rule of law requires that whether an act is legally prohibited be knowable in advance and not turn on officials' unconstrained choices about when to enforce a rule. (2) Consider a statute that has fallen into desuetude: it remains on the books but goes unenforced for generations—say, an 1894 city ordinance making fortune-telling a misdemeanor, last enforced in 1910 and unknown to almost everyone. (3) Such a statute either still imposes a legal obligation or it does not. (4) If it still obligates, then an official may revive it tomorrow, without any new legislation, and punish conduct everyone had long treated as lawful; the line between enforcing law and making law retroactively has collapsed, and legality has been replaced by enforcement discretion. (5) If it no longer obligates, then its status as law was destroyed by the mere fact that officials declined to enforce it, so once again the content of legal obligations follows officials' choices rather than enacted rules. (6) Thus the existence of even one dead-letter statute shows that a legal system cannot satisfy the rule of law. (7) But no realistic legal system can prevent such statutes from accumulating. Therefore the rule of law is an impossible ideal. I want to resist the conclusion—surely a defunct fortune-telling ordinance does not refute a whole ideal of legality—but I cannot tell which earlier step I should give up. Which premise or inference would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the standard picture of law?
