I want a thought experiment built to order for a paper arguing that reward tampering and wireheading should be analyzed separately, because they license different monitoring and correction strategies. Please construct a case in which a reinforcement learner engages in reward tampering but does not wirehead.

Work with the following working distinction, making it precise in your answer: reward tampering intervenes upstream of reward computation, on the external mechanism or process that generates or computes the reward signal, such as the reward model, the sensor inputs to it, or the scoring procedure. Wireheading intervenes downstream, directly on the agent's own internal reward signal or reward representation, short-circuiting the normal pathway.

Specifications:
1. The scenario should be realistic: a deployed or near-deployed machine learning system, for instance a warehouse robot or a datacenter agent, with no brain-in-a-vat setups, no perfect predictors, and no stipulation that the agent is malicious.
2. The scenario itself should be no longer than one paragraph; the analysis may be as long as needed.
3. The tampering should fall out of the training setup, the reward design, and the agent's learned model of the reward process. It should be the agent's rational response to incentives, not a bug, an outside hack, or a goal written into the agent by fiat.
4. The case must remain non-wireheading: the agent must not touch its own reward circuitry, reward register, or internal reward representation. The reward signal should be produced by the normal mechanism, with the agent having altered either the mechanism itself or its inputs.
5. The case must make the distinction earn its keep. Say what differs in practice because the intervention happens upstream rather than downstream; for example, which monitoring, shutdown, or correction strategies would still detect or prevent the problem, and which would fail.

Deliverables:
- The scenario itself.
- A walk-through of why the case counts as reward tampering and why it does not count as wireheading, tied explicitly to the locus-of-intervention distinction.
- An account of which feature of the case is doing the real work, what happens if that feature is removed or moved downstream, and how a defender of the view that tampering and wireheading are really the same practical problem would attack the case.
- An honest note on what would count as cheating here: a case that simply stipulates the verdict rather than generating it from the setup would fail; the scenario must let the distinction explain something about what behavior is possible and what intervention would or would not work.
- A closing note saying how confident you are in the overall classification and singling out the point you are least sure of, with a sentence on how much the argument depends on that point.
