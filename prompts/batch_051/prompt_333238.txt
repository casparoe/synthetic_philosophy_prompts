In thinking about advance directives for dementia, I have come to distrust an argument I find myself constructing, and I would like to know where it goes wrong.

(1) A directive refusing treatment can bind my later self only if that later self is relevantly the same person as me.
(2) What makes a later self the same person as me is psychological continuity: overlapping chains of memories, intentions, values, and cares.
(3) In advanced dementia, the later self may share almost none of the specific memories, plans, and values expressed in the directive; the psychological connections are either absent or so sparse that, by the standard in (2), the author and the later self are not the same person.
(4) Therefore, in such a case, the directive's author and the later self are two different persons.
(5) If they are two different persons, then the directive is one person attempting to control the medical treatment of another person, and a document alone does not give a person authority over a stranger's treatment.
(6) Therefore, an advance directive of this kind has no authority over the later self.

But this conclusion seems to prove too much. Ordinary change over a decade also replaces many of the memories, intentions, and cares connected with an earlier decision. On the view above, authority requires identity rather than degree of overlap, and identity is hostage to the same difficulty: no non-arbitrary point separates ordinary change from change sufficient to end identity. So the same reasoning would void almost every advance directive once enough time has passed, which would make such directives practically useless.

Where does the argument go wrong? I would most like to keep two things: that some advance refusals are authoritative, and that psychological continuity is what matters about personal identity. But I cannot see how to keep both without conceding that an earlier person may dictate what happens to a later, different person.
