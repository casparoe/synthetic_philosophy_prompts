I got into an argument with my roommate about whether a virus is alive, and now I’m trying to figure out what I actually think about life in general. Here are my gut calls on cases, and I’m wondering if there’s a principle that captures them, or close to it. A mule: alive, obviously, even though it can’t have offspring, so reproduction can’t be necessary. A bacterial spore that sits in permafrost for ten thousand years and then revives: I want to say it was alive the whole time, even though it wasn’t metabolizing or responding to anything while dormant. A fire: not alive, no matter how much fuel it “eats,” how much it grows, or how it sends out sparks that start new fires — it lacks something, maybe internal organization. A virus like influenza: not alive, but it feels weird to call it dead either; it has a genome and it evolves, which is the whole reason we need new flu shots every year. If it evolves, how is it not alive? A red blood cell in my own body: alive, I’d say, but it can’t reproduce and it’s lost its nucleus, so it’s kind of like a little machine that happens to be inside me. A prion that causes other proteins to misfold and thereby “replicates”: definitely not alive. A robot that can extract raw materials from its environment, maintain itself, repair itself, and build copies of itself: I have a strong urge to say not alive, but I can’t articulate why that’s different from a bacterium doing basically the same thing.

My current working principle is something like: alive iff it has a membrane, does metabolism, and belongs to a lineage shaped by natural selection. But that would exclude the fire (no membrane), include the mule (metabolizes, in a lineage), include the spore (lineage, even if currently not metabolizing), and then break on the designed synthetic cell — if we built a cell that metabolizes and divides, it seems alive to me even though it never underwent natural selection. So maybe my principle is just wrong. Can you give me a principle — or a small set of candidates — that gets most of these, and tell me what it says about a few cases I keep going back and forth on: (a) a fully synthetic cell built in a lab that metabolizes and divides but has never evolved, (b) a computer program that copies itself, mutates, and gets selected in a simulated environment, and (c) a extraterrestrial thing we find on another planet that takes in chemicals, grows, and reproduces but has no cells in any recognizable sense? And I’d appreciate an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle can’t accommodate, so I know what I’d have to give up.
