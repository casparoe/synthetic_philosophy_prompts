I hold the regulative principle of worship in its strong form: in the public worship of God, whatever is not commanded in Scripture is forbidden, so that every element of a service needs positive biblical warrant before it may be admitted. I want to know what would count against this view, and how exposed it really is.

Please give me the evidence and arguments that should move a convinced regulativist, ordered from decisive to merely awkward. Cast the net wider than the usual polemics. I am interested in:

- exegetical findings that would bear on the principle's assumption that Scripture functions as a sufficient command-manual for liturgy, or that would show the apostolic church tolerated an element of worship without recoverable textual warrant;
- historical findings about what the earliest churches did and whether the principle can accommodate them without the element/circumstance distinction doing all the work by fiat;
- internal instabilities, especially the line between elements of worship and mere circumstances such as time, language, seating, and length, and the line between something commanded and something reached only by good and necessary consequence;
- the basic asymmetry the principle assumes: why Scripture's silence should count as prohibition in worship rather than permission, and whether that asymmetry can be justified without already assuming the principle;
- pressures that would force the strong principle to collapse into the weaker normative principle that whatever is not forbidden is at least permissible, or into a purely local tradition.

I want an honest map of the view's exposure. Which of these would a committed defender concede as counting against them, as opposed to dismissing as already handled? Which are fatal rather than merely awkward? And tell me whether the principle as it actually functions, with its standard qualifications, risks anything at all, or whether it has been insulated so thoroughly that no possible finding could refute it — and if so, whether that insulation is a problem for a principle that presents itself as a test of true worship.

End with a single-sentence summary of your overall verdict, as the last line.
