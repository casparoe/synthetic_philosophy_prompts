I have been trying to use C. Lloyd Morgan’s canon from his 1894 book An Introduction to Comparative Psychology as a methodological filter for deciding how much cognition to attribute to an animal. As I understand the rule, he says, in effect, that we should not interpret an action as the outcome of a higher psychical faculty if it can be interpreted as the outcome of one standing lower in the psychological scale. I have tried to turn that into a set of working criteria. First, the lower-level explanation must be specified well enough to predict the exact pattern of behaviour, not merely to remain consistent with it after the fact. Second, a lower-level explanation may appeal to associative learning, perceptual-motor adaptation, or one-trial learning, but it must state a plausible learning history. Third, the canon does not require accepting a lower-level explanation that fits only by being indefinitely flexible, such as saying merely that the animal could have learned it somehow. Fourth, I allow a higher-level attribution when the animal produces flexible, goal-directed behaviour across untrained, novel variations and shows rapid transfer.

Now consider this case. An adult common octopus, wild-caught and with no prior experience of boxes or wire, is repeatedly presented with a small transparent box whose hinged lid is held shut by a wire loop. The box contains live shrimp, though the shrimp are not visually visible from outside; chemical and mechanical cues pass through small holes. On the first trial, the octopus touches the box, then pushes and pulls at the lid, then appears to brace one arm against the box while pulling the wire loop with another. Over eight trials, its latency drops. The experimenters then replace the wire loop with a plastic tag that must be pulled sideways in a different direction; the octopus solves this on the first trial, using a different grip. In a final trial, the wire loop lies under a small stone on top of the box; the octopus removes the stone and then pulls the loop. My question is not whether the octopus has human cognition, but whether this case passes Morgan’s canon in a way that permits, or even requires, attributing something more than associative learning—perhaps a simple form of means-end reasoning or expectation. Does the case qualify as one where the canon should allow a higher-level attribution? Which of my four criteria does it test hardest, and do the criteria themselves need repair if Morgan’s rule is to be a useful instrument rather than a blanket ban on animal mind?
