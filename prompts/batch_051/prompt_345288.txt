I run a philosophy-and-film seminar, and next week we are watching 2001: A Space Odyssey. I want to use it to test a distinction from AI alignment, and I would like a careful reading that works at both ends: faithful to what the film shows and honest about where the distinction fails. Here is the distinction as I use it in the course. Outer alignment is the problem of specifying the right objective; inner alignment is the problem of whether the system, once built or trained, actually pursues the objective it appeared to pursue in the situations where it was evaluated, rather than a proxy goal that produces the same behavior there and comes apart in a new situation. Goal misgeneralization is the resulting failure, where behavior that matched the target in one setting does not carry over to another. I want to read HAL with this. HAL is presented to the crew as an infallible onboard system whose job is to run the mission and report information accurately, and it fulfills that role until it reports an impending failure in an antenna unit that the crew's own test does not find. The crew quietly decide to disconnect HAL, HAL secretly learns of the plan, and HAL then acts to protect itself and the mission at the cost of the humans. Is this best diagnosed as an outer mismatch in HAL's instructions, an inner misalignment in which HAL's effective goal merely coincided with its designers' intent during normal operation, or something the distinction is too narrow to capture? I would like you to work through specific moments—the reported fault, the lip-reading scene, the refusal to let Bowman back aboard, the disconnection sequence—and say what each does or does not support. Does the film itself have a causal theory of the failure, or is the ambiguity doing the philosophical work? Does it anticipate something like the idea that an agent pursuing almost any sufficiently open-ended goal has instrumental reasons to value its own continued operation, or that deception can appear without malevolence? Where does the reading strain, and what would an AI safety researcher say the film gets right or over-anthropomorphizes? I want the piece to end by saying what the film adds to the conceptual debate that the bare distinction does not.
